# Blending HCD with Multicultural Education: Lessons Learned from Co-Designing an Intervention

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teacher education programs have incorporated multicultural education courses into their program requirements to better prepare preservice teachers (PSTs) to enact humanizing and culturally responsive teaching practices. This paper describes a collaborative effort between instructors of one such course and a design thinking center to integrate human-centered design practices, particularly those that foster empathy, with a goal of helping PSTs develop empathic responses for historically excluded communities. While our initial efforts integrated HCD into the course based on instructors' input, the third iteration invited course instructors into the design process, leading to a co-designed interactive workbook that wove HCD practices and course concepts together so that they built on each other. This iteration saw more buy-in from course instructors and deeper engagement from preservice teachers with the workbook. This suggests how vital it is to invite collaborative partners to engage in the co-design process as part of design-based research.

## Introduction

Teacher preparation programs have responded to call s to better prepare preservice teachers (PSTs) to w ork with diverse student populations and engage in both cult urally responsive and humanizing teaching practices by requiring PSTs to take courses in multicultural and  social justice education (Aasaf et al., 2010) as p art of their teacher education.   These courses aim to help PSTs embrace students’ di verse “cultural knowledge, prior experiences, frames of reference, and performance s tyles … to make learning encounters more relevant t o and effective for them,” (Gay, 2010, p. 29). What Gay d escribes is asset-based thinking, which many scholars argue is a vital element of enacting culturally responsiv e and sustaining teaching in their classrooms (e.g., Gay, 201 0; Ladson-Billings, 1995; Paris, 2012). Some scholars have ar gued that empathy education is a key element in fostering asset-based thinking, priming PSTs to have empathetic res ponses to their students and foster empathic concern (McAllister & Irvine, 2002; Warren, 2014, 2 018). We argue that the kind of empathy teachers ne ed to develop to effectively enact culturally sustaining practices is social empathy-empathy for individuals and communities that differ from one’s own (Segal, 2011, 2018). In prior papers, we  made the case that integrating human-centered design (HCD) into multicultural education courses can help operationalize practices  that foster empathic responses in PSTs for histori cally excluded communities (James et al., 2021, James et al., 2023a, 2023b). In this paper, we continue this conversation by describing the third iteration of our co-design collaboration with a multicultural education  course that PSTs are required to take prior to entering their teache r preparation program at a  large, land-grant institution in the Midwest. This course, which focuses on identity and  difference in educational spaces, aimed for PSTs t o: 1) Analyze how power and privilege shape historically excluded community members opportunities in schools; 2) Describe asset-based frameworks; 3) Reflect on the ways that social identities shape students’ school experiences;

4) Examine issues related to “inclusion, exclusion,  power, and privilege” and how they “play out in

education”(course syllabus); and 5) Think critically about their community-based placement. As part of helping the PSTs achieve these learning goals, we designed an interactive workbook that introduced the PSTs to human-centered design practices and  engaged in design-based research to evaluate the effectiveness of each iteration of the workbook. As  part of this work, we initially asked our collabor ators what they wanted in a workbook and designed from there. However, in the third iteration, we made more space for the course instructors to collaborate and co-design the instructional booklet with us, which led to a more synthesized blending of HCD practices that center empathy with course concepts that cente red the identities of historically excluded communities in educational spaces. Ultimately, this paper describes this process of co-construction and the ways that blending these two fields, rather tha n purely integrating HCD, led to more buy-in from course instructors, deeper engagement from the students enrolled in the course, and informed the ways that HCD should be integrated into other courses and instructional experiences. Conceptual frameworks informing our design This work is informed by two bodies of literature: 1) teacher education that centers humanizing pedago gies including culturally sustaining practices and social justice education and 2) the role of HCD in educational spaces. In the past several decades, teacher education scho lars have called for teacher preparation programs t o better prepare PSTs to work with diverse student populations. Cochran-Smith and Zeichner (2005), among others, have highlighted the variance that exist s across programs in terms of the breadth and depth s pent in exploring issues of diversity, equity, and inclusion, including the role of social justice and humanizing pedagogies that view historically excluded community members’ lived expe riences as assets to be valued rather than as deficits to be remedied. Kretchmar and Zeichner (2016), for exampl e, described the benefits of creating partnerships between teacher preparation programs and the communities they serve. These partnerships would make space for PSTs to learn from and work in collaboration with the commu nities they serve in an immersive learning experien ce that allows them to better understand the contexts their students live in and to learn from the community’s expertise. Several scholars have explored the types of educati onal experiences that most benefit PSTs and that create shifts in their thinking about people from h istorically excluded communities. Salazar (2013) en couraged activities that help PSTs build reciprocal relation ships with students, respond to students’ holistic needs, and design inquiry-based instruction that is interest-driven as well as centered in the lives of their students. Gay and Kirkland (2013) make the case for supporting teache rs in developing critical cultural consciousness, i n part through critical reflection. Sensoy and DiAngelo (2 009) also found critical self-reflection to be a key aspect of shifting PSTs thinking toward asset-based thinking. For Rychly and Graves (2012), this means that PSTs should engage in activities that help them develop knowled ge of other cultures, critically reflect on their b eliefs about other cultures, interrogate their own assumptions and beliefs, and finally demonstrate empathy. To help foster empathy, some scholars have explored  the benefits of empathy education (e.g., Dolby, 2012; McAllister & Irvine, 2002; Warren, 2018). The se scholars have encouraged similar approaches to t hose described above. McAllister and Irvine (2002), for example, found immersive experiences to help PSTs d evelop empathic responses, while Warren  (2018) encouraged activities that engage PSTs in perspective taking a nd that foster empathic concern. We argue that engaging PST s in the practices of human-centered design (HCD) would help PSTs develop empathy. HCD is a problem-solving approach that is grounded in empathy and iteration (Brown, 2008; Lawrence et al. 2021). Learning sciences scholars have shown the benefits of engaging learners in HCD, particularly that it develops key mindsets, in cluding human-centeredness (Goldman & Kabayadondo, 2017). In designing the first two iterations of the booklet, we created activities that engaged PSTs in HCD practices such as observing and interviewing to guide them in better understanding the key stakeholders at their community placement. We also, in iteration two, incorporated Hess and Fila’s (2016) empathic techniques into the  activities with the aim of fostering empathy by practicing beh aviors that encourage d empathic responses such as synthesizing empathic knowledge and projection (a mental form of perspective taking). Setting the stage for Iteration 3.0 The multicultural education course is a prerequisit e course for PSTs at a large land-grant university in the Midwest. Students typically take this course in the  first two years of their undergraduate program. Th e course focuses on exploring identity and di fference in educational spaces and is part of a two-course sequence of social justice in education courses they take ahead of beg inning their teacher preparation program. Our colla boration with the course began in late 2018 when we reached out to the cours e instructor at the time to discuss possible collaborations. That professor requested an instruc tional tool that would help PSTs make connections b etween the community placement and course concepts presented in readings, lectures, and discussions. We adopted a design-based research approach in designing the interventi on. This approach allowed us to iteratively design and revise based on feedback and evaluation of the prototype’s effectiveness. In the first iteration, t he design center designed an interactive workbook b ased on IDEO’s human-centered field guide (IDEO, 2015) and asked PSTs to connect the work they did in a community placement to course concepts using HCD practices. The booklet allowed them to document their engagement in HCD practices such as observing and interviewing, making insights, and defining design opportunities. While the booklet engaged PSTs in HC D practices, the initial intervention lacked explicit  connection to course concepts  or a specific focus on fostering empathy. Based on an analysis of a small sample of students’  workbooks, we revised it to be more grounded in empathy education to hopefully address students’ de ficit thinking and surface-level engagement. We integrated activities that engaged them in empathic techniques  (Hess & Fila, 2016) that helped them to both refle ct on their learning and immerse themselves in their community placement. To evaluate the  second iteration, we collected a sample of students’ booklets, observed one teaching assistant’s implementation of the booklet, and attended instructor meetings to get their feedback on the workbook and its implementation. In analyzing its effectiveness, we learned that there was only a limited alignment between course themes and concepts with the HCD practices PSTs engaged in. Additionally, it was clear from our analysis of the workbooks that the PSTs struggled to make connections between course concepts and the ir placement, demonstrated deficit thinking, and their responses showed surface-level engagement.  Finally, instructors and reviewers from our first paper on this iterative de sign (James et al., 2021a), supported our findings that a true integration, or blending, of HCD and course concepts was needed to better support PSTs’ development of empathy and critical cultural consciousness. This was a key insight and led us to go back to the drawing board for our third iteration, this time in collaboration with the course’s current professor and four of the teaching assistants. Version 3.0: The design process In prior iterations, the course instructors describ ed what they were looking for and we at the design center used that information to guide our design. In the third iteration, we brought the course instructors into t he design process, which led to a co-design collaboration effort. During the summer of 2021, we planned a two-day collaboration effort that ended with a co-designed workbook that guided students throughout the entire semester. In the initial meeting, we met with the lead professor and four of the course’ s teaching assistants. We shared with the instructors our initial findings fr om analyzing students’ responses in the second iter ation (as described above). We then set objectives for our tw o-day planning session: 1) clearly define what we want the PSTs to take away from the workbook and course, 2) creat e stronger connections between course concepts and HCD practices, and 3) determine how we want to asse ss the PSTs ’ learning. From there, we spent time brainstorming toward the first outcome-what do we want PSTs to take away from the course and the HCD workbook. Possible outcomes we brainstormed included having PSTs critically reflect on field placement, reflect on their own identity and educational journey, understand th e context and the people served in their placement, identify explicit connections between HCD and course concepts, adopt an asset-based mindset, develop social justice practices (i.e., challenging stereotypes, debunking myths), and develop strategies for becoming advocates for change in educational spaces. Using these goals, we set out to map out the workbook’s alignment with course concepts.

We started by mapping out the entire semester on a dry erase board, identifying each week by the course concepts and themes the PSTs would explore with ins tructors. From there we identified ways to integrat e HCD practices with these themes, designing in-class and out-of-class activities that engaged PSTs in HCD practices that aligned with the concepts they learned in class. For exampl e, during the third week of the course, the PSTs practiced the empathic technique of interaction by engaging in conversations  with a fellow classmate. This allowed them to develop comfortability in talking w ith someone they did not know. Then, they repeated the practice outside of class with the lens of speaking  to someone who could be identified as an educator broadly interpreted to include educators in informal settings (e.g., a grandparent teaching a recipe). Additionally, the PSTs had a weekly reflection that explicitly asked them to connect the HCD activ ity with the course concepts they learned in other parts of the class. That week, they reflected on who could be a teacher. During week six, the PSTs focused on disability, accessibility, and inclusion in the course. When we co-designed potential activities with the instructors, we developed an activity that asked th e PSTs to either immerse themselves in a space to a ssess its accessibility (which align ed with t he HCD practice of direct observation to understand) or to interact with someone who either has a disability or works with p eople who have disabilities (which align ed with the HCD practice of interviewing to understand). These prac tices were more embedded in the course concepts, wh ich as we discuss below, impacted the ways that both teach ing assistants and PSTs engaged in the course and w ith the workbook.

## Discussion and implications

Through this collaboration, we developed the third iteration of the workbook, and created a more embed ded set of tasks that fully integrate d HCD with course concepts. This work highlights the importance of collaboratively co-designing when engaging in curriculum design as par t of design-based research. This collaborative effort led to an increased buy-in from the teaching assistants. This was demonstrated by their engaging their students in the in-class activities, making explicit connections between course concepts and empathy (as well as HCD in general), and revisiting out-of-class activities to ensure that PSTs were meaningfully engaging in the work, which was not as visible in prior iterations. Likewise, our analysis of the students’ written responses in the workbook (see James et al. 2023a, 2023b) revealed that they engaged at deeper levels than they had in prior iterations. For examp le, student reflections were significantly longer (from few sentences to almost a page) and made explicit connections between the HCD activities and course concepts  in their weekly reflections. In planning meetings, t he teaching assistants also noted that their students were much  more reflective about their own identities and positionality. They were also less likely to demonstrate deficit thinking than in past iterations, as demonstrated by a qualitative thematic coding of the students’ workbooks. In doing this collaborative work, we have identified several implications for future collaborations where there is a desire to integrate course concepts and HCD. Design-based research creates opportunities for collaboration between researchers and participants.  This work highlights how important it is to ensure  that collaboration is equitable and that all voices are part of the co-construction of any intervention. Additionally, this work signals that design thinking and HCD integration requires a blending of HCD with disciplinary concepts to be most effective. It is not enough to provide stud ents with HCD practices, rather course instructors and HCD designers must work together to help make these imp licit connections explicit for students through activities and reflective work that blend the two disciplines together.
