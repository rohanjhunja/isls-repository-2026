# Are You Seeing What I Am Talking About? Joint Attention Instruction for Collaborative Learners at A Digital Whiteboard

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Joint attention is a crucial element in collaborative learning, often facilitated by group awareness tools highlighting the partner’s visual focus. Little is known, however, to what extent joint attention can also be differentially instructed guiding shared and mutual gaze. To determine if joint attention can be effectively instructed for, we conducted a 2×2 experimental laboratory study with the factors shared gaze instruction (with versus without) and dyadic gaze instruction (with versus without), consisting of 47 dyads (n = 94). Participants discussed the impact of social media use supported with a concept map on a digital whiteboard. We built on a multi-modal learning analytics framework to analyze participants’ behavioral responses via EDA and gaze tracking measurements. The results revealed that instructing for dyadic gaze reduces shared attention. Furthermore, there are differential correlations between shared moments of arousal and joint attention. Overall, joint attention instruction can guide learners’ focus in CSCL settings.

## Introduction

Joint attention has become one of the focal points for improving learning in computer supported collaborative learning (CSCL) scenarios. Prerequisite to learning, joint attention is defined as when two (or more) people attend to the same thing (e.g., object, idea, or other item) and are aware of the other persons’ attending to the same thing (Tomasello, 1995). Joint attention contributes to coordinating each other’s attention, developing a situational awareness to recognize partner perspectives,  and building mutual understanding (Tomasello, 2020). Such awareness, for instance, allows one partner to communicate the need for their partner to shift attention to an object of interest. This process is demonstrative of how “recursive perspective taking” can align attention for a purpose (Tomasello, 2020). This guidance may be part of the  collaborative learning situation  that eventually  leads to knowledge convergence, which is learners ’ understanding or knowledge becoming more similar  (Weinberger et al., 2007). Different forms of joint attention needed to be coordinated, shared gaze  (towards a shared object), dyadic gaze (towards each other), and triadic attention  (shifting attention between an object and each other) coregulating focus and intention  (Oates & Grayson, 2004). So far, there is little knowledge of how these diff erent types of joint attention can be differentially supported. The awareness of differing perspectives allows coll aborative learners to coordinate their own and thei r partner’s attention to attend to an object (Battich et al., 2020). Coordination is necessary for collaborative learning as it allows for the regulation that is indicative of high interaction collaborative learning situatio ns (Järvelä et al, 2018). Gaze capture, gestural (incl. deictical) and verbal interactions are forms of regulation and coordination between collaborative learners (Battich et al., 2020). This regulation is transactive, i.e., that each learner is part of the knowledge construction process and is mutually scrutinizing and building the shared knowledge base together (Järvelä et al, 2018).

From a multi-modal learning analytics perspective, it is possible to evaluate learning in the CSCL context with a variety of tools collecting data on multiple  modalities (Lawrence & Weinberger, 2022). Taking multiple modalities into account, there is some research linking levels of joint arousal with cognitive processes of learning, e.g., showing how learners may jointly experience stress within a collaborative exam (Malmberg et al., 2019). So far, there are hardly any studies about how the coordination  of joint attention is related to joint arousal and joi nt focus in discourse, which would show how learners transactively converge in knowledge within CSCL. A multimodal approach allows for identification and analysis of synchronicity that may not be seen in other contexts during the learning process, which i n turn enables scaffolding joint attention, e.g., with awareness tools (Schneider & Pea, 2013; V ogel & Weinberger, 2018). Visualizing the gaze of others  does not always bear beneficial results but sometimes seems to interfere with learners ’ ability to coordinate their gaze. For example, modeling an expert’s gaze on a document hampered novices’ learning processes (van Marlen et al., 2022). So far, alternatives, such as gaze instruction, have not been sufficiently explored.

## Research question

While joint attention has been identified as beneficial in CSCL contexts, little is known about the effectiveness of instruction for facilitating joint attention. This study addresses this gap by examining whether share d and dyadic gaze instructions can effectively support joint attention in collaborative learning: To what extent do different forms of instruction for  joint attention, namely shared gaze instruction (with vs. without), dyadic gaze instruction (with vs. without), and their interaction affect joint attention, shared moments of arousal, and learning?

## Methods

Applying the multi-modal learning analytics framework (Lawrence & Weinberger, 2022) this 2×2 experiment foresees a control group without additional instructions for learning behavior (CG), a group receiving shared gaze instruction (SGI), including to point to the concept on the map being discussed, and a group receiving dyadic gaze instruction (DGI). The combination of SGI and DGI suggests to turn and look at one’s partner when talking  as well as guiding the gaze towards a shared focus, wh ich is called triadic gaze instruction (TGI). Participants first reviewed material on the potential impacts of socia l media usage for individuals and groups. The 94 pa rticipants (N=94, age M= 24, SD=4.46, 64% were female) were randomly paired into 47 dyads. We record eye-tracking measures of recurrence rates (RR) of partn ers’ gaze within defined areas of interest and electrodermal activity identifies shar ed physiological arousal events (SPAEs) to measure synchronal RR of the SPAEs, and measure learning through pre-and post-tests. For eye-tracking measurements, the concept map is displayed on a tabletop device  and predesignated areas of interest allow measures of frequency and moment that an area of interest is viewed, and that is compared to the partner s’ data. When the partner’s viewpoints overlap within a sh ort period of time, measured as the RR and designated as an occurrence of visual synchrony and representative of joint attention (Holmqvist et al., 2011; Seemann, 2011).

The EDA device measures changes in skin conductance resistance (SCR) to identify emotional arousal changes of the participants. The recurrence of SPAEs are indicative of emotional synchrony and the RR can show the frequency of emotional synchrony between conditions (Dindar et al., 2022). Using EDA data to identify SPAEs and emotional synchrony is another measure of JA in collaborative learning.

## Discussion

While learning occurred across all conditions, for better or worse, learning outcomes were not impacted by any of the JA instructions. The learning outcomes, while important to show that they occurred, may not be the only question for understanding the role of joint attention in the learning process. To what extent do different forms of instruction (SGI and DGI)  for joint attention  and their interaction affect joint attention  and the joint emotional experience?

In the eye tracking data, we see that there is no main effect of SGI  on looking at the object of attention. The difference in the eye-tracking results shows that while SGI could not foster JA, DGI could reduce it. Telling learners to look at each other stopped them from looking at the learning object they should be focusing on together. This negative DGI effect on joint visual attention showing that the partners are focused on a different physical object, namely each other, thus may be coupled with a different emotional experience and explain the lack of effect that DGI has on SPAE RRs. When it comes to shared emotions in contrast, SGI, may contribute to a cinema effect: like the experience of watching a film with others, the shared experience of seeing the same thing  may result in similar  simultaneous emotional experiences (Deroy et al., 2022).  Moreover, all but the SGI condition show moderate negative correlations between shared gaze and the SPAEs. This could indicate that  SGI couples the JA moments to shared emotions, not by fostering joint visual attention, but by highlighting the shared challenge that some learning objects pose.

## Conclusion

Analysis of multimodal data, including eye-tracking and EDA data here provides insight into how joint attention relates to cognitive and emotional processes of collaborative learning. Moreover, understanding JA may reveal ways of supporting CSCL. Multimodal analyses have shown before that there appears to be promise in guiding JA in CSCL with group awareness tools (Schnaubert & Bodemer, 2022; Schneider & Pea, 2013). Such scaffolding of joint attention may be unambiguous, but subtle t o not interfere with the learning process. However, explicit instruction for JA may de-automatize and detrimentally influence JA and synchronicity, especially as the control group showed the highest RR associated with occurrence of JA. Still, instructing for shared gaze did bear cinema effect-like influence  on synchronicity of arousal  of learners (Deroy et al., 2022)  and guided learners’ activity. Research on co-present TEL scenarios based on a shared knowledge r epresentation such as a digitally presented concept map may offer further insights on how parti cipants coordinate  and regulate  their and their partner’s attention.

Future research could include the use of deixis, oral statements, and body language as a means to establish intentional joint attention during discussion (Batt ich et al., 2020). These modalities  allow participants to intentionally communicate learning points and to gu ide their partner’s attention followed by the act of looking at one another to confirm attention to the object and further knowledge convergence through dialogue. Differently explicit awareness tools targeting such modalities would potentially produce different levels of regulation of joint attention and accordingly allow for further exploration of joint attention as a means of shared cognition.
