# What Does It Mean to Be a “Quantum Kind of Person”? Identity Formation in an Emerging STEM Field

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Quantum information science is an emerging field largely absent from K-12 settings, creating challenges for identity formation. Through cognitive interviews with six high school students and five undergraduate students, we examine how students evaluate quantum identity. Identity and participation in STEM Researchers have conceptualized ‘science identity’ as comprising multiple dimensions, including a sense of community belonging and recognition (Carlone & Johnson, 2007; Vincent-Ruz & Schunn, 2018). Yet, STEM identity research has mostly focused on established disciplines such as physics and engineering (Liu & Schunn, 2020). Emerging STEM areas such as quantum information science (QIS) present a different context within which to explore questions of STEM identity. As a rapidly developing field at the intersection of physics, computer science, and information science (Nielsen & Chuang, 2010), QIS is largely absent from K-12 curricula (Holincheck et al., 2024). Identity research suggests that perceptions of recognition by others are central to developing one’s identity (Vincent-Ruz & Schunn, 2018), and we suggest that identities linked to emerging disciplinary areas may be particularly fragile or difficult to construct. Given students’ limited exposure to QIS, we are interested in investigating what rationales student s might use to determine whether they are “a quantum kind of person” and/or whether they believe others view them as “a quantum kind of person.” Understanding these rationales matters both theoretically and practically. Theoretically, it is unclear whether students draw on perceived math/science ability (Carlone & Johnson, 2007), stereotypes about “hard” or “cutting-edge” science (Master et al., 2016), media representations (Steinke, 2005), or identification with related fields (Hazari et al., 2010). Practically, insights into these rationales can inform identity measur es that capture early, tentative identification, and guide the design of experiences that counter exclusionary stereotypes and connect quantum concepts to students’ existing interests. Using cognitive interviews with high school and undergraduate students, we ask: What reasons and justifications do students provide for identifying (or not identifying) as “a quantum kind of person,” and/or for believing that others see them (or don’t see them) this way?

## Method

We developed a new instrument adapted from prior research (Malespina et al., 2023; Vincent-Ruz & Schunn,

2018) for the quantum information science (QIS) context at the high school level. ‘ Quantum’ was defined as the

physics of matter and energy at atomic/subatomic scales, and ‘ QIS’ as applying quantum effects to process, transmit, and store information through technologies like quantum computers and sensors. We conducted one-onone cognitive interviews (Willis, 2004) via Zoom with participants across three levels of expertise: one postdoctoral researcher, five undergraduates with relevant coursework (e.g. Principles of Quantum Computing, or Quantum I), and six recently graduated high school students with no prior coursework in quantum. Participants responded to three items using a four-point scale (YES!, yes, no, NO!): (1) “I think of myself as a quantum kind of person,” (2) “My family thinks of me as a quantum kind of person,” and (3) “My friends think of me as a quantum kind of person.” Here we report on th e interviews conducted with undergraduates and high school students. We used emergent coding to unders tand students’ own rationales rather than apply predetermined frameworks. Given the small sample, findings are exploratory and intended to generate hypotheses rather than support broad generalizations.

## Preliminary findings

Ambiguity or lack of experiences as barriers to identity claims Students across expertise levels struggled to evaluate quantum identity due to limited experience and unclear boundaries around what ‘ quantum’ encompasses. High schoolers repeatedly expressed confusion about what “quantum” means (“Uh, not really sure what that would mean” [HS-1]) and requested clarification before they 2791 could evaluate their fit. Identity claims felt premature or impossible with this uncertainty (“I feel like  I would answer more  confidently if I had more information ” [HS-2]). Undergraduate students tried to calibrate what threshold of engagement “counted” as quantum identity. Some suggested that in the absence of experience, identity claims could only reflect interest (“Because for quantum information and scientists,  you need to have a lot more research background, right? You need to be able to research much more.” [UND-1]; “it seems like it’s about interest, because especially if you ’re aiming this towards high schoolers, it’s going to be about interest rather than experience” [UND-5]).

Reliance on broad disciplinary identities (CS, physics) or disciplinary distinctions for making identity claims In the absence of quantum-related experience, high school students leveraged existing identities in related disciplines such as computer science and physics as heuristics for quantum belonging (“just because I don’t know anything about the area, but also, as I mentioned, like, I want to enter computer science” [HS-3]; “since I’m not really big on computer science, or I’m not really big on physics” [HS-2]). However, these varied by expertise. Undergraduate students made finer subdisciplinary distinctions, recognizing that quantum represents one specialized area within physics (“[someone] that’s really interested and enthusiastic about quantum mechanics?” [UND-2]; “Um, I don’t think of myself as a quantum kind of person. I think of myself as… A pretty big physics type of person.” [UND-3]).

Inferred capability for high schoolers, observable engagement for undergraduates For high school students, family recognition was based more on inferred general capability in related areas (being “good at” computer science, technology, or science) (“I want to do computer science, and [my family] know that, so…  probably they would infer that I cando this too.” [HS-3]; “[my mom] knows I’m not that good at science.” [HS-2]). Undergraduate students described others’ recognition of them as quantum people as contextual and situational rather than stable. Recognition w as portrayed as dependent on specific circumstances, whether they were currently taking quantum courses, actively discussing quantum topics, or visibly engaging with quantum materials at a given moment (“My friends [are] familiar that I do stuff with physics, but specifically qua ntum mechanics.” [UND-2]; “That’s probably because I have a lot more discussions about quantum with [my family].” [UND-1];  “…with my friends. It’s just not something I talk about.” [UND-5]; “Maybe it even depends, semester to semester, like, whether or not I’m taking a course that involves a heavy quantum mechanics.” [UND-4]). These findings extend existing identity frameworks (e.g., Carlone & Johnson, 2007; Lave & Wenger, 1991) into a novel context where institutional pathways are largely absent. Students’ ambiguity about what ‘quantum’ means points to a foundational barrier: they cannot evaluate fit within a field they cannot define.  For most pre-college students, QIS communities of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991) remain too limited to support identity formation, leaving students to draw on existing disciplinary communities. Recognition flows through related disciplinary competence (Carlone & Johnson, 2007), with families and peers identifying students as physics or CS people, and students carrying those recognitions into their tentative evaluations of quantum belonging. For undergraduates, recognition is situational and contextual, aligning with Lave and Wenger’s (1991) notion of legitimate peripheral participation, in which belonging is tied to visible, ongoing engagement with a community of practice. Quantum identity, for these students, appears contingent on active  course enrollment or engagement  with quantum materials, rather than an enduring identification.

Beyond its foundations in physics, computer and information science, engineering, and mathematics (Nielsen & Chuang, 2010), QIS carries implications across domains from medicine  and finance to cryptography and national security policy (NSTC, 2018). This breadth means students from many disciplines have a  potential entry point into the field. Yet our findings suggest that neither high school nor undergraduate students are drawing on this multiplicity when evaluating their quantum belonging. For high school s tudents, this reflects the same limited exposure that drives definitional ambiguity: W ithout meaningful encounters with QIS as its own field, students can only map it onto disciplines they already know. For undergraduates, even students with relevant coursework tended to anchor their quantum identity within a single disciplinary home, suggesting that current encounters with QIS may inadvertently narrow disciplinary identification rather than opening  up the field’s broader possibilities.

Early interventions should address definitional ambiguity about what QIS is, connect quantum concepts and practices to students’ existing interests across disciplines, and create recognition opportunities that do not require extensive prior experience.
