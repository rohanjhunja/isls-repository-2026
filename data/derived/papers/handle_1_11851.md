# Using Collaborative Video Annotation to Foster Epistemic Coherence in Teacher Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In order to achieve epistemic coherence in teacher education, student teachers must connect theoretical and practical forms of knowledge in meaningful ways. This study investigates uses of collaborative video annotation as a method for facilitating epistemic coherence among teacher education students. Through a design-based approach and interaction analysis, we examine how student teachers in co-located groups annotate video clips of teaching from elementary classrooms. The analysis displays student teachers’ knowledge practices and illustrates how they structure their collaboration and connect key concepts from their pedagogy course to observed events in the particular video annotation context. The findings show how concrete video annotations stimulates joint negotiation of meaning when student teachers connect diverse forms of knowledge, thereby fostering professional development and epistemic coherence in teacher education.

## Introduction

Learning to become a teacher involves a complex transition: Moving from viewing everyday classroom practices as a student teacher to perceiving and acting upon them as a professional (Blomberg et al, 2013; Feiman-Nemser, 2008). In this paper, we explore a specific method for student teachers to develop such a professional perspective by observing classroom situations on video and conceptualizing and annotating what they observe. We report on a design-based research project in which small groups of student teachers collaborate on annotating video clips from actual elementary school classrooms. Through this collaborative activity, they discuss  classroom situations and apply an analytical layer of theoretical concepts to scenarios that are relevant to their future work as teachers. In teacher education, there is a need to foster an integrated understanding of professional knowledge that can be termed epistemic coherence (Heikkilä & Hermansen, 2024). This coherence develops as a result of student teachers actively engaging in knowledge-creating processes in which they consider various knowledge resources in relation to concrete tasks and professional practice problems. Consequently, epistemic coherence also involves understanding the potential utility of different knowledge resour ces to explain  and understand practical cases. Thus, epistemic coherence in teacher education is a matter of agency, forming a meaningful relationship with knowledge that will be significant to student teachers’ future roles as professional teachers. In this study, w e argue that uses of collaborative video annotatio n (VA) can provide enhanced opportunities for fostering epistemic coherence, and through analysis of student teacher conversations we examine how such opportunities may be acted upon in a university teaching situation. We adopt a sociocultural perspective on learning, where language is seen as a tool that shapes and is shaped by our thoughts  and actions – culturally developed art efacts, such as forms of talk or ideas, can shape our interactions with the world and vice versa (Danish & Gresalfi, 2018; Furberg & Silseth, 2022). This perspective emphasizes co-construction of knowledge among participants in an activity, focusing on how they actively use, negotiate and connect concepts and forms of knowledge that they find relevant and meaningful within the setting (Silseth et al., 2023; Suthers, 2006). In the teacher education context, student teachers are expected to work intently with unpacking professional concepts. Importantly, when student teachers jointly inquire into issues of professional practice  their stance s towards knowledge candevelop and shift when they encounter different perspectives. Here, analytic attention is shifted from individuals’ accounts of knowledge in isolation to the study of knowledge practices that emerge socially and interactionally in specific collaborative activities (Knight & Littleton, 2017). Even though it is easy to recognize the need for this active connecting work in teacher education, it has proved difficult to create learning situations that combine conceptual thinking with situated work practices (Cochran-Smith et al., 2020). Against this background, several CSCL studies have shown the affordances of video technologies for professionals to collaborate and reflect upon situated actions in a professional setting (Johansson et al., 2017) and for transcending the ordinary time/space configuration in teacher ed ucation (Beal & Steier, 2024).  Accordingly, video can create objectivized teaching moments  – captured situations that can be transformed into le arning artefacts. While typical discussions about video often occur sequentially after viewing, video annotation (VA) technology affords comments to be tied to precise moments within the v ideo timeline.  Thus, VA technology provides a unique method as part of video analysis in professional learning. Recent review studies poi nt out that VA can help student teachers  collaborate and communicate about professional knowledge  and professional practices (Evi-Colombo et al, 2020; Ramos et al., 2022). This is enabled when student teachers formulate ideas and reflections that are tied to concrete events in  the videos, which then evoke re-consideration, elaboration and negotiation of initial ideas (e.g. Näykki et al, 2022). Whereas most studies focus on online environments, only a few studies tap into the opportunities for analysis using VA in a face-to-face setting (e.g. Ruf et al, 2023; Zottmann et al, 2013).  In the following, we provide a detailed analysis of  the socio-technical configuration of a learning design that engages student teachers in collaborati ve analysis and writing annotations. We portray student teachers’ interactions with a VA tool and point at opportunities for this approach to sup port student teachers in collaboratively developing epistemic coherence in t heir professional education.  Our research question is: How does a video annotation tool become a resource for student teachers in conceptualizing situated classroom events?

## Research design, collected data and analytical approach

The current design-based study (Sandoval, 2014) seeks to contribute tonew research-based practices that use VA in teacher education. With a team of researchers and teacher educators, we developed a video analysis activity that was integrated into an existing pedagogy course in the five-year master’s programme in teacher education at a Norwegian university. The participants in our study were a cohort of 30 (consent-giving, out of 40) first-year student teachers  enrolled in the course, divided into ten study groups of four students, as part of the course structure. A full day of coursework included an introductory lecture provided by the teacher educator, covering central topics and theoretical constructs relatable  to the videos. This was followed by unsupervised group-based analysis work in seminar rooms. The student teachers were presented with the task to jointly analyse and annotate two 5-minute, edited video clips recorded in an authentic eleme ntary classroom with a professional teacher. In line with the teacher educator’s framing of “inclusive classroom management” in the course lecture, the video clips were chosen by the teacher educator for their affordances to provide a wide range of inquiry points relevant to the course content and readings. The groups were asked to discuss especially interesting situations and use VA to both describe and explain the phenomena of interest. To guide and focus the analysis, the teacher educator provided the following optional topics in the task  for support: classroom management, teacher–pupil relations and movement in the classroom. The students were explicitly asked to consider alternative concepts and perspectives to elicit multiple viewpoints and broaden out their analysis. To address the research question, we collected in-room and on-screen video data, as well as annotation logs from the video  analysis group work  of three randomly selected group. Interaction analysis (Hall & Stevens, 201 5; Jordan & Henderson, 1995)  allowed us to discover how the student teachers structured their discussions to conceptualize classroom events using VA collaboratively to connect their ideas to specific instances on the video timeline. Analysis From our overall data, it is evident that the videos evoke a range of responses that show how student teachers make sense of and relate to classroom situations with an emerging teacher professional perspective. The student teachers explore and negotiate different conceptual understandings w ith their peers, sourced from different domains of knowledge. Writing VAs slows down the student teachers’ proces s of considering how different interpretations might be justified when held up aga inst the video data. Whereas the groups’ notions in spoken conversations are transient, the written VA s become permanent  – which directs attention to how their understandings are formulated. The three studied groups organize the co-writing of comments slightly different. Some groups connected their laptops to a shared wall-mounted screen to write together, while in other  groups – such as the one we focus on in the example below  – the participants view the videos in-synch, but each on their own screen. In all the groups, the student teachers comment on the way the teacher in the video clip moves around in the classroom and uses her positioning, her voice a nd physical presence as a way to keep the pupils’ a ttention and engage them in the topic about how earlier historical periods have affected the present time. Video data captures how the three studied groups of student teachers determine how pedagogical concepts and perspectives can be appropriately used to describe and explain the phenomena they are observing. Some of these concepts are well known in the research literature, such as cognitive schemata, dialogic and monologic teaching, and progressivist v ersus traditionalist teaching approaches. Other concepts used are expressions and perspectives introduced by lecturers and in-practice teachers, and some are drawn from the curr iculum and government guidelines. A typical feature of the group work is that the student teachers start and pause the video to examine the teacher’s specific actions and interactions with the pupils. The sample for this paper was selected to illustrate how student teachers draw on and negotiate different conceptual resources when interpreting an issue of practice. This sample allows us to present and critically examine how the student teachers intersubjectively develop meanings aimed towards shared and coherent understandings. In the extract below, we enter the conversation in one group as the student teachers bring up the teacher’s way of increasing and lowering the volume of her voice in class, and try to connect her classroom talk to a general monologic teaching approach (Figure 1). This episode represents an interactional pattern of how student teachers use the VA tool as a resource in their analysis.

In this excerpt, Zoran first makes strong epistemic claims about the video clip, instr ucting Sana directly what to write. He introduces two suggestions for a conceptualization based on the video segment they are analysing: “Very monologic teaching” and “uses the voice” (1). However, in the continuing dialogue, we see how only one of these initial suggestions is pursued by the group, that is the teacher’s use of voice volume. When analysing t he verbal contributions and how the annotation text is writte n in the black sections (lines 2 –7), we see how the student teachers collaboratively compose an annotation. San a writes as part of her turn in the conversation, partially reading out loud as she types, which allows the students’ shared understanding of the video clip to take shape. Once the VA is published, Zoran asserts his  initial interpretation, justifying it with additional observations (8). In line 10, he then begins to give a new instruction on what Sana should write  but pauses and formulates a question instead. He reintroduces “monologic teaching” but reframes this  concept as an open question to the group, asking if they agree that the teaching they see on the vid eo is monologic. Responding to this opportunity to disagree with Zoran’s claim, both Sana and Joakim challenge his strong claim about this being a case of “very monologic teaching”. Subtly, Sana starts by s upporting Zoran’s claim, confirming “It’s monologic”, but progresses to suggest  there is “maybe” some more nuance to uncover (11). Joakim expands Sana’s comment by asserting that, while the result is monologic, it was not intended to be so “by desi gn” – assuming that the teacher did not mean to create a monologic teaching situation (13). The student teachers remark that the teacher nonetheless ends up doing most of the talking because the pupils do not engage in who le-class conversation. Joakim therefore claims that the teac her “might” be aiming for a dialogue on a normative  level, which Zoran, who initiated the claim about “very mo nologic teaching”, clearly endorses in the last tur n of the excerpt (18).

This analysis gives insight into how student teachers co llaboratively establish shared conceptual understanding in and through their engagement with the video of classroom practices. The VA tool mediates and influences the joint enterprise of knowledge construction, as the focus on situated actions in the video clip prompts student teachers to articulate, justify, contest and negotiate knowledge claims. When a written VA is published without prior group consensus, it becomes a negotiable artefact within the discourse. The analysis shows in detail how the student teachers shift epistemic stances sequentially in conversation, modifying their claims in ways that accommodate and elicit alternative perspectives. Wh en the student teachers integrate diverse concept ions of knowledge, as illustrated in this excerpt, their sp ecific use of VA promotes collaborative efforts aim ed towards epistemic coherence.

## Discussion

In this short paper, we explored how a VA tool was used as a resource for student teachers to  analyse and reflect upon situated classroom events. We focused on how the use of VA in this setting supported student teachers in jointly negotiating and conceptualizing concrete issues observed in an authentic classroom video. Actively linking theoretical concepts to classroom events and making sense of these connections contribute to epistemic coherence (Heikkilä & Hermansen, 2024).  Thus, examining this sequential development of understandings at the interactional level can shed light on the complex processes invol ved in creating epistemic coherence on an organizational level. Our analysis revealed the frequent practice of writing comments closely connected to situated actions in the video clips, and shows how knowledge  construction and developing shared understanding s is socially constituted and situated, as a sociocultural, mediated process (Knight & Littleton, 2017). In the analysis, we saw how the student teachers collaborated in the authoring of the annotation, engaging in coordinated verbal and written actions. The VA allowed the student teachers to critically o rient to the claims that were being composed, and later published. The analysis reveal ed tensions when two of the student teachers compose d and published a video annotation about notions that were not fully established and shared in the group, which prompted further problematization and nuancing in the group  leading to a more coherent understanding of the relations between the concept of monologic  versus dialogic teaching and the observations of classroom  practice. The analysis investigated how negotiations toward a shared understanding unfolded in the dialogues and interactions. We identified markers that indicate how student teachers positioned their knowledge stances as tentative or certain and saw how the strength of claims shifted during interaction. In an interaction analytic perspective, the content elements of education cannot be separated from the language and symbols used to convey it, and learning contexts are shaped by what participants find relevant to their practical activities (Hall & Stevens, 2015). Accordingly, the analysis showed how shifting epistemic stances, and the development of coherent understandings were supported and mediated using the VA tool. Through VA, student teachers engaged in discussions and created written records that represent valid and relevant knowledge connections within their institutional setting. This study has some limitations. The value of collaborative video annotation depends on factors such as the quality of video materials, framing and scaffolding, task design, and technological affordances. Nonethe less, the findings demonstrate how students co-construct epistemic coherence and provide concrete examples of how an annotation process frames and furthers discussions. VA has specific affordances for supporting student teachers that actively engage with connecting diverse forms of knowledge by making written knowledge relations concrete, visible and contestable. We hop e that future studies will explore the concept of epistemi c coherence and how it can be scaffolded through VA, using  larger dataset s that supports observational  studies on how to configure students’ collaborative work with this technology.
