# Designing for Augmenting Teachers' Understanding of Children with ADHD Behaviors Using a Smartwatch

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines expert perspectives on identifying, evaluating, and monitoring ADHD behaviors in children in the classroom. Eleven clinicians and educators participated in semi-structured interviews. Using open coding, three themes emerged: variability of ADHD behaviors, monitoring ADHD behaviors in context, and opportunities and challenges of wearables for teachers. Guided by Bronfenbrenner's Ecological Systems Theory, our findings suggest that wearable tools can act as mediators within the c hild's learning ecosystem, enhancing communication among teachers, clinicians, and caregivers through objective, context-aware data.

Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is the most prevalent childhood psychiatric condition, affecting approximately 9.4% of children ages 2-17 in the US (Danielson et al., 2016). ADHD is characterized by differences in attention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity (APA, 2013) that challenge academic performance and peer interactions (Fried et al., 2016; Hoza, 2007). Assessing ADHD remains difficult because it depends heavily on subjective reports shaped by developmental stage, gender, cultural norms, and socioeconomic barriers (Hamed et al., 2015). Teachers are often the first to raise concerns, occupying a unique observational position to compare children's behavior against peers in a structured environment (Degroote et al., 2022). However, teachers are not qualified to diagnose ADHD, and this observational role places a heavy burden on them, requiring lengthy documentation across multiple students and leading to reporting fatigue and variability (Sherman et al., 2008). Clinicians rely on multi-setting, multi-informant data as a gold standard for diagnosis (Tamsut et al., 2024). Researchers have begun exploring sensor-based approaches to complement traditional evaluation. Inertial sensors have demonstrated feasibility in detecting ADHD-related movement differences (O'Mahony et al., 2014)and gamified prototypes have been explored for non-invasive attentional screening (Zhang et al., 2023). Systems like MoodGems (Stefanidi et al., 2024) and CoolTaco (Silva et al., 2023) explored naturalistic behavior tracking outside of school. However, most computational approaches remain confined to controlled settings, leaving a clear gap in tools designed for real-world classroom use. This study is grounded in Bronfenbrenner's Ecological Systems Theory (EST), which frames development as shaped by nested environments, from the microsystem (family, teachers) to the macrosystem (cultural values, norms). Wearables can serve as mediating artifacts that extend teachers' observational capacity, facilitating data-informed communication across the mesosystem connecting home, school, and clinic.

To address this gap, we conducted a qualitative study with 11 clinicians and educators guided by three research questions: (1) How do experts describe the variability of ADHD behaviors across domains such as movement, speech, attention, emotion, and sensory processing? (2) How do experts determine when behaviors warrant further assessment? (3) What potential do experts see in wearable or sensor-based tools to support existing diagnostic practices?

Eleven participants (6 clinicians, 5 educators) were recruited via email and flyers based on experience working with children with ADHD or other neurodevelopmental disabilities. Clinicians included active practitioners and those with prior clinical experie nce; educators had 6-14 years of classroom experience supporting students with ADHD. Interviews of 40 semi-structured questions were conducted in person or via Zoom, covering ADHD symptom identification, behavioral monitoring, movement and speech differences, sensor use concerns, and useful data visualizations. Each interview was recorded, transcribed, and analyzed using open coding (Khandkar, 2009), with codes organized collaboratively on a shared Miro board to surface thematic trends (Castleberry & Nolen, 2018).

644 Variability of ADHD behaviors in the classroom Experts consistently identified movement, speech, attention, emotion, and sensory processing as the primary behavioral domains distinguishing students with ADHD from neurotypical peers. Across all five domains, behavior was characterized not by quantity but by quality: movement tended to be sporadic and disorganized rather than merely excessive; speech involved inappropriate loudness and difficulty with turn-taking rather than simple talkativeness; and attention oscillated between distractibility and hyperf ocus, with educators most concerned about the former during sustained tasks. Emotional dysregulation manifested as heightened reactivity that created barriers to peer interaction, while sensory differences often presented as movement-seeking behaviors that educators misread as ordinary fidgeting. This multidimensional variability underscores ADHD as a spectrum rather than a uniform profile, and reflects how child-level characteristics interact dynamically with the immediate classroom microsystem, including teacher expectations, peer dynamics, and task demands. Monitoring ADHD Behaviors in the Classroom Experts noted that behaviors become clinically significant when they exceed developmental expectations, impair learning or participation, and persist across time and contexts. A central finding was the contextual dependence of ADHD behaviors: what is disruptive in a structured classroom may be unremarkable at home, and all children exhibit disruptive behaviors at some point. This underscores the need for longitudinal, multi-informant assessments that integrate contextual information across settings. Experts  also cautioned that interpretations are susceptible to bias rooted in cultural norms and gender expectations, as ADHD assessment has historically centered white, middle-class male presentations, leaving many children underidentified. Girls in particular a re socialized to mask symptoms, and teachers must remain alert to hidden compensatory behaviors in students who appear compliant or quiet yet are experiencing significant internal distress. From an EST perspective, these dynamics span the mesosystem and macrosystem, shaping how teachers perceive and respond to children's behavior in ways that can perpetuate inequities in identification. Opportunities and Challenges for Wearables in Supporting Teachers Experts saw significant potential in smartwatches to provide objective, continuous, and context-sensitive data on ADHD behaviors, capturing variability across tasks, times of day, and weeks in ways that episodic observations cannot. Data of interest includ ed frequency and quality of movement, voice modulation levels, and behavioral patterns across different classroom contexts such as structured lessons versus recess. Smartwatches were seen as less disruptive than tablets or cameras, and their data could be exported at varying levels of granularity to suit different stakeholder needs. Engaging children in self-tracking was also viewed as promising for motivation and compliance. However, experts cautioned that wearables must align with practical classroom real ities: concerns included device removal, discomfort, altered behavior due to monitoring awareness, and the risk of data overload. Wearable summaries must augment rather than replace teachers' observation logs, be culturally contextualized, and integrate co ncisely into existing workflows. When thoughtfully designed, such tools function as mediating artifacts within the mesosystem, fostering shared understanding among teachers, clinicians, and caregivers. This study explored how clinicians and educators interpret ADHD behaviors in classrooms and how smartwatchbased tools could augment this process. Our findings confirm that ADHD identification is an inherently ecological process shaped by child characteris tics, teacher perceptions, family context, and broader sociocultural norms. Technologies designed to support educators must model individual baselines, adapt to contextual shifts, and enhance cross-system communication rather than imposing uniform detection thresholds. Experts emphasized that wearable data is most valuable when it surfaces behavioral variability across contexts, supports collaboration among stakeholders, and is presented in formats that complement rather than complicate teachers' existing practices. These findings suggest three design directions: (1) Model contextual variability across tasks, routines, and social settings; (2) Support cross-system collaboration through glanceable, shareable summaries aligned with teachers' workflows; and (3)  Promote equity and cultural sensitivity by actively challenging biases embedded in existing diagnostic practices.
