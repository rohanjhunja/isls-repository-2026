# From Makerspace to Marketplace: What Counts as Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines how dominant U.S. cultural values, particularly entrepreneurialism, shape success in STEM and Maker education. Through illustrative cases, we analyze how ideas of productivity, individualism, and impact frame who is valued as a learner. Drawing on constructionist and critical traditions, we argue for redefining success to include epistemic diversity, reflective practice, and “non-linear” learning, challenging outcome-driven norms, and expanding what counts as meaningful engagement.

## Introduction

Over the past decade, the Maker Movement (Halverson & Sheridan, 2014) brought constructionist, project-based learning into schools. As it became institutionalized, it began reflecting dominant U.S. values and epistemologies (Blikstein, 2013; Buechley et al., 2008; Vossoughi et al., 2016), especially around productivity, efficiency, and economic utility, while overlooking the broader motivations for making (Blikstein et al., 2024). The original openended, reflective ethos gave way to frameworks focu sed on solving real-world problems, producing me asurable outcomes, and stewarding change (Lemée & Blikstein, 2024). These shifts elevated an “entrepreneurial mindset,” pointing to business skills and assertiveness and outcome-driven behaviors as markers of who and what counts as successful learning. This paper critiques how cultu ral norms shape definitions of learning in STEM and  Maker education. Though distinct, these domains are often conflated in policy and practice through shared emphases on hands-on problem-solving and 21st-century skills. Because of this overlap, we analyze them jointly, focusing o n how dominant framings manifest in New York City pub lic schools. In this large and diverse district, entrepreneurial logics are deeply embedded in polic y and cultural expectations. We offer a conceptual and discursive analysis of how cultural values appear in public-facing educational narratives. We ask, with Ackermann (2016): “How might we create a culture where being quiet, o bservant, thoughtful, and contemplative balances with being a doer, entrepreneur, mover, and shaker?” Framing the Ideal learner Problem-solving has long been central to learning, from Piaget’s (1954) view of children as natural investigators to Papert’s (1980), gateway to powerful, collaborative, and exploratory ideas. Framed as educational tools, problems become more than steps toward solutions; t hey help students build analytical and adaptable skills that extend beyond individual challenges. Yet, over time, problem-solving has been recontextualized, not just as pedagogy but as cultural mandate. What began as open-ended and reflective inquiry  has shifted toward entrepreneurial mindsets  tied to measurable outcomes and innovation, often f ramed as essential 21st-century skills (World Economic Forum, 2015). The result is a narrowing of what counts as meaningful engagement  and success. The rise of the “entrepreneurial mindset” (Halverso n & Sheridan, 2014; Martin, 2015) reflects broader cultural values around market logic and optimizatio n, beyond business skills, now embedded in the educ ational discourse (Zhao, 2012; Lackéus, 2015). We use the term not in its business sense but to highlight how these values circulate as taken-for-granted cultural norms. Students are cast as proactive change agents expected to solve problems with scalable and publicly visible results. This mirrors a longer history of tasking schools with solving societal problems (Tyack & Cuban, 1995), placing pressure on students to “have an impact” and change the world, often at the expense of inquiry. These framings privilege confidence, asser tiveness, and risk-taking while undervaluing collaboration, iteration, and community-centered work. This reinforces “dominant epistemologies” (Agarwal & Sengupta-Irving, 2019), that marginalize youth who resist  or redefine these success models. In this way, the entrepreneurial mindset becomes a proxy fo r cultural values, shaping not just what students l earn, who is positioned to succeed, and who students are expected to become. As Apple (2004) notes, schools teach norms, not just content. In STEM and Maker education, these norms i ncreasingly reward a particular ethos: the ideal learner as efficient, assertive, high-energy who produce tangible results. Students who l earn through careful observation, quiet iteration, or collaborative sens e-making may find themselves misaligned with this sup er-doer archetype. This could particularly affect neurodive rgent students, those from collectivist cultures, o r Black and immigrant youth, who may be misread in their behavi or when their modes of learning fall outside domina nt expectations. Technologies and design cultures carr y embedded assumptions about autonomy and problem-ownership (Engle et al., 2011; Haraway, 1991), ofte n reinforcing individualist, technocratic values. E levating entrepreneurial logic narrows the space for diverse epistemologies, a structural, not incidental form of exclusion. In the spotlight These cultural framings are not abstract; they appear in educational programs, materials, and public discourse. To show how they are embedded in practice, we examine two programs spotlighted in the New York City Department of Education’s STEM Handbook (2018): Middle School 88  and the Laboratory School of Finance and Technology, chosen not for representativeness but as publicly celebrated STEM models. These discursive artifacts reveal how dominant values shape design, assessment, and definitions of the “ideal learner.” Both serv e diverse student populations and reflect broader trends in p ublic education. Our analysis dra ws from critical discourse analysis, attending to how program language, goals,  and framings reflect cultural values and how even wellmeaning initiatives can reinforce exclusionary norms while offering space for reimagining. Middle School 88 is noted for using “design thinking to address real-world problems,” emphasizing competitions, awards, and tangible products. This highlights a value system that prioritizes  visibility and performance. While engaging for some, it risks sidelining those who excel in slower, more reflective environments. The model rewards speed, confidence, and public demonstration, traits associated with entrepreneurial success (Apple, 2004). Dominant maker discourses often favor performative displays of innovation, obscuring practices rooted in care and collective action (Greenberg et al., 2020). Competitions could instead focus on speculative or narrative-driven work, exploring emerging technologies or doc umenting local issues, supported by mentors who encourage reflecti on and context. Redefining success involves reimagi ning what counts as doing and thinking, creating space for more learners to flourish. The Lab School of Finance and Technology emphasizes  “real-world applications” and “21st-century skills,” aligning closely with career readiness goa ls. This frames learning as a transactional set of skills that enables students to “work productively” toward  predefined outcomes, demonstrating professional pro mise. Problem-solving transforms into performance, resonating with the entrepreneurial ethos. However, in many fields, the process matters as much as polished results. Practices like design logs and reflective documentati on can shift the focus, not abandoning career readiness but expanding it. This reframing helps avoid replicating dominant epistemologies (Agarwal & Sengupta-Irving, 2019) and better equips students for diverse futures.

## Conclusion

STEM and Maker Education have reintroduced to mainstream education the possibility of more student-centered, project-based, constructionist pedagogies. Yet, this has inadvertently placed too much emphasis on solving “realworld” problems, which surprisingly align closely w ith discourses of entrepreneurship, that framing success in outcome-driven terms. Programs that reward competition, spe ed, and visibility reflect broader societal values of efficiency and control, shaping who is recognized as an “ideal lea rner.” But, that was n ever part of the original constructionist scholarship (see Ackermann’s quote  opening the paper). While the entrepreneurial mindset may serve some, it leaves little room for slow inquiry, reflection, or non-linear growth. This critique urges us to broaden what learning looks like in Maker Education. All students benefit from environments that honor process alongside product and allow for ambiguity, iteration, and curiosity (Blikstein & Worsley, 2016). Reimagining assessment through portfolios, narrative projects, or design logs can foster habits of thinking that transcend disciplinary boundaries. These practices deepen learning and democratize success by decentering performance and impact as the sole marker of growth. As the field evolves, we must ask not only what problems students can solve but how they are invited to engage, explore, and define value. This entails designing learning that resists urgency, welcomes difference, and fosters humility. It is within this expanded makerspace, not marketplace, that all learners cando and think.
