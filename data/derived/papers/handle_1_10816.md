# How Activists did Heterogeneous Civic Data Science to Hold a Social Services Agency Accountable to the Public

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents an ethnographic case study of a team of activists who used data science to hold a social services agency accountable to the public. The activists engaged in six data science practices that we describe as heterogeneous, to mean that they involved both the organizing of humans and technical work (Law et al., 1987; Stevens et al., 2013; Suchman, 2000). K-12 youth who want to use data science to improve public social services in their cities, beyond the classroom, should also learn to engage in heterogeneous data science practices.

## Introduction

Data science is having a moment, a moment that may last a while. So educators are seeking guidance abo ut how to teach data science, which in turn raises the question of where educators should get their images of data science. Should it be from data science uses in corporations  using ‘big data’ to carry out surveillance capital ism (Zuboff, 2018)? Perhaps. But we argue that images should also be built from contexts where data science is used for social good. Such research grounded images can then be a r esource for educational efforts that take data scie nce in a social good direction (Irgens et al., 2020). Our research offers one set of images from  a n adult  non-profit organization, where data science is used for social good in a large midwestern city (Nguyen & Stevens, 2023). The current case tells the story of how a team of activists did heterogeneous data science in order hold a transit agency in their city accountable to the public. By calling the team’s data science heterogeneous, we mean that they worked to assemble not only technical elements but also human actors into a stable network (Law et al., 1987; Stevens et al., 2013; Suchman, 2000). We documented six heterogeneous data science practices: accessing historical public data, using a tool to watch the agency, building a tool to track the agency’s attempts to publicize a misleading metric, recruiting journalists, navigating legal regulations, and inserting allies inside the city agency.

## Methods: Research context, data collection, and analytic methodology

Our research context is a non-profit called Civic Technology Volunteers (CTV) whe re volunteers meet weekly, after working nine-to-five jobs as data scientists, to address social justice issues in their city. In a previous paper, we studied how a CTV Team  moved to expose a city agency’s failure  to provide a key service,  at a public contestation (Nguyen & Stevens, 2023). In the present paper, we document what follows that contestation. For our methodology, the first author conducted ethnographic participant observation. He attended and video-recorded the team’s weekly meetings, conducted semi-structured interviews, and collected documents from the Team’s GitHub software repository and Slack communication channel. The material in this paper represents approximately 70 hours of video data and 5 hours of interview data. For our analysis, we documented how humans and tool s stabilize together intonetworks, extended over time and space (cf. “tracing associations”, Latour,  2007) and at the level of tasks (cf. “cognitive et hnography”, Hutchins, 2003). We arranged all the collected records in a chronological sequence start ing in June 2022 and ending in October 2023. Then, we examined our records, identified controversies surrounding data, identified the humans and tools involved, documented their associations over time, documenting if and how they  stabilized. Finally, we selected events that emerged repeatedly in our analysis. ## Chronological Sequence of Events in this Case Study

Data and analysis In a previous paper, we followed a team at the CTV as they used data science to advocate for social justice in their city (Nguyen & Stevens, 2023). The focus of that case  was, in the aftermath of the COVID pandemic, citizens noticed that the city’s Municipal Transit Agency (MTA) had become unreliable — citizens experienced what they called “phantom runs”, which is when scheduled trains or buses never arrive. Transit riders voiced concerns about phantom runs to the MTA but were ignored. One transit rider, a software developer named Fred, started Citizens Take Action (CTAction) to organize a grassroots movement to get the MTA to address phantom runs (Event 1). Inspired by Fred, CTV volunteers formed a team in June 2022 (Event 2) to use data science to definitively document the issue of phantom buses to compel the MTA to confront rather than ignore it. The “Phantom Buses” (PB) Team learned about an upcoming contestation — a special committee on transit at the next meeting of the MTA Oversight Board in September, where local politicians called aldermen planned to interrogate the MTA’s president about phantom buses. The audience for the special committee would be the  Oversight Board, city officials, and citizens. To prepare a “public comment” for the committee, th e PB Team “drew together” (Latour,

1987) publicly-available MTA data into a synoptic map showing the extent and inequitable distribution of

phantom buses across the city. The phantom buses metric was represented by the ratio of the bus’s actual trips to its scheduled trips. It appears that the MTA was aware of the phantom buses issues because on September 1st, two weeks before the Oversight Board Meeting, the MTA unveiled their #ChoosingTransparency initiative (Event 3), where they promised to share all aspects of their operations, and to show “performance metrics”, including the same as the PB Team’s. In the previous paper, we showed how the PB Team prepared their map for the anticipated contestation (Nguyen & Stevens, 2023). In what follows, we will tell the story of the aftermath of that contestation. On the day of the long-awaited contestation at MTA Headquarters, something happened that surprised all who were present — the MTA president simply failed to appear (Event 4). The aldermen questioned the only MTA staff present, who relayed  that the MTA would provide better service by “optimizing” its schedule, which means changing the number of bus trips that were scheduled to run. The MTA staff did not say whether these scheduled service numbers would go up or down, nor by exactly how muc h. At their next we ekly PB Team meeting on September 20 th, Lilah expressed a concern that MTA was intentionally tryi ng to mislead the city by cutting scheduled service to boost their performanc e metric (Event 5). Lilah said, “we don’t want it to get to the point where the [MTA] is like ‘oh we updated our schedule s, all the buses only run once an hour’, and that w ay the data would always make it look like they’re bea ting their service targets”. Given that the phantom buses performance metric is calculated as the ratio of actual service divided by scheduled service, any cut in scheduled service (the denominator) would result in an improvement in the overall metric. Thus, Lilah was concerned that the MTA would try to mislead the public into believing that the MTA had fixed the issue of phantom buses more than it actually had, by publicizing only the boosted performance metric but not the schedule cuts. In the following weeks, the  Team prepared to track the MTA should they attempt to quietly cut their scheduled bus service to boost their performance metric. The PB Team opened a GitHub Issue to track the MTA’s data going back to before the COVID-19 pandemic (Event 6). The Team learned that the MTA was only legally required to publicly release their data for a short time before deleting it. So, they drew on the work of a nonprofit called BusFeeds, that had already archived  government transit data from countr ies across the world. BusFeeds granted the PB Team access to the MTA’s long-deleted historical data (Event 7). Thus the PB Team had assembled an institutional actor (BusFeeds) and a technical element (the historical MTA dataset) into their network, the first instance of heterogeneous civic data science in this case study. By the end of October, the Team had  not heard any news of an attempted schedule service cut. But, still watchful, they set up a computer program to follow the MTA’s web traces and to send the team a notification should the MTA ever attempt to quietly cut scheduled bus service to boost their performance metric (Event 8). Thus the PB Team had assembled a nonhuman (the computer program) into their network, marking another heterogeneous data science practice in this case. A few months later, the PB Team’s suspicions  — that the MTA would mislead the public by cutting service to boost their performance metric — started to become a reality. On January 9th, the MTA quietly cut their schedule effective immediately and updated their website with updated schedules (Event 9). That same morning the PB Team alerted one another of the schedule cut  through a Slack  post (Event 10). Fred immediately replied with a cautionary tale from his experience with MTA trains  (Event 11). Fred explained that a few months ago back in October, the MTA told the public they had made train service more frequent and reliable by “optimizing” it, while his calculations showed they had cut it by 12% overall. Fred was so frustrated  at the MTA for misleading the public about cutting train schedules  back in October, that he offered to help the PB team prevent the MTA from doing the same for buses. The last two weeks of January (Event 12), Fred and the PB Team analyzed the MTA’s schedule changes and concluded that scheduled service had been cut by more than 10% for all lines taken together, and up to 45% for some lines. In the next two months, Fred extended the PB Team’s network with two more actors: a journalist, and a “public commen t”, marking two more instances of heterogeneous data sc ience in this case study. In February Fred worked w ith a journalist publish a news article about the cuts on February 7 (Event 13). And a few weeks later, at the next MTA Oversight Board meeting on March 8 th, Fred submitted his completed data analysis through an official written “public comment” (Event 14). Fred explained to the Board that the MTA president had, unable to deliver more service, simply “adjust[ed] the benchmark” and was “playing the Board for a fool”. The MTA president rebutted, said it was he who had originally provided the open data  that Fred had used  (Event 15), though open data was required by a 2015 State Legislative Act. Following the president’s rebuttal, the Oversight Board did not address Fred’s public comment, instead they complimented the president’s post-COVID leadership. Thus Fred’s had not convinced the Board that the MTA’s metric did not represent the actual extent of the phantom buses problem. In the following months, the MTA continued to publicize a misleading performance metric, through new tools and media. On  March 27 th, the MTA released an “interactive scorecard” that allowed viewers to see the phantom buses performance metric not just for all b us lines in aggregate, but also for each individual bus line. This time the MTA used  the social media platform Twitter/ X as a medium to circulate their facts.  The MTA tweeted that the scorecard was “just one of the ways we’re #ChoosingTransparency for our riders” (Event 16). Once again, the PB Team inspected the MTA’s scorecard (Event 17). The scorecard, for one bus line, reported an increase in the performance metric (a 3.5% increase scheduled buses that did in fact arrive, up from 65.7% to 69.2%) while omitting the cuts to scheduled service that comprised the denominator of the performance m etric. For the next week, Diego complete data analysis which showed that scheduled bus service, on the whole,  had decreased 10.5% and the metric increased a corresponding 10%, whereas actual service had only increased 0.78%. He shared to the Team that the new metrics were, indeed, an artifact of cuts to the scheduled service (Event 18). In the following months, the Team worked to build a tool of their own to track the MTA’s representations by each separate bus route. In April, the team decided that their tool would display time-series charts to make the MTA’s attempts to mislead apparent to the public (Event 19). From May to July, the Team worked to make their tool compatible not only on desktop computers, but also on mobile devices (Event 20). The MTA used the one year anniversary of the #Choosing Transparency initiative in August to agai n present its misleading metric (Event 22). The Team once again reassessed the MTA’s data and were disappointed, but not surprised, to learn that the MTA was actually doing even worse than before — they had decreased actual service, and even further  decreased schedules, to maintain the inflated overall metric that they had grown accustomed to presenting to the public (Event 23). By the end of October the Team completed their tool (Event 24). Figure 2 shows the tool which, for one example bus line that services the city’s South Side, juxtaposes how  from November 2022 to February 2023  the  performance metric increased from 80% tonearly 100% in Figure 2(a), while the raw actual and scheduled decreased in Figure 2 (b). In late October the Team circulated the tool via social media networks (Event 25).

From May to October 2023, the PB Team worked to get members from two allied groups onto the newly announced Citizen’s Advisory Board (CAB), where they would have a literal “seat at the table” to hold the MTA accountable — the last heterogeneous civic data science practice in this case. Where things stood at the time of this writing, the PB Team was cautiously hopeful th at perhaps this time, after a year of effort, their  data science work might shape the actions of the MTA and therefore the transit experiences of citizens. ## Discussion

In this case study, the PB Team sought to use data science to get the MTA to be more transparent with the city’s public about how it was addressing the issue of phantom buses. The PB Team anticipated that the MTA might try to mislead the public  into believing that they had fixed phantom buses more than they actually had —by cutting scheduled service to artificially boost their performance metric. The PB Team watched the MTA closely and, when the MTA did attempt to mislead the public, the team moved very quickly, building a tracking tool to reopen public debate about MTA’s misleading claims. Thus the case study illustrates a distinction that Bruno Latour has made between two ways data scientists can approach data, which are seeing data as a “matter of fact” that are closed to debate, versus as a “matter of concern” to be re-opened to public debate (Latour, 2007). Throughout the case study, the PB Team’s civic data science involved six practices that we described as heterogeneous, in the sense that  they assembled human and nonhuman  actors into stable networks  (Law et al., 1987; Stevens et al., 2013; Suchman, 2000). In the chronological order we presented in the case, the PB Team (a) cultivated human allies in the BusFeed nonprofit to gain access to nonhuman long-deleted historical MTA dataset,

(b) built a nonhuman computer program  help them keep tabs on the MTA’s every move, (c) composed a

nonhuman time-series-based tool to track the MTA’s continued attempts to publicize a misleading metric, (d) recruited human journalists to help them circulate their data to the public, (e) wrote an “official public comment” to the MTA’s Oversight Board with data-backed evidence of the MTA’s deception (f) and put human allies inside the MTA’s Citizen’s Advisory Board where they will literally get a “seat at the table”. K-12 educators who wish to see students learn to use data science to positively impact public social services in their cities, rather than to use it for  surveillance capitalism (Zuboff, 201 8), may engage youth in heterogeneous data science practices, like the PB activists did in this case. If this case study were  to be used as the design template for a K-12 data science education experience, then by engaging in the practice  labeled (c), youth would get experience with the data science practices of collecting, anal yzing, and visualizing data. Furthermore, by engaging with the other five practices, youth wo uld learn how to take the results of their data science work beyond the classroom, to have a real impact on  city life. Thus, this case study joins with efforts to teach students to use data science for social justi ce (Irgens et al., 2020). In sum, we argue that K-12 data science learning environments should draw from ethnographic ally grounded case studies of data science used for  civic advocacy like this one.
