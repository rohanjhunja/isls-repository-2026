# Designing for Affective Equity in Partnerships

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research-practice partnerships (RPPs) have become central to the learning sciences as infrastructures for addressing educational challenges. While much scholarship emphasizes their design principles and effectiveness, less attention has been paid to the affective life of partnerships. This paper advances a conceptual argument that RPPs function as pedagogical sites of affectivity. They not only rely on dispositions such as trust, humility, or perseverance but also cultivate, normalize, and organize which affective orientations become legitimate forms of participation. I call this the ontological goodness of RPPs – the assumption that collaboration is inherently moral and transformative which imbues partnerships with affective demands that shape who can participate fully and on what terms. Reframing purpose as an affective terrain highlights a hidden curriculum of partnership and raises new learning and design questions: what would it mean to design RPPs for affective equity where multiple orientations are legible and recognized as legitimate forms of participation?

Research–practice partnerships (RPPs) have become central to the learning sciences as infrastructures for connecting researchers, practitioners, and community organizations to address pressing educational and social challenges. Defined as long-term collaborations aimed at producing knowledge that advances both research and practice, RPPs are valued for their ability to bridge silos, improve systems, and promote equity (Farrell et al., 2021). Much of the existing scholarship highlights their design principles, organizational structures, and effectiveness in generating usable knowledge (Farrell et al., 2019; Farrell et al., 202 1; Penuel, Allen & Farrell, 2015).

Yet beneath this emphasis on effectiveness lies another dimension: the affective life of partnerships. RPPs do not simply rely on dispositions such as trust, humility, perseverance, or empathy; they also cultivate, normalize, and circulate these affective dispositions through their organizational norms, discourses, and routines. I understand affect not as individual emotion or internal state, but as relational and socio-culturally organized orientations that emerge through participation in activity (Vea, 20 20), and as the ways bodies are drawn toward and away from others, objects, and worlds, shaping interaction (Ahmed, 2013). From this perspective, affect is not only brought into RPPs, but learned, negotiated and stabilized through participation. This paper draws on both dimensions: affect as something learned through participation in activity (Vea, 2020), and affect as something that circulates through social forms, organizing which orientations become sensible and legitimate within them (Ahmed, 2013; Berlant, 2011). Together, these perspectives make it possible to examine not only how partnerships function as sites of affective learning, but also how the form of the partnership itself conditions what can be felt, expressed and recognized as partic ipation. This paper advances a conceptual argument that RPPs are not only organizational arrangements but also pedagogical sites of affectivity. In other words, partnerships can function as sites where participants learn how to orient affectively, shaping who can participate fully and under what terms.  Attending to this dimension also complicates the very language of “partnering with purpose”. As Fishman and colleagues (2013) remind us, partnerships must be more than technically and procedurally sound – they should also be culturally responsive, locally contextualized, and collaboratively built. Yet, the phrase “partnering with purpose” itself presumes a shared onto-epistemological and affective grammar (Gamez-Djokic, 2024). By foregrounding how affectivity and cultural politics are historically and locally formed, this paper suggests that partnering with purpose is always contested terrain. Partnerships not only organize collaborative goals, but also circulate affect ive norms about what counts as good, desi rable, or legitimate participation.

By reframing "purpose" as affective terrain, I suggest that RPPs  function as sites where affective orientations are not only brought into collaboration but also shaped, scaffolded, and taken up through participation. This reframing builds on learning sciences scholarship that conceptualizes affect as socioculturally organized and learnable (Vea, 2020) and demonstrates how designed interactions can support the emergence and extension of particular affective orientations (Potvin et al., 2024). At the same time, it highlights a critical gap: while this work shows how affect can be cultivated, less attention has been paid to how RPPs also organize which affective orientations become normative, desirable or legitimate forms of participation. Recognizing RPPs as pedagogical sites of affectivity raises new questions for the learning sciences: what does it mean to design for affective equity? In conceptualizing “affective equity,” I refer to the extent to which a range of affective 1114 orientations, such as skepticism, frustration, ambivalence, in addition to trust or optimism, are recognized as legitimate forms of participation within partnership work. Affective equity attends to how collaborative arrangements organize affective formations and how individuals are drawn into particular positions and orientations within them.

Affect in the learning sciences & RPP literature Recent work in the learning sciences has begun to theorize learning as involving not only conceptual or epistemic change, but also shifts in affective orientations and relations. For example, Vea (2020) conceptualizes emotion as sociocultural practice, highlighting how participants learn to feel through participation in activity. Similarly, Bang and Vossoughi (2016) argue that participatory design can support “axiological innovation,” or the reorganization of how people relate to one another and to what is considered good or desirable. More recently, Potvin and colleagues (2024) demonstrate how co-design environments that foreground affect and relational experience can support participants in developing and extending orientations such as compassion across contexts. Taken together, this work suggests that learning in RPPs may involve not only knowledge production, but also the cultivation of particular ways of feeling and relating.

While this body of work foregrounds affect as something learned through participation, much of the RPP literature treats affect differently, approaching it primarily as a set of dispositions needed to sustain collaboration and achieve partnership goals.  For instance, studies examining distrust between researchers and practitioners, divergent timelines, differing priorities, and the challenge of forging a shared language for improvement (Farrell et al., 2019; Farrell et al., 2021)  highlight the need for cultivating particular dispositions that make collaboration possible. Davidson et al. (2019) identify perseverance, humility, empathy, and open-mindedness as "habits of mind" most conducive to working in partnerships. Wilcox and Zuckerman (2019) demonstrate that motivational states such as goal orientation, self-efficacy, and anxiety (or will) are crucial to achieving partnership outcomes like school improvement. Other work stresses belonging, motivation, and trust as central to sustaining collaborative practic es (Popa et al., 2023; Fischer -Schöneborn & Ehmke, 2023; Doucet, 2021). During the COVID-19 pandemic, flexibility and openness were emphasized as dispositions that enabled partnerships to adapt to disruption (Korhonen et al., 2024).

Across these examples, affect is often oriented toward supporting effective partnership functioning. Dispositions are cast as traits participants bring with them or as characteristics that can be cultivated to sustain collaboration, build trust, or improve outcomes. While this work increasingly recognizes that affect matters – and in some cases, that it is shaped through participation – it tends to frame affect in relation to what makes partnerships work. In this sense, partnerships are often treated as contexts in which affect is mobilized or supported, rather than as sites that actively organize which affective orientations become normative, desirable, or legitimate. Affect, then, is not simply a variable to be managed, but a dimension of participation that is unevenly valued and structured within partnership activity. The learning sciences has shown that affect is learned through participation in designed environments; it has paid less attention to how partnership arrangements themselves constitute that en vironment – organizing which orientations become recognizable as legitimate collaboration, whether or not this is intended.

Dispositions as pedagogy What this framing overlooks is that RPPs themselves function pedagogically. When partnership protocols emphasize buy-in, for instance, as essential for participation, they position agreement as inherently more valuable than questioning.  Similarly, early partnership meetings often include activities designed to secure alignment around shared goals, where expressions of agreement are taken up as indicators of readiness to collaborate, while hesitation or critique may register as  recalcitrance or lack of commi tment. When meetings are structured around building consensus, participants learn that harmony is the desired affective state rather than productive tension (Ghiso et al, 2019). In such settings, disagreement may be softened or deferred in favor of maintaining group cohesion. And, when “political will” (Anderson, 2023) becomes a prerequisite for partnership engagement, participants may come to learn that enthusiasm and commitment must be performed as evidence of legitimate participation. Displays of skeptic ism or ambivalence, in turn, may become less legible as valid forms of engagement. I argue that these organizational practices and norms also carry affective demands that are embedded within the very architectures of partnership work. Calls for affective qualities like buy-in, trust-building, and political will, among others, implicitly instruct participants in how to feel, when to feel it, and what kinds of affect count as legitimate.

If affect is organized through the social forms and norms that structure participation (Vea, 2020; Ahmed, 2013), then the form of the RPP itself matters. From this perspective, RPPs are not just research infrastructures, but genres in the Berlantian sense (Berlant, 2011); that is, they are recognizable social forms that magnetize 1115 participants by organizing expectations, roles, and affective orientations. The cultivation of trust, humility, or perseverance is an intentional part of how  RPPs function. This understanding helps explain why positive dispositions often appear to improve collaboration – not because they are inherently necessary, but because they offer an aesthetic sense of partnership coherence and continuity. This enables a kind of ongoingness or endurance (Berlant, 2011) that allows  collaboration to “keep on,” even if this does not produce deeper relational transformation.

This implicit curriculum rests on what I call the ontological goodness of RPPs — the assumption that participating in collaborative research is inherently valuable and transformative  (Gamez-Djokic, 2026). This goodness imbues partnerships with moral weight, encouraging participants to endure uncertainty (given RPPs’ unclear timelines, shifting priorities, ambiguous outcomes), invest emotional labor, and perform affective dispositions even when outcomes re main uncertain. While the literature acknowledges the  importance of affect, it rarely interrogates how RPPs themselves cultivate it, or the implications of these affective lessons for equity. In a micro-interactional analysis of one RPP, for instance, moments of epistemic alignment in which participants oriented toward shared understanding were taken up as evidence of productive collaboration, while instances of questioning or reframing were m anaged at the interactional level in ways that preserved surface consensus (Gamez-Djokic, 2024). This suggests that the affective demand for buy-in is not only a structural feature of partnership design but is actively reproduced in the turn-by-turn organization of partnership talk. Situated and temporal specificities RPPs are not abstract or universal. They emerge within specific cultural, historical, and organizational contexts that shape how their affective demands are experienced. Recent scholarship emphasizes this situatedness. Fjortoft and Sandvik (2021) argue for  “situated strategies” that reflect the cultural and institutional specificities of an RPP's context. Shirrell et al. (2023) demonstrate how local histories and political conditions shape which institutional logics—research, practice, or community —become m ost salient in determining legitimacy. Oyewole et al. (2023) call for “honoring local contexts” in RPP design, warning that partnerships often neglect the cultural, historical, and economic realities of the geographies in which they are embedded. From an affective perspective, this means that the “hidden curriculum” and affective lessons of RPPs are always historically and locally specific. While the ontological goodness of RPPs creates general expectations about collaboration, the ways participants learn to feel and orient affectively toward partnerships are mediated by the particular structures of feeling (Williams, 1977) of their context. In a district with a history of reform fatigue, for instance, orientations toward partnership may be shaped by guarded hopefulness; in a community where deficit narratives about schools and students proliferate, they may be shaped by desires for recognition and dignity. In each case, the orientations participants bring to RPPs are formed in relation to situated histories, politics, and cultural logics. How RPPs organize affect, then, depends on the spatiotemporal assemblages in which they are embedded. Recognizing this situatedness underscores the need for the learning sciences to study not only how partnerships are structurally designed but also how they function affectively as pedagogical environments. RPPs as pedagogical sites of affective learning This paper initiates two interrelated contributions to the learning sciences. First, I surface the implicit positive affectivity that underlies partnership work. While RPP research has highlighted the importance of dispositions such as trust, humility, and perseverance, it has often treated these as qualities participants bring to or develop for collaboration, rather than as effects of the partnership form itself. Building on work that conceptualizes affect as socioculturally organized and learned through participation (Vea, 2020), as well as research demonstrating how collaborative design can support shifts in relational and affective orientations (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Potvein et al., 2024), this paper highlights how these seemingly neutral affective demands function pedagogically, suggesting that there is something inherently moral and transformative about participating in RPPs. By conceptualizing RPPs as pedagogical formations, I argue that partnerships  cultivate dispositions through organizational norms, routines, and discourses, implicitly making certain ways of feeling and relating more legible and recognizable as participation. This reframing shifts the focus from affect as an external input to affect as a pedagogical output of partnership design. Together, these contributions expand how the learning sciences conceptualize and study RPPs. Partnerships are not only sites for knowledge generation but also spaces where affective lessons are taught, learned, and circulated with real consequences for equity.

## Implications for learning sciences

Viewing RPPs as pedagogical sites of affectivity raises critical questions for research, theory, and design in the learning sciences: 1116 For research: How do RPPs cultivate affective dispositions, and what are the implications for participation, identity, and equity? How do organizational routines, meeting structures, and discourses function as pedagogical mechanisms of affect? Methodologically, researchers might employ ethnographic approaches to trace how affective orientations shift over partnership duration, analyze meeting transcripts for implicit affective demands, or examine how different participants experience and navigate partnership's emotional expectations. For theory: Recognizing the implicit positive affectivity of RPPs – both the assumption that certain affects are inherently valuable and the hidden requirement that participants embody them – challenges fundamental theoretical assumptions about collaborative neutrality. This necessitates expanding how learning sciences conceptualizes partnerships beyond approaches that treat affect as something to be cultivated for collaboration, rather than as an effect of the partnership form itself. Instead, theory must account for how partnership forms compel particular affective orientations, functioning as pedagogical sites with their own hidden curricula of “good collaboration”. This theoretical reframing invites analysis of whose affective labor is demanded, whose orientations are privileged, and how inequities are reproduced through particular collaborative designs and practices. Such framework acknowledges that while positive dispositions often enhance collaboration, theoretical approaches that naturalize their necessity can obscure how requiring their performance excludes participants whose experiences or cultural orientations lead them to engage differently. For design: If partnerships organize  affectivity, then affective conditions are designable. This does not mean that particular feelings should be engineered into partnership life, but that design work must grapple with the affective lessons partnerships transmit. What kinds of affective orientations do partnership routines and protocols invite, and how do these invitations shape participation? What would it mean for RPPs to acknowledge that their structures and routines cultivate not only practices of collaboration b ut also particular ways of feeling? How might designers of partnerships attend to the implicit expectations that shape which orientations are legible and legitimate?  Considering design from this perspective raises several points of inquiry. One is how partnerships might surface and reflect on their affective assumptions as active forces in the partnership. Still another is how organizational, cultural and historical contexts shape the affective possibilities available to participants. Design for affective equity, then, is less a set of techniques than a stance; it is an openness to noticing how affect circulates through partnership forms. This does not entail designing for a particular set of “better” affective norms. Rather, it involves attending to how partnership arrangements organize affective life, and considering how conditions might be created in which multiple orientations can coexist without being rendered illegible or incompatible with participation.

The 2026 ISLS theme of “partnering with purpose” emphasizes building cross-sector collaborations that are impactful, inclusive, and equitable. This paper has argued that purpose is not only a matter of goals, outcomes, or design principles but also an affective terrain. RPPs organize the conditions under which particular affective orientations become recognizable and legitimate forms of participation, shaping how collaboration is experienced and enacted.

Reframing purpose in this way extends the scope of the learning sciences in three important ways. First, it highlights affective learning central to RPPs, not simply as something cultivated through design, but as something structured through participation in particular forms of collaborative activity. Second, it surfaces the hidden curriculum of RPPs, questioning the ontological goodness that makes particular qualities, such as optimism, trust, and buy-in, appear as moral imperatives rather than as situated  and contestable expectations. Third, it repositions design not as the engineering of desirable affective states, but as an inquiry into how partnership arrangements shape what kinds of affective orientations are legible and valued. In this sense, RPPs can be understood not only as sites for cultivating particular orientations, but as sites for learning about how affect circulates, is mediated, and becomes reconfigured within collaborative practice. This shift opens new possibilities for designing toward affective equity – not by prescribing which dispositions partnerships should produce, but by attending to how their routines and structures enable or constrain a range of ways of feeling and relating. In short, if the learning sciences are to take seriously the theme of “partnering with purpose,” they must grapple with how purpose itself is affective. RPPs are pedagogical sites not only for knowledge generation but for the organization of affective life  in collaboration. Recognizing this dimension allows us to reimagine partnerships that are not only effective but also equitable in their affective demands; that is, collaborations that expand what it means to participate, feel, and belong in collective work.
