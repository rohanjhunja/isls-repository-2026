# Cognitive Load and Epistemic Emotions Interact to Determine Students’ Performance in a Technology-Rich Environment

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Researchers have examined the separate roles of cognitive load and epistemic emotions in influencing students’ performance; however, more evidence is needed to explore the interaction of these two constructs and their joint effects on performance. In this study, a total of 107 students solved a clinical problem within a technology-rich environment, during which they reported their perceptions of cognitive load (i.e., intrinsic, extraneous, and germane load), surprise, confusion, curiosity, and enjoyment. Task performance was indicated by diagnostic accuracy and efficiency. Results of path analyses revealed that intrinsic and extraneous load positively predicted surprise and confusion, whereas germane load negatively predicted curiosity and enjoyment. Moreover, this study found four significant mediation effects: intrinsic loadsurpriseaccuracy (negative); extraneous loadconfusionefficiency (negative); germane loadenjoymentaccuracy (positive); and germane loadcuriosityefficiency (positive). Overall, these findings suggested that the predictive effects of cognitive load could be mediated by different epistemic emotions.

## Introduction

Educational researchers are increasingly interested  in how students experience cognitive load in technology-rich learning environments (TREs). Cognitive load descri bes the amount of working memory capacity occupied by cognitive processes while solving a task (Sweller, 2011). According to the cognitive load theory, learners ma y experience three types of cognitive load in TREs: i ntrinsic, extraneous, and germane (Sweller, 2011). Intrinsic load (ICL) is inherent to task materials; the design factors o f TREs, such as presentation modes of auditory and verbal information, are a source of extraneous load (ECL); germane load (GCL) is associated with the construction and automation of cognitive schemas.

In addition to cognitive load, students are very likely to experience epistemic em otions  (Muis et al.,

2018) that are closely tied to the quality of knowledge a nd knowledge generation processes  in TREs. More

specifically, cognitive disequilibrium or incongruity students experienced during the cognitive task is assumed to be an essential trigger for a range of epistemic emotions (D’mello & Graesser, 2012). For example, surprise occurs when students encounter information they did not ex pect or cannot explain (Vogl et al., 2020), and the  intensity of surprise depends on the novelty and complexity o f the incoming information. When discrepant informa tion is perceived as highly novel and complex but still understandable, students may experience curiosity (Silvia, 2010). However, when the discrepant information is deemed incompreh ensible, confusion will be triggered. Enjoyment is elicited once the conflicts between discrepant information and knowledge are resolved or the formed hypotheses are verified (D’mello & Graesser, 2012).

Moreover, cognitive load and epistemic emotions are not isolated, and their relationships have also been explored for several decades. For example, research ers have proposed that the generative process of em otions requires learners to self-appraise the value of a task and assess their contr ol over it, which in turn demands cognitive resources and leads to cognitive load (Plass & Kalyuga, 2019). A typical example is that anxiety was a positive indicator of perceived cognitive load (Eys enck et al., 2007). However, most propositions only highlight the influences of emotions on cognitive load but overlook the possibility that cognitive load could also affect the generation of various emotions, especially epistemi c emotion s. Specifically, cognitive load and epistemic emotions have many inherent connections. They both relate to information and information processes. Cognitive load theory identifies information and cognitive pr ocesses as sources of three types of cognitive load  (Sweller, 2011), while the object focus of epistemic emotions  is also the information and processes of knowing  (Pekrun et al., 2017). Furthermore, epistemic emotions arise f rom the cognitive incongruity caused by the novelty, complexity, and unexpectedness of incoming informat ion, and perceived intrinsic and extraneous load in dicates the degree of cognitive incongruity to some degree (Kirschner et al., 2011). Additionally, germane loa d signals the possibility of solving discrepant information. High germane load indicates that learners are inves ting more cognitive efforts in integrating incoming informati on into prior knowledge (Debue & Vande Leemput, 20 14), and there is a greater likelihood of resolving cogn itive incongruity, which may lead students to exper ience enjoyment. However, more evidence is still needed t o verify the predictive effects of cognitive load o n various epistemic emotions, especially taking into account the types of cognitive load. Therefore, the present study is to address the abov e gap in the context of dynamic and recursive diagnostic problem-solving, during which medical practitioners collect  patient information, order lab tests, propose diagnostic hypotheses, and make treatment p lans (Kuiper, 2013). More specifically, we addresse d the following research questions: (1) Do intrinsic, extraneous, and germane load predict students’ epistemic emotions (i.e., surprise, confusion, curiosity, and enjoymen t)? (2) Do intrinsic, extraneous,  and germane load have direct effects on diagnostic performance? (3) Do epistemic  emotions mediate the relationship between cognitiv e load and diagnostic performance? Moreover, we propose a hypothesized model based on theoretical and empiric al considerations (Figure 1). Specifically, we hypothesize that bo th ICL and ECL  will positively predict surprise, confusion, and curiosity and negatively predict enj oyment. GCL will negatively predict surprise and confusion and positively predict curiosity and enjoyment. Mor eover, we expect positive effects of surprise and c onfusion and negative effects of curiosity and enjoyment on performance. Finally, we also hypothesize that  ICL (negatively), ECL (negatively), and GCL (positively) directly predict diagnostic performance. Figure 1 The Hypothesized Model Note. ## Participants, learning context, and task

A total of 107 undergraduate medical students, cons isting of 78  female and 29 male students, voluntee red to participate in this study. Participants were tasked with diagnosing a virtual patient using BioWorld, an intelligent tutoring system that supports students in their dia gnostic problem-solving processes. To complete the task, students first read a case summary describing patient information and symptoms. Then they ordered medical tests or searched the online library to collect more evidence. Based on the gathered evidence, students could formulate their diagnostic hypotheses and evalu ated the possibility of these hypotheses to submit a final diagnosis. Afterwards, they wrote a reflective summary of the task process and reviewed the performance feedback automatically generated by BioWorld. In the present study, students were instructed to diagnose the virtual patient Amy in BioWorld, and the correct diagnosis was Diabetes (Type I). Procedures This study involved a training session and a task session. During the training session, students were given a sample task to familiarize themselves with the functions o f BioWorld. In the task session, they completed a f ormal diagnostic task and answer several questionnaires. More specifically, students were required to report  their emotions at three different time points: after revi ewing the case summary (Time 1), submitting their f inal hypothesis (Time 2), and receiving performance feed back (Time 3). They also reported perceived cogniti ve load after reading the performance feedback.

## Measures

Cognitive load To capture students’ cognitive load, this study ada pted the subjective rating scale developed by Leppi nk et al.

(2014) to the clinical reasoning context. Specifica lly, this scale included 13 items, with four items for ICL, five

for ECL, and four for the GCL. The Cronbach’s alpha values for ICL, ECL, and GCL were.84,.75, and.91. Epistemic emotions We modified the epistemically related emotion scale  (EES; Pekrun et al., 2017) to measure students’ ep istemic emotions. In particular, this study involved four t ypes of epistemic emotions: surprise, confusion, cu riosity, and enjoyment. Each emotion was measured by three items, and each item consisted of a single adjective des cribing the emotion. The Cronbach’s alpha values for surpri se, confusion, curiosity, and enjoyment were.88,. 88,.89, and.95 at Time 1,.93,.87,.85, and.93 at Time 2,.94,.92,.91, and.93 at Time 3. Diagnostic performance This study captured two performance metrics: diagno stic accuracy and efficiency. Diagnostic accuracy r efers to whether medical students achieved a correct diagnos is, with the values of 1 and 0 representing an accurate  and inaccurate diagnosis, respectively.  Diagnostic efficiency  indicates how efficient students’ diagnostic processes were. Specifically, we considered the total number of lab tests students conducted during clinical rea soning and calculated the ratio, i.e., the number of necessary lab tests /the total number of lab tests, to represent efficiency.

## Data analysis

This study first aggregated epistemic emotion score s measured at Times 1, 2, and 3 and used the averag e scores to indicate students’ epistemic emotions during the task process. To examine the relationships between cognitive load, epistemic emotions, and ta sk performance, we employed the PROCESS module in t he SmartPLS 4.0 software (Ringle et al., 2022) to perform path anal ysis. In this study, ICL, ECL, and GCL were entered as three independent variables, the four epistemic emotions (i.e., surprise, confusion, curiosity, and enjoyment) served as parallel mediator variables, and two performance metrics (i.e., diagnostic accuracy and efficiency) were dependent variables. Additionally, this study employed the bootstrapping technique (Davison & Hinkley, 1997) with 10000 bias-corrected bootstrap subsamples to test the statisti cal significance of estimated path coefficients. In  addition to path coefficients, this study also used f-square (f2) to evaluate the model (Ringle et al., 2022), with the f2 values of.35,.15, and.02 reflecting large, medium, and small effect sizes, respectively.

## Discussion and conclusion

This study examined the predictive roles of cogniti ve load in epistemic emotions and their joint effec ts on task performance. As expected, intrinsic load positive ly predicted surprise and confusion, which could be explained by students’ varying prior knowledge. All participants solved an identical task in this study, indicating that those with high intrinsic load were likely to have less prior knowledge and experience a greater discrepancy betw een incoming information and existing knowledge. This d iscrepancy led to cognitive incongruity and ultimately resulted in surprise. More importantly, students with less p rior knowledge may perceive the task information as complex and beyond their current capacities, leadin g to the feeling of confusion (Silvia, 2010). Likew ise, extraneous load also positively predicted surprise and confusion. The high extraneous load indicated that students had invested considerable cognitive resources to de al with learning-irrelevant information, such as the inappropriate functional designs and presentation modes. However,  such information is not essential to solving the task. As such, the high extraneous load can mak e it difficult for students to locate critical evid ence and solve discrepant information, exacerbating feel ings of confusion.  Moreover, the study found that germane load positively predicted curiosity and enjoyment. A hig h germane load suggested that students put a lot of  cognitive effort into incorporating the incoming information into their prior knowledge through schema construct ion and automation. Through these cognitive processes, students deepened their understanding and perceive the discrepant information as complex but comprehensible, elicitin g the experience of curiosity. Additionally, when s tudents conducted sufficient germane processes, they could resolve the discrepant information and experience enjoyment. Furthermore, the findings revealed a negative impact of surprise on students’ diagnostic accuracy. In this study, students with intense surprise felt the discrepant information was implausible and might ignore the critical but incomprehensible evidence, accounting for a wro ng diagnosis. Moreover, surprise negatively mediated the relationship between intrinsic load and diagnostic accuracy. Beyond our expectations, however, confusion had a positive effect on diagnostic accuracy but a negati ve effect on efficiency. Students who experienced i ntense confusion tended to collect all possible evidence by conducting more  lab tests; thus, they achieved an accurate diagnosis with the collected information. However, frequent lab tests would lower students’ diagnostic efficiency (i.e., the count of matched lab tests/the total cou nt of lab tests). As well, we found a negative mediation effect of confusion on the relationship between extraneous load and diagnostic efficiency. The positive effects of curiosity on diagnostic efficiency were in line with the stud y conducted by Muis  et al. (2015), who found that curiosity indirectly enhanced learning outcomes via elaboration and metacognitive self-regulation strategies. Additionally, intense enjoyment corresponded to a more accurate diagnosis  since it was associated with resolving discrepant information. Regarding the mediation effects, curiosity positively mediated the relationship between german e load and diagnostic efficiency, while enjoyment pos itively mediated the relationship between germane l oad and diagnostic accuracy.

In conclusion, these findings provided insights into the interactions  of cognitive load and epistemic emotions, as well as their joint predictive functions in diagnostic performance. Future investigations could expand the scope of the clinical reasoning task to different contexts to provide more generalized conclusions.
