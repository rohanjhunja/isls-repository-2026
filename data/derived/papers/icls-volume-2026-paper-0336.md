# Social Capital by Design: Community-University Partnerships and Pathways into Technology Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This practice-oriented paper describes the design of a partnership between StreetCode Academy and the Stanford Institute for Diversity in the Arts, created to connect community learners in a technology education program with university students and resources through a co llaborative learning model. In this endeavor, social capital was emphasized as a lever for engagement to create opportunities for relationship-building across community and institutional networks. These relationships functioned as pathways for reciprocal learning and network expansion, which supported students’ and instructors’ engagement with technical skills and their development as innovators. Preliminary findings indicate increased student interest in continued participation in technology education. Instructors and mentors also reported an increase in confidence, skill development, and sense of purpose. This paper presents design considerations for operationalizing social capital and it shows how community-university partnerships can serve as a two-way bridge for reciprocal learning and network-building to expand pathways into technology education. Objectives and theoretical grounding Despite the growth of opportunities in the innovation economy, participation in computing and technology education continues to be marked by inequity, particularly for students from communities historically excluded from access to institutional networks. While several factors influence engagement and persistence in this area, including inequitable access to digital resources and high-quality STEM learning experiences, another critical factor is how learning environments are designed to recognize and extend existing relationships and forms of support that enable participants to navigate and persist in technology pathways. S ocial capital refers to the relational supports people access and share through family, friends, teachers, and communities (Bourdieu, 1980; Coleman, 1988), and as a potential factor contributing to ongoing marginalization, should be examined more thoughtfully. In STEM research, social capital is often used as an explainer of disparities in tech participation. In this paper, however, social capital is discussed as an existing asset that can be leveraged and designed to activate new, mutually beneficial relationships across institutional networks that expand access and support participation in technology pathways. This paper presents an ongoing partnership between StreetCode Academy, a community-based technology education nonprofit, and Stanford University, a world-renowned institution of higher learning known for its culture of innovation. Founded in 2014, StreetCode Academy is committed to bridging the technology education gap by offering free coding, entrepreneurship, and design classes to youth and their families in communities with longstanding histories of creativity and technological ingenuity, where access to technology learning opportunities is not always equitably available. The Stanford Institute for Diversity in the Arts is a “student-facing and serving organization" committed to offering opportunities to practice leadership, creative expression, and social responsibility through the arts. This partnership extended StreetCode ’s community-based learning model for communities historically underrepresented in formal technology institutions onto Stanford’s campus, a university with a strong institutional emphasis on technology and innovation, intentionally repositioning who participates in and belongs within these spaces. This created opportunities for students and their families to build new connections and engage with university spaces that are not often available or accessible to the public. For many participants, this included taking classes on a university campus for the first time, which positively influenced their sense of belonging and possibility within technology and higher education pathways. It also created meaningful opportunities for 2171 university students pursuing fields adjacent to technology, education, design, and the arts to contribute as mentors and instructors while developing their own perspectives and skills. Through this initiative, they were able to apply their disciplinary knowledge in authentic, culturally grounded contexts, which helped them experience affirmation and support that reinforced their sense of belonging in fields where they may have otherwise experienced marginalization (McGee, 2020). Positioning the role of social capital in tech education is significant because it can serve as an existing and malleable design resource within educational systems. Students bring with them unique social and cultural resources that serve as valuable assets in supporting their learning trajectories (Yosso, 2005). V aluing and leveraging students' existing networks and intentionally curating expanded connections, educators can engage relational dimensions that can be critical to fostering greater participation in technology-related learning pathways and academic settings, particularly among historically underrepresented groups. These relations can positively impact learners’ feelings of inclusion and their self-esteem as they navigate technology-related fields, which positively influence learning and identity development. In this context, social capital was treated as a catalyst as well as an outcome, cultivated to foster social and academic growth among the students and educators participating in the program. In this process, relationships were mutually constituted through ongoing interaction, as participants both drew on and contributed to shared relational networks. These dynamics created opportunities for co-development, where learners and instructors built confidence and capacity through shared engagement. This has the potential to extend existing research on social networks and opportunity (Chetty et al., 2024), demonstrating how social capital can be intentionally designed within learning environments to encourage reciprocal relationships that help shape participants’ engagement and aspirational trajectories. These theoretical perspectives informed the program’s design, guiding its intention to respect and position all participants as active contributors to the learning culture, and structure community engagement in a way that centered reciprocal identity development, where students, mentors, and instructors developed in distinct but interconnected ways through their engagement in teaching, learning, and collaboration. Design The StreetCode Academy and Stanford University Institute for Diversity in the Arts (IDA) partnership shared three intersecting goals: the first was to increase accessibility to top-tier learning opportunities. StreetCode Academy is committed to providing learning experiences that help participants gain foundational skills in a variety of coding, design, and media platforms. Stanford University is a global research institution of higher education, known for its contributions to the innovation economy. The Institute for Diversity in the Arts, founded 25 years ago, was established to educate, activate, and transform students at Stanford and extend that mission to the local community. The second goal was to advance the conversation around culture, arts, and technology, exploring how these intersect to inspire creativity, expression, and innovation. The third was to bridge perceived and actual divides across communities by fostering belonging and community across institutional boundaries between the university and the local community. Designing for access, belonging, and shared participation To support these goals, the partnership was designed for both entities to contribute in complementary ways. Regarding accessibility, the Institute for Diversity in the Arts provided financial resources so StreetCode could coordinate free transportation, provide free laptop rentals and usage for students, and even offer free meals for participants, removing many of the logistical barriers that can often prevent engagement. Framing access as a design principle helped to promote more equitable participation and expand participants’ connections to social networks through the structural organization of the learning environment. The program design prioritized a family-centered learning environment. This partnership offered intergenerational learning spaces where youth, elders, children, and parents could learn together. This is atypical for many K12 or university classrooms, which are traditionally designed for a specific age range. One participant, a 76-year-old Spanish-speaking StreetCode student, spoke at the final-quarter Demo Day, sharing that it had been her dream to take a course at Stanford, and that, through this program, it was finally achieved. This example illustrates how designing for intergenerational participation supported a spirit of lifelong learning and expanded who could belong in the space while also creating opportunities for individuals across generations to l earn alongside one another and reimagine themselves as technology innovators. 2172 Building on these design choices, this program aimed to operationalize social capital so that learners and instructors could interact as mutual recipients and co-producers of value. This was an intentional move away from traditional one-directional service-oriented models of technology education, toward a more connected and empowering model of shared learning. Grounded in the principle of “learning through participation,” the project centered an epistemology of development that recognized access to diverse people, experiences, and environments as integral to how participants form affirming mindsets and identities as technologists and innovators. Across the program, three design considerations for operationalizing social capital were prioritized: reducing barriers to participation, centering shared participation and belonging, and expanding opportunities for purposeful network-building to support sustained engagement. Implementation A fourteen-week program was developed, encompassing a variety of courses. Courses were thematically designed around the topic of “culture and tech” and introduced participants to cutting-edge and emerging technology. Each course included opportunities to engage in practical, technical use of technology and in theoretical and philosophical discussions about how each person could use these tools to preserve and positively advance their cultural traditions and personal goals. This approach reaffirmed education as a force of personal liberation and advancement for each student as well as their local and global communities. Courses were offered in two seven-week rotations, with sessions held once a week for 2.5 hours each. Offerings included courses like “Storytelling & Tech,” “Music & Tech,” “Design & Tech,” “Jr. Design & Tech,” “Intro to Code,” and “STEMLabs Mechanical Engi neering.” Classes were hosted on Stanford’s campus at the IDA “house” and the Black Community Services Center, where the community room and lobby became classrooms that “felt like home” for students, filled with art, music, and warmth that reflected the students’ community aesthetics and values. Enacting bonding through shared rituals Classes began with a 30-minute ritual called “Huddle-up,” intended to inspire students for the upcoming courses and connect them with one another over food, engaging activities, and presentations by featured students or special guests. Huddle-ups began wit h a shared dinner, during which announcements highlighted past student accomplishments and upcoming events. Activities were designed to have students interact with one another so they could learn about each other. Discussing a topic around a table or acting out a scenario would bring people together. Huddle-ups also featured guest speakers or showcased the work and talents of community members, which often led students to follow up with each other or with a guest speaker about an opportunity they might not otherwise have known about. Following Huddle-Up, students would break into their courses, which were facilitated by several lead instructors and taught by StreetCode alumni, industry experts, and Stanford students. Each class had one or two lead instructors and was supported by three to four mentors. While 66% of classes were taught by Stanford students, every course had a lead teacher or mentor who represented the geographical or cultural background of the StreetCode students. StreetCode community members, industry representatives, a nd the Stanford affiliates were all treated as co-designers who collaborated on curriculum design, logistics, and pedagogy throughout the quarters. Enacting network expansion across community, university, and industry To deepen the connection to industry, each quarter had a day committed to exploring the field. Experiences included a trip to the Stanford innovation program, hosted by the director of publications and co-director of the K-12 Lab, and a visit to OpenAI, where a workshop was led by a former StreetCode instructor and Stanford alumnus and colleague. These trips were designed to strengthen the relationship between these organizations and the StreetCode community, empowering the social capital participants could access through more formal network development. Quarterly courses ended with a Demo Day, a community-wide public celebration that invited members of the broader community to experience a showcase of what students had learned and accomplished. Nearly 20 employees from Amazon and 15 members of Links, a professional organization of African American women, attended one of the Demo Days, along with numerous Stanford students, StreetCode community members, family, and friends. Students presented their work and exchanged information with visiting guests. One student who 2173 created a movie using an AI platform even raised $250 from attendees who wanted to support his continued subscription to the platform beyond the course. Through outside speaking engagements, field trips, and Demo Days, these relationships were nurtured beyond what was gained in the classroom, with enrolled participants expanding their personal and professional networks of opportunities and support. The implementation of each activity and the program overall was thoughtfully curated to expand the realization and potential of social capital across residential, academic, and industry communities, connecting StreetCode students with professors and students at S tanford University; also connecting Stanford University and industry partners and affiliates with genuine opportunities to engage with local community members around relevant and resonant partnerships. In doing so, the program strengthened individual trajectories and cultivated an ecosystem of shared learning and mutual investment. Practice-based learnings There are many context-specific factors that may have affected the efficacy of these efforts. However, early findings are encouraging. Participant feedback collected through post-class surveys indicated that 94% of students expressed a desire to continue learning about technology; and 70% reported an increase in their sense of identity as innovators. These data suggest positive implications for this approach, providing a generative introduction to technology for community participants. In addition, instructor feedback indicated that assuming the role of mentor or teacher supported the development of confidence and skills as they, too, navigated exploratory conversations and reflected on their growth as emerging professionals in computing and education. They also gained valuable first-hand experience designing learning environments aligned with principles they apply in their STEM courses (e.g., design thinking, Universal Design for Learning, Understanding by Design), reinforcing their technical skill development. Teachers also discussed how this experience acted as a space for personal mindset renewal. It provided opportunities to reflect on their relationships with technology and to reconnect with their motivating hopes and aims, empowering their journeys as developing professionals and technologists. In this way, teaching became a creative means of sustaining motivation and recharging their sense of purpose. Meaning-making is recognized as a dimension of well-being (Seligman, 2011). Connecting their academic work to an authentic teaching experience, student-teachers were able to deepen the connection between their learning and real-world practice. This contributed to their positive identity development and overall personal wellness. Outcomes experienced by all participants, students and instructors, point to a reinforcing developmental experience which was supported by shared participation, strengthened relationships, and expanded network connections. Instructors’ growing confidence in their skills and renewed sense of purpose were nurtured by their experiences teaching and supporting students, who affirmed their developing expert ise. Similarly, students’ increased confidence and emerging innovator identities developed through their engagement with near-peer instructors, whose mentorship and presence as accessible role models helped shape their identities as technologists. Relevance Preliminary learnings from this project implicate the need for more research to better understand the degree to which social capital, and in what forms, influences levels of student engagement and sustained participation in computing pathways. Future research might also inquire how a model like this could help to address gaps between student needs and available school offerings, particularly in expanding opportunities to develop technical skills and strengthen psychological supports. Implications for future practice-based initiatives include identifying community assets and opportunities to triangulate practice, bringing together resources that connect community organizations and academic institutions, with opportunities to connect to industry to spur individual and communal development through designed pathways across locally situated learning ecologies. This partnership’s preliminary outcomes suggest the potential for more strategic programming to expand equitable participation, positioning social capital as a designable mechanism for reciprocal learning and network-building across community and university contexts.
