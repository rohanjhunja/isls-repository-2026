# Sustaining a Learning Community Approach to Foster Digital Age Competencies: A Study of Process, Outcomes, and Infrastructures

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The digital age has drastically changed many aspects of human life, with significant implications on education. Transforming school structures so they can foster digital age competencies sustainably is a grand challenge for educational systems and research. Learning community pedagogies provide a potent way to approach this challenge. We present a large, multi-year study that is supported by the national department of education that attends to the learning processes, outcomes, and infrastructures necessary to do this.

## Introduction

Transforming school structures so they can foster the digital and information literacies, as well as inter-and intrapersonal skills necessary in today’s society (OECD,  2018) – what we refer to here as digital age competencies – requires rethinking curriculum, pedagogy, technolog y, assessment, and school policy (Collins, 2017). B ringing stakeholders together in research-practice partnerships (RPPs) has been a central method in the learning sciences to reach goals like this (Fishman et al., 2013). We  present a large study in collaboration with the na tional department of education that investigates the proce sses, outcomes, and infrastructuring of an innovative learning community approach that aims to support schools in fostering digital age competencies.

## Background

Digital age competencies are a global concern, refl ected in large organizations such as the OECD (2018). They often include intellectual, social, emotional, and physical domains such as having students develop self and social awareness and taking care of one’s health and well-being. They also extend to relatively new domains s uch as digital and information literacies, including teach ing kids how to share information responsibly or ma intain prosocial interactions and norms online. While liberal educational movements and scholars have long been calling for schools to move beyond knowledge acquisition and teach skills relevant for that particular day and  age (Dewey, 1916), digital age competencies appear to h ave entered through the “back door” and have now be come broadly well-accepted as contemporary educational goals (Blikstein, 2018). Translating digital age goals into school curricula, pedagogical and assessment practices, and school cultures remains a key challenge (Robert & Reinitz, 2023). Standardized evaluations, compartmentalized disciplines, and hierarchical educational systems a re some of the complex constraints that schools fac e to make digital age competencies central to their mission. The learning sciences is uniquely positioned as an interdisciplinary field to address these challenges, for a number of reasons. First, as a design science, researchers are engaged in solving complex problems of practice  by working in the field instead of laboratories. One of the pioneering innovations that has come from this pers pective is the classroom learning community app roach (Bielaczyc & Collins, 2009), which is well suited t o the challenge because they are designed around au thentic practices such as collaboration, complex problem so lving, and distributed expertise. The interdiscipli nary nature of the curriculum in learn ing communities thus offers a model that disrupts i nstructionist approaches. Another reason that the learning sciences is well suited fo r these challenges has to do with its focus on impl ementation research (Fishman et al., 2013). Recently scholars have encouraged the formation and study of partnerships between researchers and practitioners with an under standing that creating sustainable change requires working with educational infrastructures (Penuel, 2019). Fi nally, scholarship in the learning sciences activel y addresses many digital age competencies, including digital literacies and dealing with misinformation (Fischer et al, 2019). Our research combines these foundations in an effor t to work in partnerships with a middle school to support digital age competencies in a way that is s ustainable. We are guided by principles of the Huma nistic Knowledge Building Communities (HKBCs) approach (Ho d & Ben-Zvi, 2018), which makes  the person-and idea-centered goals that the school aims to achieve expl icit. Taking a principled approach (Zhang et al., 2 011) grounds all participants in the community with a pa rticular philosophy that creates a space for a diverse range of contextually-sensitive local innovations.

To embed these principles into the school culture, we work as an RPP on mutually-supportive participatory structures that exist within and outside the school that include (a) aligning goals and processes with the school leadership team; (b) observing and co-designing with a selection of teachers from multiple disciplines;

(c) supporting the full teaching staff with a professional development program; (d) involving parents and families

into teaching and learning processes; and (e) worki ng within a network of department of education proj ects focused on RPPs to share best practices, challenges, and knowledge around similar innovations. We work across these structures with the aim of impacting the schoo l culture so that the multitude of small decisions and actions that are carried out on a daily basis can accumulate and support sustained innovations. Our research questions cut across these structures, focused on learning outcomes, processes, and sustaining innova tive learning: (1) What digital age competencies does participation in HKBCs foster as compared to traditional teaching methods? (2) In what ways do digital age competencies become embedd ed within student s’ learning practices? (3) What infrastructural changes support the implementation and sustenance of these competencies?

## Methods

We take a design-based implementation research approach to investigate structural changes around the innovation (Fishman et al., 2013). First, we will examine multiple digital age competencies using a combination of validated pre-and post-measurements of the  HKBC interventions. Instead of running control and experimental groups simultaneously, a historical-control design (Bonapersona et al., 2021) allows us to examine the effects of teachers’ pedagogical methods in one year versus the next year after the school culture begins to transform. Answering the second question involves in-situ observations and interviews throughout the HKB C instantiations, across a number of subject areas and grades. We have a large team of researchers and assistants to extensively collect data from a large sample of classes, to be analyzed using grounded methods (Charmaz, 2005). Lastly, we will examine the infrastructuring processes by observing leadership meetings, local, community, and network design meetings to create a multi-level, ethnographic account of school change (Rogoff, 1995).

## Discussion and conclusion

One of the grand challenges of educational research is to figure out how to collaborate with the educational system so that scholarship can stay relevant and schools c an implement innovations sustainably. Design-based implementation research has offered new insights about how to do this. Yet, there is a need for more case studies that can help refine theories about how to sustain innovation, as well as research on supporting digit al age competencies through learning community pedagogies.
