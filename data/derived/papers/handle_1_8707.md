# Developing Computational Thinking via Knowledge Building

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explored the impact of knowledge building pedagogy within a collaborative learning community on students’ understanding of computational thinking (CT). The participants were 101 middle school students who attended a 6-week course to work on Scratch programming guided by knowledge building principles. An international challenge test on computational thinking called “Bebras Challenge” was employed to evaluate students’ CT. Primary findings indicate that students significantly improved their CT skills in the end of the course.

## Introduction

Computational thinking (CT) involves adopting a set  of problem-solving skills and techniques which sof tware engineers use to design programming code, including  the core competence to break down complex tasks in to simpler components, algorithm design, pattern recognition, generalization and abstraction (Wing, 2006). While integrating CT has already in K-12 school in many countries, it is still at its early stage in Taiwandue to lack of innovative teaching and pedagogy. Under this contex t, a community-based learning site, MIT’s Scratch, has become one of the popular learning environments to develop learners’ CT in K-12 courses in Taiwan recently. Accordingly, a recent call for education reform in Taiwan has begun to transform curriculum guidelines by asking teachers to provide more learning opportunities for  students to learn together collaboratively. Learne rs thus are expected to contribute collaborative ideas and work  creatively in their community. While using Scratch  as a programming learning context can help learners develop CT (Zhang & Nouri, 2019), educators in Taiwan are still on their ways to explore diverse pedagogical approach and to engage students in developing CT competency in a collaborative learning environment.

In this study, we adopted knowledge building pedago gy and engaged students as a knowledge community (Hong & Scardamalia, 2014) to work on Scratch progr amming via collaborative grouping and re-grouping strategies. Through interactive discussion and mutual assistance among group members, students were guided to collaboratively complete the programming problems/tasks assigned, learn problem-solving skills and develop the core competence of computational thinking. By facil itating the whole class as a knowledge community, w e investigated if such learning environment can foster the development in CT.

## Context

Participants were middle school students (n=101) fr om two classes (7 th grade and 8 th grade) in an urban city in Taiwan. They enrolled in a 6-week course called “Scratch Programming” with 45-min per week. One researcher was the primary facilitator to lead the course. Under the guidance of knowledge building principles of “real idea, authentic problem” and “idea diversity” (Scardamalia, 2002), students were asked to work in small group and to work on guiding examples via Google Form. They then solved the assigned programming tasks while guiding by the principle of “real idea, authentic problem: The n they exercised the CT by translating group-genera ted and-shared ideas (as guide by the principle of “idea diversity”) into practice to test out their programming ideas on the Scratch platform. Afterwards, they documented group  results in Google sheet. Specifically, students we re in homogenized grouping of 3 to 4 people in a group based on the pre-test results of Bebras Challenge. Data Bebras Challenge was the instrument to evaluate CT performance. We conducted it at the beginning and the end of the course to collect pre-and post-test for quantitative analysis. Paired-sample t-test was performed to examine whether there was any significant difference betwee n the pre-test and post-test. There were 10 single-choice questions to evaluate students’ the core competences of computational thinking and problem-solving skill, such as the ability to break down complex task into simpler components, algorithm design, pattern recognition, pattern generalization and abstraction. The scoring rubrics  including 3 points for correct answers, with 1 poi nt being deducted for wrong answers, zero points for unanswered questions, and the maximum score was 30 points. ## Result

Table 1 shows the overall results. It was found tha t after engaging in group-based collaborative knowl edge building for 6 weeks, regardless of the grades, all  the participants’ understanding of computational t hinking significantly improved from pre-test to post-test (t=-5.018, p<.01). Table 2 further shows that there is a significant difference between 7th and 8th grade students, with 8th graders significantly outperforming the 7th graders in the pre-test scores (t=-3.830, p<.001). This is due to the fact that 8th graders were more knowledgeable with one more year of schooling ahead. But there is no significant difference in the post-test (t=-1.157, p<.243), indicating that the 7th graders were able to benefit more from knowledge b uilding guidance than the 8 th graders. In brief, after this course, all participants demonstrated a more informed understanding of computational thinking. ## Conclusion

In summary, the findings indicate that students imp rove their understanding the concepts of computatio nal thinking after the study. Prior to the course began, 7th grade students have not learned the method of computational thinking to solve problems while 8 th grade students have already had a year of learning. Therefore, there was a significant difference between 7th and 8th grade students in the beginning. However, after the six weeks course, students have improved their core competences of co mputational thinking. Finally, there is no signific ant difference in the post-test. In conclusion, after engaging in knowledge building pedagogy for six weeks, students demonstrated a significant improvement in the core competences of computational thinking. In future research, we will focus on “how” students changed and further  analyze their collaborative learning process durin g this course.
