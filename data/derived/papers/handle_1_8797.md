# Actor-Network Theory as a New Direction in Research on Educational Dialogues

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The analysis of educational dialogues has been traditionally undertaken according to pre-given coding schemes, which are often teacher-centered, and in which multiple time scales are not considered. In this paper, we consider the use of Actor-Network Theory (ANT) methodology. While ANT has already been used in education, its application for analyzing the deployment of educational dialogues over time is novel. We analyze some educational dialogues in a program dedicated to dialogic pedagogy. We show that ANT enables us to consider the observation of educational dialogues as the formation and dissolution of networks between humans and non-humans, where roles are not pre-determined but translated in interactions, and the educational design of instructions is black-boxed.

The gaps between dialogism and dialogic teaching Dialogism is a vast domain that has inspired scholars from various traditions (Marxism, semiotics, structuralism, religious criticism) and disciplines such as history, philosophy, sociology, anthropology, and psychology. It has also revolutionized the world of education. This re volution is firstly linked to Bakhtin's communicati on legacy. According to Bakhtin, every utterance is related to another utterance, true to turn-taking. The conversational norms are followed for a discourse to have a cohesive flow in which individuals respond to one another (Bakhtin, 1935). Bakhtin’s enterprise critiques the monologization o f the human experience that he perceived in the dom inant linguistic, literary, philosophical, and political theories of his time (Baxter, 2006). While dialogism breaks the monology of humanism, it is still bound by its Humanist threads: it focuses on the human as the instrument of knowledge-making. Dialogists focus on ‘culture’, sociolinguistics, classroom interactions, and communication patterns, which are all human-centered. This is evidenced, for example, by takeup ‘the utterance’ as the basic unit of analysis in dialogical research (Bakhtin, 1935). Davies and Renshaw (2019) stretch the notion of human beyond the unfinalisabi lity of dialogism and its humanist legacies; they a dopt a posthumanist political approach that 'dislocates the foundations of the humanist worldview’ (Braidotti, 2006, p. 203). For them, human is neither a biological nor s ociological category, but an interface, a field of intersecting material and symbolic forces; a surface where multiple codes (sex, class, age, race, etc.) are inscribed’. Dialogic education is a pedagogical movement based on dialogism. Its implementation is constrained by the educational structures, practices, and norms in  place in schools, though. Consequently, the major ideas of dialogism – the interrelation of voices and their u niqueness and the polyglossy of communication – do not fit classroom talk ruled by traditional pedagogies. The  fact that voices should be aligned to what is reco gnized as correct, the centrality of the teacher as the autho rity of official knowledge, the attainment of clear  learning outcomes, are few phenomena that make it difficult to implement dialogic education. The term Educationa l Dialogues points at a change, which envisions classroom talk as more fit to dialogism. The recent volume Research Methods for Educational Dialogues (Kershner et al., 2020), which is aimed at presenting various methodologies for observing the quality of educational dialogues, stresses in fact the passage from old tonew practices. In the ICLS2022 Proceedings 258 © ISLS volume, the Scheme for Educational Dialogue Analysis or SEDA elaborated by Sara Hennessy and her colleagues is presented. It transcribes the dialogue, divides and classifies it according to pre-given code schemes (Hennessy et al. 2016). SEDA served as a tool for showing that Dialogic Teaching improves learning outcomes (Howe et al.,

2019) which provides an important answer to the det ractors of Dialogic Education. However, SEDA does n ot

enable us to look at the development of dialogues over time. Also, it is anthropocentric and does not relate to nonhuman actors and how they shape classroom dialogue. Actor Network-Theory Actor-Network-Theory (ANT) seeks to give an account  of how the social world endlessly disassembles and reassembles itself through the complex interactions of its actors, which may be animate or inanimate. ANT spurns traditional social 'forces' as determinate of actio ns, seeking to account for social structure by anal yzing the processes and connections through which networks are assembled. Latour and Woolgar's (1979) ethnography of a biomedical laboratory draws on citational data, and research reports from the lab supplemented with fieldwork and interviews. They turned the lab into a place wh ere facts are 'secreted' through processes that inv olve researchers as actors assemble and interpret data.

A key assumption in ANT is symmetry (Latour &Woolgar, 1979). ANT researchers oppose approaches that concentrate only on humans, while objects, machines, and materials remain often overlooked. According to the ANT approach, all things, human and non-human, can assemble, exert force, change, and be changed. According to ANT researchers, nothing is given or a priori  defined. Entities, human and non-human, are transformed through to process of becoming part of a collective network (Fenwick & Edwards 2010). The title of an actor does not convey a pre-given role; it is tr anslated by his/her/its actions, connections, and t he change he makes in a specific network. Agency, power, identity, and knowledge are relational effects of a certain network and not pre-given attributes. For example, McGregor  (2004) shows how a science teacher becomes a knowi ng location through the interaction of materials, routines, texts, and people. She describes how laboratory artifacts, instructional practices, and teacher traits are assembled into a network and create knowledge and agency. Certain assemblages stabilize and move about from one network to another network and extend its original network's power. When these assemblages move tonew networks, they can be taken for granted. Fenwick and Edwards (2010) bring an example of the Black Boxing in education and describe how curricula become Black Boxes. A curriculum is an effect of a heterogeneous  network of people and things, and historical and p olitical networks, which often solidify and become durable. Then it spreads and shapes the learning in classes.  The teachers (and the students) might take the curricul um for granted, leaving behind the ongoing discussi on and interaction that led to its creation.

In this paper, we exemplify the use of Actor-Network Theory (ANT), a methodology that has not been used to analyze educational dialogues. Educational dialogues are viewed through networks of teachers, students, and actants that exert power on each other. The rol es in the networks are not pre-determined but trans lated in interactions (e.g., the teacher may become a discus sant whose status is comparable to that of the stud ents). Educational activities designed to promote dialogue  are often black-boxed, and the original process and circumstances of their creation might be ignored. Still, this black-boxing is possibly reversed in further educational ICLS2022 Proceedings 259 © ISLS dialogues. We show then that the adoption of ANT for analyzing educational dialogues uncovers the shifts from less dialogic to more dialogic and explains the mec hanisms that lead to these shifts. Shifts from one network to other convey the change of power relations between the different actors and the determining role of non-human actors (instructions, pictures, etc.). ANT emphasizes how these actants shape the dynamics of networks in which educational dialogues lay. We draw from ANT’s analysis implications both for research and educational practice in dialogic education.

## The educational context

In September 2019, we initiated a multidisciplinary, dialogic instruction program in four schools in a  mid-size town in Israel. A school professional development (PD) program was implemented in which teachers that taught in Grade 7 in mathematics, humanities, and science. The school principals encouraged the teachers to participate in the program in order to enact dialogic practices  in their classes. We negotiated the program with a ll the stakeholders involved. The teachers and superintendents responsible for schools pointed at the need for a program that would raise strong interest and engagement. We preconized the enactment of a dialogic pedagogy for enabling interdisciplinary processes among students. We and the stakeholders decided to instigate an out-of-school series of interdisciplinary activities to foster dialogic argumentation. 130 Grade 7 students participated in  a series of activities organized in "climax days". Each climax day focused on a different societal issue, and incl uded disciplinary sessions (in mathematics, science and philosophy) during which students learned about ideas relevant to the societal issue, preceded an interdisciplinary session in which a joint product (e.g. a presenta tion, a clip, a written story, a petition) was produced. Deliberations around texts that invited inquiry and dialogic argumentation were conducted in all disciplines and all sessions.

## Discussion

The use of ANT is not new in educational research. ANT has been used for investigating curricula, policy analysis, and the relations between technology and education (Hamilton, 2012; Wright & Parchoma, 2011). Yet ANT has not been used for contexts of learning in s ocial interaction. The present paper focuses on educational dialogues and capitalizes on ANT to consider dialog ues as networks of human and non-human actors who constantly assemble, dissolve and reassemble, and exert force and agency on each other. Although the analysis of several protocols is not s ufficient to validate the usefulness of the ANT methodology in research on educational dialogues, i t points at several phenomena that we recurred in f urther analyses. First, the maintenance of educational dia logues is fragile: They may assemble due to instruc tional designs and paradoxically dissolve because of these  very designs. We showed that the documents, instru ctions, etc., that these designs generate might play as act ors whose agency interrupts the deployment of educa tional dialogues. More generally, the ANT methodology stre sses the central role of inanimate things – Bristol, instructions, texts, and various tools in inhibitin g or catalyzing the deployment of dialogues. The AN T ICLS2022 Proceedings 264 © ISLS methodology suggests that designers should plan met iculously settings to not impair the flow of educat ional dialogues. Moreover, we saw that ANT helps identifying black-boxed networks, that are solidify and exert power in further activities as the original process and circumstances of their creation might be ignored. The use of the ANT methodology enabled us to exemplify how actors do not have fixed pre-determined roles. Instead, this role emerges in specific netwo rks that assemble or dissolve: The role of the teac her or the students varied in the protocols we presented from a peremptory figure to a facilitator of dialogues; students alternatively complied with instructions or opposed  the injections of the teacher. We claim that the c ontext of educational dialogues, which is a context of educational change, is characterized by frequent passages, back and forth, between traditional and novel roles. The insights we reached should be backed up in further res earch, but the examples we presented point at an important role that ANT should play: when the term “dialogue” is overused, this methodology enables to distinguish between dialogic and the non-dialogic in the classroom discourse.
