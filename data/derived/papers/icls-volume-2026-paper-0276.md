# From Empirical Challenges to Methodological Innovation: Intersecting Traditions of Video- based Research for Dual Math Identity

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Mathematics identity research has primarily been studied from a sociocultural perspective, leading to the psychological and introspective aspects being overlooked. Notably, the self-identity of mathematics education researchers has rarely been examined. This paper addresses the empirical challenges of exploring the author’s dual mathematics identity as both a college mathematics instructor and a learner in graduate mathematics classrooms. To do so, it proposes the use of videos in self-study to create a syn ergy among the diverse traditions of video-based research (discursive psychology, interaction analysis, video-cued ethnography). This methodological approach: 1) provides a platform to bridge the acquisition-action divide in identity research, 2) mitigates power dynamics between researchers and participants, and 3) addresses the lack of mathematical content in mathematics identity research. Purpose Although identity research in mathematics education has gained traction over the past two decades (Black et al., 2010; Darragh, 2016), as Langer-Osuna and Esmonde (2017) argue, “the development of mathematics-linked individual and membership identities both need to be further theorized and studied” (p. 66). In a literature review on identity research in mathematics education, among 188 articles published in 85 journals from 1997 to 2014, in fewer than 2%, the authors examined their own mathematics identity (Darragh, 2016). To address this gap through empirical methods, I collected video data for the self-study of my dual mathematics identity—as a learner in graduate mathematics classrooms and as an instructor of college algebra at a U.S. -based university. Engaging with video data revealed the limitations of the disparate conventions in transcription and analysis in video-based educational research relevant to the learning sciences. These include the transcription and analysis of conversation analysis (CA, Stivers, 2015), discursive psychology (DP, Wiggins, 2016), and interaction analysis (IA, Jordan & Henderson, 1995), as well as the data collection process of video-cued ethnography (VCE, Adair & Kurban, 2019). Building on previous scholarship on intersecting traditions of video-based educational research (DeLiema et al., 2023), this study extends this methodology to center learner’s identity. It also suggests a model for the development of individualized support of one’s mathematics identity, which could inform mathematics teaching and learning. ## Limitations of traditions of video-based educational research

Adhering to the conventions of CA coding schemes, CA researchers are exclusively attentive to transcriptions of video records and observing conversational devices and patterns (Childs & Hepburn 2015; DeLiema et al; 2023). DP and IA researchers explore how speakers make their beliefs, motivations, emotions, and opinions visible through conversational interactions in public and how these are then challenged in social interactions (Jordan & Henderson 1995; Wiggins 2016). However, what can be acquired from “participants’ internal knowledge states is limited, relying only on what people voluntarily discussed and made visible collaboratively in public, such as “reference to evidence on the tape” (Jordan & Henderson 1995, p. 46). 1867 Recognizing that participants’ internal landscapes cannot be substantially understood through (raw) video data alone, IA and all VCE researchers have involved participants (e.g., teachers, students) to observe the video data and engage with the process of the data analysis (Hall, 2000; Vossoughi & Escudé, 2016; DeLiema et al., 2023). Although participants have a space to reflect on the data and their reflections may then become the raw data through the ethnographic approach (Adair & Kurban, 2019), participants are nevertheless separated from the researchers. As researchers still only rely on what participants are willing to express publicly, it does not resolve the tension as DP researchers state that what people say may be different from what they think (Wiggins, 2016). The approach of self-study mitigates this tendency to self-censor, as the researcher is simultaneously the participant and reflects on both teacher and student identity roles. Modes of inquiry Self-study I vividly remember my anxiety as I tried to juggle my roles as both a learner in the graduate mathematics classroom and an instructor of college algebra at the university during the first semester of the Ph.D. program, despite my received and was expected to give in mathematics education, the experience of teaching probability to my college algebra students while learning the graduate version of the subject myself intrigued me to explore my dual mathematics identity.

I adopt self-study as the methodology since self-study is self-initiated and self-focused in that “researchers are both actors and judges” (LaBoskey, 2004, p.820), where I am the participant, researcher, narrator, and interpreter. Although the term self-study might suggest a focus solely on the individual, it is widely recognized that effective self-studies should incorporate interactive elements, in this case the dialogues between my two selves.

Data sources The six self-video recordings from September to December 2024 comprise the majority of my data. I intentionally chose to record these specific reflections on video, describing emotional moments (e.g., feelings of frustration or sadness) as a learner, or salient reflexive moments as a teacher. This approach incorporates self-validation processes and aligns with scholarship on the context of justification, because I myself determined and justified the empirical adequacy of relying on these videos (De Ruiter & Albert, 2017). However, a potential downside of this approach is increased subjectivity.

## Substantiated conclusions

The use of videos in the self-study of dual mathematics identity forges a synergy among the incommensurate traditions of video-based research (DP, IA, VCE). The dialogues between my two selves are constructed with words and phrases (DP), and the transcript  documents all details of these conversations (CA and DP). However, instead of adhering to the transcription conventions of CA and investigating conversational devices, the goal here is to understand the interactions between participants (DP and IA). One innovation is that these interactions occur between my two selves in a private space, allowing me to document my authentic psychological states. This approach transcends the limitations of DP and IA, where only what is publicly stated can be accessed, and “participants’ inner worlds are not fully knowable” (DeLiema et al., 2023, p.21). In addition, the psychological activities are authentic because I am vulnerable to making them visible, addressing the tension in DP that what people say may differ from what they truly think (Wiggins, 2016). By modifying VCE methods to facilitate the interactions between my two selves, I was able to dissolve the separation between researcher and participant as I embodied both roles. This allows me to record my momentto-moment experiences —such as my frustrations as a math learner today—and later reflect on them from a teacher’s perspective as well as document my psychological states in brackets. Applying the synergy of video-based research traditions, this self-study examines my dual mathematics identity as “a true self residing somewhere inside, in some privileged space” (Gubrium & Holstein, 2001, p. 1). It emphasizes the nuances of my psychological aspects as an individual (Darragh, 2016; Graven & HeydMetzuyanim, 2019). The study examined two research questions: 1) How do I negotiate my dual math identity in academia? 2) In what ways do my two math identities relate? These research questions wou ld be challenging to explore using other research methods.

## Scholarly significance

This research builds upon intersecting video-based research traditions within the context of an individual’s dual mathematics identity. By integrating raw data with authentic psychological activities (data integration), a person’s interactive self-dialogues reflect both their experiences and their thoughts, therefore alleviating the tension present in DP analyses (Wiggins, 2016). Furthermore, data integration, combined with the embodied approach of VCE— where the researcher is also a participant —helps to eas e the tension arising from the fact that participants’ inner landscapes cannot be fully understood through videos alone (DeLiema et al., 2023). Hence, the method not only addresses the gap of neglecting individual and psychological aspects of identity rese arch, but also offers a venue to connect the acquisition-action divide (Martin, 2012). For example, the psychological states in the bracket from the teacher’s perspective (acquisition) inherently informs the author’s future teacher practices (action). Furthermore, incorporating video data into the (dual mathematics) identity research pushes boundaries of the overload “identity-as-narratives” (Graven & Heyd-Matzuyanim, 2019, p.364). Rather, the fluid, multimodal, and insight information provided by this method addresses the “ontological collapse” resulting from 1869 the limited “IntervieweeIntervieweeResearcher” structure of collective storytelling (Graven & Heyd-Matzuyanim, 2019, p. 370).

In addition, this method addresses the absence of mathematical content in mathematics identity research. As Graven and Heyd-Matzuyanim (2019) state, only 9 papers out of the 47 articles on math identity research published in major mathematics education journals over the past five years (when they conducted their study) presented mathematical content. Using videos to explore my mathematics identity enhances the prominence of mathematical content, as they capture memories of how I felt about my own developing identities in the moments of learning and teaching specific mathematics concepts.
