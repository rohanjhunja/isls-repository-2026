# Undergraduates’ Prototyping Process in a Design Class about Engineering Education Games and Community-Based Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
STEM learning games can engage students across subjects at higher levels than lectures and provide avenues for students to “try on” STEM careers. However, many STEM learning games are not positioned around specific STEM concepts within a real-world community, a drawback for teaching highly contextualized topics in STEM. In this study, we investigate learning experiences of undergraduates who co-created game prototypes focused on a local engineering challenge in partnership with young students in local culturally and linguistically diverse communities. Preliminary findings indicate undergraduates understood and were critical towards the iterative co-design process while refining their games in collaboration with younger community members. This research suggests involving undergraduates in designing engineering learning games with and for local communities fosters an understanding of engineering practices and establishes a connection between undergraduate students and the communities they may eventually serve, offering a valuable educational approach for first-year undergraduates majoring in engineering.

## Introduction

STEM games have been used in a wide variety of contexts, from teaching computational thinking skills (Poole et al., 2022), to crowdsourcing RNA folding structures  to advance medical treatments (Andreasson et al., 2 022). While STEM learning games cover numerous topics, ma ny do not consider student’s local community in their setting or narrative and even fewer seem to include K-16 students as co-developers. Valuing where students come from and the funds of knowledge they bring to the classroom may help make content more accessible and engaging to them (Gonzalez et al., 1995; Ladson-Billings, 2014), especially for students who have b een historically marginalized in STEM disciplines  (Smith & Lucena, 2016). In exploring how to invite students as equal intellectual partners in engineering processes, we draw on high-leverage practices (Calabrese Barton et al., 2020) and situated learning (Brown et al., 1989) to investigate how undergraduate design teams and elementary students co-design learning games about engineering  concepts inspired by the realities of  local communit ies in Massachusetts. In this work, elementary students are positioned by the instructors of the course as the community experts in an effort to shift funds of expertise and authority to younger community members as previous design work has investigated (Tan et al., 2019). For this introductory engineering course, we investigate how  prototype games about engineering developed by undergraduate design  teams  evolved over time  through the lenses of engineering design education, community involvement, and principles of educational game design. Community-based engineering education games Even outside of educational game designs, the histo ry of games and game developers has broadly been st ories about, designed for, and created by white cis gender men. Though indie studios are turning the tide on t he kinds of stories being told, there remains a lack of learning games that are focused on minoritized communities. STEM games can be useful for students to “try on” STEM c areers (Beier et al., 2012), yet there seems to be a lack of learning games that allow students to do this in the context of their own community or in connection to the realities of people like themselves. To continue to push for the creation of STEM games that are focused on specific communities, we pull from the concept of “resonant games” or learning games that focus on a large conn ected system of meaningful learning, not just in schools but learning through a student’s homelife and interests (Klopfer et al., 2018). Providing a low-risk, playful way for students to engage with commu nity-related engineering concepts may offer ways for students to feel that t heir funds of knowledge can be valued in creating S TEM solutions and increasing their engagement with STEM.

## Data collection and methods

This study took place in an introductory engineerin g class on community-based engineering and educational game design in a private suburban New England university that is approximately 42% White, non-Hispanic and 55% female. Participants were 13 first-year undergraduates in three design teams who volunteered and co-designed games with K-12 students at a large public elementary school and in a local Boys and Girls Club (BGC) in communities near the university.  Both the school and BGC have above 80% minoritized population of Latino/e/a/x, Black  or African-American, and/or immigrant background. Though specific demographic data was not collected on the undergraduate students, the class make-up was very similar to that of the overall universi ty. Data collection included audio and video recordings of the game design conversations in class from each of the teams, classroom artifacts (i.e., homework and game prototypes), and  researcher’s field notes. For this paper, we describe the evolution of the design teams between the initial game proposal and their final prototypes. The three teams, names anonymized, were (1) the Parks to Beat the Heat team, (2) the Flood Management team, and (3) the Soccer for Engineers team. Each of the teams focused on different aspects of engineering and types of game designs. The Parks to Beat the Heat team created a collaborative trilingual board game design about reducing the heat island effect and civic engagemen t to design and build parks. The Flood Management t eam designed a Jenga-style game in which players built local prominent community buildings and tested them against a simulated flood. The Soccer for Engineers team created a tabletop soccer game based on solving math problems as a team to practice interpersonal  and collaborative  competencies needed for engineering.  Due to space constraints, we have chosen to spotlight a team’s design process and selected moments from the other two teams. The design of the games focused on three main tenan ts: community-based learning, real world engineering problems, and authentic learning opport unities. Initially, undergraduate students were asked to contextualize their game proposals within a specifi c community (either the elementary school or the BG C community) and a relevant engineering challenge while also considering how their design met a specific learning goal. Before meeting with the K-12 students from the community, the instructors of the  class (both authors) had undergraduate students research about the culture and histories o f these areas of the city and also led discussions about the opportunities and systemic issues present  in the communities with the undergraduates, includ ing conversations with teachers and community members. Because many of the undergraduate students came fro m both social and financial positions of privilege, t hese activities were important to prepare undergraduate students to adapt and adjust to the cultures and communities the elementary school students were coming from to be more effective and empathetic co-designers.

As undergraduate students developed different iterations of their ga me prototypes with K-12 students based on their initial proposal, they were asked to  playtest their design with their community members  multiple times and integrate their feedback into subsequent designs. These playtests are opportunities for stud ents as designers to give prototypes to potential players to research specific questions about their game and elicit data and feedback on their designs (Barrett, 2020). In addition to having the me chanics and aesthetics of the game being focused on a local engineering challenge or opportunity, undergraduate students were also required to consider at least one aspect of Universal Design for Learning (UDL) in their designs (CAST, 2018). Because designers who engage in rapid prototyping and iteration create be tter final prototypes (Neeley et al., 2013), we encouraged students to “fail early and often” in the class by having undergraduate students develop mini-game designs under very limited time constra ints, receive feedback, and revise their designs. A s this work is in progress, we began with analyzing how undergraduate students designs reflected changes over time of their modeling-building process for the community and engineering concepts. We based our analysis on prior work on how children create a board game about climate change through a lens of  model-building and systems thinking (Parekh et al., 2021). We also examined how their game prototypes aligned with best design practices in educational game  design research, which included elements such as personali zation, real-world connections, challenge, and cooperation (Bjørner & Benedikte Søgaard Hansen, 2011; Laine & Lindberg, 2020).

## Findings and discussion

From this data, we see that undergraduates were abl e to take on the challenge of iterative prototyping  with co-designers and also considered political aspects of engineering and game development in their designs a s their connections with community members grew. Student design teams created games that were informed by and with elements of critical game design (Mawasi & Gee, 202 1), especially in how they conceptualized personas design more broadly, going from the individual level to be ing able to see the importance of a community in de sign. An important part of engineering and game design processes, personas allow designers to think of a hypothetical user of the product or service and their needs. The desi gn teams outright rejected the idea of an imaginary  user and several argued about how could they design somethin g without meeting and understanding their players. Additionally, teams in their initial proposals, the first milestone assignment of the class, wrote about the systematic issues that affected engineering in their communities, often taking on stances of engineering as deeply political. As a case example, Parks to Beat the Heat researche d the concerns the BGC community had regarding the heat island effect (materials used in city construction absorb and release more heat) and lack of green space in their neighborhoods. In investigating this urbandesign and engineering opportunity for their initial game proposal, the team also wrote how they wanted their design to  encourage civic engagement, making it one of their  game’s key learning goals, stating, “players [will] understand the civic engagement aspect of incorporating greenspace in urbandevelopment.” In this analysis, the team considered not only the engineering challenges of the lack of green space, but also connected it to potential health co ncerns for community residents and opportunities to  encourage civic actions through their game’s mechanics. For t heir initial game design, inspired by games like Forbidden Island, players work collaboratively in community roles (ma yor, activist, student, engineer) to collect resources to assemble six park puzzle pieces and come together to build a new park in the community. As this team moved through playtests and design conversations with students from the community, they learned more about the language and culture of the students and incorporated these elements into the d esign. For instance, this team originally proposed that the ga me include Spanish and English materials and rules,  but they found out from the  youth at the BGC that there is a significant Haitian population in t he community as well, so they revised the cards to include all three languag es (Figure 1). Additionally, this team considered w ith the elementary students how they could encourage young learners to focus on the game while waiting for their turn. The team planned to change the simple player pawns in the game to pawns with fidget toy aspects, so that players’ need for stimulation could be accommodated.

Figure 1 Trilingual Prototype Cards An aspect of design that all teams struggled with was that of failure. Many undergraduate students in these teams took feedback about their games to feel very personal. We encouraged undergraduate students to think of playtests not as negative experiences, but to reframe their f eedback as positive opportunities for growth in the ir designs. Additionally, we talked with undergraduate students about differences between failure in schoo ls and failure in games and how failure in games is not punitive and encourages players to try again (Klopfer et al., 20 18). After these conversations, design teams began to see feed back in a less negative light and several teams inc orporated aspects of intentional failure into their designs. One student from the Soccer for Engine ers Team, Charlie, wrote in a personal reflection, “I have never really thou ght of games as a safe place to fail where you get to try again and learn, but that really is what it is. […] All o f the times where I corrected work [in my STEM clas ses] I was able to get so much more out of what I was doing. I want to apply this in our game with the math questions.” This reflection allowed the team to shift a game mechanic in which teams of players were punished for getting a math question wrong to having the question reshuffled in  the deck so that they could try again later in the  game. A student from the Flood Management Team, Taylor, echoed this thought saying, “The most important par t of this is that after every change [to our game] we came ba ck to [the community] ready for even more information. It's important to fail quickly to rebuild better.” An additional team member, Kendall, commented, “We also hope that when the children in [community] play our game, they are not discouraged when they fail but rather excited to try something new.” The reframing of failure from punis hment to process of learning in students’ thinking helped propel their designs forward. Teams were more willing to give new ideas a try, learn from them, and scrap them, instead of being hesitant and limited in their design ideas.

## Potential implications and conclusions

While researchers and game designers alone cannot b e the main source of information on engineering in a community, the students and their communities can b e positioned as these experts and become co-designers of games that highlight their experiences and p ractices, both cultural and linguistic. Additionall y, games with a community-relevant engineering context may help students more readily see themselves as future engineers who can have direct impacts on their communities. Stude nts who participated in this study were able to take a critical stance on the engineering and game design process in a way that showed they significantly valued the importance of community expertise, from their initial persona building, to first prototypes, to more advanced implementations. From a broader stance, these kinds of activities in first-year engineering classes, in which undergraduates take on a co-designed project with members of a community, may p rovide the opportunity for emerging engineers to connect with and more deeply value the existing knowledge of communities.
