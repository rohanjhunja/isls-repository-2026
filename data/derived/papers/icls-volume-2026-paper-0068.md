# Examining the Efficacy of AI-based Scaffolds for Multilingual Learners’ Science Inquiry

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Traditional STEM instruction for multilingual learners (MLs) often reflects a deficit-oriented approach that focuses on the rote memorization of vocabulary and decontextualized facts, which restricts MLs from meaningful engagement in authentic STEM tasks. Consequently, research has found persistent disparities between MLs and non-MLs’ STEM achievement. This study investigated the efficacy of AI-based scaffolds designed to lower barriers for MLs’ participation in NGSS-aligned science inquiry practices. Using fine-grained log data from an Intelligent Tutoring System (ITS), we analyzed how scaffolds supported MLs’ development of science inquiry com petencies. Results indicated that the automated scaffolds supported MLs’ development in forming a testable hypothesis, testing their articulated hypothesis, and drawing correct inferences about their data, even on a different topic and at a later time when scaffolds were no t available. These findings suggest that AI-based scaffolds can promote equitable access to authentic STEM learning for MLs while supporting their conceptual and language development.

Multilingual learners (MLs) now comprise over 10% of K-12 students in the United States (NCES, 2024). Largescale assessments, such as the National Assessment of Educational Progress [NAEP], continue to highlight substantial gaps in math and science scores between MLs and their non-MLs peers in grades 4, 8, and 12 (NCES, 2024). These disparities have been linked to traditional methods of teaching, which often assume a deficit-oriented view of MLs by policy-makers (Buxton & Lee, 2014; Poza, 2018) and teachers (Buxton & Allexsaht-Snider, 2017; Lee, 2021). In turn, science instruction for MLs prioritizes rote memorization of science vocabulary and academic language (Grapin et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2018), which compresses complex ideas into a few words (Afitska & Heaton, 2019). Consequently, traditional instructional approaches further marginalize MLs by restricting them from meaningful engagement in authentic STEM tasks (Grapin et al., 2023). Specifically, the Next Generation Science Standards (2013) expect students to develop rich disciplinary core ideas, science and engineering practices, and cross-cutting concepts reflecting real-world science tasks. Likewise, the WIDA standards, which view language as “tools that are used to  communicate…and to act upon the world” (WIDA, 2020, p. 20), seek to have MLs learn knowledge for doing science (Lee & Grapin, 2024). Given these performance expectations, it is important that researchers identify ways to support MLs in the classroom (Buxton & Lee, 2014; Lee & Grapin, 2024) to narrow the persistent gaps and to better prepare them for future STEM career opportunities.

Prior studies have explored various supports for MLs, including reducing linguistic complexity (Afitska & Heaton, 2019; Kachchaf et al., 2016), incorporating visual aids and graphic organizers (Suh et al., 2020; Turkan et al., 2019), and providing bilingual dictionaries and glossaries (Abedi et al., 2020; Wolf et al., 2022). While these approaches offered partial benefits, the results have been mixed or inconclusive. Additionally, the extant research typically relied on traditional multiple-choice assessments, which may not be precise enough to capture students’ learning processes. Unlike traditional multiple-choice assessments, virtual simulations contextualize the task or problem by offering perceptual affordances to real apparatuses (Gobert, 2005; Gobert et al., 2015), promote student-driven learning (Gobert et al., 2015), and can be paired with scaffolding to support students’ conceptual understanding (Wang et al., 2022) including for NGSS-type practices (Chen, 2016; Firmayanto et al., 2021).  In this paper, we assume that MLs can learn science by meaningfully engaging in authentic NGSS practices with virtual simulations, as their peers do (Davenport et al., 2018; de Jong et al., 2013; Quellmalz et al., 2020). Another key advantage of virtual simulations is that they are instrumented to generate log files of students’ interactions with the simulation, allowing for detailed analyses to better detect students’ NGSS competencies and learning processes (Gobert et al., 2012; Gobert et al., 2013; Gobert et al., 2018; Reilly & Dede, 2019; Teig et al., 2020). Log files enable researchers to observe how students are doing science in real time, rather than relying on the assessment of students’ end-products, such as written responses or multiple-choice selections (Gobert et al., 2013). While there are no studies examining the log files of MLs in science, various studies have 575 analyzed non-ML data to better understand students’ learning processes in the context of science inquiry. For example, regarding students’ data collection processes, a key inquiry skill (Kuhn, 2005), studies found that systematic students ran targeted and controlled experiments (i.e., changing one independent variable at a time) (Gobert et al., 2013; Teig et al., 2020). Conversely, students exhibiting haphazard data collection processes often performed redundant actions (Buckley et al., 2010), collected dat a that was not relevant to their question/hypothesis, or changed too many variables at once (Gobert et al., 2012). This study leverages an intelligent tutoring system (Inq-ITS; Gobert et al., 2013; 2023) to examine the efficacy of automated, AI-based Spanish scaffolds for MLs’ learning of science practices, and the effects of those scaffolds on the transfer of practices to a different topic at a later time. Prior studies using Inq-ITS have evaluated the efficacy of automated scaffolds to support learning of critical NGSS practices (e.g., Planning and Carrying Out Investigations; Analyzing and Interpreting Data, Gobert et al.,  2024a), as well as how scaffolds support the transfer of science practices (Moussavi et al., 2016; Sao Pedro et al., 2014), but no studies to date have examined MLs’ data with Inq-ITS. The present study seeks to empirically test supports for MLs and in doing so contributes to the field in three primary ways: (1) it engages ML students in authentic tasks aligned to the NGSS (2013), which are crucial for grade school achievement and future STEM career opportunities, (2) it empirically addresses the effects of AI-based Spanish scaffolds and supports used in conjunction with simulations, and (3) it provides nuanced data about how these scaffolds support ML students’ learning.

## Participants

Student data was culled from a larger dataset that included both MLs and non-MLs in grades 6 to 8. The data analyzed for this study included eighth-grade MLs (n=14), as this was the largest group of MLs who completed the same set of virtual labs. These students were either in a scaffolded group (n = 9) or a non-scaffolded group (n = 5), based on their English proficiency, such that the average proficiency in each group was the same. Additionally, an independent samples t-test was conducted on students’ English proficiency (p =.278) and pre-test scores (p =.645) to ensure that the groups were not statistically different. Materials All students completed two virtual labs using Inq-ITS, which is designed to support students as they engage in four NGSS-aligned science inquiry practices and their respective sub-practices through virtual lab experiments (Gobert et al., 2024a). The first stage of the lab is Hypothesizing, where students are required to form a question by selecting their independent and dependent variables as well as specify the relationship between the variables. In the Collecting Data stage, students select a nd manipulate variables and conduct trials with a simulation to test their hypothesis. During the Analyzing Data stage, students evaluate their results by constructing claims and selecting trials that provide appropriate evidence for their claim. The final stage is Exp laining Findings, where students write their Claim, Evidence, and Reasoning (CER; McNeill & Krajcik, 2011) statements based on their data and analyses.

Inq-ITS automatically assesses student interactions with widgets and the simulation in real time using previously validated educational data-mined algorithms (Gobert et al., 2012; Gobert et al., 2013; Sao Pedro et al., 2013). Each action, such as adjusting variables or running trials, is recorded to generate log files, which are automatically evaluated to generate sub-practice scores. These sub-practices are scored between 0 and 1, as is typical in ITSs (Anderson et al., 1995). Practice-level scores (e.g., Analyzing Data) are then calculated by aggregating the sub-practice scores. For example, the Analyzing Data stage includes nine sub-practices (e.g., selecting trials where the target independent variable changes), resulting in this stage having a maximum score of nine. Scaffolds in Inq-ITS are provided by a virtual assistant named Rex and are automatically triggered when students are auto-detected via algorithms to be struggling with a particular sub-practice (Gobert et al., 2012, 2013). For example, if a student ran too few trials, the student could potentially receive this automated scaffolding message from Rex: “You can’t tell if the (independent variable) affects the (dependent variable) because you only tried one value for the (independent variable).”  Currently, the automated Rex scaffolds are only available for the first three stages of the labs: Hypothesizing, Collecting Data, and Analyzing Data. Rex scaffolds for the Explaining Findings stage only indicate whether a student’s response is too short, or if their response contains spelling errors.

Students are automatically assigned anonymized IDs to de-identify all data to ensure that student information is stored such that privacy and confidentiality are protected, while enabling educational researchers to safely use their data. Students’ personal identifying information (PII) is stored in a database separate from this data to further protect students’ privacy. Inq-ITS’ algorithms only operate on students’ work within the InqITS activities themselves, and do not include any demographic or PII about any students.

## Procedure

Teachers evaluated their MLs’ English proficiency using a five-point rubric derived from the WIDA Proficiency Level Descriptors. The scaffolded group completed a version of a virtual lab on Genetics (NGSS Standard MS-LS3-2) with a Spanish version of Inq-ITS with scaffolds (see Figure 1). Students in the non-scaffolded group completed the same Genetics activity but did not receive scaffolds. Since students often face challenges with applying previously learned material tonew contexts (Kaminske et al., 2020; Kimball & Holyoak, 2000; Tiruneh et al., 2018), we examined whether the scaffolds helped support the transfer of competencies to a new lab at a later time. To address whether transfer occurred, the same students completed another virtual lab on Natural Selection (NGSS Standard MS-LS4-6) approximately one month later, where neither group received scaffolds. During the data collection, teachers were asked to monitor the room to ensure that students worked independently. In addition to the virtual labs, students completed a pre-test to ensure that the groups were not statistically different in their science proficiency. The pre-test consisted of seven multiple-choice questions that were based on standardized tests such as the ACT and New Jersey Student Learning Assessment for Science (NJSLA). Since the practices have a different number of sub-practices, the practicelevel scores were converted to percentages to streamline interpretations regarding performance results across the practices. One-tailed significance tests were reported for the results, since students receiving Rex scaffolds typically outperform students without scaffolds (Gobert et al., 2013; 2016; 2024a). A mixed-design ANCOVA and one-way MANCOVA were also conducted to examine if differences between the two groups across the various inquiry practices were statistically significant during the transfer activity. In addition to practice scores, student log files were used to determine whether the translated scaffolds and translation document supported the development of science inquiry competencies at a finer-grained level. In order to better understand how the scaffolds may have supported students’ competencies on the practices, Python scripts were used to extract, clean, and sort the raw student log files into an interpretable format that detailed every action a student performed during the labs. These log files were manually annotated to create binary indices based on prior research on student log files in science inquiry (Buckley et al., 2010; Gobert et al., 2012; Teig et al., 2020). For example, if a student only ran controlled trials (i.e., trials that change only one variable at a time) while collecting data, they would receive a “1”. These indices included: percent of hypothesis-aligned trials, percent of controlled-trials, percent of repeat trials, whether their claim aligns with their data, among others. To explore students' learning processes during the transfer lab, group-level percentages were calculated from the log file indices and then compared.  The indices are reported as a percentage of the group successfully demonstrating a given competency.

The following research questions were the focus of this study:

1. What is the effect of scaffolds on ML students’ learning of NGSS-aligned science inquiry practices (i.e.,

asking questions, collecting data, analyzing data)?

2. To what extent do scaffolds for MLs promote the transfer of students’ competencies of science inquiry

practices on a different science topic one month later?

3. What fine-grained data do log files provide about the effects of the scaffolds on the development of

science inquiry sub-competencies for MLs?

578 RQ1: Effect of scaffolds on ML students’ learning on science inquiry practices There was a significant multivariate main effect for scaffolding condition on the performance across the four inquiry practices, F(4, 8) = 17.16, p  =.001, ηp2 =.896, indicating a large effect size. Overall, students in the scaffolded group yielded higher average scores compared to the non-scaffolded group. The univariate analyses (see Table 2) indicated that the scaffolded group significantly outperformed the non-scaffolded group on three of the four practices in the activity: Collecting Data (p =.002, ηp2 =.590), Analyzing Data (p =.002 ηp2 =.620), and Explaining Findings (p = <.001, ηp2 =.831). No significant difference was found for the Hypothesizing stage. The null finding for the Hypothesizing stage is not surprising, as prior research has found that question formation is generally considered less difficult than other inquiry practices (Klahr & Dunbar, 1988; Sadler, 2004). The significant finding favoring the scaffolded group for the Collecting Data stage is important, as accurate data collection underpins all subsequent data analysis and explanation-based practices (Kuhn, 2005). Furthermore, the scaffolds supported students during the Analyzing Data stage such that they greatly outperformed students in the non-scaffolded group. Interestingly, the scaffolded students also showed a stronger performance during the Explaining Findings stage, even though Rex scaffolds for this stage are minimal (i.e., only related to length and spelling errors). This suggests that the scaffolds provided in earlier stages fostered a deeper understanding that students were able to use in generating their CER statements. 579 RQ3: Analyzing the development of students’ inquiry competencies using log files Log file indices were analyzed for the first three stages of the transfer lab to examine how the scaffolds may have affected the development of sub-competencies across the science inquiry practices at a later time. As previously mentioned, log file indices were tabulated and reported as the percentage of students in each group demonstrating competency for each index (see Table 4). In the Hypothesizing stage, scaffolded students were more likely to select the target independent (Hypothesizing: Selected corre ct IV) and dependent (Hypothesizing: Select correct DV) variables. Sixty-six percent of scaffolded students correctly selected the target independent variable (foliage), compared to only 20% of non-scaffolded students. Furthermore, only 20% of non-scaffolded students selected any independent variable (e.g., temperature) as their independent variable (Hypothesizing: Selected any IV as IV). These findings highlight how the scaffolded students were better equipped to form a testable hypothesis that was relevant to the driving question of the lab, which has been long understood as a typical challenge for students in science inquiry (Chinn & Brewer, 1993).

During the Collecting Data stage, students in the scaffolded group continued to outperform the nonscaffolded group on the transfer lab. Students in the scaffolded group were more likely to collect data relevant to their original hypothesis, which is another common challenge for students engaging in science inquiry (Kuhn et al., 1992). However, on average, there were minimal group differences regarding the running of controlled trials (Collecting Data: Ran only controlled trials). While some students in the  scaffolded group appeared to benefit from the scaffolds during the learning lab, other students required continued support from the scaffolds to successfully transfer competencies that would allow them to more efficiently collect data. Given that collecti ng controlled trials is complex and difficult to learn (Kuhn, 2005), these findings are not surprising. The log files for the Analyze Data stage indicated that 55% of the students in the scaffolded group were able to correctly select the target independent variable and dependent variable to formulate their claim, whereas none of the students in the non-scaffolded group were able to do this successfully. Furthermore, 33% of the students in the scaffolded group correctly stated their warrant (e.g., “This supports/refutes my hypothesis”), while none of the students in the non-scaffolded group were able to do this. These findings provide further evidence that the scaffolds helped support MLs’ development of discrete science inquiry competencies. This contrasts with the non-scaffolded group, which could not develop an accurate claim about their data and drew incorrect inferences on their data, which is a common challenge among students (Baur, 2018; Kuhn & Dean, 2005). Collectively, the log data provided fine-grained evidence that the scaffolds supported the development and transfer of key inquiry sub-competencies.

In order to address persistent performance gaps (Estrella et al., 2018; NCES, 2024), this study introduced automated Spanish AI-based scaffolds to MLs with the goal of expanding their access to and participation in authentic science practices. While there have been some studies i nvestigating scaffolds for MLs in STEM (Abedi et al., 2020; Turkan et al., 2019; Wolf et al., 2022) there have been none that scaffold ML students in real time while they conduct authentic inquiry, and none that use log files to better understand MLs’ learning of NGSS practices. Comparing log files between groups allowed for an examination of competencies in development which would not be possible with traditional performance data that relies solely on aggregated data and student end-products. The fine-grained student log files revealed subtle yet important distinctions between 580 MLs who received scaffolds and those who did not. By analyzing student  log files, we were able to see how the scaffolds supported students’ learning of science practices such that they could be applied at a later time and on a different science topic, reflecting distal transfer (Barnett & Ceci, 2002; Penuel et al., 2019). These findings are very promising, especially given that scaffolded students were only provided with the Spanish scaffolds for one virtual lab, and still achieved a moderate amount of transfer across multiple stages of the transfer lab. This invites future research of this kind, given that empirical evidence of transfer is notoriously difficult to obtain (Chen & Klahr, 2008; Kaminske et al., 2020).

The findings from this study closely align to prior work with Inq-ITS (Gobert et al., 2013, Gobert et al., 2024a), which has consistently shown that students who receive scaffolds while conducting inquiry outperform students without scaffolds. Additionally, the study aligns with prior work on transfer with [ITS] (Moussavi et al., 2016; Sao Pedro et al., 2014), showing how students receiving scaffolds are able to apply what they have learned on various competencies to different topics at a later time; and in one study, these effects were still robust 170 days after the learning lab (Gobert et al., 2024b). In addition, the log file analyses provide process-level evidence of meaningful learning gains to precisely identify the sub-competencies and processes that were positively impacted by the scaffolds. This included critical NGSS competencies such as forming testable hypotheses, testing one’s articulated hypothesis when collecting data, and drawing correct inferences about one’s data during data interpretation, all of which have been identified as persistent challenges among students (Chinn & Brewer, 1993; Kuhn et al., 1992; Baur, 2018).

Despite the rigorous demands from the NGSS, this study demonstrates that virtual environments paired with Spanish AI-enabled scaffolds can lower the barrier for MLs to meaningfully engage in authentic science practices. The findings from this study emphasize the importance of providing opportunities for MLs to participate in meaningful STEM activities (i.e., through scaffolds) along with their English-speaking peers, despite their English proficiency. The findings also underscore the importance of viewing language as a product of engaging in science, rather than a prerequisite for participation. Additionally, by leveraging the analyses of student interactions (log files) generated in a virtual lab, we were able to focus on students’ processes of “doing” science, rather than relying on final products (e.g., multiple-choice selection). Future research should continue exploring the effects of AI-based scaffolds and other supports for MLs to expand equitable access to STEM learning. Despite the compelling findings of the study, future work should seek to recruit a larger number of participants to examine the robustness of the scaffolds in order to use alternative statistical methods (e.g., mixed-design ANCOVA, cluster analyses). Additionally, a larger sample size would allow for comparisons to be made within-group regarding English proficiency.
