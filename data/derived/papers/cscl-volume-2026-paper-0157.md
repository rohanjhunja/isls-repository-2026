# Between Modalities: Observing How Learners Move between Verbal and Digital Spaces in Face- to-Face Collaborative Knowledge Building

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how learners move between verbal and digital modalities during face-to-face collaborative knowledge building with the Idea Wall, a spatially manipulable digital canvas. Using qualitative multimodal interaction analysis, we examined a te n minute small group session where middle school students jointly discussed pollution. We found that verbal narration often amplifies digital actions, supporting joint attention and shared understanding. Learners frequently moved between verbal and digital communication, however, misalignments occasionally occurred when verbal and digital exchanges failed to synchronize, creating temporary attention gaps. These findings help us understand both the affordances and tensions of multimodal collaboration, and inform our ongoing design-based research on refining the Idea Wall for smoother cross-modality collaboration.

In face-to-face collaborative knowledge building (KB) environments, digital tools, particularly interactive surfaces such as digital walls and tabletops (Tissembaum & Slotta, 2019), enrich the physical space by providing shared platforms for organizing and visualizing ideas. This integration enables learners to externalize their thinking, sustain joint attention, and manipulate ideas collaboratively in real time (Zeng et al., 2023), supporting more tangible engagement with abstract concepts. In such settings, intelligence becomes distributed across people, artifacts, and representations, enabling collaborative sensemaking that transcends individual cognition (Pea, 1993; Hollan et al., 2000). But when verbal, digital, and embodied modalities coexist in the same space, learners must coordinate attention and actions across them, creating both opportunities and tensions in collaborative knowledge building depending on how they bridge these engagements (Sung & Nathan, 2025). Research shows that while such tools make collective knowledge visible, they can also fragment attention when learners fail to bridge verbal and digital spaces (Walkington et al., 2019; Schneider et al., 2021; Cheung, 2022). To better understand these dynamics, this paper investigates Idea Wall, a spatially manipulable digital canvas used on tablets, and asks: how do learners move between talk and digital action in face-to-face collaboration, and how do they transfer and align ideas across modalities during collaborative knowledge building?

## Participants and Activity

The participants were 12 middle school students from a public STEM magnet school in the U.S. Midwest. Students worked in three groups of three to four. Data sources included system logs, group video, and individual audio and screen recordings. We focus on one group’s first-day session as it’s representational. The activity unfolded over four lunch periods during a regular school week. On the first day, students investigated pollution through a scaffolded activity introducing the Idea Wall and its collaborative knowledge-building affordances. The interface includes a blank zone for spatially manipulate ideas and add new ideas, collective agreement (Yup Zone), and idea integration and advancement (Combine Zone). Before the activity, students individually wrote pollution examples in the digital notebook. In the “Combine Examples of Pollution” task, they were asked to merge similar notes first, and then connected related examples on the Idea Wall to generate higher-level ideas with the Combine Zone function.

This study employs a qualitative, multimodal interaction analysis approach (Norris, 2004). The analysis draws on systematic observation and detailed analytic note-taking. The coding framework used in this study was from the author’s earlier work (Zeng et al, 2023), which distinguishes between verbal(v) and digital(d) modalities across key categories of collaborative knowledge-building actions. Idea Generation (vIG/dIG) involves contributing new thoughts orally or via digital notes; Joint Attention (vJA/dJA) entails verbally or using digital gestures to draw 791 awareness; Making Suggestions  (vMS/dMS) consists of spoken proposals or moving notes across functional zones; Clarification and Negotiation (vCN/dCN)  occurs through verbal justification or repositioning notes to signal revision; Rise Above  (vR/dR) represents high-level synthesis, achieved by articulating integrated understandings or digitally restructuring ideas in the Combine Zone; Narration (N) is when students verbalize something existing (only in verbal).

The 10 minutes focal episode involved 83 verbal turns and 54 digital actions. Throughout the activity, students constantly shifted between verbal and digital modalities, using both speech and digital manipulation tonegotiate their ideas. The following sections analyze the specific ways these two modalities interacted and how students coordinated their actions to maintain a shared understanding. Silent Acceptance and Ambiguity of Digital Negotiation In multimodal knowledge building, digital actions often serve as silent yet definitive communicative turns, where acceptance must be inferred from the movement of artifacts rather than confirmed through speech. A clear instance occurred when P2 initiated a suggestion (vMS), "So microplastic?", while P3 simultaneously moved the note digitally (dMS). This lack of synchronization initially caused a modality clash; as P2 moved the note to the Yup Zone while narrating (N/dMS), P3 lost track of the artifact ("Whe re did it go?", vCN/vJA). However, following P3’s final instruction ("Put the... put in the Combine Zone" vMS), P2 offered no verbal reply but immediately moved the note (dAS). Aligning with P3’s proposal, this silent move represents a rare moment of clarity where the digital action functioned as an implicit acceptance that completed the negotiation. Yet, such clarity was not the norm. Frequently, notes were moved rapidly and silently, such as from the Yup Zone to the Blank Zone, without verbal framing. Bec ause digital manipulation inherently carries epistemic meaning within the Idea Wall, these unspoken moves create interpretive ambiguity, leaving it unclear whether they represent active agreement, passive acceptance, or simply unnoticed changes. This underscores a core challenge: when actions replace words, understanding becomes inferred rather than confirmed. This reliance on inference could potentially mask underlying conceptual misalignments, suggesting that the benefits of digital fluidity should be weighed against the explicit semiotic clarity needed for sustained collective knowledge building. Joint Attention to help transition between modalities Beyond the challenge of understanding what a move means, the digital tool can also split the group’s focus. When multiple modalities coexist, learners need to continuously shift their attention and actions between verbal and digital spaces. The digital med ium, while smooth and responsive, can absorb the learners’ attention. The imbalance becomes more pronounced when group members differ in their preferred modes of communication; some primarily talk, while others interact digitally. In some cases, modality transitions require additional cognitive effort. A question posed verbally may be answered digitally, but the speaker might not notice, leaving a temporary gap in communication. For example, at [14:21], P2 prompted the group, “Let’s think, guys… what could I add that’s same as oil?”(vJA/vMS) While P2 and P3 continued to debate the idea verbally, P1 answered at [15:00] by quietly adding a note for “fossil fuel” without comment (dIG). However, because this move was silent, at [15:04], P3 was still wondering aloud about what to add, completely unaware that the answer was already on the screen. There was no verbal acknowledgement even at the end of the session that P2 and P3 was aware of the new idea P1 generated, only digital agreement. This unsynchronized coordination illustrates how verbal and digital actions can proceed in parallel rather than in full alignment. Digital collaboration, in this sense, carries a silent yet fluid quality, interaction flows smoothly, but shared understanding must be continuously re-established across modalities. Joint Attention (waving the digital notes or narrating actions), most of the time can help transition peers’ focus between modalities. For instance, as P2 narrated, “I’m gonna put food waste,” he simultaneously added a corresponding note, while P3 responded verbally with “Gas” and “Woo gas, woo type stuff.” Speaking and acting digitally worked together, the verbal narration amplified the digital move, making it audible and visible to others. This dual-channel expression not only supported joint attention but also expanded the communicative reach of each action. Knowledge building in this sense emerged through the fusion of modalities, where ideas were co-constructed across what was said, done, and seen. Overall, these findings suggest that moving between talk and digital action in face-to-face KB is a negotiated process of navigating attentional gaps between group members. Instead of focusing solely on frictionless interaction, perhaps designing for “collaborative seams” could help learners transform silent moves into shared social markers through explicit joint attention.
