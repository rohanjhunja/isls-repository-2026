# Community Convergence Among Science Education Practitioners, Structural Biologists, and Biology Teacherspractitioners

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We present the design of a partnership among science education practitioners, molecular biologists, and high school biology teachers. Using Lave and Wenger’s (1991) construct of legitimate peripheral participation, we demonstrate how the partnership created conditions for meaningful engagement in the specialized epistemic and cultural practices of structural molecular biology. The SHAPE MATTERS project facilitated an iterative professional learning program in which 24 teachers and their students engaged in increasingly sophisticated approximations of modeling practices ––from using physical and computational molecular models to presenting molecular stories. The team also developed a continuum of molecular modeling for high school biology. The structural biologists ensured disciplinary authenticity; science educators provided pedagogical knowledge. Our case presents a proof-of-concept for a practical approach that moves beyond generalized enactments of scientific practice by grounding teaching and learning in specific research contexts, supporting teachers and their students to develop experience in nuanced enactments of scientific modeling.

In the everyday work of science, scientists carry out their work within the epistemic cultures of their sub-disciplinary communities (Bauer, 1992; Knorr-Cetina, 1999). However, the science and engineering practices presented in the Framework for K-12 Science Education (NRC, 2012) generalize across many major discipline s, spanning the life sciences, physical sciences, earth sciences,  space sciences, and engineering and technology.  In high school biology, for example, teachers orchestrate their students’ participation in multiple sub-disciplines including biochemistry, molecular and cellular biology, genetics, evolution, and ecology, but have very limited opportunities to develop a nuanced understanding of the practices across the range of sub-disciplines (Kite et al., 2021). This general and discipline-neutral approach creates substantial curricular and pedagogical challenges  for teaching canonical disciplinary content  guided by the  eight science and engineering  practices identified in the Framework (Miller et al., 2018; Elby 2019; Guy-Gaytan et al., 2019), and a s a result, settings of teaching and learning frequently l imit students’ epistemic agency to construct knowledge through epistemic practices, and instead continue to position them as receivers of facts (Miller et al., 2018; Elby, 2019; Guy-Gaytan, 2019).

To address this challenge, we partnered with a group of research scientists in molecular biology to examine enactments of structural modeling within their active research and epistemic cultures. Following methods of design-based research (Design-based Research Collective, 2003), our design and analysis was  guided by the following research question: In what ways can enactments of modeling practice approximate the epistemic cultures of structural molecular biology and simultaneously be meaningful for teaching high school biology?

## Theoretical background

Scientific research communities function as specialized communities of practice where scientists share distinctive epistemic practices learned through extended participation (Bauer, 1992; Ford & Forman, 2006; Knorr-Cetina, 1999). Drawing on Lave & Wenger’s (1991) constructs of s ituated learning  and legitimate peripheral participation, we concep tualize science learning as participation in communities of practice.  Communities of practice are characterized by mutual engagement in joint enterprises using shared reper toires of language, tools, and practices that define membership (Wenger, 1998).  Legitimate peripheral participation describes how newcomers enter communities by engaging in authentic but simple tasks, moving toward fuller participation as they develop competence. Participation must be both legitimate ––recognized as genuine contributions ––and appropriately scaffolded toward more central participation (Lave & Wenger, 1991). However, K-12 students and teachers typically have limited access to these scientific communities. Traditional science education positions teachers and students as outsiders looking in rather than as legitimate peripheral participants. This lack of access prevents teachers and students from understanding the situated nature of scientific knowledge and from developing identities as scientists (Wenger, 1998).

2142 Partnerships among science researchers, science education  practitioners, and teachers can potentially create conditions for legitimate peripheral participation  by providing: (1) access to disciplinary practices, (2) authenticity of context, and (3) community membership. In such a community, s cientists bring expertise in the specialized practices of their subdisciplines, including tacit knowledge about how to frame problems, evaluate evidence, and reason within disciplinary norms (Duschl, 2008). In addition, scientists work on problems of interest to the scientific community, using their everyday tools, methods, and standards. These forms of authenticity cannot be fully replicated through textbook scenarios or simplified demonstrations (Ford & Forman, 2006). The education partners facilitate connections through which the researchers share  their work with broader society as required by federal grants. Finally, scientists represent the community that students are learning to participate in. Science education practitioners complement these affordances with pedagogical knowledge, standards expertise, and developmental insight. Science education practitioners understand how to scaffold complex practices, make implicit thinking visible, and design activities appropriate for K-12 learners (Grossman et al., 2009). In addition, science education practitioners  have deep knowledge of the learning standards and how authentic scientific practices can address required content and competencies. Finally, science teachers and education researchers have developmental expertise or insight into cognitive, social, and emotional development that informs how practices can be adapted without losing their essential character (Grossman et al., 2009). A central tension in applying legitimate peripheral participation to K-12 science education is how to provide peripheral participation that is simultaneously authentic and developmentally appropriate. Grossman and colleagues’ (2009) concept of “approximations of practice” helps to mediate this tension. Approximations are activities that preserve the core epistemic features of expert practice while simplifying aspects that may become unnecessarily distracting to novices. Effective approximations maintain fidelity to the discipline in several ways:

(1) they engage students in authentic problems or phenomena that scientists actually investigate; (2) they preserve

the reasoning processes central to the practice, even if tools or contexts are simplified (Grossman et al., 2009); and (3) they situate individual practices within the broader enterprise of science rather than treating them as isolated skills (Duschl, 2008).

Drawing on design-based research methods (Design-based Research Collective, 2003), we present the design and (Authors 1 and 3)  to engage high school biology teachers and students in scientific modeling practices that are central to structural biology. The science education practitioners facilitate coordination among science researchers at a large research university in the mid-Atlantic region of the United States and K-12 teachers to enhance science teaching and learning in their region.

Description of the partnership The partnership, known by the acronym SHAPE MATTERS, was concerned with engaging educators and science researchers in modeling together, converging multiple communities of practice in the work of science and science teaching. The partnership included molecular biology researchers, science education practitioners, and K-12 teachers. Molecular biology researchers with active research programs in structural biology brought expertise in protein structure-function relationships, crystallography, Cryo-EM, computational modeling, and the epistemic practices of their subdiscipline. Science education practitioners  with expertise in designing practices-based science instruction, secondary biology teaching experience, and pedagogical content knowledge in biology. K-12 teachers served as co-designers of classroom implementations and engaged in their own peripheral participation in structural biology practices.

The partnership was structured to create multiple levels of legitimate peripheral participation through molecular-level investigations concerning diabetes, which we called “molecular stories”. We chose diabetes as the central phenomenon because it is a global health epidemic, making the topic relevant, relatable, and situated around a global health problem that researchers are actively trying to address. Teachers participated in summer professional learning in which they engaged directly with molecular modeling software, learned related experiences positioned teachers as peripheral participants in the scientific community, developing both content knowledge and insight into disciplinary practices. Over the course of the project, we hosted four professional development workshops with 24 teacher participants. The first professional development, offered to three different cohorts, was a two-week workshop focused on investigations of native insulin and various designer insulins. The final workshop invited all program participants back for a workshop on investigations of GLP-1 agonist drugs. The teachers then engaged their students in a molecular modeling project and were invited to bring the students to campus to present and share their molecular model and story at a symposium event with the project 2143 team, graduate students, and other high school teachers and students. SHAPE MATTERS hosted two symposium events reaching over 100 high school students. Subsequently, the partnership team collaboratively designed a continuum of modeling activities for high school biology and chemistry students. These activities were explicitly structured to move students progressively from peripheral observation and use of existing models towards more central participation in authentic modeling decisions and practices. Throughout the design process, mol ecular biologists ensured that activities preserved the epistemic core of disciplinary modeling––the kinds of questions asked, the reasoning processes employed, and the criteria for evaluating models––while science educators ensured appropriate scaffolding and developmental fit for high school level learners. Modeling molecular structures in the SHAPE MATTERS project In structural biology, three-dimensional structure is fundamental to explaining biological function. The central principle that “structure determines function” drives research (Goodsell & Jenkinson, 2018) with the aim to elucidate molecular mechanisms (Darden 1991, 2002; Keller, 2000). Structural biologists routinely develop, manipulate, and reason with structural models to explain mechanisms, predict interactions, interpret data, and design investigations (Ratti, 2020). However, molecular modeling presents unique epistemic challenges that distinguish it from modeling in other scientific domains. Molecular structures exist at scales far beyond human perception, requiring scientists to reason about spatial relationships, dynamic conformational changes, and chemical interactions that cannot be directly observed (Jones et al., 2015). Models in the field range from highly simplified ball-and-stick representations to complex computational simulations (Goodsell & Jenkinson, 2018). Scientists must constantly decide what to represent, which features to emphasize, and how to communicate threedimensional information.  These modeling practices are rarely visible in traditional K-12 biology education (Goodsell & Jenkinson, 2018). While molecular biology instruction includes many visual representations, students are not taught how to interpret visualizations, move between representations, or understand how scientists use them as epistemic tools (Dries et al., 2017; Goodsell & Jenkinson, 2018). Our project was designed to provide teachers and students with legitimate peripheral access to these disciplinary modeling practices through sustained partnership with active structural biology researchers. GLP-1 agonist drugs, such as Ozempic and Mounjaro, have rapidly risen to public awareness as a current issue in society and molecular biology. Figure 1 Example Slides from Teachers’ Molecular Stories of their GLP-1 Agonist Drug Investigation We have presented a case of strategic convergence of science education and science research to facilitate high school teachers’ and students’ authentic access to scientific practices. Through legitimate peripheral participation, partnerships between molecular biologists and science education practitioners create conditions for meaningful engagement with specialized epistemic practices. Our work demonstrates that such partnerships can move beyond the limitations of generalized frameworks  of epistemic practices by grounding learning in the actual practices of working scientists. The science researchers ensured disciplinary authenticity and that the simplified enactments of practice still reflect how they think and work. Educat ors ensured pedagogical accessibility, that activities are appropriately scaffolded, and aligned with the teachers’ learning goals (Grossman et al., 2009). By engaging teachers in molecular modeling practices central to structural biology, from interpreting existing models to constructing and presenting their own molecular stories, the project created opportunities for progressive participation in an authentic scientific community of practice. Teachers developed content knowledge and insight into how molecular biologists use models as epistemic tools. The continuum was constructed from the complementary expertise of both partners. Molecular biologists ensured disciplinary authenticity and s cience educators ensured pedagogical accessibility. This collaborative design process itself models the convergence for which we advocate—neither discipline alone could have created approximations of practice that were simultaneously authentic and accessible.

While this project focused on K-12 education, an unexpected outcome of the project was that a molecular biology researcher adapted the activities for an undergraduate biochemistry course.  This serves as a compelling demonstration of the project’s underlying curricular strength and powerful and reciprocal benefits of  the partnership between science educator practitioners and science researchers. By participating in this project, the molecular biology researcher developed  their own pedagogical knowledge, a n area most researchers are not formally trained in. Th is shift across educational levels  suggests that the partnership successfully bridged professional cultures, yielding  robust curriculum and enhancing teaching capacity within the research community. As science education continues to grapple with implement ing practices-based instruction, the convergence model proposed here offers both a theoretical rationale and a practical approach. We propose that similar partnerships between science researchers and science educators offer a pathway to making these practices visible and accessible to K-12 learners. Such partnerships require sustained engagement rather than one-time interactions, positioning teachers as co-learners and co-designers who can facilitate their students' legitimate peripheral participation in scientific communities.
