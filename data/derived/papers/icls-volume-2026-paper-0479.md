# Living the Impossible: Designing Life Happens, an AI-Supported Budget and Justice Simulation

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper reports on the design of Life Happens, a classroom simulation in which students manage household budgets across twelve months while encountering criminal justice– related financial obligations. Grounded in situated learning theory, the simulatio n develops quantitative reasoning and perspective-taking through authentic financial dilemmas. Initial prototypes relied on large language models, producing inconsistencies and outputs that reinforced harmful stereotypes. Informed by a trauma-aware framework, the team restructured the system into a three-layer architecture: a deterministic core, a curated event layer, and a bounded AI layer limited to brief affective text messages. This paper focuses on design principles that emerged from documented failures during deployments at a Hispanic-Serving community college and a Minority-Serving state university. Context, theoretical framing, and focus Faculty at a Hispanic-Serving community college and a Minority-Serving state university in the northeastern United States identified a need for experiential tools conveying how criminal justice involvement compounds household financial hardship (García et al., 2019). The interdisciplinary partnership brought together computer science pedagogy and criminal justice education to serve predominantly first-generation college students (Penuel et al., 2013). Institutional constraints, including limited class time, variable devices, and diverse student demographics, shaped every design decision. We conceptualize learning through situated learning theory (Lave & Wenger, 1991), which holds that knowledge develops through participation in authentic practices. Life Happens situates students within household financial management under justice-system co nstraints, requiring arithmetic computation, budgeting, and quantitative trade-off reasoning as disciplinary competencies that emerge through meaningful decision-making (Gee, 2007). Simultaneously, students develop perspective-taking as they experience cascading consequences of systemic inequities (Belman & Flanagan, 2010). This paper focuses on the simulation’s design and AI integration, tracing how concrete prototype failures led to the principled architecture presented here. Simulation design and learning affordances Life Happens positions students as household decision-makers managing limited resources across twelve simulated months. Each cycle requires allocating funds across housing, food, transportation, healthcare, and phone services. Justice-related obligations such as probation fees, court-ordered payments, and commissary contributions impose additional mandatory costs (Harper et al., 2020). Five well-being indicators update transparently based on decisions, with visible arithmetic enabling students to anticipate consequences (Chernikova et al., 2020). Quarterly reflection prompts emphasize metacognition and discussion (Squire, 2011). The system employs a three-layer architecture. The deterministic core maintains authored family compositions, income parameters, and cost data from regional economic sources, ensuring reproducibility and enabling instructor customization. The event layer introduces curated disruptions ranging from routine expenses to justice-related challenges, each with specified costs and well-being consequences. The affective AI layer generates brief text messages that translate numerical outcomes into personal narratives, adding emotional context while the core mechanics remain fully deterministic. AI integration: From unconstrained generation to bounded use Initial prototypes relied on large language models to generate family scenarios, event narratives, and financial parameters (Yan et al., 2024), producing three categories of documented failure. Generated family compositions contained logical inconsistencies that confused students and undermined budgeting strategies. AI-produced cost structures varied unpredictably across similar scenarios, making it difficult for students to develop reliable 2865 quantitative intuitions. Generated content also perpetuated harmful stereotypes about poverty and justice involvement, particularly affecting overrepresented communities (Lee et al., 2024). These failures demanded a framework for addressing stereotypes and trauma. Drawing on trauma-informed pedagogy (Carello & Butler, 2015), we committed to ensuring that scenarios allow full participation independent of personal history, that content represents systemic patterns while honoring individual complexity, and that instructors retain the ability to modify or disable any element they judge inappropriate. This framework guided the restructuring of AI’s role. Because educator control over sensitive elements is a pedagogical and ethical requirement, all factual elements became authored content maintained in versioned databases (Gibson, 2023). AI was retained solely for generating 280-character text messages formatted as communications from family members, employers, and service providers (see Figure 1). Prompts incorporate strict constraints on tone, relationship dynamics, and prohibited content, and each message passes validation filters before display. Pre-authored fallback messages activate when outputs fail validation or when instructors operate in non-AI mode (Aldrich, 2005). This bounded application introduces meaningful variation across simulation runs and supports empathy-building, all while the deterministic mechanics remain fully intact. Design principles traced to practice The following principles emerged from the documented failures and institutional constraints above, and we trace each to its originating episode (Wang & Hannafin, 2005). Separation of deterministic and generative components arose because AI-generated cost s tructures produced inconsistencies that undermined learning objectives. Educator authorship of core mechanics resolved this while confining AI to a role where variability adds value and fairness is preserved. Trauma-aware content governance emerged from en counters with AI outputs that stereotyped justice-involved families. Instructor override, scenario review, and explicit content boundaries ensure that every version of the simulation actively interrogates these patterns. Privacy-conscious architecture responded to institutional review board concerns, and the minimal state approach transmits only current meter values and recent decisions (Pardo & Siemens, 2014). Redundancy and fallback systems became essential after pilot-session technical failures disrupted engagement, so pre-authored alternatives for every AI feature ensure continuity. Reflection over scoring prioritizes metacognition because early testing revealed that competitive scoring distracted students from systemic insights (Li et al., 2024). Conclusion Life Happens demonstrates that AI can enhance educational simulations when carefully bounded by pedagogical goals and equity commitments. The most consequential design decisions centered on preserving educator authority over content touching systemic inequality, trauma, and identity.
