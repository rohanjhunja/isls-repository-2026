# Can GenAI Assess My Data? Teachers’ Perspectives on Using GenAI Tools for Dataset Evaluation

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) can be used for various data-related tasks; however, teachers often lack guidance or knowledge of what those tasks are and what their limitations are. To begin understanding how teachers perceive GenAI's role in data science, we examined teachers’ evaluations of datasets with and without GenAI. Seven teachers assessed self-selected datasets across five dimensions using the 5Vs for K-12 framework during a professional development session. They used a GenAI tool to evaluate the same datasets and reflected on their experiences. Responses were analyzed through deductive and inductive thematic analysis, highlighting differences between the evaluations and te achers’ perspectives on student use of GenAI. Overall, GenAI responses were more detailed than those of teachers. However, the GenAI responses also exhibited some gaps, particularly in terms of veracity. Teachers’ acceptance and trust in GenAI responses varied and were mostly conditional.

The rise of GenAI tools, such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude, has introduced new possibilities and challenges for K-12 education. GenAI tools, especially those powered by large language models (LLMs), demonstrate capabilities in supporting various academic tasks, including writing (e.g., Gayed et al., 2022), programming (e.g., Kohen-Vacs et al., 2025), storytelling, and brainstorming (e.g., M.-C. Chiu & Hwang, 2025). GenAI tools also demonstrate capabilities in supporting data analysis (Browning et al., 2024) and data visualizations (Jiang et al., 2024). However, comprehensive guidance on how to pedagogically employ these GenAI tools to support rigorous learning processes and foster critical thinking is still needed (T. K. F. Chiu, 2024). Systematic reviews of GenAI in education (e.g., Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019)  indicate a growing interest among teachers in integrating such technologies into learning and teaching. These reviews highlight the need for teacher guidance on when GenAI can be responsibly integrated into instruction and how to evaluate work generated with such tools (Ng et al., 2025). Teachers’ understanding of how to use GenAI in their instruction begins with them engaging with the tools directly. Professional development programs can enrich teachers with meaningful practical experiences and help them develop teaching strategies that leverage the various capabilities of GenAI  (Yue et al., 2024). This exploratory study examines teachers’ perceptions of using GenAI tools for data science tasks, particularly in evaluating datasets. Data evaluation is an essential,  yet often underemphasized, aspect of the data science cycle (Witte et al., 2025). For this work, w e explore how teachers evaluate  datasets with and without GenAI, pursuing the questions: (1) How do teachers’ dataset evaluations differ when performed manually compared to using GenAI tools? and (2) To what extent are teachers willing to accept and  trust AI-generated dataset evaluations submitted by students?

## Literature review

GenAI in education GenAI refers to models, such as LLMs and multimodal systems, that generate new content in the form of text, images, code, and multimedia in response to natural language instructions (i.e., prompts) by learning patterns and structures from extensive data so urces (Miao & Holmes, 2023). Given their great potential, GenAI tools have rapidly entered educational settings, offering various opportunities for teaching and learning while also introducing some significant challenges and concerns (Yusuf et al., 2024). A recent scoping review of 118 papers identified and categorized various ways GenAI can support teachers and practitioners in various educational tasks, including assessment and grading, student performance prediction, knowledge representation, and content generation (Yan et al., 2024). Another review of 112 papers highlighted that GenAI tools, such as ChatGPT, can aid in writing and enhance student engagement, communication, and access to learning resources (Ali et al., 2024). These tools can also serve as tutors that provide personalized and adaptive feedback and enhance creative learning by facilitating brainstorming (M.-C. Chiu & Hwang, 2025). 1104 GenAI in data science education Prior work has primarily focused on undergraduate and graduate education, emphasizing how GenAI tools can support data exploration and analysis, from writing code and visualizing data to explaining statistical concepts and generating insights. For example, Bien and Mukherjee (2025) described an approach in an introductory data science course where students used GitHub Copilot, a GenAI code generator, to convert written prompts into R code, thereby streamlining data analysis process. In another study, students found ChatGPT helpful for explaining data science concepts, generating Python code to build a decision tree model, and interpreting the output, although success depended on precise,  iterative prompting (Zheng, 2023). Complementing this, Shen et al. (2024) investigated the use of ChatGPT in data science assignments, revealing that prompt engineering techniques (e.g., breaking down instructions into multiple prompts and providing dataset details) improved students’ analysis. Beyond data analysis, recent studies have argued that GenAI is reshaping the broader data science workflow, including data cleaning, exploration, modeling, interpretation, and communication. Tu et al. (2024), for example, present a conceptual work that highlights how GenAI capabilities can be leveraged and integrated into data science education, while shifting the emphasis of instruction toward source evaluation and justification of claims. Empirical research  on a Gen-AI-infused data science course echoes this trajectory, highlighting GenAI’s contribution to students’ analytical skills while stressing the need for clear instruction on verification, ethical use, and awareness of the tools’ limitations (Browning et al., 2024). Despite promising research on GenAI in higher education, a significant gap remains in understanding its implementation in K-12 data science education.

## Participants and research settings

Seven teachers (two Male, five female) attended an online professional development (PD) workshop hosted in the United States. The primary goal of these workshops was to introduce teachers to the field of data science using the API Can Code curriculum (Israel-Fishelson & Weintrop, 2026). On the first day of the PD, teachers were introduced to the 5Vs for K-12 Framework (Israel-Fishelson & Weintrop, Under Review) for evaluating datasets in K-12 educational contexts. This framework revises the dimensions of the “5Vs for big data,” which was originally employed to characterize big data by Volume, Velocity, Variety, Veracity, and Value (Lomotey & Deters, 2014). Teachers were asked to select a dataset based on their interests and to complete  a Google Form to evaluate it using the 5Vs for K-12 framework. The f orm had five open-ended prompts, one for each framework dimension. On the second day, teachers used a GenAI tool of their choice to evaluate the same dataset. They then completed a Google form with their prompt and GenAI response, a reflection on the differences between their Data were collected through Google Forms and exported for analysis. The data were coded using deductive and inductive thematic analysis (Saldaña, 2016)  to identify patterns in teachers’ reasoning and perceptions of GenAI responses. To guide the analysis, we used two sets of codes. The first comes from prior research on dataset evaluation and was used to capture the presence and completeness of both the teachers’ and GenAI responses for each “V”, using a  Comprehensive, Partial, and Incorrect  classification to score each dimension. A fuller description of the codes  is presented in Tables 2 and 3 in (Israel-Fishelson & Weintrop, Under Review). The second set of codes (see Table 1) was developed inductively to capture teachers’ perceived differences between their analysis and GenAI’s, and their acceptance/trust in such outputs. Two researchers independently coded the responses using the two sets of codes. An inter-rater reliability assessment using Cohen’s Kappa (Cohen, 1960) yielded a coefficient of 0.8. Reject Explicit distrust of the GenAI analysis Uncertain Mixed or unclear views about trusting GenAI analysis Teachers’ dataset evaluations with and without GenAI tools The analysis of teachers’ dataset evaluations using the 5Vs for K-12 framework revealed variability in how thoroughly each of the 5Vs was addressed. While teachers engaged meaningfully with dimensions such as Volume and Velocity, which are readily observable attributes, their responses often lacked depth in more abstract or interpretive dimensions, such as Veracity and Value.  When describing the Volume, most responses referenced the dataset size or scope, for example, “roughly 3,00 rows and 25 columns,” but rarely connected those quantities to the stated inquiry. Velocity was often addressed comprehensively or partially, with concrete references to recency. For example, one of the teachers responded, “It was updated on 9/13/2024, and the time frame is 1945-2017.” Variety was discussed in general terms, mainly mentioning data types or variable names, for example, “The data are primarily numeric in nature,” but seldom noting the format or structure. Veracity frequently focused on single facets, such as bias, with fewer references to data completeness and reliability. For example, one teacher responded, “There was a switch in how data was collected in the 2000s, which may have biased some of the data.” Value was generally underdeveloped, often stated in vague terms without grounding the claims in a specific research question. For example, one teacher responded, “It can be easily used for multiple analyses, and I think it is relevant for high school students.” Each teacher’s response was also evaluated as Comprehensive, Partial, or Incorrect, the results of which are shown in dark blue columns in Figure 1a. These patterns suggest that the 5Vs for K-12 framework effectively guided teachers toward a common evaluative vocabulary while leaving room for additional examples and scaffolding to support deeper reasoning. To compare GenAI-generated evaluations with teacher responses, we coded the GenAI data evaluation responses using the same codes as those used for the teachers’ responses. The results are presented in the light blue columns of Figure 1a. Analysis indicated that GenAI responses were more structured and comprehensive than those of teachers in several dimensions, while also resulting in more Incorrect responses. The depth and completeness of teachers’ prompts to GenAI emerged as a key factor of output quality.  Prompts that explicitly included all 5Vs (Volume, Velocity, Variety, Veracity, and Value) and specified an analytic focus produced fully structured and context-sensitive analyses from the GenAI tools. For example, the prompt “Please analyze this data for the following attributes: Volume of data, Velocity of data, Variety of data, Veracity of data, and Value of data, in particular for the state of North Carolina,” yielded a response that systematically and comprehensively addressed each “V" for the specified state. Interestingly, a more detailed prompt that included a concise definition for each “V” also yielded a similar result, suggesting that GenAI struggle d to identify various biases. This underscores how the breadth of the prompt conditions the scope and depth of the analysis. Teachers’ reflections on the differences they observed between their own evaluations and those produced using GenAI tools help contextualize the above-mentioned trends. Three teachers noted that the AI responses tended to be more detailed than their own analyses. Two teachers acknowledged that the GenAI tool identified new patterns or perspectives they had overlooked. For example, a teacher who investigated a dataset about 1106 musicians’ popularity noted, “GEMINI brought up a variety of narratives; it indeed provided analysis that I did not include.” Other teachers did not perceive significant differences between their own responses and those of the GenAI. These cases reflect situations where the teacher felt aligned with the AI’s reasoning or conclusions. Acceptance and trust of GenAI-generated dataset evaluations Teachers’ responses on whether to accept and trust such GenAI-assisted student evaluations revealed a range of perspectives (See Figure 1b). Acceptance was mostly conditional. Three teachers indicated they w ould accept GenAI-assisted work if students could explain their thinking, verify claims, and recognize the tool's contribution. For example, in response to the question “Would you accept such a response from students in your classroom? Why or why not?”  one teacher noted, “Yes,  I would, but I would be more interested in the students’ ability to explain and provide reasoning behind the answer,” indicating that GenAI output alone is considered insufficient without student justification. A teacher who declined acceptance stated, “Not really, it’s not personalized. I would rather have them give me pieces of their own thoughts so we can work together to come up with a complete and more appropriate response. I need them to think before utilizing AI and assuming it is 100% accurate.” Perceptions of trust correspond with the conditional acceptance of GenAI content submitted by students. Two teachers expressed unequivocal trust, while four others expressed conditional trust. For example, when asked about students using GenAI in their classrooms, one teacher responded, “I would want my students to extend their critical thinking beyond the response and use it only as a guide,” positioning GenAI output as a starting point that must be expanded through classroom discourse and critique. The teacher who opposed and distrusted the use o f GenAI noted, “My experience with AI is that students use it to try to cheat,” pointing out the need to foreground transparency and student agency when working with such tools. Evaluating datasets is a multidimensional, context-dependent, evidence-based task that involves both quantitative and qualitative reasoning (Gebru et al., 2021). Our findings highlight this complexity, showing nuance for both teachers and GenAI tools. While factors like dataset size (Volume), recency (Velocity), and data types (Variety) are more easily identified and evaluated, Veracity, which encompasses accuracy, reliability, completeness, and bias, is more cognitively demanding. GenAI outputs mirrored this tendency, confidently invoking source credibility while under-specifying sampling or measurement issues. In comparing teacher to GenAI evaluations, GenAI responses tended to be more structured, complete, and detailed than teacher responses. However, the GenAI’s outputs also reveal ed consistent blind spots, particularly in the area of Veracity.  Teachers’ responses were more concise and less technical, but were often oriented towards pedagogical relevance, especially when attending to Value. These findings suggest that tasks such as dataset evaluation could benefit from using GenAI as a supporting tool to help students rather than as a tool to complete the task on its own. Thus, it is crucial that teachers guide students in questioning and validating the GenAI’s analysis. Another implication of these findings is the importance of teaching students to effectively prompt GenAI tools, given the impact strong prompts have on response s. Educational use of GenAI should be guided by critical, evidence-based practices rather than answer-seeking prompt patterns (Kasneci et al., 2023). Teachers who want to promote high-quality reasoning should teach prompt engineering and help students generate questions or instructions that will guide the tools to produce meaningful outputs. In this study, teachers’ perspectives on students’ GenAI use were generally permissive but conditional. Recent research reveal s diverse attitudes among teachers and policymakers about  students’ use of GenAI  in assignments and assessments, highlighting the need for clear criteria and  classroom-level guidelines to assess GenAI-assisted work (Xia et al., 2024). Given the rising, often unsupervised use of GenAI tools by students, it is crucial for teachers to gain hands-on experience with these tools to recognize their capabilities and limitations. Such understanding is needed for their optimal integration in classroom settings. The findings illustrate GenAI’s role as a scaffold rather than a replacement, highlighting its strengths and weaknesses in dataset evaluation. Moreover, the study demonstrates that prompt design serves as a pedagogical layer, as explicit, evidence-based prompts facilitate a comprehensive analysis. This work contributes to the growing body of  literature aimed at preparing teachers and students for our data-driven, AI-based world.
