# Students Self-Regulate Learning in Hidden Ways

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Visual Marginalia (VM) are visual, collateral, personal productions elaborated in secret during classroom lessons by students. Data collected in our third study on this topic give additional evidence to support the Visual Marginalia elaboration Model (VMeM), based on two orthogonal axes: intentionality and marginalization. VM are more or less deliberate and marginalized production, shifting along the axes. The model shows how VMs are useful for students’ self-regulation and learning processes. Results reveal how they impact emotion, attention, motivation, memory, knowledge integration and reorganization. We exemplify a new phenomenon, named Dynamic Visual Marginalia, where one VM evolves into another, showing how self-regulation interacts temporally with local context.

Visible thought in the margins and in the educational setting Visual Marginalia represent an evolutionary expansion of the traditional concept of Marginalia—an enduring and widespread human practice encompassing textual commentary, glosses, markings, caricatures, drawings, doodles, and various scribbles found within the margins of d ocuments. Its historical roots stretch back from an cient times to the present day, with numerous examples of notab le figures like Einstein and Bruner whose marginali a have been scrutinized for insights into both authorial a nd reader engagement. Within educational contexts, marginalia serve as asynchronous annotations produced by stude nts in response to lessons delivered by teachers, r evealing their interaction with texts as readers (Reid, 2018; Shechtman and Roschelle, 2010). Recent studies (Dario & Tateo, 2022; Dario, Lund & Tateo, 2022) show that students also engage in marginalia creation during active participation in lessons—a phenomenon that has, until now, received limited attention. Expanding upon the age-old tradition of margin annotations during reading,  we introduce  Visual Marginalia (VM), which encompass visual self-expressions generated by students during lessons. T hese expressions include para-writings and para-drawings, creative marginal drawings and writings, modifications to book imagery, titles, or texts, as w ell as squiggles and doodles. Our focus lies not on ly in observing their static appearance (as they are not merely the product of r eader-text interaction) but also in tracking their evolut ion throughout the course of a lesson and across developmental stages, from elementary to middle school. This inquiry into the dynamic nature of Visual Marginalia serves as a cornerstone of the research presented here. The educational field is goal-directed and generally governed by intentional acti vities of the teacher. However, we argue that the elaboration of Visual Ma rginalia is associated with two objectives, which w e link to the learner. The first is to anchor lear ning to a particular educational context and the se cond is to emphasize the active role of the learner in what is being learned.

We aim to understand the impact of VM creation on students' learning experiences, exploring questions such as the role of intentionality in VM production and its relationship to self-regulation, as well as how different types of VM reflect variations in cognitive and emotional engagement with educational settings over the lesson. In Dario, Lund, & Tateo (2022), we devised a methodology to capture VM in real classrooms and engage with students who produced them. We proposed a desc riptive model that mobilizes intentionality and marginalization axes, revealing how students self-regulate their learning process through VM elaboration. In this paper, we replicate and extend our previous studies to reinforce the evidence for our model. Through an increased number of examples and the identification of a new hybrid form termed Dynamic Visual Marginalia, we illustrate how VM evolve over lesson time, demonstrating their  role in emotion regulation, attention maintenance,  and knowledge integration. Our findings underscore the importance of intentionality and self-regulation in VM production, positioning VM along a continuum from spontaneous to deliberate processes.

## Studying VM productions in the classroom: our mixed methods approach

Our research design, rooted in phenomenological and  interpretative epistemology and informed by perspe ctives from semiotic cultural psychology (Valsiner, 2007; Valsiner & Rosa, 2007; Valsiner & vander Veer, 201 7; Anderson & Larson, 2013), builds upon the innovative mixed methods approach employed in our previous study (Dario, Lund, & Tateo, 2022) to comprehensively exp lore the phenomenon of VM as it unfolds during the teaching-learning process. This approach encompasses various  data collection methods, including teacher questionnaires, observations, video-recorded lessons, VM collection, and self-confrontation interviews.

## Procedure and materials

In Phase 1, we held kick-off meetings with school principals and teachers to  establish collaboration. Privacy/consent documents were distributed to teach ers, parents, and students to ensure ethical compli ance. In Phase 2, teachers answered a questionnaire on lesson content, learning objectives, and pedagogical methods. They were given a guide on communicating with the BigBlu eButton Platform (BBB). In Phase 3 (technical setup), webcams were installed in the classrooms to capture  lessons and to enable real-time observation by researchers (at-a-distance, during the 2022 Covid-19 pandemic). Phase 4 focused on briefing students about researchers' interests and commitments. In Phase 5, VM produced during lessons were collected, either by capturing screenshots (using BBB Platform) or receiving photos from middle sc hool students via email (on occasion, they sent VM by smartphone). Finally, in Phase 6 we did self-confrontation interviews, wherein researchers used BBB to engage students in reflective discussions about their VM creation (context, function, and development). ## Participants

The study involved 67 students (aged 6-12) and 6 teachers from primary and secondary schools in northern Italy. All participants were native Italian speakers and did not have behavioral or learning difficulties. Participation was voluntary, and guardians provided authorization. The first author supervised all phases and coordinated 3 master's students from University of Salerno, each of which wrote their thesis on collected data.

## Conclusion

In this third study, in our quest to provide additional evidence for our Visual Marginalia model (cf. Figure 3), we asked: how do semiotic and self-regulatory tools such as VM use varying levels of i ntentionality to aid in forms of self-regulation? Here, we replicated the method in Dario, Lund and Tateo (202 2), carried out in primary and middle schools in northern Italy. For each new inst ance of a VM, we presented its relevance in self-regulating emotions, attention, listening, memorization, comprehension, and personal reorganization of lesson content. Despite being often overlooked by teachers, Visual Marginalia serve as meaningful tools for students t o control their behavior and organize their thoughts. They are directly linked to self-regulation and cognitive empowerment, allowing students to establis h an internal locus of control. However, expressing  oneself in marginalized spaces requires skill, as breaking the rule of not writing in the margins is challenging for young children. Figure 3 Visual Marginalia elaboration Model (VMeM) Scribbles, stylizations, and ornaments made during lessons can enhance students' listening  abilities and maintain their interest, easing tension and apprehe nsion. Students report that  they are done without intention. Students say that the repetitive motions of non-deliberate VM lead to emotions (e.g., pleasure or fear), depending on the context. The interconnection of affect and r epetitive motion helps to remember content from the  context. Some VM (e.g., modifications) move from less deliberate motions (i.e., coloring) to more deliberate when the student realizes a direct connection to lesson content (e.g., physical descriptions in English). Deliberate VM, such as doodles, drawings, and annotations help students understand their own learning and personalize lesson content. Moreover, Dynamic Visual Marginalia, a newly identified form of self-expression among older students, combine the characteristics of 2-3 Visual Marginalia. In conclusion, Visual Marginalia  encourage students to explore ways of regaining control and power in lear ning situations, fostering a sense of responsibilit y for their learning process. Future efforts will focus on teachers’ knowledge of VM production. It remains uncertain whether teachers should guide students in this self-regulatory process or if students are best left to manage it independently.
