# Modeling-Based Learning in Kindergarten Science: Investigating Heat Transfer in a Metal Rod

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Following research on using Modeling-based Learning (MbL) in kindergarten, in this interpretive case study we seek to provide detailed descriptions of the implementation of MbL with a group of 24 kindergarten children studying heat transfer in a metal rod. Focusing on children mechanistic reasoning and their constructed models, findings suggest that participating children successfully engaged in authentic MbL activities and developed several different types of models, utilizing a variety of features of mechanistic reasoning. We use this evidence to argue that (a) different modeling tools may afford different learning opportunities; (b) kindergarten children have modeling resources that can use in order to utilize different MbL tools; and (c) different MbL tools may prevent or support children moving into a lower scalar lever of a more advanced mechanistic account of the phenomenon.

## Introduction

A growing number of studies have advocated for the effectiveness of modeling-based learning (MbL) as a productive approach to teaching and learning scienc e (e.g., Louca et al, 2011a, 2015, 2023a; Baek et al., 2011; Buckley et al., 2004; Clement, 2008; Gilbert & Just i, 2016; Halloun, 2016; Jackson et al., 2008; Khan,  2007; Osborne, 2014; Schwarz et al., 2009; Windschitl et al., 2008). The National Research Council [NRC] (2013) defines modeling as a practice involving constructi ng, evaluating, revising, and utilizing mod els to predict and explain physical phenomena. When learners engage in the process of constructing models using various modeling tools, these learner-constructed models become tools for learning, enabl ing a comprehensive exploration and understanding of the underlying mechanisms of natural phenomena (Schwarz et al., 2009). To develop models, learners must effectively transform and int egrate their observations, experiences, knowledge, and theories into tangible representations. This process empowers learners to test, evaluate, and refine their theories, thereby advancing their scientif ic thinking and knowledge construction (Zimmerman, 2007). Consequently, the process of MbL involves construct ing, evaluating, and revising theoretical structures in the form of models, leading to the development of concrete representa tions for objects, object behaviors, and processes, that can explain and predict phenomena. These learner-constructed models may then serve as vehicles for scientific reasoning development, further investigations, and meaningful learning (Louca & Zacharia, 2023). Taken all together, t he heart of MbL is the process of model construction, emphasizing the learning journey and processes of modeling rather than focus ing solely on the end product (model). As a result, MbL transcends the acquisition of scientific facts, involving learners in deciphering the mechanism underlying natural phenomena (e.g., NRC, 2012; Krist et al., 2019). De spite its value, a clear understanding of MbL's rol e in early science education remains elusive, as effective imp lementation of MbL in early grades remains unclear.  This presents numerous possible challenges for teachers and curriculum designers (e.g., Tobin et al., 2018). Additionally, modeling-based reasoning and practices are often insufficien tly emphasized in school science education, particularly in younger grades (e.g., Forbes et al., 2015; National Research Council, 2012; Schwarz et al., 2009; Windschitl et al., 2008). These issues r aise questions about the modeling practices of kind ergarten children during MbL. Thus, it is reasonable to wonder about the engagement of kinder garten children in similar practices. Involving children in MbL during kinderg arten could lead to more effective MbL as they matu re (van Joolingen et al., 2019). However, implementing MbL in these early y ears necessitates supporting teachers, developing appropriate curriculum materials, and un derstanding what Louca & Zacharia (2019), Louca (2020) and Louca & Zacharia (2023) referred to as the MbL resources that young c hildren employ (e.g., Louca et al, 2011a).

## Theoretical framework

Learners as young as kindergarten have sophisticated ways of  thinking about the natural world around them, which is translated in the K-12 Science Education Framework as seeking to engage  learners in the development of explanations of natural phenomena (NRC, 2012). Towards this direction, the framework includes the practices of students developing and using models and constructing explanations for natural phenomena. Completed models include an underlying mechanism representing the investigat ed phenomenon’s behavior. Developing this mechanism requires mechan istic reasoning (Russ et al., 2008), involving the explanation of sequential stages and the factors giving rise to a phenomenon (Krist et al., 2019). Russ et al. (2008) developed a framework that describes seven components of mechanistic reasoning: (i) descriptions of the target phenomenon (what we see happening), (ii) identification of the set-up conditions that are necessary for the phenomenon to happen, (iii) identification of entities (conceptual or real obje cts that play a particular role in the phenomenon), (iv) identification of the entities’ activities that cau se changes in the surrounding entities, (v) identification of the entities’ properties, (vi) identification of the entities’ organization (how e ntities are located, structured or oriented within the phenomenon), and (vii) chaining, that is, making claims about what happene d prior to a phenomenon and what will happen next.

Ke and Schwarz (2019) suggested that “developing and revising models that address the mechanism of phenomena lies at the core of the scientific endeavor” (p. 183). They argued that the development of models that include the appropriate mechanisms should be viewed as a “productive epistemic consideration that is aligned to the values of both disciplinary science and the classroom knowledge-building community” (Ke & Schwarz, 2019, p. 184). Of course, developing such models is both demanding and challenging, especially for young or novice modelers (Louca & Zacharia, 2015). A number of researchers have argued that t he development of such models also depends upon the role of teachers (e.g., Khan, 2011; Manz & Renga, 2017).  Thus, supporting teachers by e.g., curriculum materials and descriptions of productive classroom-based MbL is deemed important. NASEM (2022) suggests that developing and using models and constructing explanations should be part of learning in science in early grades.  NASEM (202 2) points to the fact that preschool children have been observed exhibiting substantial representational proficiencies within the context of MbL having a possible number of what Louca & Zacharia  (2019) called modeling recourses from a variety of areas such as free play (during which they manipulate objects), storytelling (durin g which they describe things happening in particula r order), drawing (during which they make, recognize and interpret representational choices and intentions) (Callaghan & Corbit, 2015; DeLoache, 2004). Louca (2023) suggested that kindergarten children are abl e to use a variety of modeling media to represent that mechanism.

Despite its merits, the research community lacks a comprehensive understanding of MbL in kindergarten science education. The effectiveness of implementin g MbL at these ages remains ambiguous, posing subst antial challenges for teachers and curriculum designers (e.g., Tobin et al., 201 8). Simultaneously, the emphasis on modeling-based reasoning and practices in school-based science education is often underemphasized, particularly in the early grades (Forbes et al., 2015; Schwarz et al., 2009; Windschitl et al., 2008). These issues raise questions concerning the modeling practices and processes kindergarteners engage in during MbL. Purpose This is a n interpretive  case study  (Yin, 2017)  investigating the implementation of MbL  with a group of 24 kindergarten children engaged in the study of heat transfer in a metal rod, taught by a teacher (secon d author) participating in a professional development program of MbL in kindergarten science education. In this study, we provide detailed descriptions of the ways these kin dergarten children engaged in MbL. With an emphasis  on the representation of the mechanism of the phenomenon, we analyzed evidence to describe the different ways that kindergarten children used different modeling tools while learning science.  Evidence from the case study at hand seeks to add to a growing body of research that children as early as kindergarten are able to engage in MbL. Design and procedures Following an interpretive case study approach (Yin, 2017), this study involved a group of 24 kindergarten children (age range: 5-6) taught by the second author at a metropolitan public kindergarten school in Cyprus. The teacher participated in a professional development (PD) pro gram for MbL in kindergarten science organized by t he first author over the school year. The case selected for this study was the teacher’s last teaching unit of MbL in science in May-June 2023. Along with the first author and the rest of the teachers participating i n the PD program (9 teachers in total), the teacher developed the unit from scratch in a series of online bi-weekly meetings, and datadriven reflections based on the videotaped lessons and photos of children's models. All 6 lessons (about 40-minute duration) were videotaped and transcribed for analysis. Children's work/models were also collected for analysis. For this study, we adopted the approach of drawing-based modeling in which children use annotated drawings to represent their model (Fretz et al, 2002). We analyzed child-developed models (artifact analysis) and their presentation and discussion (discourse analys is), seeking to provide rich, detailed descriptions  of the characteristics of these models and the modeling process children engaged in. For the analysis of children modeling conversations, we used two complementary approaches. The first analysis uses transcribed children’s conversations as a gateway to children's thinking and learning ex periences (Edwards & Mercer, 1995). We used discourse to develop detailed accounts of the context and the content of the modeling conversation describing the children's con tributions toward MbL, which we subsequently descri be in the Findings section.  To support this analysis, we also coded the same di scourse data using Russ et al’s (2008) coding scheme of 7 major components of mechanistic reasoning to code for mechanistic reasoning in stud ents’ discourse (see Table 2). To support the discourse analyses above, we also an alyzed child-constructed models using artifact analysis adopted from Louca et al  (2011b). Codes included the ways that children represente d different elements in their models: physical object s, physical entities, phy sical processes, and physical interactions.

## Findings

In this section, we present a detailed account of the case study supported by the data analyzed, and then we turn into a discussion about the emerging themes we identified. The descriptions of the findings below seek to co ver the complete unit as it was enacted, seeking to provide a detailed account of how the children worked with MbL throughout the unit.

The teacher started the first lesson with a puppet show, during which two puppets were drinking similar hot tea. While both tea mugs had teaspoons inside t hem, one puppet’s teaspoon got hot whereas the othe r’s did not. The teacher asked the children why they though t that had happened. Children suggested that one of  the teaspoons was made of plastic, while the other was a metal one, which was the reason the teaspoon did not get hot. This directed the conversation towards the idea that different materi als seem to get heated differently.  To verify that idea, the teacher guided the children  to design an experiment that included several rods made of different materials (metal, wood, plastic, PVC). Us ing gun-like thermometers, children measured the rods’ temperature before the experiment, then placed them in hot water for 2 minutes and re-measured their temperature. From the data collected from that experiment, children reached a consensus that while all rods were heated up, the metal rod reached a higher temperature.

Based on the data collected in the first  lesson, during the second lesson, the teacher asked children to draw what they thought happen ed inside the metal rod that result ed in the rod getting heated up. She gave the children a piece of paper with an outline of a glass of water with a rod inside, similar to what they had used during their experiment in lesson 1. Towards the end of le sson 2, all the children presented their drawings describing their ideas. This was the children’s first model.

In lesson 3, the children with the  support of their teacher designed a new experiment to test the theor y underlying their discussion about their models 1. This was a consequence of their discussion in lesso n 2, where the children started articulating a possible mechanism that would cause the phenomeno n they had observed. Because they did not have direct observations of th at mechanism, the children agreed to test their the ory. In the experiment they designed, they measured the temperature of a rod in hot water (a) every 2 minutes and (b) i n 3 different parts of the rod (upper, middle, and bottom). Based on the measurements collected, the teacher helped better formalize an emerging idea suggesting that the rod was getting hotter over time and that the top of the rod, was less hot than its lower part. After the experiment, the teacher asked children to evaluate their models 1 based on the results from their new experiment. This was done by each child placing a sticker onto any 2 models that they thought best described the phenomenon under st udy. Children knew that they needed to be ready to explain their voting. After identifying children’s models that received the highest number of stickers, and discussing the possible reasons for receiving the most stickers, the teacher asked the children to develop a new, revised drawing, that would include ideas discussed during the evaluation and ideas discussed as a result of their second experiment. As artifact analysis shows, children’s models were slightly improved in the representation of physical entities (heat) and physical process (the movement of the heat from the water towards the upper part o f the rod). However, the presentation of the interactions between the objects and/or the entities was limited. The mechanistic reasoning analysis also showed limited improvement of the description (not necessarily the representation) of the entities’ activities (e.g., the model in Figure 2a does not show the movement of he at from the water to the lower part of the rod, and then upwards). However, more c hildren explicitly talked about the entities’ prope rties (e.g., the heat moving), although they did not explicitly represent them as shown by the artifact analysis. The teacher was concerned about children not being able to improve substantially their representation of the mechanism of the phenomenon in their model 2. Thus, in lesson 4, she asked children to develop a new model through dramatic play. This was based on Louca (2023) previous work, where they suggest ed that different modeling tools may afford different/more productive modeling opportunities for children. To facilitate the process of developing a new model through theatrical play, the teacher marked with tape on the floor the outli ne of the glass with the hot water and the rod, and the child ren spent lesson 4 in a whole class setting explaining various ideas about the mechanism of the phenomenon. Each i dea proposed was subsequently evaluated by the chil dren. The evaluation was based on whether each proposed theatrical play corresponded with the children’s observations from the phenomenon, seeking to represent a mechanism that was able to explain the data. In lesson 5, two different models proposed were fin alized after they received acceptance from the children (see Figure 3). Both models were  performed by the children, videotaped by the teache r, and then discussed after watching the outcomes of the theatr ical plays.   It is worth noting that this was a long process for mainly 3 reasons. First, as Louca (2023) indicates, working with a new modeling medium usually takes longer for children to produce improved models as they nee d to understand the representation formalism of the  new medium. Second, a new model/theatrical play was usually proposed by a child, and then the rest of the class tried to understand how the model would play out, and whe ther it (a) correspond ed with the observations collected from the phenomenon, and (b) represent ed the parts of the phenomenon in a logical and plausi ble manner. In all cases, children needed to play the theatrical model  out in their classroom before being able to evalua te it and collaboratively revise it. Developing, revising, and playing to perfection the two models took two lessons. Out of three total models proposed, the class reached a consensus about the two of them. The third model was dismissed. In t he first model, children act ed as pieces of the heat  (physical entities, artifact analysis), moving (physical process, artifact analysis; entities’ activities, mechanistic reasoning) in batches of 3 from the hot water (physical object, artifact analysis) into the rod. After the first three children moved in the rod, three additional children moved in, but at the same time, two children (out of the three initial children) moved further into the rod (identification of entities, entities’ activities, and entities’ properties, mechanistic reasoning ana lysis). Then 3 additional children moved in, and at the same time one of the two children on the top moved further, two children from the three in the previous “compartment” moved forward, and so on. In the second model, the children acted as pieces of the rod  itself (physical objects, artifact analysis), standing in the rod next to each other holding hand s (physical interactions, artifact analysis). Then, when the hot water was added, the first child next to the rod st arted moving his hands up and down  (identification of entities’ activities and entities’ properties, mechanistic re asoning analysis). Because she was holding hands with the next child, the next child started also moving her hands, and so on, until all children started moving thei r hands (physical process, artifact analysis). As time passed, the first child started moving her hands faster and “transmitted” this motion to the next child and so on, simulating the rod getting hotter. On one hand, during this discussion, the children were able to differentiate between the process of the heat moving from the water in the rod and then towards the uppe r part of the rod. On the other hand, children were able to identify that the result of that movement  was the increase of the temperature of the rod, due to the movement of heat in the rod. During the discussion, the children were concerned a bout whether the temperature of the rod would stop increasing and start decreasing; they suggested that their models did not account for this. Unfortunately, due to time constraints, the teacher needed to move into a new topic for the next days, and there was not an opportunity to work on a revised model.

## Discussion

Seeking to contribute towards a description of the differen t ways that kindergarten children may use different modeling tools while learning in science, in this study we have investigated how a group of children were engaged in the process of MbL.   Evidence from the study adds to a growing body of r esearch that children as early as in kindergarten are able to engage in MbL (Louca & Zacharia, 2019; Louca, 2020) in a number of productive ways. Our focus in this study was on mechanistic reasoning (R uss et al, 2008; Krist et al, 2019)  as evident from the modeling discourse and the children-constructed models. Evidence from this study suggests that the participating children were able to use paper-and-pencil and theatrical play as modeling tools to rep resent the mechanism underlying the phenomenon of heat transfer in a met al rod  (e.g., Louca, 2020). In doing so, they used prior knowledge and experiences to create and embed analo gies in their constructed models, invoking what Louca & Zacharia (2019) and Louca (2020) suggested as modeling resources in order to u tilize both different modeling tools, as well as possibly epistemological knowledge using MbL in natural ways of young children learning (e.g., theatrical playing).

At the same time, our data also suggest that there might be limits to different modeling tools affordances. In modeling natural phenomena, there might exist ad ditional lower scalar levels of mechanistic reasoning (Krist et al, 2019) that children need to get into. Based on that, our data suggest t hat the paper-and-pencil tool seemed to prevent children to moving into a lower scalar lever to develop a more advance d account of the mechanism of the phenomenon (see model 2), which they were able to develop when working with dramatic play  (model 3). This might suggest that it might be difficult for children to use the same modeling tool to represent a lower scalar level mechanistic account of the phenomenon under s tudy, or that different modeling tools may not as e asily support children moving into a representation of lower scalar levers. More investigation on this issue might prove useful for supporting MbL in kindergarten science teaching and learning. Interestingly, although using the theatrical play a s a tool for representing the phenomenon seemed to help children to move into a lower scalar level of the mechanism representation, it was not apparent that the children understood the importance of the improvement of their model or the fact that it provided a better account for the mechanism underlying the phenomenon.  This is related to Upmeier zu Belzen & Krüger ’s (2010) framework of model competence. Their framework distinguishes between five aspects of models: the n ature of models, the existence of multiple models, the purpo se of models, the process of testing models, and the process of changing models. While there was not a formal ev aluation of the participating children’s levels of model competence, our data suggest that children were able to work on or discuss a number of those model competence aspects including the existence of multiple models, the process and the purpose of testing models, and the process and the purpose of changing models. Future research should further address this and investigate how the children’s understanding of models and modeling, e.g., the nat ure of models  or the purpose of models, may support their efforts to represent the mechanism underlying the phenomena under study.
