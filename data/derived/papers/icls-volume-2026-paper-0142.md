# Design and Development of a Japanese Lesson Study Project through a Research- Practice Partnershipthrough research-practice partnership

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To expand the knowledge base on Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs), this study examines an empirical case of a hybrid RPP model operating within a sociocultural context outside North America. We analyze the 16-year development of a Japanese lesson study project employing the Knowledge Constructive Jigsaw method. The project is examined as an evolving activity system, reorganizing findings from previous research to highlight the contradictions, conflicts, and challenges that emerged, as well as the design strategies implemented to address them, drawing on Cultural-Historical Activity Theory. Our findings demonstrate how RPPs can function as collaborative platforms for practitioners and researchers that can be sustained and developed iteratively by aligning with the principles of Networked Improvement Communities.

This paper presents a case study of a Research-Practice Partnership (RPP) in Japan. Penuel and Gallagher (2017) define RPPs as “long-term collaborations between practitioners and researchers that are organized to investigate problems of practice and solutions for improving educational  systems.” They identify three primary types of RPPs: Research Alliances, Design-Based RPPs, and Networked Improvement Communities (NIC). Hybrid forms are also increasingly  recognized, but the accumulation of empirical  case studies remains limited. This study addresses this gap by presenting a hybrid RPP model derived from a Japanese case study. Although RPPs have mainly been studied in the North American context by the ICLS community, their characteristics likely differ across regions because they are shaped by authentic collaborations between researchers and educators within specific sociocultural settings. This study examines the development of a lesson study project that applied the Knowledge Constructive Jigsaw (KCJ; Miyake, 2013) over a 16-year period. Specifically, we investigate the problems identified in the RPP within the context of Japanese school  education that includes teacher culture, network  structures, professional development  (PD) systems, and the technologies employed to address these problems, as well as the resulting outcomes and future challenges.

## Theoretical framework

Early forms of Design-Based Research (DBR) in the learning sciences emphasized  a researcher-driven approach intended to influence classroom learning through standardized curricula and packaged materials (CTGV, 1997). However, subsequent studies demonstrated that  this one-size-fits-all approach was  insufficient, as learning processes and products are diverse and context-dependent (Barron, 2003). In response to this limitation, the 2010s saw the emergence of  approaches in which researchers collaborated with educators to identify classroom issues and contexts and co-develop curricula and instructional designs tailored to specific problems. One such approach is Design-Based Implementation Research  (DBIR; Penuel et al., 2011). Unlike traditional DBR, these studies included approaches in the implementation phase where researchers collaborated with educators to develop and test comprehensive curricular activity systems that utilize DBR results aligned with local contexts and challenges (e.g., Roschelle et al., 2010), as well as studies that collaboratively develop and test new curricula and teacher training programs tailored to classroom needs (e.g., Cobb et al., 2013; Russell et al., 2013). Since the development of RPPs in the United States is influenced by its decentralized education system, many RPPs focus on district-level curriculum design and the establishment of localized implementation systems. In contrast, Japan’s curriculum development is highly centralized, guided by national curriculum guidelines (Courses of Study) and instructional materials from private publishers. At the same time, teachers enjoy strong professional autonomy in their teaching methods. This autonomy is highly valued in Japan as a core component of teachers’ professional identity, fostering a strong norm that lessons should be designed independently by teachers. Given this cultural and institutional context, local boards of education (BOE) have relatively limited authority or influence over curriculum development or professional development. Consequently, an RPP model centered on board-level curriculum mandates is structurally incompatible and unlikely to gain traction in Japan. Instead, in the Japanese context, lesson study (Lewis, 2002) has been considered an effective means of influencing classroom learning by  transforming teachers’ skills and their perspectives on teaching and learning 1192 (Stigler & Hiebert, 1999). In Japan, lesson study is typically implemented in three  forms: a single school developing its own materials; multiple schools collaborating with researchers; or teachers and researchers forming practical research networks (private educational organizations) focused on specific methods. These networks resemble NICs (Bryk et al., 2011), but they traditionally operate independently from local BOEs. Their activities mainly focus on sharing specific teaching methods or curricula, rather than involving a broader range of stakeholders in addressing practical issues.

By contrast, this study examines the New Learning Project (NLP), a lesson study project that adopts an RPP approach adapted to the Japanese context. We selected the NLP as a focal case because it was jointly initiated by researchers and local BOE  leaders to enhance classroom practice by improving teacher competence through lesson study centered  on a single teaching method. Over 16 years, it developed into an NIC by iteratively addressing problems related to scaling up and adapting to changing school and teacher contexts. The research questions are as follows: (1) What challenges have been identified through the RPP ’s long-term efforts to influence classrooms through lesson study? (2) What changes have occurred in the lesson study activity system through collaborative design to address these challenges? Research setting The New Learning Project (NLP), launched in 2010 by CoREF at the University of Tokyo with nine small BOEs, centers on lesson study utilizing the KCJ instructional framework to promote collaborative learning in K-12 schools. The project  addresses a key challenge identified by local government s: how to implement learnercentered lessons in which diverse students c an engage. The organizational structure included a liaison council (connecting educational leaders and researchers) and subject discussion groups (consisting  of core teachers from each BOE who develop teaching materials). The liaison council met three times a year to exchange information about local initiatives and to design the overall project framework and activities.  S ubject groups met approximately four times annually through workshops, classroom-based discussions, and online exchanges. Initially, 13  skilled veteran teachers participated. Researchers collaborated as equal partners both in designing project frameworks through the liaison council and in conducting lesson study activities within  the subject groups. Table 1 shows the quantitative development of the project. When t he national grant funding the project ended at the end of fiscal year 2011, the number of BOEs temporarily decreased. However, growth soon resumed, supported by a funding model in which each participating organization contributed more equally. As of fiscal year 2025, the project involves 28 organizations—including BOEs, schools, and teacher circles. Practitioner participation now exceeds 1,000 annually. Although the basic framework (liaison councils and subject groups) remains in place, participation patterns have evolved. Most meetings are now conducted online, reducing face-toface interactions, while lesson study activities at each local site have increased. Practical research has shifted from the development of  materials by a small group  of selected teachers to school-wide lesson study initiatives. Currently, the NLP functions as an NIC centered on lesson study mediated by the  KCJ teaching method. Lesson study is conducted independently within each school or BOE, and results are shared across the network. Data and analysis Previous research on NLP lesson study focused on demonstrating the effectiveness of specific  tools (e.g. Iikubo et al., 2022; Shirouzu et al., 2023a), often overlooking the broader context of systemic change. Drawing on Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT; Engeström, 2015), we reorganized earlier findings by examining the 16-year project as an activity system. We scrutinized the elements that comprised the system, the contradictions and conflicts that arose, the solutions designed to address these problems, and the transformations these solutions introduced. Through this analysis, we document the transformation of the lesson study activity system driven by RPP design over the project ’s 16-year span from 2010 to 2025. CHAT provides a suitable framework for this division of labor. It is widely used as a paradigm for designing transformative interventions (Engeström et al.,

1996) as well as a post-fact interpretive framework (e.g., Mama & Hennessy, 2013; Grimalt-Álvaro et al., 2019).

Traditionally, Japanese lesson study followed an implicit rule of “creating original lessons independently,” with main teachers, other teachers, and researchers as implementers, critics, and advisors.  To challenge this  structure, the NLP specialized subject groups  implemented Plan-DoCheck-Act (PDCA) cycle s in collaboration with researchers under the rule of “create better lesson plans as a team.” Researchers tried to shift the object from “producing well-designed materials” to “how students learn” by focusing on student learning during the cycles. KCJ reinforced this by providing “windows into student learning” through increased student output (Miyake, 2013). However, a complete break from tradition remained elusive, as the project evolved amid tensions between new collaborative elements and entrenched individualistic norms. Phase 2: Scaling up and instrumentalizing reflection on student learning As the NLP scaled, expanded participation from core teachers to novices challenged the original structure of close collaboration between researchers and peer-level practitioners. The problem was compounded by the tendency of many teachers to focus on introducing interactive activities, while often overlooking how individual students learned. In the initial KCJ implementation, more than  60% of teachers omitted the key step of asking the same questions before and after each lesson to monitor changes in student learning (Iikubo et al., 2022).

To address these challenges and the limits of the existing division of labor, the team introduced a key instrumental intervention in 2012: a revised “reflection sheet,” prompting teachers to track  three randomly selected students ’ pre-and post-lesson answer s (Iikubo et al., 2022). T his embedded “reflection based on the 1194 learning process” into lesson study (Figure 2) via iterative cycles of design and assessment  (Bryk et al., 2011; Yeager et al, 2013). Although these efforts influenced participating teachers, their impact did not extend fully to surrounding colleagues. Phase 3: Transforming the rules and division of labor through new lesson study methods To address these conflicts, the team transformed lesson study by fostering a shared perspective on “how students learn”. In 2017, two approaches were introduced: “student learning simulations” and “Hypothesis-Testing Lesson Study (HTLS) sessions.” In simulations, teachers a cted as learners in pre-lesson meetings, shifting focus from lesson plans toward students’ learning processes; consequently, more than 60% of discussion time focused on students (Iikubo et al., 2018). In HTLS, the main teacher formulated hypotheses about the student learning process and shared them with observers in pre-lesson discussion; all teachers test them in lesson observations and reassess them in post-lesson discussions. These instruments restructured  the division of labor into a collaborative cycle where teachers alternated roles to generate and test hypotheses (Shirouzu et al., 2023a). These developments coincided with the 2017 –2018 national curriculum revision, emphasiz ing “proactive, interactive, and deep learning” and “curriculum management.” The central challenge was translating student-learning insights from interactive lessons into school-wide practice. By reshaping roles, traditional norms were gradually replaced with the principles of  “reflection based on the learning process” and “creating better lesson plans as a team” (Figure 3).

Phase 4: Responding to systemic challenges: Technology for scalability and networked learning Despite the successes of Phase 3, the effort to create  “better lesson plans” through school-based teamwork often generated professional value conflicts. Veteran teachers sometimes became frustrated with the limited expertise of younger teachers, which was exacerbated by large-scale teacher turnover. BOEs faced the challenge of helping these younger  teachers—often  hired during periods  of low selectivity —move beyond a heavy reliance on textbooks and manuals. As these young teachers struggled to adapt, some BOEs and schools deemed KCJ-based lesson study too advanced and withdrew from the project.

To address these systemic challenges, two technological instruments were fully implemented in 2020: the “Learning Note (LN),” a materials-and-discussion database, and the “Learning Recorder (LR),” a system for recording and visualizing group dialogue. LN allowed teachers to access past practices, discussions, and reflections, interpret these records, and adapt them without designing  lessons from scratch. This supported short after-school sessions in which  novice teachers drew on previous  examples (Iikubo et al., 2023). LR enabled collaborative interpretation of group dialogue. For example, a novice teacher reviewed previously unobserved dialogue with a BOE supervisor to  reassess both his teaching and student learning (Shirouzu et al., 2023 b). Government-funded design research aligned t hese technologies with national educational challenges and the project’s goals. With these tools in place, the guiding rule of lesson study shifted from “teamwork to create better lesson plans” toward a stronger emphasis on “reflection based on the learning process,” firmly centering attention on “how students learn.” LN also connected “local lesson study communities” with “online teacher networks,” allowing the broader community to function as a “Network of Networks” (Figure 4). The NLP project reflects the distinctive features of NICs, with core teachers collaborating nationwide on lesson study. However, structural contradictions  arising from the involvement of surrounding teachers in local teachercentered activities generated conflicts within the system. Curriculum revisions and evolving expectations for lesson improvement encouraged teachers to adopt a shared perspective focused on “how students learn,” creating a lesson study space for collaborative professional learning. Technological developments directly  addressed emerging challenges, aligning the project with design-based RPP principles through iterative collaborative design. Due to the nature of the  NLP, researchers cannot fully track the progress of lesson study across all individual BOEs and schools. Variations in leaders hip commitment, policy positioning, and human resources mean transformations are often limited to pro active BOEs. Unlike comprehensive DBIR approaches, the NLP’s structure allows diverse participants within a shared project structure, highlighting both a limitation and a strength: the partnership does not end when collaboration with a single organization concludes. This foundation enables new design research and further structural evolution. Through iterative collaboration between practitioners and researchers, the NLP addresses challenges associated with NIC development and demonstrates the potential of RPPs as platforms for long-term educational improvement. 1195 Barron, B.
