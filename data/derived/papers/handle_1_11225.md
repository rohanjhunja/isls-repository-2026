# Emergent Patterns of Dyadic Interaction around an Embodied Simulation

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Gesture-augmented learning environments offer unique opportunities to facilitate learning by enabling tangible physical actions. While the literature on these learning environments is well documented, there remains a need to explore its facilitations for dyads (pairs of students) working together. This study investigates the role of dyadic interactions in learning a scientific concept through an embodied computer simulation. This simulation prompts the pairs of students to manipulate scientific representations using two representational gestures and discuss their observations. We present two such unique interactions of dyads in this paper and in-depth analysis of these two cases reveals how monitoring a partner’s gestural interactions with the simulation serves as a pivotal trigger for conceptual shifts. The findings highlight how gestures and dyadic interactions foster collaborative sensemaking, providing insights into how embodiment mediates learning processes.

## Introduction

Research has shown that embodied interactions, such as gestures, can help b uild conceptual understanding by connecting physical actions to abstract ideas (e.g., Nathan, 2021). Research in embodied cognition, su ch as Thomas and Lleras (2009), suggests that directed ge stures (movement guided or prompted) can support th e acquisition of new insights. When thoughtfully integrated into designed learning environments, embodied actions have been shown to enhance student performance (Lindgren et al., 2016; Lindgren et al., 2022; Walkington et al., 2022), supporting anchoring of the abstract concepts in tangible physical actions, facilitating deeper learning. While the efficacy of such gesture-augmented learning environments is well documented for individual contexts (Mathayas, Brown & Lindgren, 2021; Johnson-Glenberg & Megowan-Romanowicz, 2017), there remains a need to explore it in collaborative setti ngs, particularly in the dyadic context. Dyads, whe re a pair of learners collaborate on tasks or share resources, a re common in practical classroom sce narios. From the sociocultural perspective, learning is a social pro cess. Vygotsky’s concept of the zone of proximal de velopment highlights the significance of social interaction in expanding learners’ content boundaries. In previous studies, we have found that a well-designed gesture augmented simulation facilitates t he learning for individuals (Mathayas et al., 201 9; Mathayas, Brown & Lindgren, 2021). Now, we can le verage the social interaction dynamics of a dyad to push on content boundaries, in an environme nt supporting directed gestures within the simulati on. In dyadic settings, joint attention-a shared focus b etween two participants serves as a foundation for coordinated actions, dialogue, and sense-making (Moore & Dunham, 1995). Embodied activities,  like gesture-augmented simulations, have the potential to further scaffold  these interactions by providing a com mon frame of reference for learners to observe, interpret, and act upon collaboratively. To explore the role directed gesture plays in a dya d interaction, we conducted a multi-case qualitative analysis of students’ knowledge development while w orking in pairs with a gesture-augmented environment to learn causal mechanisms of seasons. Our study is guided by these research questions:

1. What are the affordances of a gesture-augmented simulation for facilitating dyad meaning-making of

an earth science concept?

2. What patterns of dyad engagement with the simulation are associated with productive discourse?

The analysis of the cases offers insights on the nuances of dyadic interactions within a gesture-augmented simulation. It demonstrates how directed gestures, and dyadic interactions mediate a collaborative sen semaking process. Both the cases revealed uniqu e patterns of interaction that highlight the role o f embodied cues and joint attentions in constructing scientific knowledge. This study aims to contribute to the growing body of literature on embodied cueing, dyadic learning, and the design of collaborative STEM learning environments.

## Background

Research in the learning sciences has demonstrated that physical interaction plays a crucial role in h ow people learn, as evidenced by numerous studies on embodied learning across content areas (Lindgren et al., 2022; Danish et al., 2020; Johnson-Glenberg et al., 2016; Manches et al, 2020; Nathan, 2021). Hand gestures, in particular, have been studied in educational settings due to evidence for gestures' role in learning and problem solving (Alibali et al., 2011; Chu & Kita, 2011; Novack & Goldin-Meadow, 2015; Roth, 2001). One promising area of research has found that guiding students with cued/directed gestures can seed their nascent understanding of new concepts and ideas, suggesting that thoughtful design of such ge stures can facilitate learning (Gallagher & Lindgre n, 2015; Goldin-Meadow, 2011; Walkington et al., 2022). By directed  or cued gestures, we refer to gestures that students are prompted to perform as part of some learning ac tivity. Advances in technologies enable innovative ways of gesture cueing (Lindgren, 2015;  Huang et al., 2023) and analytic frameworks for int erpreting embodied actions in technology-facilitated learning environments are being developed (e.g., Xu et al., 2022). Most research on gesture cueing focuses on the indi vidual. However, in schools, students often work jointly on problem solving and sensemaking, raising  the question of how gesture cueing can support sha red learning processes. Joint attention, a foundationa l mechanism in collaborative learning, is establish ed when individuals share focus on an object or action, often facilitated by gaze, gestures, or verbal communication (Moore & Dunham, 1995; Tomasello, 1995). In dynamic system s research, social motor coord ination occurs when participants perceive each other’s movements, and n ot verbal interactions alone are insufficient for c oordination (Knoblich et al., 2011). The studies from action si mulation (or action perception) suggest that people  are able to predict the consequence of their own action movements more precisely than observing somebody else’s movements (see Knoblinch & Flach (2001) for dart th rowing; see Aglioti et al., (2008) for basketball).  But the literature of joint attention mostly addresses spon taneous bodily movements. The literature on cued ge stural movements, especially in dyadic setting is scarce (for example if a person is prompted to throw the da rt using a specific technique, how does it change the experien ce of the actor and observer). The learning of gesture cueing in a dyad will inform our decisions of producing practical education resources.

## Method

Stimulus tool The simulation used in this study was developed und er an NSF funded investigation-The GRASP (1) Project (GestuRe Augmented Simulations for enhancing exPlanations). The project aimed to design computer simulations to prompt students aged 11 to 13 to use hand gestures as representations while learning and explaining observable scientific phenomena. One such simulation –the seasons simulation –integrates gesture-based int eractions to support the learning of mechanisms driving season c hanges. It features three interconnected views: the Orbit-view, showing the Sun-Earth orbit; the Ground-view, showing sun rays striking a specific patch of  land; and the Earth-view, depicting the Earth as seen from the Sun (see  Figure 1a). Two key gestures control aspects of th e simulation: a single-hand gesture manipulates the angle of the sun rays (Figure 1b), while a two-hand gesture modifies the distance between the rays (Figure 1c).  Students are prompted to interact using gestures a nd observe the resulting changes in the different views, follo wed by a discussion. This enables them to connect t he simulation's visualizations and the gestural interactions with their reasoning of seasons. ## Procedure

For this study, we used the gesture-augmented computer simulation mentioned above with the leap device. The simulation and device were shared between pairs of students (dyads). The study began with individual p reinterviews to assess participants' initial understanding and their natural use of gesture to explain t he same. These were followed by the collaborative gesture direct ed simulation experience. Afterward, similar to pre-interviews, individual post-interviews were conducted to assess any conceptual shift and use of gestures to explain the concept.

Data and analysis The data corpus includes video recordings of 8 dyad s (Camera recording of them interacting with the si mulation and the screen recordings of the simulation interfa ce) from the middle school students at a public sch ool surrounding a large Midwestern universi ty. Two representative cases from the data were sel ected for this study. The choice of the cases is informed by the unique i nteraction strategies, both the dyads equip and dem onstrates how monitoring their partner’s gestural interaction s with the simulatio n acted as the trigger for the conceptual shift. We used a grounded theory approach for the v ideo analysis of the sessions, focusing on dyad collaboration/interaction and gesture-based learning dynamics during the simulation exper ience. We employed inductive coding, where we coded for participants actions with the simulation and the discourse happening while interactions (see Table 1 for the major themes emer ged from the coding). We created the CORTDRA diagra ms for both the cases (Figure 2, Figure 3) that provid es us with visualizations of participants’ interact ion patterns, sensemaking and instances of conceptual shifts over  time. To assess their knowledge of seasons occurre nce reasoning, we scored them on six key concepts of th e seasons reasoning (Table 2,  Table 4). To measure the learning gain, we used a three-point rubric based on the evidence of presence of k nowledge in their explanations (Yes (green), partial (orange), no (red)).

## Discussion

The analysis of the two cases offers insights on th e nuances of dyadic interactions within a gesture-augmented simulation. It demonstrates how directed gestures, and dyadic interactions mediate a collaborative sen se-making process. Both the cases revealed unique patterns of interaction that highlight the r ole of embodied cues and joint attention in constructing scientific knowledge.

Embodied cues and conceptual understanding Directed gestures in both the cases acted as mediat ors of developing canonical explanation of the scie ntific concept, which is consistent with theories of embod ied learning (Gallagher & Lindgren, 2015; Goldin-Meadow, 2011; Nathan, 2021). In Gavin and Jorge's case, the gestural interaction of the simulation (both one-hand and twohand) instigated a more precise articulation of the causes of season change. Though Gavin did not directly interact with the simulation himself, he attentively monitor ed Jorge’s gestures and contributed to the interpretations and verbal explanations, demonstrating a shared orienta tion toward a canonical understanding of the concep t. In the case of Abel and Percy there was a similar shift. In this case Percy’s monitoring of Abel’s gestures helped him to offer a more nuanced explanation of the phenomenon.  Percy described the “spreaded outness” of sunlight  in December and its “closeness” in June. In contrast, despite leading most of the embodied interactions, Abel remained at his initial conceptual level. This difference highlights that the performance of the cued gesture alone may not always be sufficient to scaffold advances i n understanding. Rather, it may be the attention to  the effects of the cued gesture in context –regardless of who  enacts the gesture –that determines the impact on conceptual understanding. These results suggest a need for additional research to examine the specific factors that contribute to learning from cued gestures in a dynamic social context. Dyadic interactions and joint attention Joint attention is defined as a social-cognitive skill that involves two individuals coord inating their attention toward a common object or event, facilitating share d understanding and communication (Moore & Dunham, 1995). It supports a shared focus that facilitates mutual contribution to the meaning making. For the two dyads, the quality of joint attention varied significantly. Jorge and Gavin demonstrated strong evidence of joint attention through their collaborative interactions. Although only Jorge phys ically interacted with the simulation, Gavin monitored attentively and verbally contributed to t he shared sensemaking process. The reciprocal engag ement helped both participants to build on each other's i deas. This aligns well with Gallagher’s notion of p articipatory sensemaking when joint attention is achieved through bodily movements (Gallagher, 2010). In contrast, the Abel-Percy dyad never fully succeeded in reaching full j oint attention. Though Percy's interaction with the simulation was limited, he use d Abel’s interactions as the basis for sensemaking.  However, Abel was contributing at a minimal leve l to the sensemaking, despite being the driver of p hysical engagement with the simulation. The absence of reflective actions in his engagement suggests that he was not working to align his perspective with Percy’s, thus limiting the extent to which shared sensemaking could be achieved. The failure to achieve sustained joint attention may explain th e disparity in the learning outcomes between them, with Percy moving to a canonical understanding and Abel remain ing at his initial conceptual level. One possible  direction for future study would be to more explicitly control for joint attention, such as including prompts for the students to attend to or even explain the gestures of the other student when they are not the one controlling the simulation. Likewise, t he learning environment could prompt the student ma king the gesture to “talk aloud” with their interpretation of the effects of their gestures in an effort to build a shared understanding. In all t hese potential study variations and extensions, it would be  useful to have more refined measures of joint atten tion in order to more precisely examine its effects on learning.

## Conclusion

This study highlights the role of embodied interact ions, particularly through directed gestures, in en hancing students’ understanding of scientific phenomena suc h as occurrence of seasons. By examining the dyadic interactions with a gesture-augmented simulation, in both the cases we found mo nitoring partner's gestural interactions often acted as triggers for conceptual  shifts, highlighting the importance of joint atten tion and collaborative interpretations. It suggests that it is not just the enactment of t he directed gesture, but the attention directed toward its effects within the simulation c ontext that contributes to the conceptual understanding, regardless of who performs the gesture. Learning fr om this research suggests further research to under stand how effectiveness of the directed gestures can be enhanced by nurturing partners’ ability to observe and interpret their effects in that context. This could involve incorporating prompts that encou rage students to attend to or even explain the gestures made by their partner when they are not the ones controlling the simulation. Additionally, the learning environment could prompt the student making the gesture to "talk aloud" and interpret the effects of their gestures, thereby fostering a shared understanding between the participants. In all these potential studies, it would be valuable to utilize more refined measures of joint attention to better examine its impact on learning. End notes
