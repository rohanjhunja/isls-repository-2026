# Exploring Epistemic Network Analysis Diagrams as a Tool for Developing Teacher Noticing of Classroom Dialogic Features

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Our designs for teacher learning focus on generating representations that help teachers attend to how they structure opportunities to learn for students of color. This paper explores the affordances of Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) for generating representations of epistemic networks in science class discussions for teacher reflection on their facilitation. We examine representations of data of two secondary science teachers and discuss the possibilities of ENA for mapping the intellective nature of science discussions.

Our work takes the view that equity can take shape in micro-level interactions in classroom dialogue, and teachers play a critical role in providing robust opportunities to learn science, particularly in classrooms that serve students of color. There is a growing body of research that highlights the impact of dialogic pedagogy on student learning (e.g., Clarke et al, 2015). However, large-scale surveys of classroom dialogue highlight the scarcity of these forms of pedagogy in low-performing schools that serve minoritized children (Kelly, 2008). In this paper we ask, how do we build teachers’ noticing of how their classro om discussion facilitation shapes opportunities to learn for their students? This paper proposes an amalgamation of the techniques of Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) by Shaffer et al. (2016) and Hennessey et al.’s (2015)  analytical frame for decomposing classroom dialogu e, to generate representations of classroom discussions that afford possibilities for teachers to notice (e. g., van Es & Sherin, 2006) how dialogue is deployed in their classrooms. Theoretical Frameworks ENA helps identify and quantify connections among e lements in coded data and represents them visually and statistically through network models (Shaffer et al., 2016). This is useful for data where patterns of  associations between different elements of the data may be meani ngful in illuminating the extent to which the dialo gue is academically productive. The ENA representations, therefore, offer a powerful way to help teachers develop their professional vision (Goodwin, 1994) for productive dialogue. To codify the classroom discussion data for ENA, Hennessey et al. (2015) offer a Scheme for Educational Dialogue Analysis (SEDA) which speaks directly to our interest in academically productive dialogue. Their  scheme helps classify parts of discussion data or “communicative acts” according to their dialogic function.

## Methods

ClassInSight is a study of teacher professional lea rning around classroom dialogue. It is a research-p ractice partnership with two school districts in north and south county Sandiego, which serve a diverse (ethn ic and linguistic) population of students. Science classroom discussions of two high school science teachers were audiorecorded, transcribed, and verified. These lesson transcripts were then segmented into turns and coded by a team of three researchers using a coding scheme adapted from SEDA (Hennessey et al. 2015). Each pair of cod ers achieved a Cohen’s Kappa of.7. Data were segmented into sequences using Wells and Arauz’s (2009) notion of a sequence as an intermediate unit of analysis betw een an episode and an exchange. The coded files wer e then appropriately formatted according to the recommendations of Shaffer et al., (2016) and uploaded to the  webkit. ‘Whole conversation’ was used instead of a ‘moving stanza’ because all associations within a given seq uence were meaningful.

## Discussion

Such ENA diagrams can highlight for teachers how codes co-occur in their discussions and, therefore, help them reflect on the extent to which they align with their objectives and/or on how they think discussions operate in their classroom. Hypothetically if teacher H intended to use this classroom discussion as a way to gauge stu dents’ comfort with the concept of isomers, then the extent to which they were guiding the students instead of observing them would be a cause for reconsideration of their approach to this discussion. Thus, there is ample possibility for the ENA diagrams developed by facilitators of teacher professional development to help teachers notice dialogic features of their classroom which structure the learning in it and instigate dissonance (Clarke et al., 2020) for the teachers, which could be triggers for reflection, learning, and refinements in dialogic practices.

## Conclusion

This paper sought to offer a possibility of using t he techniques of ENA and a coding scheme for classr oom dialogue to help scaffold teachers’ noticing of how dialogue operates in their classroom discussions to structure learning experiences. Further inquiry in this regar d is necessary but the approach appears to provide promising opportunities for teachers to reflect on how they u se classroom dialogue to structure students’ opport unities to learn.
