# Dynamics of Interest and Competency Belief in Coding among Middle-schoolers

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines the impact of a two-week long automated greenhouse project on middle school students' interest and competency belief in coding. This intervention involved aspects of physical computing, block-based programming, use of data, and understanding of plant science, which aligns with ideas of transdisciplinary, situated, and project-based learning. By analyzing pre-and post-surveys and interviews, we found an overall increase in students' interest. However, competency belief showed a varied pattern: it increased among students in the lower quartile but decreased among those in the higher quartile. Insights from the interviews highlighted potential improvements to the design of the project, aiming to maximize its benefits for all participants.

## Introduction

For the last five years, our research team has been involved in developing a project where middle-schoolers are introduced to physical computing through a table-top automated greenhouse (see Figure 1 a and 1b). In physical computing, both software and hardware are combined to build  interactive system s interacting with  the environment (Hodges et. al., 2020). It integrates STEM practices in technological design, scientific investigation, and engineering, enabling students to forge connect ions between the digital and physical worlds. This allows students to create tangible artifacts and solutions to tackle c omplex real-world problems  (Kelley & Knowles, 2016). In our study, students work in groups learning coding and building a “smart” automated greenhouse using low-cost Micro:bit technology coupled with plug and play sens ors (no soldering or breadboarding  is required). This reduces the complexity of physical computing and enables students to focus on how they can program sensors to monitor and control their greenhouse through dat a collection. Using a shield for Micro:bit, students can easily wire and program digital relays, control fans, ligh ts, etc. (See Figure 1a and 1b). The curriculum is available online, which can be accessed using this link: https://bit.ly/SG2023spring. In this study, we explored how students’ interest and their competency belief in coding change and how the impacts differ among the students. ## Conceptual framework

The conceptual framework guiding our study is a synthesis of existing theori es on situated and project-based learning (Krajcik & Blumenfeld, 2006; Ataizi, 2012), self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan, 2008) and self-efficacy theory (Bandura, 1997). In this project, students immerse themselves in real-world multidisciplinary project contexts, where they build an automated tab le-top greenhouse using aspects of physical computing  (See Figure 1a and 1b). As noted by the National Science Council (National Science and Technology Council, 2022), there are few exemplars of “convergence” or transdi sciplinary driven projects that teachers can use as  models in a b c the K-12 classrooms. In this work we try to engage students in learning and applying knowledge, concepts, and practices from across disciplines of science (e.g., understanding phenomena), data scienc e/mathematics (e.g., noticing and defining patterns), engineering (iterating to optimize material selections), and applying them to solve problems. This approach also aligns with the ideas of the self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan, 2008) which suggests that when students' needs for competence, autonomy, and relatedness to the content are satisfied, it can positively impact their motivation. Given Bandura’s self-efficacy theory, which emphasizes the role of perceived efficacy belief in intellectual performance  (Bandura, 1997, p. 214), students’ competency belief  in coding was measured, which provided a well-rounded understanding of the impact. Technology design Aligned with the conceptual framework described abo ve briefly, this project involves coding while connecting the program to scientific and engineering design processes. We simplify the coding process for students, many of whom are beginners, using a block-based drag-and-drop interface named Makecode (like “Scratc h”), designed for Micro:bit. (See Figure 1c). During this project, s tudents engage in challenges like keeping the green house conditions optimum for plants, using sensors and re lay switches to turn o n fan(s) or light(s) (see Figure 1b). In this way, students can investigate real-world phenomenon, testing with their greenhouse, and drawing upon a range of disciplines (such as physics, plant scienc e, engineering design, data science, computational science) to explore the driving phenomenon and answer their questions and solving the problems at hand.

## Participants

In this study, middle-school students from two semi-urban middle schools from the North-East part of the US participated in a two-week long “Smart Greenhouse” project. From the initial pool of 278 students, we selected 153 for quantitative analysis based on their consen t and assent responses and after verifying that the y completed both surveys properly  (see Table 1 for demographics). For qualitative analysis, we conducted pre-and post-interviews with 16 students, who were randomly selected based on their consent and assent responses. ## Research design

This study employed a mixed-methods approach, where quantitative findings were derived from statistical analyses of pre-and post-survey data, and qualitative insights were found from the analysis of pre-and postinterviews. We adopted this approach to understand the trends that we found from the qua ntitative analyses. For the surveys, students completed  5-point based Likert-scale questionnaires, asking about students’  Interest and Competency Belief in coding. There were six items for Interest: “I think coding is interesting. ”, “I would like to learn more about what people cando with coding.”, “I would be interested in learning more about coding.”, “I am interested in learning coding. ”, “ I would like to create new computer code. ”, and “ I am curious about how computer code works.”. On the other hand, there were six items for Competency Belief: “I can solve problems by using coding.”, “I can learn to code well. ”, “I am comfortable with learning coding. ”, “I can work on a group project that uses coding.”, “I am good at coding. ”, and “I am good at fixing computer code. ”. These items were based on the study by Rachmatullah et. al. (2020). The semi-structured interviews included a variety of questions, including these two: “Are you interested to learn more about coding?” and “Do you feel like you cando coding?”, which were associated with Interest and Competency Belief, respectively.

## Quantitative analysis

First, we conducted confirmatory factor analysis us ing the lavaan package in R to assess the extent to  which the items formed an efficient psychometric scale indica tive of a student’s Interest and Competency Belief as a latent trait. All the items from the Interest and Competency Belief scalesconsistently showed high reliability across both pre-and post-survey assessments  Model fit indices, including the Comparative Fit In dex (CFI) and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), indi cated acceptable model fit, with CFI ranging from 0.82 to 0.98 and SRMR less than.08 across scales. We the n applied a Polytomous Rating Scale Model to estima te scores for each scale. Subsequently, these scores were scaled within the interval of 0 to 5. We conducted a pairedsamples t-test to examine whether there were significant chan ges from pre-survey scores to post-survey scores. Additionally, univariate ANOVA was employed for both scales to investigate these changes across four quartiles. Qualitative research Initially, we gathered quotes associated with interest and competence belief from students’ interviews both before and after the project. Then, we analyzed and wrote summaries of their responses to compare the changes in each student's responses from pre to post. Subsequently,  we conducted descriptive coding based on these sum maries, developing a set of inductive open codes.  Initial steps of coding were done  by a single researcher and then some of the authors met to discuss those open codes to finalize thematic coding, resulting in refining these codes into a concise set, clearly delineating distinct themes.

## From quantitative analysis

A paired-samples t-test revealed a significant increase from pre-survey scores to post for the Interest scale only (t152 = 12.54, p < 0.001). However, when student gains were examined  across quartiles, we observed more significant differences. Students exhibited significant gains across all quartiles on the Interest. The F value for the quartiles one to quartile four were F(1, 74) = 12.09, F(1, 74) = 44.17, F(1, 62) = 81.80, and F(1, 88) = 48.81, where p < 0.001 for every quartile (see Figure 2a). But, the Competency Belief scale displayed a different pattern, with only students in the first quartile demonstrat ing a significant increase (F(1, 74) = 5.56, p < 0.05). Students in the second and third quartiles did not exhibit significant changes (F(1, 44) = 0.05, p = 0.83 and  F(1, 90) = 0.45, p = 0.50 for the second and third quartile, respectively), while those in the highest quartile displayed a significant decrease (F(1, 90) = 4.34, p < 0.05) (see Figure 2b).

## From qualitative analysis

In the first level of inductive coding for “ Interest”, initially, 15 open codes emerged, which were  distilled into four themes (see Table 2). For instance, a student expressed his enthusiasm to explore untaught Micro:bit features in the future. Another  student, being new to coding, found it to be interesting, likening it to “putting the puzzle pieces”. Future career concerns also played a role, as one student noted how automation may make jobs obsolete in the future, and he needed to prepare for such sc enarios. Conversely, facing challenges led to  some negative experiences for some  students, as they found coding to be confusing. But a few other students also shared  that they found coding to be easy  and interesting. For “Competency belief”, we identified 13 open codes, leading to five themes (see Table 2). Many students felt they needed help s or specific instructions, showing a lack of confidence in coding independently. Others grew more confident with increased learning experience. One student, familiar with the concepts of JavaScript, found this project's physical computing aspect more approachable, saying “...Now it's physical, and I feel I'm more competent with that…”. The difficulty of coding seemed variable among students, impacting their competence belief  positively or negatively. Similarity to familiar co ncepts, like blockbased coding compared to puzzle pieces, also influe nced students’ perception. Future challenges were acknowledged, with a student commenting on how the advanced level of coding can be harder for him. ## Discussion

From the results, i ncrease of interest in coding being  least pronounced in the first quartile and being most pronounced in the fourth quartile suggests that students with pre-existing interest in coding are likely to develop even greater enthusiasm following participation in such a project. However, it also indicates that less codinginclined students can also benefit through this. The interviews highlighted how coding being fun and easy, and a growing curiosity to explore more about coding, signficantly  impacted students’ interest. Furthermore, many students expressed their concerns about future job prospects and wondered how coding skills may benefit them in the future. These insights suggest that designing e ffective interventions should focus on making the c oding experience enjoyable and less challenging  at the beginning; highlighting potential advanced stuff  that can be explored by the advanced students; and emphasizing the relevance of coding skills in futur e job markets.  For “Competency Belief”, the pattern from the results can be partly understood through insights from the interviews. Students with less coding experience, particularly from the first quartile, reported that learning more about coding positively impacted their competency belief  in coding. However, several students, including those with h igher initial competency beliefs  in coding, mentioned requiring others’ help to code, finding it to be challenging, or being concerned about future advanced levels  of coding. This suggests that the decrease or stagn ation in competency belief among students in the higher quar tiles might be attributed to the realization of the challenges in coding and the need for assistance. To enhance c ompetency belief, a balanced approach allow ing for independent coding tasks, where students feel confi dent without constantly asking for help, may prove to beneficial. Additionally, ensuring that coding challenges are appropriately scaled to avoid overwhelming students could positively impact their perception of their o wn coding competencies.  Future work involves conducting a more rigorous qualitative analysis, involving analyze the interviews with the teachers and look at other portions of the student interviews to get a well-rounded understanding of the students’ interest and competency belief.
