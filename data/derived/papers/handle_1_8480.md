# “Do We Have to Do This Every Time New Germs Come Out?” and Other Questions From Children About Covid-19

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Uncertainty and concern marked the spring of 2020 as the Covid-19 pandemic rapidly disrupted daily life and brought up highly consequential questions about living in a public health emergency. In this paper, I present an analysis of questions that 109 caregivers of elementary schoolers reported receiving from their children about the early pandemic. Understanding what questions children were asking of their caregivers and how the caregivers respond to their questions helps reveal both children’s informational needs and caregiver approaches to handling those questions. Findings indicate the salience of questions about daily life and highlight the importance of the affective valence of the questions and responses.

## Introduction

The start of the Covid-19 pandemic was a massive disruption that introduced new, highly consequential questions for people around the world. Parents and other caregive rs of young children found themselves in the tricky  position of answering questions about daily life that did not necessarily have clear-cut answers. “What is the coronavirus?,” “Will we have school next year?,” “Do we have covid?,” “Will you die?,” and “Did China make the virus?” are all examples of questions from children that reflect the uncertainty of pandemic while varying in the level of factual and emotional difficulty. For some caregivers in the U.S., the pa ndemic may have been the first time they were confr onted with questions from their children that they were not sure how to respond to. Others may have had more practice fielding difficult questions, but not in times as uncertain as the beginning of the Covid-19 pandemic. Asking questions is a purposeful information-seekin g mechanism that children use to respond to and understand the events in their lives (Chouinard et al., 2007). Research in child development suggests that questions are sparked by “first-person cues (such as novelty or surprise), third-person cues (such as a knowledg eable adults’ surprise or question), and future-oriented cues (such as expectations about information gain or future value)” (Linquin & Lombrozo, 2020, p. 14). As children encounter con cepts and ideas for which they do not have direct e vidence or experience, such as gravity or the existence of a God, they depend on more knowledgeable others (Vygotsky, 1978) for testimony. Through these processes, children learn ways of knowing and questioning that are culturally salient to their communities in interactions with their caregivers (Callanan et al., 2012; Menendez, 2020; Crowley et al., 2001; Sak & Shain-Sak, 2020). The dynamics around question asking and responses are complicated, yet they provide insight into the norms of communities and how they mediate information. Specifically with regards to the Covid-19 pandemic, understanding what questions children were asking and the responses from the caregivers to those question s help us understand the children’s information nee ds and how caregivers supported the mental, physical, and emotional well-being of their children in adjusting to the pandemic. An early study in June 2020 by Nelson and colleagues (2020) surveyed a sample of caregivers of 5-10 year-old listeners to the science education podcast BrainsOn! found that 97% of the caregivers reported their children asking a question about the coronavirus, and 62% indicated that they were “very uncomfortable” answering questions their children had about the coronavirus. Nelson and colleagues (2020) also found that 68% of caregivers said their children expressed worry or fear about the pandemic, primarily about loved ones catching the virus and the uncertainty of how long the pandemic would disrupt their lives. This paper pres ents a complimentary analysis of elementary school-aged children’s questions close in time tonelson and colleagues’ study (May 2020). Examining children’s questions from early in the pandemic reminds us that our youngest participants in communities are paying attention to the forces that structure their routines and are concerned for the well-being of their communities.

## Methods

This paper presents preliminary findings from an analysis of questions caregivers reported hearing from their children. I ask, what can we learn about children’s information needs in times of crisis from the questions they  asked of their caregivers about Covid-19? To explore this question, I describe the topics of questions that caregivers reported their ICLS2022 Proceedings 1333 © ISLS children asking about the pandemic and offer examples of conversations caregivers had with their children about the major categories of questions that emerged from the  analysis. The data used in this analysis is part o f a larger fivepart diary study conducted with 109 families of ele mentary school children from across the U.S. in May  2020 (c.f. Barron et al, 2021; Pozos et al, 2022). The caregivers were recruited through the dscout platform which was also used for multimodal data collection via smartphone. They lived in 28 states and were mostly women (67%) with children in public elementary schools (84%). Racially, 55% self-identified as white, 16% as Black, and 15% as Latinx, 9% as Asian, and 4% as Middle Eastern or North African. S lightly over half (55%) of the caregivers reported an annual household income of at or above the national median income of $75k. In one part of the study focused on learning about  Covid-19, the caregivers responded to a series of prompts eliciting reflections on the questions their children were asking, conversations they were having, what resources they were using to learn about Covid-19, and any challen ges they faced in communicating with their children  about the pandemic. We collected two sets of child-initiated questions: general questions about the pandemic and  questions about the pandemic that the caregivers found diffic ult to answer. Both sets of questions were coded us ing the same codebook consisting of deductive drawn from Chouinard et al. (2007) and Nelson et al. (2020) and induc tive codes generated from open-coding (Saldaña, 2013). Inter-rater agreement was calculated to be 0.88 using Cohen’s Kappa, indicating strong agreement (McHugh, 2012).

## Findings

Most frequently asked questions point to daily concerns In total, 353 examples of general questions were coded (M = 3.0, S  = 2.0) Seven caregivers did not offer an example question in their response or stated only that thei r children were not asking questions (five also sta ted the latter, but also offered examples of questions.) Overall, 75.2%  of the caregivers gave an example of at least one question that was coded as one of the four most common topics (school, impact on self, length of the pandemic, changes in routine, see Table 1.) A few significant differences were found between families with higher/lower incomes. Children from higher income families were more likely to ask questions about impacts on themselves (impact on self, X2 = 5.825, p = 0.015). Children of lower income families were more likely to ask about the nature of the virus (X2 = 7.568, p = 0.006). Girls were more likely to ask about virus transmission (X2 = 11.293, p = 0.004). Any further interpretation as to why these differences may have been significant would be spec ulative. However, it is interesting to note that th ere were few significant effects within the categories that make sense to run those tests on (i.e., there are enough questions included in those participant groups to make a Chi-Square meaningful). Importantly, the data do not indicate differences in how caregivers were responding to girls compared to boys, a pattern that has been documented in multiple s tudies about sensitive topics (e.g., sex; Sak & Sahin-Sak, 2020) and science talk (e.g., Crowley et al., 2001). Again, the most frequently asked questions were about how long the pandemic would last (n=29), how the pandemic restrictions were impacting them personally (n=13), and when schools would reopen/ how they would operate (n=9). Caregivers found questions that did not have specific answers at tha t point in the pandemic, questions that they were u nsure about themselves, and questions to which they had an emotional reaction the most difficult to answer. As one mother of an 8 year-old daughter commented, “It's hard telling children their [sic] is no cure for something that has killed so many and has affected thousands of people.” A father of a 6 year-old son confesses that he responds to his son’s question about  if they will all be safe, “I say yes but its [sic] not something I can garuntee [sic] and I feel a little poorly when I say it.” Another father of a kindergartener wonde red if his son would still trust him as an informat ion source after not having answers: “to be honest I would not trust us either, just because we just don’t have a lot of answers to critical questions in his mind.” The caregivers’ reflections  on their responses to their children’s questions, especially their young children, show concern for their mental wellbeing and also conflict as their desire to protect their children meet the reality of the all-consuming nature of the pandemic in the early months.

## Discussion & directions for future research

In May 2020, children were most concerned about how  the virus was going to impact them personally as t hey experienced significant disruptions in their routines, especially concerning school. However, they also showed interest in knowing about the virus itself and how the pandemic would shape the future. Some of the questions were sparked by disruptions in routines, while others came from what children were hearing through the media or from friends and family. The reflections caregivers shared on questi ons they found difficult show discomfort with the q uestions that did not have concrete answers. The lack of concrete answers sometimes meant that children kept asking, as they are known to do when they want to check in case an answer has changed (Linquin & Lombrozo, 2020). Though this study did not ask caregivers to record the frequency with  which their children asked each question, many car egivers volunteered that they regularly responded to the sa me questions from their children. For some this may  have been their first experience of not being able to be the expert or authority on a topic that was consequenti al for the health and well-bring of their children. This raises an interesting issue for learning and media designers about what children’s information needs actually are and how to meet them. Emotion regulation and the knowledge frameworks t hat the caregivers draw on to offer reassurance shape the h ome learning environment and implicitly teach children ways of knowing and questioning during emotionally difficult times. What do children need to know about sensit ive topics, like severe illness and death, if people in their s phere of influence are not being directly impacted?  How should we ICLS2022 Proceedings 1335 © ISLS think about the potential differences between what the caregivers are comfortable answering and what q uestions children have that caregivers might not have answers to or feel that they cannot be completely honest about? As the Covid-19 pandemic continues to unfold, new q uestions emerge, and old ones are revisited as new scientific studies are released and political battl es shape discourse about health and social responsi bility. As new developments occurred, the children likely began to ask different questions based on the national news  (e.g., maskwearing controversies, vaccine approvals and hesitation), as Nelson and colleagues (2021) found in the ir follow-up study. Further research might continue to explore the role of children’s questions about health topics and caregivers’ affective responses in the development of health literacy and decision making.
