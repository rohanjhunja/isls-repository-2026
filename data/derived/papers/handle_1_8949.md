# Cognitive Apprenticeship: A New Lens for Better Understanding Online Doctoral Provision

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
By and large, the specific characteristics of online doctoral education is unknown. This qualitative study is the first attempt to unpack what it means to be an online doctoral student. I employ the concept of cognitive apprenticeship to help us better understand the individual trajectories of students in online programs. The data show that when the technological affordances are coupled with the structural design, doctoral students in online programmes seem to encounter further challenges that hinder their development as scholars. This research provides both theoretical and practical accounts for further discussion and debate on online doctoral provision in general and doctoral studentsÕ experience in these programmes in specific. It concludes that rethinking the theory and practice of online doctoral provision in relation to the concept of cognitive apprenticeship can provide more nuanced means for understanding the specific characteristics of online doctoral provision.

Research Background Doctoral experience is a Òjourney from apprentice or novice to master (Bastalich, 2017, p. 1146), during which Òthe successful student will typically develop from a state of relative dependency to competent autonomy over the period of candidatureÓ (Gurr, 2001, p. 85). Here, I want t o understand one fundamental component necessary fo r this transition: apprenticeship. In specific, I want to understand the individual trajectories of online PhD students through the lens of cognitive apprenticeship. Whilst appren ticeship is hardly a new concept for doctoral educa tion (Jones, 2013), research on this topic limited its scope wit h explaining supervisory relationships. I propose t o utilise the concept of apprenticeship to understand doctoral ex perience, particularly the process of enculturation  of online doctoral students.

As a natural way to learn, Òapprenticeship was the vehicle for transmitting the knowledge required for expert practiceÓ (Collins, Brown, & Holum, 1991, p. 6). It is possible to extend this definition to doctoral education: students acquire sophisticated research skills or practical tacit knowledge (e.g., interview skills, research ethics) through their interactions with their supervisors and other exper ienced researchers, as well as their peers. It is t hrough this relationship that discipline-specific knowledge is communicated, utilised, and cultivated. This type of apprenticeship, however, often operates at the cognitive level. I employ the concept of cognitive apprenticeship to explore both the implicit and explicit processes by which online doctoral students can acquire the knowledge and skills necessary for scholarship. Research I report in this manuscript i s concerned with one of the four dimensions of the cognitive apprenticeship framework: sociology.

## Theoretical Framework: Doctoral Education as Cognitive Apprenticeship

Research has long shown that apprenticeship is one powerful framework for conceptualising learning imp licit knowledge and skills (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Accordi ng to this framework, learning occurs through vario us apprenticeship arrangements through a relational pr ocess of legitimate peripheral participation, sugge sting that Òactivities, tasks, functions, and understandings do not exist in isolation; they are part of broader systems of relations in which they have meaning. These systems of relations arise out of and are reproduced and developed within social communitiesÓ (Lave & Wenger, 1991, p. 53). In this model, learning is about acquiring domain-specific methods through a combination of observation, coaching, and practice (Lave, 1991). The concept of cognitive apprenticeship is built on these principles, but it goes beyond practical ski lls and focuses on cognitive skills. Cognitive apprenticesh ip is concerned with knowledge required for experti se: not only learning subject matter specific concepts, facts, and procedures but also acquiring nuanced knowledge about how to learn and when to apply, new concepts, facts, and p rocedures. Two major differences between cognitive apprenticeship and traditional apprenticeship should be noted here. First, in traditional apprenticeship, the demands of the workplace define the nature of the relations hip between the apprentice and the master, not the concerns with pedagogical scaffolding; whereas, in cognitive apprenticeship, problems are designed with increasing complexity to demonstrate the skills or techniques required for the task. Second, traditional apprenticeship is context specific whilst cognitive apprenticeship prioritises generalizing knowledge. These two major differences operate to produce different relationships; that is, Òindividual learners and gr oups of learners navigate complex matrices of inter secting ICLS2022 Proceedings 1437 © ISLS sociomaterial and epistemic practicesÓ differently (Simmons, Parchoma, & Koole, 2018, p. 1).

## Research Question

How and what are the ways in which the patterns of relations performed and recursively emergent within the sociomaterial environment of online doctoral courses?

## Research Design

This phenomenological study was conducted in five different online doctoral programmes in two different researchintensive public universities based in the UK and C anada. I only focused on the students who were clos e to the completion of their thesis project or recent graduates of the programmes within the past five years per discussed above in the introduction.

I employed the descriptive and critical discourse analysis in order to construct meanings across participants (Fairclough, 2003). Several meanings emerged through this process and I combined, compared, and contrasted them in order to create more comprehensive yet more distinct meanings.

## Findings

There are four characteristics affecting the sociol ogy of cognitive apprenticeship: situated learning,  community of practice, intrinsic motivation, and exploiting coop eration (Collins et al., 1991). Here, I present the  findings with respect to these four categories.

Situated Learning The data suggest that sense of support and being ab le to personalise the tasks were deemed essential f or situated learning in online doctoral programmes. For example, Judith, a fourth year PhD student, stated that she felt that the instructor and the members of the cohort did a great job of supporting each other and she felt especially Òreally got a lot out of the [synchronous] collaborative sessions Ó. Till found it easy to use the synchronous and as ynchronous communication. For him, the text and multimedia-bas ed broadcast resources were Òessential for completi ng assignmentsÓ. Bruce stated that he appreciated the ability to Òchat about the assignments using the au dio [synchronous] featureÓ with both peers and the inst ructor. Maggy particularly valued opportunities to Òtalk in real time via ongoing [asynchronous] text and periodic [synchronous] audioÓ with both her peers and the ins tructor provided within the course website. Sherry stated t hat she wanted to Òtalk with the instructor and the  scheduled synchronous sessions provided those opportunitiesÓ. Elaine echoed this sentiment and went on to say, t hat Ò2-way communicationsÉ during the live [synchronous] session(s) was easyÓ. These accounts are important: if students are to understand the purposes or uses of the knowledge they are learning, they should be able to Òlearn by actively using knowledge rather than passively receiving itÓ (Collins et al., 1991, p. 45). I was, however, not able to find evidence that students acquire knowledge in a dual form, both tied to the contexts of its uses and independent of any particular context. The abstraction of knowledge is an important goal for situated learning: the unbinding of knowledge from a specific context fosters its transfer tonew proble ms and new domains. It is this fluidity of the patt erns of relations that allows movement of knowledge depending on the emergent characteristics/membership of the assemblage. Community of Practice The sense of community of practice was strong; however, the sense of isolation caused by the impersonal nature of online spaces seems to dissolve relatively strong s ocial ties that may have developed earlier in the p rogramme. For example, Josh, a fourth year Ph.D. student explained how he failed to continue these relationships: we had the residential [meeting] in the first year, we had the residential [meeting] in the second year É Because we felt that we really wanted to maintain that sense of community and it was actually really important to us. So, we tried to ex tend it as long as we could, and then we all went off after the residential [meeting]É. we were really motivated. And then it all sort of started to [fade] unfortunately.

For Josh, sense of community was important but despite their best efforts, they failed to maintain these relationships: I think the community aspect just sort of dissipate d really, and having spoken to a few people in previous cohorts, I think they felt the same thi ng. How do you maintain a sense of community? I donÕt know, I É would have managed to sustain it, it would have been nice because we had a really strong sense of community, but we couldnÕt find a way [to maintain it]. When the patterns of relations within the assemblag e are not maintained, students can struggle with de veloping a sense of personal investment and mutual dependency.  Along with time, geographical proximity emerged as  an important factor for online doctoral studentsÕ socialisation. For Russell, a fifth year Ph.D. student, physical distance ICLS2022 Proceedings 1438 © ISLS was played a role in his attempts to keep the social ties and connections: thereÕs one of my cohort whoÕs here in [my country], and the beginning of [thesis work] we still help each other out and chat. I think like itÕs good if you have someone geographically sort of close, which is really, really helpful.

However, whether it is due to time constrains or ge ographical proximity, individual attempts to promote a sense of community of practice seemed to have a short life span. Unfortunately, students seemed to internalise and normalise the sense of isolation for their development as a researcher or scholar. In fact, the personal nature of the doctoral thesis was the main theme in their explanation of the thesis process.

Intrinsic Motivation The data show that students were able to comprehend their position shifted within the pattern of relations. Participants in this research unanimously acknowledged learning academic writing as the catalyser for the transition from studentlike mindset to developing their own identity as a scholar. I was able to find traces of intrinsic motivation in our data, albeit scarce and weak. Angela, a recent graduate, for example, explained that: Learning to write academically and learning to [give] feedback was very important to me. The process of doing it; looking at other peopleÕs work was very interesting. I learned my peers have very different perspective. The most important thin g I learned is that you can you can argue anything; you just have to explain why.

When Angela was asked to articulate as to why she i dentified learning academic writing skills was an i mportant process for her, she continued: Learning how to write helped me so much into becomi ng an independent researcher and also someone who can É um, who is able to defend her position. My self-confidence was just, yeah, that has changed completely. Now I see PhD as something sensible to achieve. I managed to get something published last year and there was a great sense of achievement in it. AngelaÕs accounts can explain how doctoral students internalise their role expectations through the integration of the self and the professional role. ÒIndividual and soc ial roles, personalities and social structures beco me fusedÓ and students Òform a professional identityÓ (Weidman, T wale, & Stein, 2001, p. 14). Students, then, can id entify themselves as full members of academic or professional community. In terms of sociology of cognitive apprenticeship, this means that a new identification emerges out of a new pattern of relationships. Exploiting cooperation Whilst the asynchronous text-based group discussion s were intended to support triads of students in pe er review processes, these fora were not strictly private. Anyone in the class could read and even respond to posted draft work. This design within the course was intended to be an  optional way for individual learners to engage wit h variety of their peers in order to gain insights into what additional peers in the course were doing and discussing. Maggy noted that the group discussion provided opportunities to Òget feedbackÓ on draft work and Òoffer feedback on othersÕÓ draft work, which resulted in her Òusing the [assessment] rubrics several timesÓ and this process provided opportunities to also check on Òother peopleÕs feedbackÓ. Till comme nted that he was Òable to understand and learn some  new principles for supporting others' learningÓ. These accounts indicate that the online space provide flexible patterns of relations that can support group work. Scarcity of opportunities for cooperation, however, suggest tha t these opportunities are not inherent in online doctoral provision. Peer and instructor feedback both in the asynchrono us threads and during synchronous sessions provided opportunities for students to review their peersÕ sets of expertise. For example, Maggy noted that she Òlearned a lot from othersÓ. Jenny noted that engaging in the course tasks Òintroduced us to a variety of skills that could be applied to support learners in many different settingsÓ. Sherry remarked that she became a proficient multimedia developer: Ònot only did I learn how to create using different programs I feel I learned how to learn new programs/techniquesÓ. SherryÕs articulation is important to note because it shows that the online component of the assemblage played a role in her identity formation.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Doctoral experience can be understood as Ònetworks of learning relationshipsÓ, whereby students are positioned Òat the centre of a constellation of othersÓ (Cullen et al. (1994) as cited in Boud & Lee, 2005, p. 502). Precisely because the contextual and situated nature of this experience, doctoral education, particularly in online programmes, requires different level of understanding and commitment depending on individual needs, goals, profession, and the nature of ICLS2022 Proceedings 1439 © ISLS the discipline. Cognitive apprenticeship, I believe, is the most productive theoretical framework to u nderstand and appreciate these nuances: it is a useful paradigm to explicate and instil the notion of scholarship to doctoral students rather than a packaged formula for teaching. The le vel of expert support doctoral students receive should gradually reduce as they move from peripheral participants to  central members; and, completely fade when doctora l students slowly become independent scholars. Data I reported in this manuscript explains this process. I can provide some key observations for this process in online doctoral programmes: 1) the choice of tools (and other elements of the assemblage) permits a variety of patterns of relations; and, 2) as learners engage with the assemblage, their needs, desires, and activities co-structure the assemblage, and as such, the develop ment of broad and/or sub-groups emerge. This process, nevertheless, as the data show, does not naturally occur in on line doctoral programmes. In fact, it is evident in the data that the lack of scaffolding for apprenticeship appears to be disconcerting: as the nature of the assemblage shift, the identity of doctoral students shifts; however, this transference is not enough for students to identify themselves as a scholar. Doctoral students need a sense of community of practice; in specific, they need a cognitive scaffolding for this metamorp hosis to occur. Our challenge, then, is to inscribe  potentialities into programme designs to allow for multiple assemblage formations that can help with situating different learners in the complex virtual continuum.

Data of this research show that structural elements  inherent in online doctoral programmes occasionall y support the characteristics affecting the sociology of cognitive apprenticeship. Participants in this research confirmed that some opportunities exist for situated learning to occur and community of practice to be formed; however, these are not pedagogically designed elements of teaching /learning but serendipitous experiences. Opportunit ies for intrinsic motivation and exploiting collaboration, on the other hand, are poor and short. Taken altogether, the findings convincingly show that students lack enculturation in the principles of cognitive apprenticeship. This  lack of enculturation is manifest in how they internalised their isolation as a natural process inherent in the thesis work. The result is that they are inaccurately equating the unstructured and personal nature of the thesis work with the experience of working alone which has resulted from the lack of program structure at this phase of the doctoral journey. Overall, there is a lack of knowledge and frameworks for understanding online doctoral provision. I suggest a shift in the research agenda concerning this issu e. Perhaps, the first step towards a more effective  direction is to focus less on quantitative measures for success, li ke enrolment statistics or graduation rate but rath er to employ qualitative judgements for the evolution of the doctoral experience. What might be the guidelines for such qualitative approaches? Here, I explored one option: cognitive apprenticeship. According to this perspective, doctoral education is not just about completing a series of coursework followed by a thesis work; rather, it is a transformation from that of a knowledge consumer to a knowledge producer. I believe cognitive apprenticeship can be utilised as a framework for studying this metamorphosis.

The infancy of research agenda for understanding doctoral experience as cognitive apprenticeship warrants that I should be cautious with the conclusions. However, it is evident that we have to rethink the theory and practice of online doctoral provision. It can be argued that when the technological affordances are coupled with the structural design, doctoral students in online programmes seem to encounter further challenges that hinder their development as scholars.
