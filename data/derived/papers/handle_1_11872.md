# How Students Ground Mathematics Through Distributed Gestures: Designing Computer-Supported Collaborative Embodied Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We explored how students interact with a computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) system for embodied learning in geometry. With The Hidden Village-Online (THV-O), fifteen high school students (six dyads, one triad) used movements to construct directed actions that depict geometry transformations collaboratively. THV-O is a web-based CSCL video game that uses a motion-capture to detect players’ movements, save movements they create, and play those created by their peers. By observing dyads’ co-design processes and semi-structured reflections, researchers found two important shortcomings in the design of THV-O for embodied learning: (1) players experienced difficulty in conveying geometric transformations through poses, which could hinder their ability to represent generalizable properties of space and shape; and (2) players faced challenges in jointly co-constructing a single mathematical object, limiting their ability to collaborate actively and develop shared mathematical ideas. We offer guidelines for an improved design of embodied collaborative reasoning and learning.

## Introduction

Theories of embodied cognition suggest that the bod y playing a central role in shaping cognition (e.g., Wilson, 2002). Gestures—spontaneous hand movements often accompanying speech—are particularly powerful tools for reasoning across domains, including mathematics (Al ibali et al., 2011). Gestures have been shown to re veal information not expressed in speech, correlate with conceptual performance, and provide actionable ideas (Goldin-Meadow et al., 2009; Nathan et al., 2014). Gestures  can also bridge gaps in students’ understanding  and support logical reasoning (Chapin et al., 2009). However, details of implementing embodied learning—especially the facilitative role of gesture—are still making their way from research to classroom practice. Previous research demonstrates that action-based interventions using Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) systems can effectively support mathematical learning (Abrahamson & Sánchez-García, 2016). The Hidden Village-Online (THV-O; Fogel et al., 2022, 2023; Nathan & Walkington, 2017), a motion-capture video game, is one such CSCL system designed for em bodied geometry education. THV-O instructs students to create and perform directed actions (DAs)–physical movements that students are directed to perform (Thomas & Lleras, 2009)–relevant to geometric conjectures while providing v erbal explanations of their reasoning process. This approach grounds geometric concepts when DAs are co-articulated with speech. Prior evidence supports the effectiveness of THV-O in fostering foundational mathematical insights t hat lead to more complex understandings, such as logical, operative, and generalizable proofs (Fogel et al., 2022, 2023). Moreover, studies using the non-online version of THV by Walkington and colleagues (2019) found that collaborative gesture production significantly enha nced students’ mathematical reasoning compared to n oncollaborative gesture production. Distributing and extending body-based resources across multiple players, combined with tools like motion capture and visuali zation in THV-O, highlights additional ways in which embodiment facilitates cognition. However, to fully support collaborative embodied learning, CSCL systems like THV-O must effectively accommodate students’ abilities to co-create body-based representations of conceptual knowledge. To address these considerations, this study examines how students collaboratively design DAs using THV-O, exploring how they ground mathematical ideas thr ough their speech, gestures, and the DAs as enactive and extended representations of geometric objects and their transformations.

## Theoretical framework

Grounding mathematical ideas in gesture Gestures are a powerful form of bodily action that convey and influence cognition. Gestures simulate actions (Hostetter & Alibali, 2019) that can emphasize spat ial-motoric information (Kita et al., 2017).   In turn, these gestures can be used pedagogically as intentional movements that facilitate the conceptualization of mathematical transformations (Nathan & Walkington, 2017; Walkington et al., 2022). Representational gestures, which depict objects and  processes, reflect students’ mental simulations (Alibali & Nathan, 2012). Representational gestures  can be dynamic or non-dynamically depictive (Garcia & Infante, 2012).  Dynamic gestures simulate transformational operatio ns (e.g., dilation, skewing, scaling) to generalize properties of shapes and space—such as tilting a parallelogram to show area invariance. Non-dynamic gestures represent static properties of geometric o bjects (e.g., shapes) or mathematical symbols. Nath an and colleagues (2022) found dynamic gestures were linked to deeper insigh ts and valid proofs, while non-dynamic gestures supported correct intuitions. Experts used  dynamic gestures more frequently (79%) than non-experts (52%). Since transformational operations are key to unders tanding mathematical continuity (Núñez et al., 1999), dynamic gestures help ground abstract concepts in perception and action. Collaborative gestures for mathematical reasoning Collaborative gestures are co-constructed body movements used during social learn ing (Walkington et al., 2019).  These gestures distribute mathematical reasoning across l earners and support shared representations. Walkington and colleagues (2019) identified four types of collaborative gestures during mathematical reasoning:

(1) Echo, (2) Mirror, (3) Alternate, and (4) Joint gestures. Learners may repeat others’ gestures by e choing or

mirroring, or build upon each other’s gestures by alternating, or physically co-construct a joint gesture. Research shows collaborative gestures are associated with higher performance in proofs (Walkington et al., 2019). Among these, joint gestures distribute cognition across individuals  (Walkington et al., 2019), enabling learners to co-construct ideas (joint sense-making) and reduce cognitive load.

## Research question

Prior research shows that dynamic gestures support grounded conceptualization of continuity and transformational operations, while joint gestures foster collaborative sense-making and distributed reasoning. Building on this, we ask: How do students collaboratively design directed act ions (DAs) for their peers within a CSCL system ? We focus on students’ collaborative design of gestures to represent generalizable properties of geometric objects.

## Participants and procedure

Fifteen high school students in a summer psychology  course participated in this in situ study. Students were randomly assigned to seven groups (six dyads and on e triad) across morning and afternoon sessions.  The study included three phases. First, in a 20-minute co-design session, groups collaboratively created directed actions (DAs)—three poses (start, intermediate, end)—to represent transformations for a given geometry conjecture using THV-O. Second, in a 20-minute play session, groups used THV-O to perform DAs for three different conjectures created by peers. Third, a 30-minute reflection session  prompted group discussions on their co-design and gameplay experiences. Video data of speech and gestures from all three sessions were transcribed and coded. To examine how students collaboratively reasoned and d esigned DAs, the analysis focused on their dialogue  and gestures during the co-design session and their reflections on the process. Materials: The Hidden Village-Online The Hidden Village-Online (THV-O) is a web-based motion-capture game for embodied geometry learning. In Edit mode (see Figure 1), players (1a) create new conjectures, (1b) design DAs by setting start, intermediate, and end poses using avatars that simulate body movement s in real-time, and (1c) test the DA’s calibration and adjust tolerance levels. Completed DAs are stored in the online database (Google Firebase) and are accessible for other users to play. In Play mode (see Figure 2), players (2a) perform the DAs by matching the poses in sequence, then (2b) evaluate the truth or falsehood of the conjecture, and (2c) provide a verbal explanation of their reasoning. Notable in Figure 3 is that most student groups (71.4%) focused primarily on representing static elements (marked in black bold) rather than transformational operations (marked in red bold). In the reflection session, a student in Group F poi nted out the discontinuity of DAs while discussing Group G’s DA (see Figure 3): “Thinking back on it, I guess moving from this [sta rt pose] to this [end pose] makes sense because it gets bigger, but at the moment, I didn’t  understand it, especially this [intermediate pose]. I didn’t see how it connected to the other poses.” The difficulty in conceptualizing the continuity of  mathematical transformations was also mentioned by many students, recognizing only static elements that the  individual DA poses represented, such as object shape, but fell short of connecting the stat ic elements together to reason the given geometric conjecture. One student commented, “It was more like complex id ea [complete representations of geometric shapes], and I needed to imagine  [the transformational operations].” These findings highlight the need to encourage students to design dynamic DAs that include not only static elements but also transformational operations to represent the continuity of mathematical conjectures. Difficulty of creating fine motor movements and joint actions During the DA co-design process, most groups naturally created joint  DAs (Figure 4). Initially, all groups used fine motor movements to represent static elements —Group B a triangle, Group C a rectangle, and Group E intersecting lines —establishing shared understanding through detailed hand gestures. While fine motor articulation offers precision, many students struggled to get these movemen ts recognized by the motion-capture system. As a result, they shifted to gross motor actions involving arms and torsos, which evolved into joint, multiperson gestures. Reflecting on this process, a stud ent from Group C noted, “We tried doing it this way  (forming a rectangle with fingers), but it wouldn’t capture the exact shape.” Currently, THV-O only supports single-person body detection, preventing groups from storing joint DAs for gameplay. In reflection sessions, students expr essed frustration and a desire for multi-person detection. ## Discussion

This study explored how students collaboratively design directed actions (DAs) for peers in a CSCL system. First, most student-designed DAs represented static elements rather tha n transformational operations. This was tied to the system’s design, which captures three discrete poses rather than continuous movement. As a result,  students often failed to recognize  the need to construct a continuous transformation, limiting their engagement with key geometric concepts and reducing the clarity and com municative power of their DAs.  Second, we observed a common pattern in the co-design process: students began with fine motor actions (e.g., using hands to form shapes) but struggled with detection, leading them to switc h to gross motor and ultimately joint actions. This shift highlights two critical limitations: (1) the system’s difficulty capturing fine motor detail at varying distances, and

(2) its lack of support for multi-person representations. To address these, future ve rsions of THV-O should offer

clearer po sitional guidance and enhanced computer vision capa bilities for multi-user detection, supporting the creation of extended and distributed embodied representations  (Walkington et al., 2019).  This study offers a focused but limited perspective on CSCL systems for  embodied learning in advanced high school geometry. By examining how students co-construct dynamic embodied representations, it prov ides key insights into the collaborative processes i nvolved in representing abstract mathematical ideas. These findings highlight the importance of designing CSCL tools that (1) encoura ge dynamic and continuous gestures, and (2) enable distributed and shared representations through joint actions.

The study’s limitations include a small, self-selected sample of 15 summer course students, who m ay have been more inclined toward collaboration than t ypical learners. Additionally, all groups worked wi th seven pre-designed conjectures developed by the THV-O team. Future studies that allow students to creat e their own conjectures and include more diverse participants may yield different insights. Despite its limitations, this study provides valuab le insights into the design of collaborative embodi ed learning systems. It underscores key considerations  for future CSCL research on distributed and extend ed representations, especially how students collaborat ively represent abstract mathematical ideas. By exa mining group reasoning, the study reveals factors that sha pe students’ understanding of generalized geometric  concepts and highlights when collaboration is supported or constrained  by system design. These findings are crucial for addressing gaps between designers’ intentions and students’ actual experiences.
