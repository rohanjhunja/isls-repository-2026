# From Body to Brain: Using Coordination Class Theory and Neuroimaging to Understand Embodied Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Interest has grown in understanding how embodied learning experiences support students’ learning of complex science concepts. Grounded in coordination class theory, this study used functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) to investigate the neural res ources students recruit when translating between representations of a science concept after learning in an embodied environment that integrates haptic and gesture-controlled technologies. Results showed that, during the representational translation task, students engaged more frontal regions when translating from diagrams to graphs than from graphs to diagrams, reflecting the greater cognitive demand of extracting information from representations with complex visual features. However, after embodied learning experiences, students relied more on sensorimotor than frontal regions when translating across representations, regardless of task type. Moreover, high-performing students exhibited greater activation in sensorimotor areas than lower-performing peers, suggesting that effective learners may draw more extensively on embodied resources. Together, these findings suggest that embodied learning enables students to coordinate reasoning with sensorimotor systems when extracting information and generating inferences about physical quantities.

Research on human cognition has increasingly moved beyond treating it as an activity confined to the head. Today, we emphasize instead its situated nature and the importance of context (Hickey, 2022). Coordination Class Theory exemplifies this perspective by conceptualizing how people learn and solve problems through the extraction  of information across different representational forms and modalities, and the coordination of these elements to make relevant inferences (diSessa & Sherin, 1998). We argue that embodied cognition complements this theory of concepts by showing that bodily engagement can facilitate such processes, as the human body is intertwined with cognition (Barsalou, 2008; Glenberg, 2010; Clark, 1999; Gallagher, 2005; Lindgren & Johnson-Glenberg, 2013). For instance, people frequently move their bodies while thinking and reasoning—manipulating objects, gesturing with their hands, or moving their whole body to act out an idea. Such movements have been shown to directly support cognition and learning (Goldin-Meadow, 2003).

However, the specific mechanisms through which embodied experiences support science students’ perceptual extraction and inference generation remains unclear. Neuroimaging techniques provide critical insights into underlying cognitive processes that behavioral methods alone cannot capture. In this study, we use a neuroimaging approach to investigate how embodied experiences support students’ learning in science by examining the neural resources they recruit after learning in an embodied environment. Coordination class theory in science learning Coordination class theory (CCT) sharpens our understanding of learning and conceptual change. Specifically, it focuses on operationalizing the broad idea of a ‘concept’ as the ways that learners go about ‘ seeing’ a given concept in the world (diSessa & Sherin, 1998). The theory builds on the knowledge-in-pieces (KiP) perspective which posited that cognitive structures are not large, stable theories but instead are comprised of many fine-grained pieces that are dynamically coordinated and highly context-dependent (diSessa, 1993). CCT expands on this perspective by proposing a strategy-like model for the way a concept exists  in a learner’s mind, which they call a coordination class. Central to this coordination process are two key features: extraction  and the causal net (diSessa et al., 2016). Extraction involve s patterns of (often visual) inspection that an individual examines to identify a concept. The causal net involves a dynamic assembly of knowledge elements used to draw conclusions about a concept (Levin & diSessa, 2015).

CTT takes a situated approach to cognition by emphasizing how thinking and learning emerge from diverse, context-dependent situations that together enable a more reliable interpretation, or “readout,” of information. This conceptual lens has synergy with embodied cognition, as they both embrace the idea that 380 knowledge emerges through dynamic interactions between perception, action, and context. For example, w hen interpreting velocity–time graphs, novices often “see” the slope as the shape of the motion (e.g., thinking a rising line means moving upward). As expertise develops, learners coordinate visual readouts (e.g., graph slope) with embodied intuitions about movement, e.g., speeding up  or slowing down (Parnafes, 2007). Specifically, CCT captures the restructuring of visual attention and reasoning; embodied cognition explains why bodily experiences of motion support that restructuring.  Therefore, CCT offers a framework for examining how learners coordinate perceptual and conceptual information about concepts like physical quantities (Lira & Stieff, 2026). Because such coordination appears in gesture and interaction with multiple representations (Parnafes, 2007), we seek dialogue between CCT and embodied learning.

Embodied technology support information extraction and inferences Research in embodied cognition has demonstrated that embodied engagement can facilitate complex cognitive processes by grounding abstract ideas in the motor system (Goldin-Meadow, 2003). From the perspective of CCT, such embodied experiences tune students ’ perceptual acuity to visually extract salient features of a phenomenon and make appropriate inferences, thereby supporting the coordination of knowledge elements across representational modalities. In recent years, the design of learning technologies such  as gesture-controlled computer visualizations (Kim et.al., 2024) and haptic interfaces (Zohar & Levy, 2019; Tang et.al., 2025 2) demonstrate that sensorimotor experiences facilitate students’ embodiment. These technologies provide effective means to represent distinct aspects of scientific phenomena and, importantly, render otherwise unobservable features—such as force s—conceptually salient in ways that cannot be achieved through traditional representational modalities (Rau, 2020).  In addition, these technologies  prompt students to engage in bodily movements such as producing representational gestures (Tang et.al., 20251). Working memory models have been viewed as a parallel—and sometimes competing  (Goldinger et al., 2016)—framework to embodiment theory (Zou et.al., 2025; Sepp et.al., 2019). From the perspective of traditional information-processing models, tasks that require extracting information from representations and making corresponding inferences are cognitively demanding because they rely heavily on the limited capacity of working memory (Baddeley, 2000). Accordingly, when students perform such tasks, the prefrontal cortex—responsible for working memory and executive control —is expected to exhibit greater activation. E mbodiment perspectives conciliatory with working memory models  posit that embodied learning experiences  can “offload” some of this cognition by engaging perceptual and motor systems to support reasoning and information integration (GoldinMeadow, 2003). In the present study, we designed a task to empirically test the predictions of embodiment theory within a representational reasoning task that varied the perceptual extraction for identifying physical quantities. Embodied technologies: In this investigation, we build upon Haptic Bridge work (Davis et.al., 2017) whereby we designed an immersive embodied learning environment that integrates haptic technology with a gesture-controlled computer visualization to support students in understanding a physical quantity in biology: the Resting Membrane Potential (RMP). Understanding the RMP requires students to coordinate two processes: chemical and electrical potentials. Prior research demonstrates that students often neglect the electrical potential because it involves a force that is difficult to represent through static images (Lira & Stieff, 2026; Lira, 2020; Tang & Lira, 2023).

To address this challenge, we designed an immersive learning environment that allows learners to embody a designated ion within a cellular system. By gesturing toward a large screen, learners can control the ion’s movement through hand motions. Hand tracking is achieved using a Leap Motion controller positioned on a desk facing upward beneath the learner’s hand. By default, the simulation tracks the right hand, although handedness can be adjusted before starting. The (x, y, z) coordinates of the palm are tr ansmitted to Unity3D to localize the particle (K⁺, an ion) in real time. Within the visualization window, the electrical potential is depicted through the alignment of oppositely charged ions along the membrane.  To complement this gesture-based interaction, we incorporated a wearable haptic glove that delivers tactile feedback—vibrations on the palm and back of the hand—to simulate the electrical potential experienced by ions in the system (Figure 1). Through this integration of visual, gestural, and haptic modalities, learners can both control the ion’s movement and experience the corresponding sensory feedback as it traverses different locations in the environment. 381 Representational translation task: Coordination classes encompass strategies for directing attention and for identifying, organizing, and integrating observations to extract  meaningful information —often about physical quantities. Therefore, to determine how the embodied technology grounded students’ cognition, we implemented a representational translation task after  the embodied learning activity. This task required students to extract information from one representation of the RMP and make inferences to accurately translate it into another form (Ainsworth, 1999). Specifically, students either (a) began with a diagram of ionic distributions and membrane structures and identified the corresponding point on a graph that represented changes in potential (Diagram-toGraph; see Figure 2a), or (b) began with a graph depicting an electrical potential and identified the corresponding ionic configuration in a diagram (Graph-to-Diagram; see Figure 2b). To accurately translate from a diagram to a graph, students must extract the following information: (1) identify the concentration ratio across the membrane, (2) determine which type of ion the channel is permeable to by recognizing its color, and (3) note the charge difference across the membrane. Based on this information, students then need to infer the cell’s  membrane potential and select the graph that best represents the corresponding voltage. To translate from a graph to a diagram, students must first identify the voltage of the graph (positive, zero, or negative), then infer the direction of ion movement and the relative concentrations on each side of the membrane to reconstruct the corresponding diagram. fNIRS offers a tool for assessing embodied learning Despite the unique affordances of embodied educational technologies, the mechanisms through which these experiences ground abstract concepts in concrete experiences and support students’ cognitive processes during learning remain unclear. Although behavioral methods provide insight into students’ performance, such performance reflects the outcomes of multiple underlying cognitive processes. Neuroimaging methods, by contrast, offer a window into these cognitive processes, revealing dimensions of learning that behavioral measures alone cannot capture. Some studies have utilized fMRI to explore how embodied experiences such as gesture 382 enhances learning by engaging the motor system (Wakefield, 2019). However, fMRI is highly influenced by motion artifacts. This prevents gesturing during problem-solving. We leverage an innovative neuroimaging system called Near Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS). fNIRS is less influenced by motion artifacts than fMRI thus allowing gesturing, delivers better spatial resolution than EEG, has better temporal resolution and lower cost than fMRI, and can be used in naturalistic environments (Boas et al., 2014). There fore, fNIRS is often used in studies of motor movement and cognitive processing (Boas et al., 2014). Theoretical assumptions concerning the effectiveness of embodiment approaches are buttressed by tacit beliefs about the neurocognitive resources students recruit during learning and problem solving—we aim to deliver a rigorous test of these assumptions by determining the neurocognitive systems students recruit during problem solving. Thus, neuroimaging methods test  the specific tacit assumption that experience with embodied technology will ground students’ reasoning in sensori motor systems as opposed to visual or language networks implicated by CCT. This leads us to the overarching question: what is the neurocognitive mechanism for embodied learning about physical quantities? Specifically, we aim to address the following research questions:

• RQ1: What neural resources do students recruit when extracting and making inferences across

representational forms after learning with embodied technologies, and how do these resources vary across problem types (e.g., diagram-to-graph vs. graph-to-diagram)?

• RQ2: What factors (e.g., prior knowledge) are associated with variation in students’ neural activation

patterns during representational translation tasks?

## Method

This study was part of a longitudinal investigation. Twenty-eight undergraduate students majoring in biology or psychology were recruited from a large Midwestern university. After providing informed consent, participants first completed an explanation task in which they responded to a set of question prompts assessing their knowledge of RMPs. They then took a seven-item multiple-choice test. Next, participants watched an instructional video on RMP while a research assistant fitted and adjusted the fNIRS cap. This arrangement was used because the cap fitting procedure typically required approximately 10 minutes, allowing the setup period to be used productively for instruction. Following this, students were randomly assigned to one of two conditions: (1) 14 students completed a think-aloud protocol using a Haptic-and gesture-controlled simulation, and (2) 14 students completed a think-aloud protocol using a Gesture-controlled simulation only. Last, all participants completed a representational translation task on a computer  (Figure 2). The task employed a block design. In the first phase, participants completed three blocks of diagram-to-graph translation. Each block contained three trials: in each trial, a diagram was shown for 10 seconds, followed by three graphs displayed for 10 seconds, from which participants selected the graph that best corresponded to the diagram (Figure 2a). A 15-second fixation period was inserted between blocks. In the  second phase, participants completed three blocks of graph-to-diagram (Figure 2b) translation, following the same structure and timing as the first phase, except that they were presented with a graph first and asked to select the corresponding diagram  (Figure 3). This study investigated the neurocognitive mechanisms underlying perceptual extraction and conceptual inference after embodied learning experiences. It offers empirical insights into what it means to ground “abstract” concepts through embodied engagement. The results yield four major conclusions. First, when translating from diagrams to graphs, students exhibit greater frontal activation than when translating from graphs to diagrams. This pattern aligns with coordination class theory because diagrams contain a wider visual array of representational features. Under time constraints, extracting visual elements from diagrams and generating inferences about physical quantities requires significantly more cognitive effort than when the graph first presents quantitative information explicitly. Second, the finding of overall higher sensorimotor activation, irrespective of task type or experimental condition, suggests two key subpoints: (1) embodied learning experiences that grant students motoric control over visualizations prompt students activation of sensorimotor systems during later symbolic problem solving, and (2) when learning from dynamic visualizations, gesture-control alone was sufficient to ground scientific concepts in these embodied resources. This undermined the impact of haptic feedback on learning. Third, both behavioral measures—the explanation task and representational translation task accuracy —indicate that higher-performing students more heavily activate their sensorimotor systems during problem solving than their lower-performing peers. Performance here reflects both prior knowledge, as measured by explanation task scores, and accuracy during the translation task.  This suggests that, rather than relying solely on frontal cognitive systems, effective learners integrate embodied resources into their problem-solving processes, particularly when extracting information from complex representations and generating inferences about physical quantities. These findings extend prior work on embodied cognition by illustrating a potential mechanism for how perception and action ground scientific reasoning. Coordination class theory describes concepts as knowledge systems for extracting and inferring relevant information across contexts  (diSessa & Sherin, 1998). Subsequent studies (Levrini & diSessa, 2008; Parnafes, 2007; Barth-Cohen & Wittmann, 2017; Rodriguez, Stricker, & Becker, 2020) have shown how learners integrate sensorimotor and symbolic resources when reasoning with visualizations. Our data provide neural evidence that corroborate these claims: embodied interactions with visual representations shifted activation from frontal executive to posterior sensorimotor regions. This demonstrates that symbolic abstraction and reasoning in science is indeed sometimes grounded in bodily engagement. We sought dialogue between CCT and embodied perspectives, but embodiment is sometimes enmeshed with classic information processing and cognitive load theory (Zou et  al., 2025; Sepp et  al., 2019).  The two approaches, however, differ in epistemic foci. Strong embodiment views do not share cognitive load assumptions, in part, because theorists here focus claims on knowledge itself being structured by sensation and action  as opposed to purely symbolic  (structuralist). In contrast, load theorists view  the motor system as a processing resource that can ease thinking and speed up learning (functionalist). We have weighed in on this tension (ClinganSiverly et al., 2024; under review) and maintain the modern assumption that knowledge is multi-modal. In relation to these results, a load perspective we have considered is that attentional networks delegate processing to motor systems similar to visual or language systems  (Sepp et al., 2019). This attentional view, however, does not 385 adequately explain the decoupling between neurocognitive systems —i.e., that high knowledge individuals decreased frontal and increased  sensory motor regions. Such results ostensibly support traditional offloading accounts. Rather than accept these results as  confirming “offloading,” we call for future work that discriminates with a micro-genetic neuroimaging approach. Such an approach with naïve learners  will resolve the issue with prior knowledge effects to reveal the dynamic interactions between frontal and sensorimotor systems. In summary, t he present study contributes a neurocognitive test of the situated, resources-based assumptions of  CCT from  Knowledge-in-Pieces (KiP). Although KiP has treated embodiment as implicit in learners’ coordination of perceptual and conceptual resources, neuroimaging has not previously confirmed these embodied learning dynamics. By combining coordination class theory with fNIRS, we show that developing expertise sometimes involves a shift from extraction via  ostensibly effortful frontal  areas to extraction via embodied cognition—a finding that bridges information-processing and embodied accounts of learning. In short, expertise in reasoning with symbolic representations can emerge from embodied interactions with visualizations. Ainsworth, S. (1999). The functions of multiple representations.
