Learning Sciences as a domain emerged with the purpose of understanding how learning (and teaching) happens in real world contexts (Sawyer et al. 2022). In this pursuit, the domain draws from educational research, didactics, cognitive science, psychology, computer science, social sciences, anthropology, culture studies, philosophy, engineering, and technology (used for educational purposes). The domain and the community is expanding fast across the globe connecting various knowledge parts and sources. Numerous labs and researchers are emerging, setting up learning research labs across universities in the Global South. Learning Sciences can now be seen in the curricula of various programs. Many young researchers are at the forefront of these endeavours, working towards the purpose of understanding learning in their respective contexts. It is pertinent to remind ourselves that learning is a highly sociomaterially mediated phenomenon. As researchers with diverse identities from diverse contexts study learning, the sociomaterial realities become central factors in shaping their practice — t heir knowing and doing. This shapes the kinds of topics researchers choose and turn down, the kinds of designs they build, the kinds of methodologies they use and leave out, the kinds of theories and frameworks they use, modify and build, and the kinds of insights generated as a result. The community is hence not monolithic. It is a community with multiple sociomaterial realities, multiple centres, united by a purpose. We, as a self-reflective community of practice, recognise and actively strive to embrace this 2480 multiplicity of methodologies, contexts, and frameworks. These aforementioned decision-points are also points of tension encountered by the researchers, taking explicit and implicit forms. These are not just material, but epistemological tensions. These te nsions come into play, especially when early career researchers practice the Learning Sciences. What are the dynamics involved in shaping their practice? What are the conditions and factors that are (in)visible, yet real? Are there patterns or heuristics emerging in the practices of the community? What does it take to pursue this common purpose of “understanding learning” while remaining cognizant of the multiplicity? What does this do to the discipline — the ways of knowing and doing? In this symposium, we attempt to raise and answer some of the above questions and argue for multiplicity in the ways of knowing and doing learning sciences, using a series of cases emerging from the Global South, with contributions emerging from all the major continents — South America & Latin America, Africa and Asia. We present a series of cases of individual researchers from the Global South and their practice. We voice the stories from the Global South, as these together present the points of tension between the multiple realities of each of the researchers in their engagement with the LS community. Further these are not sufficiently documented and foregrounded, and without these stories, the pursuit of understanding how learning happens remains incomple te. Through these cases, we seek to demonstrate the tension, dynamics, struggles, involved in this pursuit of common purpose, while staying true to the reality of multiplicity. These cases show how these studies adopt, modify, and expand the conventionally accepted/legitimised LS research and practice. Though the aspect of multiplicity is acknowledged and encouraged, the tensions, reconciliations and the evolution of the discipline, especially driven by the researchers in the Global South, happening through the work of these researchers, are yet to be actively reflected on. We are very wary of a possible deficiency trap that this might fall into. Through this symposium, we seek to invite a dialogue within the community of LS, as a self-conscious community, to gather, reflect and discuss ways in which the discipline is expanding. This can potentially help us take concrete and deliberate actions for the future of our community.

Contribution 1: The curious case of makerspace research in India Alekh Velayudhan, Nisumba Soodhani As the maker movement is getting widely adopted at the global level, it is imperative to understand how the movement plays out, and how design-based research approaches can (re)inform the maker pedagogies in varied and diverse settings (Greenberg et al., 2020). To that end, India provides a particularly compelling site for extending makerspace research. Amongst many, one such notable conceptualization is that of tinkering in terms of jugaad-resourceful improvisation under constraint-researched and set into action as a distinctive innovation strategy in making (Radjou et al., 2012). In a similar vein, it can be argued that other kinds of knowledge pieces can be added from the Indian settings towards the global repertoire regarding maker education research and practices. This paper weaves together the personal experiences and reflective inquiries of the authors who identify themselves as learning science research scholars from Global South who engage in Makerspace Research in India. Each of the authors reflected on previous experience of engaging in Makerspace Research in India, looking at challenges or tensions emerged in the research experience, workarounds, and new learnings surfaced in the course of action. We conducted content analysis (Mayring, 2015) of the corresponding verbal reflections and recorded notes. For the analysis, we read the reflections and discussed similarities and differences in our experiences during structured peer debriefing sessions across multiple rounds. We then arrived at the emergent themes below that abstract from our lived experiences as learning science research scholars engaging in makerspace research in the Global South.

Theme 1: Partnership and epistemic barriers Makerspace activity in India sits in a peculiar gap between policy, practice, and research. Many university spaces operate with student-led ethos aligned with global makerspace norms. K–12 spaces span both formal and informal initiatives. The school-based makerspace is dominated by Atal Tinkering Labs (ATLs), which number ten thousand. The informal makerspaces are mainly led by NGOs and civic efforts which usually adapt to local constraints and opportunities. These conditions pose specific challenges for le arning-sciences research oriented toward: authentic tasks, tracing how learners develop skills, navigate trouble, and extended timelines. Author 2 reported the aforementioned subtleties: “. Making activity levels tend to spike before mid-November in preparation for showcase events like the Mega Tinkering Activity, then taper during the rest of the year. Implementation is often top-down, and outcomes depend heavily on the interest, capacity,  and continuity of onsite staff. Field visits to multiple ATLs betw een 2022 and 2024 revealed that some spaces operate more like traditional laboratories, with equipment kept under lock and key. I found it difficult to find authentic makerspace 2481 settings to do my research on creativity. This was mainly because we didn't have access to or no connections with the existing makerspaces”. For India-based maker-education researchers situated in Learning Sciences, epistemic barriers are structural and intellectual: thin mentorship networks, paywalled literature, volatile sites, and review norms calibrated to Global-North infrastructures. The  tacit norms embedded in infrastructures, networks, and publication expectations make it further challenging. As author 1 noted: “The Maker movement is still on the way of getting traction. Institutions and NGOs are gradually understanding the nuances and benefits of such spaces for alternate ways of learning. Academic makerspaces are such examples. In India, the learning scientist s are few in numbers and in the case of makerspace research it is very rare. There’s no one to ask who has done related research, every design decision feels like first principles. So we rely on literature from other parts of the world … where these kinds of research works are established and ongoing. Few of us have reached out to these researchers for potential collaborations and guidance”. Theme 2: Moving beyond the traditional theoretical rootings and practices Traditionally, makerspace scholarships have largely been framed through a narrow canon of established theories and evidence-based practices. A point of departure from the traditional approaches can open space for building local theories and related knowledge pieces, centered around the specificities and foregrounding the multiplicities in terms of sites, languages, materials, and temporalities. In other words, these points of departure, normally coined as “workarounds,” are epistemic moves to hybridize and expand research orientations toward the overarching goal of gathering and positioning knowledge streams of “how learning happens”. Author 1 highlighted: “ My research in the makerspace domain began with using constructionism as theoretical and analytical lenses. The main reason being that these are well established and extensively followed globally. But as the research progressed, I saw the findings very generic, inline with the findings shown in mainstream literature. This pushed me to shift to relationality perspectives and lenses, which later on helped in identifying and portraying the not so apparent findings. I am not discounting the prevalent theories but trying to say that there are alternative ways of knowing and doing these makerspace research works”. By looking at the sociomaterial conditions entangled with our research accounts, we presented  emergent themes to show the multiple ways of knowing and doing amidst the existing tensions and challenges. The barriers mentioned in the findings are not merely inconveniences; but are knowledge-shaping agencies. The peculiar Indian makerspace infrastructure-specifically research partnerships-calls for context specific research designs which need to be recognised as principled adaptations and not as methodological deficits. Further, the epistemic moves involved in departure from traditional ways reiterate the potential of multiple ways of knowing and doing Learning Science research and underscores the situatedness of research inquiries. Contribution 2: Rethinking Communities of Practice (CoP) in Indian science classrooms Zun Phoo Mo This work is situated in studying classroom science teaching in urban schools in India, focused on how teachers navigate cultural and linguistic diversities. Preliminary findings from the analysis of teacher interviews revealed the usage of multiple situated strategies like bilingual instructions, storytelling, cross-institutional collaborations, and so on. Additionally, the interactions between stakeholders, such as, teachers, parents, counselors, external experts, and leadership revealed core CoP features  with various micro-CoPs (Ervin-Kassab & Drouin, 2021). While constructs of legitimate peripheral participation (LPP), shared repertoire, and boundary practices (Wenger, 1998), remain useful, they do not fully capture the lived practices of Indian middle s chool science teachers. For example, LPP in our study was not unidirectional; junior teachers actively contributed novel activity designs while seniors provided cultural mediation, producing a reciprocal contribution dynamic. Similarly, the notion of a shared repertoire extended beyond formal instructional resources to include culturally situated artefacts such as multiple projects in a calendar year for celebrating festivals, and bilingual worksheets. Boundary practices also went beyond professional teacher networks to include parents, leadership (principals), external experts, and counselors, elucidating the extensions of teachers’ professional communities. Moreover, practices centered around informal interactions (such as WhatsApp exchanges) with parents and peers are context sensitive, and shows how educators collaboratively build flexible, locally grounded infrastructures for learning in resource-constrained environments. These divergences indicate t he limits of a straightforward translation of CoP theory and call for adaptations that reflect multilingual, culturally plural learning environments. Also, it points to a necessity of generating new theoretical insights (and not only adapting existing fram eworks 2482 of LS research) that can enrich global understandings of learning in diverse, resource-constrained, and culturally layered contexts.

While doing LS research in Indian classrooms a researcher confronts some assumptions to bring about the nuances of these culturally layered contexts. Urban classrooms in India often have large class sizes, and resource constraints. Though it may not immediately be perceived as an unconventional research context, these high-density, multilingual, resource-limited environments create novel test conditions for theories. Another aspect of "shared repertoire”, which is one of the foundational dimensions of CoP, is dynamic in nature, and as per our findings the artifacts (worksheets, study guides, etc.) are regularly adapted to account for class diversity.

## Contribution 3: The challenge of context: Navigating the double burden of

global demands and local relevance Amrit Pal Singh, Meera Pawar, Rajashri Priyadarshini, Ram Das Rai A fundamental question for any Learning Sciences researcher is, "What is the context of learning I am studying?" While researchers in the Global North may often be guided by theoretical interests, those of us in the Global South can face what feels like a "double burden." We have a pressing need to select contexts that are both deeply meaningful to our local learners and simultaneously perceived as legitimate by the gatekeepers of the global academic conversation. We adopt a reflective inquiry approach, gro unded in the principles of hermeneutic phenomenology (van Manen, 1990) and hope to illuminate the often-invisible intellectual labor shaped by a dual mandate: to address local educational needs while contributing to the global Learning Sciences (LS) discou rse. In this setting, success is measured by both local impact and publication in “international” journals, which are predominantly from the Global North. The first author's research investigates how learners design tabletop games around socio-scientific issues and explores how the design process itself, how prototyping, iteration, and refinement might reveal systemic reasoning. The second author’s doctoral work seeks to understand how middle school students develop Structure –Behavior–Function reasoning about complex biological systems, exploring how systemic analogical stories may support students in connecting structures, functions, and behaviors. The third author’s work focuses on understanding how learners use embodied intuitive resources, The fourth author’s doctoral study offers a multimodal exploration of emotion in Computer-Supported Collaborative Argumentation. Building upon the work of Polo et al. (2010), this research develops a multimodal analysis to account for embodied emotion.

A notable challenge arises from clashes between disciplinary norms over what constitutes "legitimate" evidence of learning. In the first author's research on tabletop games, the LS value for early, unpolished prototypes as windows into learner reasoning conflicts with expectations from other communities of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991) that may prioritize the polish of a final artifact. This creates an epistemological dilemma where the very artifacts that most authentically capture the messy, iterative process of learning are judged as incomplete from another disciplinary gaze. This is a tension over what "counts" as a valid representation of knowledge. A deeper tension emerges when globally accepted methodologies prove ill-suited for eliciting authentic phenomena locally. This was starkly evident in the fourth author's study on argumentation, where a Western "gold-standard" topic (climate change) failed to generate the desired emotional engagement. A method decontextualized from the learners' lifeworld failed to produce the target phenomenon, necessitating a shift to a locally resonant topic, homeopathy, that risked being judged as less rigorous by global reviewers. Similarly, the second author found that implementing student think-alouds, a common method, e ncountered significant cultural and administrative hurdles in local schools, again showing the friction between a decontextualized "standard" method and the situated reality of the learning environment.

For many of us, English is not a first language, creating hurdles in articulating nuanced ideas for an international audience. These constraints, coupled with the pressure to conform to established methodological paradigms, are a tangible part of our research lifeworld, framing a persistent need to justify not just our findings, but the very legitimacy of our contexts and methods, a process that aligns with broader calls to decolonize research methodologies (Smith, 2012). In response to these tensions, we have begun to see a pattern of practice that we tentatively term “strategic calibration”. It involves a continuous, thoughtful adjustment of methods, framing, and focus to maintain scientific integrity while navigating complex sociomaterial landscapes. One approach, which might be called “calibrated translation”, involves carefully reframing research artifacts and processes to be understood by multiple audiences. The first author’s work illustrates this, where rough game prototypes are explicitly articulated  not as incomplete products but as rich data sources revealing how learners grapple with complex systems. These translations where the narrative around the research is constructed to bridge different epistemological expectations, thereby preserving the authenticity of the local work while ensuring its global resonance. Another crucial strategy is “deliberate methodological calibration”, where methods are adapted to fit 2483 local realities without sacrificing scientific goals. When the second author found think-alouds unfeasible, the shift to stimulated recall interviews represented a contextually sensitive alternative that maintained the core intent of accessing student reasoning. For the fourth author's study, the choice of homeopathy was a strategic calculation to elicit authentic data, while the conscious avoidance of a more volatile local topic (Ayurveda) demonstrates a principled negotiation of the socio-political landscape to protect the study's integrity. These adjustments are not simply pragmatic fixes; they are central to the work of Design-Based Research (DBR) (Barab, 2014). Our lived reality of "double burden" is a direct manifestation of the field's foundational theoretical commitments. We experience firsthand that learning is a cultural process, a reality that demands that research researchers. The failure of Western-centric gold standards in our settings is an empirical demonstration of the importance of situated cognition (Greeno & Engeström, 2014), reminding us that meaning is co-constructed within specific activities and communities. Our response of "strategic calibration" can be understood as a form of practitioner-led Design-Based Research, where we iteratively adapt our own research designs in response to the authen tic, complex system of global academia. By making this multiplicity visible, we argue that the LS community must move beyond simply acknowledging diversity. It must recognize these navigational strategies not as peripheral challenges but as a generative source of methodological and theoretical innovation. Contribution 4: Position of LS faculty in Indian academia and concerns of their students Rafikh Shaikh, Aditi Kothiyal We write as early-career faculty in India, where education research centers on schools —teachers, curricula, sociology—while Learning Sciences (LS) and its learner-centered, design-oriented stance remains marginal. Our work unfolds amid project-based funding and national priorities that privilege outcome-oriented studies. Author 1, temporarily appointed in a central government social-science institute and now a one-person team, constantly negotiates India’s results-driven mandates with ISLS’s process-oriented inquiry. Author 2, an Assistant Teaching Professor in Humanities and Social Sciences at a government-supported institute of technology, likewise on a temporary contract, must meet institutional imperatives—service in a “supporting” department, heavy teac hing, master’s supervision, while pursuing in-situ LS design and study. Institutional logics and material constraints complicate LS uptake even as we work to sustain it.

A rigid science/social-science split permits interdisciplinarity only within silos, so LS, drawing on both, is typically parked in Education or Social Sciences, where learner-centered, design-based inquiry must be continually defended. This marginality erodes institutional legitimacy and unsettles LS identity for scholars and students. Yet these pressures have reshaped practice: Author 2 expanded beyond cognitive and sociocultural LS to foreground power, inclusion and marginalization, and now runs an LS sur vey course (critical, cognitive, sociocultural) open to science, engineering, and HSS students. Framed as epistemic negotiation, the course bridges quantitative/cognitivist “sciences” and critical/ethnographic “social sciences,” enabling boundary crossing (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011) and legitimizing multiple ways of knowing. Practically, it has seeded a small interdisciplinary community, drawn master’s and doctoral students into LS, and strengthened Author 2’s legitimacy within a social-science unit of a technical institute. In recent years, our work has examined how science education unfolds in Indian schools and how cultural tools mediate learning. Most schools rely on lecture-based instruction, with limited hands-on practice; we frame this as an access problem: equitable science requires access to the cultural tools of science, not just textbooks or teachers. Author 1’s studies of the Foldscope —a frugal paper microscope (~140×) —use design-based research to investigate how democratizing such tools shape both processes and outcomes. Institutionally, our projects skew toward outcome-oriented mandates—state-commissioned action research, teacher PD, OER, and online course design—reflecting Global South priorities to build systems at scale. The prevailing two-phase model—pilot and scale-up—discourages gradual refinement of interventions, leading to an epistemological bias toward acquisition of metaphors of learning (Sfard, 1998) and away from sociocultural, participatory understandings. Outcome-oriented research is preferred because it answers urgent questions about what interventions work, for whom, and under what conditions, questions essential for nations constructing educational infrastructure. Conversely, process-oriented research, sustained investigation of how learning unfolds  through detailed analysis of interactions, dynamics, and meaning-making, reflects the sociomaterial reality of contexts where basic educational infrastructure is established and deeper questions about learning mechanisms become tractable. Although we use mixed methods to capture learning as multidimensional, national and institutional preferences for quantitative evidence, narrow methodological and theoretical latitude. These conditions mirror broader 2484 tensions in Indian education research, where linear evidence models often eclipse cyclical, design-oriented inquiry.

The tension we experience is not between "constrained" versus "ideal" research, but rather in navigating between these systems and encountering what appears to be differential reception. Author 1’s outcome-oriented project work and process-oriented studies  have generated insights they believe are relevant to understanding learning with material tools. However, their submissions based on outcome-oriented research to ISLS conferences (2019, 2025) were not accepted, while work from process-oriented studies conducted through unfunded research was accepted to ISLS 2024, and met with enthusiastic encouragement. We acknowledge the limitations of drawing broad conclusions from an individual experience; there may be multiple reasons for these outcomes, including the quality and framing of specific submissions, reviewer expertise, or conference review processes. However, an awareness of this implicit pattern also drives Author 2 to follow a similar approach of conducting process-oriented research through unfunded proje cts with Masters and Doctoral students. This pattern raises questions about what kinds of knowledge and inquiry the ISLS community recognises and values. Does the emphasis on process-oriented inquiry, while theoretically important, potentially limit engagement with outcomeoriented research prevalent in the Global South? The recently introduced Practice-Oriented Papers track is a laudable initiative that suggests the community recognises this gap and is working to address it. We wonder whether more could be  done to value insights from research that asks different questions, about scaling, implementation, and impact under resource constraints, questions that learning sciences scholars in the Global South must answer to serve their societies. The recognition of our process-oriented work has catalysed local interest: significantly more students and colleagues now express interest in ISLS-style inquiry. This suggests that competence exists but may be suppressed by local institutional conditions. Also, what may be  a structural dependency: legitimacy appears to derive more readily from conforming to Western research norms rather than from addressing locally urgent questions through rigorous outcome-oriented work. We propose that this multiplicity must extend to epistemic pluralism that values both process and outcome orientations as legitimate responses to different sociomaterial realities. Our experience suggests that such learning requires the global ISLS community to reflect on whether current recognition mechanisms adequately engage with the diversity of learning sciences practice emerging globally. As we in the Global South attempt to align ourselves with the orientation of the global LS community, how might the community ensure that outcome-oriented research addressing questions of scale and implementation generates knowledge that the community actively seeks to learn from, rather than viewing it as less central to the field's identity? This symposium offers an important opportunity to examine these questions collectively. Contribution 5: Epistemic tensions in learning sciences: Consequences of sociomaterial issues and structural conflicts in the Global South Shamin Padalkar, Rafikh Shaikh This contribution addresses a critical epistemological mismatch experienced by Learning Sciences researchers in the Global South, in communicating their research to peers in developed countries. The tensions are two-fold: i) topics relevant to socio-material conditions in the Global South are often marginalized by the dominant interests of developed countries, and ii) prevailing theoretical frameworks are not fruitful for addressing local, structural concerns. Our experience stems from the Connected Learning Initiative (CLIx, 2025), a large, trust-funded project spanning four state governments in India. Knowledge co-production was integral to this project: establishing computer labs in government schools, designing and implementing ICT-based science modules, and delivering comprehensive, blended teacher professional development. These schools serve children from low socioeconomic backgrounds, requiring navigation of persistent challenges like electricity and internet inconsistencies, and fundamental digital literacy gaps among teachers. Against this context, our research foregrounded teacher adaptation, student learning outcomes, and system-level change. Ironically, when this research was submitted to mainstream academic journals and conferences, reviewers questioned the "generalizability" of findings, deeming them "context-specific." The moot point is, can issues that are highly pertinent to the majority of the world, namely the Global South, be so easily dismissed as “context-specific?”. Studying the process of adaptation, while identifying and addressing the gaps from classroom to system-level, is essential to understanding and improving our methods of teaching and learning through ICT. The “generalizability” issues raised by our reviewers starkly bring out persistent bias from the perspective of the developed world. The currently dominant topics in major LS journals assume basic infrastructure deployment and focus on high-level concerns like Artificial Intelligence (AI), which suit the conditions in develope d nations. While AI in education is of interest to the Global South as well, the foundational issues of deployment cannot be blindsided. Another consequence of this bias is that the onus of describing the context in detail and explaining the broader relevance of our selected topics disproportionately 2485 falls on authors from the Global South, despite their contextual factors being more prevalent globally. In contrast, authors from developed countries assume readers’ familiarity with their context and typically do not address the relevance of their findings for the developing world.

On another note, till the first decade of the 21st century, learning research was carried out largely within the constructivist and cognitive frameworks. For example, Vygotsky’s social constructivism, which took roots in Indian academia, is a robust and broad framework with applicability in diverse contexts. However, today’s trend in the learning sciences community is to rapidly evolve new sociocultural frameworks and discard old ones, even before exploring the full potential of existing frameworks in diffe rent contexts. Unwittingly we become participants in an absurd race to keep up with whatever frameworks are currently in vogue. Adding to this, in emphasizing mind-world connections, and network-based understanding of agency, the current theoretical frameworks end up very similar to each other. For example, a socio-cultural framework can well subsume situated cognition, distributed cognition, actor network theory, and sociomateriality. Despite this proliferation of relational theories, their application in the Indian context remains problematic. Situated as we are in a highly hierarchical and rigid society and education system, we find it more fruitful to focus on structural conflict rather than relational networks. Indian educators often aim to improve skil ls like reasoning, interpreting data, validating claims, argumentation and critical thinking. Frameworks like dialectic materialism would be more appropriate for us. Besides issues of hierarchy, our students often come from linguistic backgrounds which are  different from their medium of instruction. In classroom situations, they are often reticent and inarticulate. Consequently, language-oriented frameworks like conversation analysis, critical discourse analysis, and their associated methodologies are less suited. We are rather interested in identifying the rich, usually unacknowledged resources available to students, including indigenous knowledge, experiences with nature, the use of frugal tools and their own bodies. Frameworks like funds of knowledge, pla ce-based learning, and embodied cognition have proven useful in this pursuit.

These experiences raise a critical epistemological question: how might Learning Sciences accommodate epistemic diversity, the recognition that knowledge production takes different forms across different contexts? While embracing participatory research and knowledge co-production, can we provide space for the distinct issues that arise in the course of building infrastructure and making systems functional? We appeal to the global community to acknowledge the deep conflicts that exist in the process of education in the Global South, opening scope for responsive use and development of frameworks and methodologies. Contribution 6: Learning sciences, equity and the Latin American lens: reclaiming the territory Paulo Blikstein, Carolina Sotério, Fabio Campos, Renato Russo, Fernanda Dias The Learning Sciences community has long emphasized that learning is cultural, situated, and political (Greeno & Engeström, 2014). However, despite this theoretical perspective, the field remains predominantly focused on U.S. institutions and values. The papers presented at the International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS) conferences are about 80% authored by North Americans. Even Europe and Asia, which together make up roughly 15% of the submissions, rarely shape the field’s conceptual center —let alone the Global South. This imbalance influences not only which problems are studied but also how equity and inclusion are defined. The categories used to describe people — “Latinx,” “students of color,” “underrepresented minorities”— stem from U.S. racial and cultural contexts. When these labels are exported, they flatten profound differences among learners elsewhere. For instance, in a Mexican public school, should a researcher label students as “Latinx,” “Latino,” or “Latin American”? Or should they descr ibe local demographics that are meaningful within Mexico, such as Indigenous, Afro-Mexican, mestizo, or Europeandescent? A child considered “Latina” in the U.S. (simply by being born to Latin American parents) would have a very different social standing in her home country. Yet, to communicate effectively with an audience that knows little about global realities, Latin American scholars often feel they must “massage” their language and data to fit these imported categories. The contradictions go beyond just identity categories. Think about the economic dimensions of equity. How do we compare a Latinx family in the U.S. earning the minimum wage of $15 an hour with a family in Brazil making $1.25 per hour? Both might be conside red “working class,” but their material lives are vastly different. Still, in discussions at conferences about equity, poverty, or inclusion, these global differences are often reduced to a single, U.S. -focused perspective narrative.These asymmetries exten d into our citation networks. Even equity-oriented LS papers overwhelmingly cite U.S. and English-language sources. Latin American researchers are often cited mainly as cases rather than as theorists. When Paulo Freire appears, it is usually through only the best-selling book, Pedagogy of the Oppressed (Freire, 1970), stripped from its context and with little 2486 where knowledge from the Global South circulates only after translation and simplification. For Latin American researchers, writing for English-language journals involves translating not just words but epistemologies — adapting local concepts to align with d ominant paradigms. This challenge is not solely linguistic but also one of epistemic justice. If the field genuinely values inclusion, it must acknowledge that equity in São Paulo, Bogotá, or Oaxaca will look—and feel —different from equity in Chicago. Building a truly global LS community requires broadening what counts as theory and rigor. Multilingual citation practices, collaborative publishing efforts, and recognition of regional traditions are crucial steps. The Latin American tradition—ranging from Fre ire to modern community-based pedagogies—provides frameworks that see learning as relational, political, and connected to struggles for dignity. These ideas often predate similar “Global North” frameworks by decades. They should not remain on the margins; they can and should reshape the field’s foundations. The goal is not just to “include” Latin American voices but to enable them to redefine the field’s categories, questions, and vocabularies. Only then can the Learning Sciences truly become global —a discipline that respects multiple ways of being, knowing, and doing. Contribution 7: Bridging global competencies and local realities: Egyptian teachers’ approaches to 21st-century learning Christine Tewfik, James Slotta This paper documents our collaboration with Egyptian public-school teachers to embed 21st-century competencies in the curriculum, paired with research that resists colonial framings by cultivating teachers’ localized understandings of project aims and centering their epistemic framing. In the context of non-hegemonic countries (Schenoni, 2019), consideration of teacher’s educational and cultural context includes the notion of cultural imperialism, as Mains (2009) describes: "a process of disproportionate influence over social practices and ideologies by one sociopolitical group over a politically weaker and (frequently) less-wealthy group". Thus, whenever they encounter new pedagogical ideas or approaches, teachers in non-hegemonic countries or groups have an added challenge of critically reflecting how such practices may serve to undermine or ignore their own culture.

This study examines how a Professional Learning Community (PLC) of teachers in Egypt can support them in adopting pedagogical innovations. This PLC was organized in the context of the Critical Action Learning Exchange (CALE) project from the University of Toronto, which is concerned with empowering teachers to design pedagogical activities that incorporate critical action and allow for the inclusion of students’ voices. For the past two years, we have engaged a group of Egyptian teachers from different schools; elementary, secondary, and technical, representing a range of student age levels and topics of teaching. This project comprised: the design workshop phase and the enactment and reflection phase. The workshop phase was designed to include 4 three-hour meetings. The first session provided the teachers with an overview of 21st-century competencies and the Education 2.0 policy, an initiative by the Egyptian ministry of education that calls for a shift in teachers’ educational practices from rote learning and memorization to competencies and student-centered approaches. Following the model of formative intervention, teachers were introduced to the idea of cultural-responsiveness and practitioners' inquiry and engaged in reflections about their own classroom practice. In the second session, teachers collectively continued to look at their existing teaching practices in relation to the Egyptian cultural and historical aspects. The CALE framework, together with a discussion about teachers’ readiness for change (Jones et al., 2023) served as reflective artifacts that allowed for contradictions between the teachers’ status quo and what they aspired their practices to be. The third session focused on engaging teachers with the design framework. Brainstorming lesson plans are done by design teams, using CALE authoring guides, followed by CALE mentor feedback. The fourth session was dedicated to a round of peer feedback and discussing new ways of teaching. Participants then finalized designs and documented them. In thi s phase, teachers implemented their designs in classrooms as part of the CALE Egypt professional development cycle. Participants left the final workshop session with a follow-up meeting with a CALE project mentor keeping in touch. As participants start ena cting their designs, they face challenges and obstacles, including the simple loss of motivation or engagement, with which they can benefit from close contact with project mentors. All teacher designs go through interactive cycles based on peer feedback, as well as teachers’ own evaluations of their students’ experiences with early versions of the lesson plans.

This study provided a set of findings about teachers’ perceptions and integration of 21st century competencies in their classrooms shaped by their own epistemologies which are influenced by their own cultural and societal values, beliefs, and traditions. N otably, teachers’ proficiency level and awareness of these competencies contributed to their understanding and readiness to implement such innovative pedagogies. Varying understandings: Several teachers showed a solid awareness of how to integrate competen cies within their 2487 curricula, while others perceived them as discrete sets of skills rather than embedded and integrated through the taught content. Time constraints: Teachers who perceived competencies as separate from core curricula often mentioned lack of enough time as a challenge because they have to get through the whole curriculum first and then implement the 21st century competencies if time allows. Deeper integration: Teachers with a more developed understanding recognized that competency integration is a process that takes time and unfolds through iterative learning experiences. These teachers understood that instances of effective integration occurred when teachers designed extended units that allowed students to research locally relevant issues. Superficial practices: Teachers with limited understanding of competency integration often used activities or tools such as mindmaps just for the sake of including them, without paying attention to the students’ genuine use and acquisition of such skills. Meaningful integration of 21st-century competencies depends on teachers’ conceptual understanding, access to culturally responsive pedagogical frameworks like CALE, and ongoing professional development that bridges global competencies with local realities.

To conclude, it is important to emphasize that teachers’ understanding of competencies gets influenced by their culture. Egyptian teachers’ interpretations were influenced by their societal norms and traditions that value respect for authority, collective harmony, and moral behaviors. This highlights the need to contextualize the understanding of “competency-based education” through the practitioners’ and students’ cultural lenses. It is more effective to understand, acknowledge, and accept the culturally m ediated implementation of such competencies rather than viewing this difference as a misunderstanding or lack of comprehension. Contribution 8: Local practices and global theories-connections and tensions: Epistemic agency in an Indian classroom Sugat Dabholkar In most schools in India, student participation in science and mathematics classes is limited to listening to disciplinary ideas presented by a teacher, understanding those ideas, and applying them in various problem-solving contexts (Singh et al., 2019). In contrast, theories of learning that center epistemic agency advocate for supporting students’ participation in the classroom as producers and evaluators of knowledge, rather than mere consumers (Miller et al., 2018). This contribution discusses a research project conducted in a western Indian city focused on co-designing high school curricular units for science and mathematics teachers that support the epistemically agentive participation of learners. The goal of the co-design was to integrate computational thinking and scientific inquiry (CTSI) activities with high school topics. Eleven teachers from five different schools participated in creating five curricular units on topics ranging from coordinate geometry to selective reabsorption in the kidney. These units, integrated with computational models, included learning activities that enabled students to design and conduct investigations, collect and analyze data, and construct explanations to understand disciplinary ideas. The teachers taught these units in their classrooms. Additionally, these units were published on a free, open-access platform (Excretory system: reabsorption). Teachers played pivotal roles in the co-design process, from choosing curricular topics and determining features of computational tools to crafting learning activities. In the classrooms, students actively engaged in scientific practices to construct and evaluate knowledge using computational tools. A closer look at the teacher interviews revealed that their participation in the co-design process and the emphasis on student agency in the classroom was transformative for both teachers and students. The vision of epistemic agency in this work was shaped by the idea that agentic student participation can potentially transform their relationship with ‘knowledge’ —from viewing knowledge as something established and to be acquired, to something they can evaluate and generate themselves. Such engagement is meaningful and consequential in the Indian educational and political contexts, where textbooks are considered the primary source of knowledge and students are often expected to accept even problematic content at face value (Darak, 2018). Therefore, designing learning opportunities that position students as evaluators and producers of knowledge can shift their perceptions of their roles in the classroom toward more agentic and meaningful participation (Dabholkar & Wilensky, 2025). However, we identify the following tensions in connecting Indian educational practices with theories of epistemic agency: The first tension concerns the framing of expert practices in science classrooms—specifically, whose practices  are considered expert from the perspective of teaching and learning science. From a teaching standpoint, even reform-oriented Indian classrooms often focus on imparting existing knowledge, as teachers adapt within the constraints of rigid educational syst ems. This authoritarian positioning of teachers as conveyors of scientific practices severely limits students’ opportunities to expand their social futures through meaningful engagement with science. To counter these dominant practices, we designed learning environments that promote student agency through participation in scientific inquiry. In this work, we drew on the values of professional scientific communities —primarily influenced by Western approaches to 2488 science—to support students’ epistemically agentic learning. Although this work challenged established expectations regarding teacher and student participation in the Indian educational context, the underlying epistemic logics for student agency in knowledge building were predominantly shaped by Eurocentric, Western notions of science (Miller et al., 2018).

The second tension involves co-designing learning experiences that use contexts meaningful to youth from nondominant communities, while also incorporating contemporary scientific ideas and practices. We believe learning about computational thinking practices and their ethical use is important for students from nondominant communities to engage with, so that global discourse around these ideas becomes more enriched and equitable. The challenge lies in co-designing learning experiences that support equitable participation in learning these practices. One of the goals of the project was to explore how to co-design curricular materials with teachers that are meaningful and relevant to students from nondominant gender, caste, and religious backgrounds. Although all the co-designed curricula successfully integrated scientific inquiry practices and disciplinary concepts, these curricular units could not be situated in contexts that students from nondominant communities find meaningful. Given the limited discourse ar ound supporting the participation of students from nondominant communities in India, it was difficult to prioritize this aspect during our co-design discussions. The above contributions highlight the specific realities of researchers from across the 3 continents. We see the following key themes of tensions emerge from these contributions.Structural barriers: There are multiple barriers that the LS researchers of Global south (GS) faced in active participation with the Global North (GN) dominant LS community, such as (1) cultural, institutional differences as emerging from the stories of teachers in the Egypt, or differences in the priorities of the researchers in India, (2) access barriers to peers and settings. Double burden: While the researchers have to be true to their local realities, this is an additional intellectual labour in communicating with a West centered LS community. This reflects while choosing the context of the study, or the theoretical and methodological choices. The researchers always have to sub-consciously be worried about falling in line drawn by the GN dominant LS community, which is often not fully educated about the GS. Epistemically unjust oversimplification or flattening:  As highlighted in the previous section, the GN dominant LS community may not have a way of appreciating the nuances of the different GS contexts. In the name of inclusion, often with a well-positioned intent, the community eventually in practice, flattens  and strips off the details, hence losing nuances at multiple levels, as emerging from the scholars in LS. This treatment of dissolving the nuances to a homogeneous bundle of fuzziness is not epistemically just, even to our own standards. To navigate these tensions and move forward, we see that carefully devised strategies and openings are necessary. Mutual Strategic Calibration and education:  The current strategic calibration as portrayed by the scholars from India can be mutual. The GN reviewers and the GN centered community can make efforts to educate themselves wherever possible about the differences. This can take the form of actively seek ing collaborations with local scholars, not just having students of diversity, but sending GN students to the GS contexts. Transition from Theoretical and methodological adaptations to theory and methodology co-creation: The works from GS need not be seen merely as theoretical or methodological adaptations. But seen as authentic innovation and theory building in a different socio-material reality. The priorities, the values of the LS community need to be widened for co-creation. Platforms and fora enabling the so-called marginal to redefine the field: These must take shape through an active engagement. Sufficient platforms and fora need to be envisioned, institutionalised and driven by the LS community. These, of course, should not take shape from a point of benevolence of the Global North, but from a vantage point of letting the Global South take their rightful positions in all its multiplicity. This should not be reduced to taking the shape of any affirmative or supportive actions by the dominant Global North, but a deeper acknowledgment and active engagement with the multiplicity of the contextual, theoretical, methodological, analytical and organisational frames to emerge from the multiple socio-material realities. This should not take the shape of merely recognizing the double burden but reflect as an intentional striving to understand the hitherto in-visible or unheard, not with intellectual curiosity but out of necessity of the discipline. The symposium is not a feeble cry of the weak for legitimacy and support, but a clear voice of the strong for a partnership, equal at all levels, in the pursuit of our collective purpose. This is hence not an agitation, but an invitation. And after the symposium, we seek to arrive at some concrete intentional steps that can be taken by the community as a whole in this direction.
