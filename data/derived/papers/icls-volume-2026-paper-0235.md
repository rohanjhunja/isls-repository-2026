# How Middle School Students Engage in Computational Literacies in an Agricultural Class

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Integration of computing in K-12 education has grown with the advent of computational tools in daily life. Computing education researchers now call for understanding of computing as a literacy which is not well understood, especially in the context of non-CS classrooms like agriculture. This study presents insights from integrating computing in a middle school agricultural classroom. The students developed and implemented an experiment using computational tools to investigate the effects of environmental factors on plant health. A comparison and contrasting engagement with computational literacies of two student teams is presented. The paper illustrates computing driven collaboration, tool-centered interactions, and new forms of computing driven engagement through the lens of computational literacies.

The past decade has seen unprecedented momentum in the integration of computing in K-12 education (MoralesNavarro & Kafai, 2022). This move has shaped from a call for computational  thinking (Wing, 2006) to a call for computational perspectives (Grover & Pea, 2013) to now a call for computational literacies  (Kafai & Proctor, 2022). Often, computing education research has focused on the view of computing as a skill or practice to investigate a general definition (Shute et al., 2017), identify disciplinary practices (Weintrop et al., 2016), and operationalize it specific to disciplines (Hurt et al., 2023). Understanding of computing in education research as a literacy remains relatively unexplored (Guzdial, 2022; Kafai & Proctor, 2022), especially in disciplinary classrooms such as agriculture. This is increasi ngly important as such classrooms must  balance computing activities and disciplinary goals and competencies (Margulieux et al., 2024). Thus, what computational literacy means in K-12 context, specifically in such integrated classrooms is important and not well explored. This paper attempts to address this gap by presenting how middle school learners in an agricultural classroom engage in computational literacies. In this study, students designed and conducted experimental research to understand the effect of environmental factors (air, water, and soil) on plant growth using computational tools such as A rduino and sensors. Specifically, we seek answers to the following research questions:

• RQ1: In what ways do middle school student groups in agriculture class engage in computational

literacies during the data collection using computational tools like Arduino and sensors?

• RQ2: How does engagement of computational literacy compare and contrast among two teams with

contrasting status of data collection task?

## Literature review and theoretical framework

Computing has been incorporated in K-12 education in multiple ways that include directly teaching computer science (CS) topics (Guzdial, 2016), interdisciplinary integration of CS in other subjects such as science (Dickes et al., 2020) and through use of makerspaces (Kafai et al., 2014). Computing education research has also focused on understanding computing in disciplinary contexts such as mathematics and science (Hurt et. al, 2023; Weintrop et. al, 2016). While these efforts have enhanced the students’ computing knowledge and skills (Lee et al., 2022), how these efforts manifest as literacy is not well explored (Kafai & Proctor, 2022). In a recent study, Odden and Zwickl (2025) investigated engagement of undergraduate physics students with computational concepts and provided an illustration of how computational literacy is more than coding (a skill). Similarly, Dickes and Farris

(2019) emphasized viewing computing as an ensemble of material literacy and disciplinary practice. More such

studies with diverse contexts are required for a comprehensive understanding of the emerging perspective of computing as literacy (Kafai & Proctor, 2022). This paper addresses this gap and examines middle school students’ computational literacy engagement.

Our theoretical framework is based on three pillars of computational literacy discussed by diSessa (2000). We present computational literacy within the context of using computational tools in a disciplinary practice (e.g., agriculture). The first pillar is material, which is the material understanding of the tool and involves the understanding computational symbolic and notional meaning of the tool (e.g., understanding that “5V” on the 1657 Arduino board stands for positive power terminal) (Dickes & Farris, 2019). The second pillar is cognitive, which involves human intelligence to couple with material intelligence to achieve certain tasks. For instance, cognitively understanding the sensor data and identifying outliers based on observation notes (Weintrop et al., 2016). The third pillar is social literacy which involves development of computational understanding as a community (e.g., developing the sensor circuit as a group) (Kafai & Proctor, 2022).

## Participants and context

This research was conducted as a part of integrating computing (17 lessons; 48 minutes each) in an eight-grade agricultural classroom in the southern United States. The students participated as a team of three to four members assuming a disciplinary role (e.g., soil or water scientist) and conducted an experiment to understand the effects of environmental factors (air, water, and soil) on plant growth. As a part of the unit, the students used computational tools such as Arduino and sensors to collect data. The students received step by step guidance (and a sensor guide) to develop the sensor circuits using wires and breadboard. The study presented in this paper focuses on the lessons in which students devised a data collection plan (lesson 9) and developed sensor circuits and collected data (lessons 10 and 11). The class consisted of 87 students participating as 23 groups from which we purposely sampled two groups for analysis. First, we analyzed the data collection status at the  end of lesson 11 (task involving: collecting and logging data and making plant observations for two weeks). Seven teams completed all the tasks, five teams did not collect sensor data for one or both weeks, eight teams did not make plant observations for one or both weeks, and three teams did not complete both data collection and plant observation. We selected two teams with contrasting status of this task completion. One team (Team A) completed all tasks successfully and another team (Team B) logged sensor data but did not make plant observations. Among the multiple teams with such a status, we selected the teams having sufficient team member interactions to enable us to observe and analyze the student engagement with computational literacies. Data sources and analysis The student interaction data during lessons 9, 10, and 11 was gathered through audio and video recordings and observation notes. Parental consent and student assent was obtained from 60 students (out of 87), including all students in the groups considered in this study, to collect and analyze the data. For analysis, we followed a comparative case study approach (Yin, 2009). The audio recordings were transcribed verbatim,  and students’ videos were analyzed to understand student behaviors of engagement with literacies. The transcripts and behavioral data were coded using constant comparison method until saturation to develop a codebook (see Table

1) based on material, cognitive, and social pillars of literacies discussed by diSessa (2000). Depending on student

engagement with computational literacies, the utterances were assigned none or multiple codes that were thematized and triangulated with observation notes (Mayring, 2015; Yin, 2009). The coded data were further analyzed using a cross-case analysis approach of quantitative comparison followed by a time series analysis. ## Result

Student engagement with computational literacies

## Team A: Successful data collection and plant observations

This team consisted of four members investigating the effect of soil moisture on plant’s leaf color. Two themes emerged from analysis of this teams’ interactions. First, the social engagement within the members was driven through computational artifacts (e.g., data collection plandeveloped in lesson 9) and computational literature (e.g., sensor guide). For instance, before beginning with the data collection task, the agronomist asked other members, “what are you gonna do today?”  and clarified the roles as per the plan. The agronomist reiterated, “I am an agronomist. Sophie, you are the soil scientist. Liam is the water quality specialist,  and you (Ella) are the plant pathologist.” New forms of engagement emerged during the data collection as team members helped each other. For instance, when the soil scientist was unable to follow the guide and was confused on how to start the prominent theme for this team was cognitive reflection. Members in this group engaged in frequent reflection at various stages. For instance, before recording the data, the plant pathologist asked for confirmation from other members if they also found leaf color to be yellow. Similarly, the agronomist reflected on collected data and clarified with their group if they should have multiple entries, “is she supposed to have like 50 mi llion different recordings?” This team’s engagement was thus computing-driven and involved frequent cognitive reflection.

## Team B: Successful data collection but incomplete plant observations

This team consisted of three members investigating the effects of air temperature and soil nitrogen on plant’s leaf color. Similar to the previous team, this team also demonstrated high collaboration but paired with intuition driven steps and high engageme nt with material literacy. The members of this team often relied on informal methods over literature. For instance, before starting with data collection, the members divided tasks spontaneously without referring to their data collection plan. A team member said, “I want to do the kit. I will do the air.” Further, during circuit development, the team members relied on intuition “I am pretty sure everything  is connected” rather than verifying the connections based on the sensor guide. Another theme for this teams’ interactions was high engagement with material literacy. For instance, while following the guide to develop the sensor circuit, the soil scientist reflected tha t he does not have a yellow wire as suggested in the guide. So,  he chose another wire and connected it at designated places and demonstrated material literacy that the color of wires did not matter as long as they are connected at correct locations. This teams’ engagement thus demonstrated intuition driven collaboration and a high level of material literacy.

Cross-case analysis The cross-case analysis of both teams’ engagement with computational literacies revealed similar instances of literacies engagement (131 for Team A; 114 for Team B). Further, the engagement was similar for sub literacies of co-creating artifacts (19.4% for Team A; 26.3% for Team B) and cognitive reflective use (6.2% for Team A; 5.3% for Team B). However, Team A (29.5%) engaged 2.5 times more with social literacy of understanding tools as compared to Team B (12.3%) and frequently used unplugged tools (12.4% for Team A; 1.8% for Team B). Whereas Team B engaged more with functional (6.2% for Team A; 19.3% for Team B) and structural (5.43% for Team A; 11.4% for Team B) aspects of material literacy. Thus, although both the teams’ overall instances of engagement with computational literacies were similar, their engagement varied regarding instances of individual sub literacies. Next, we analyzed the engagement of both teams’ computational literacies over time (see Figure 1). The analysis suggests that initially both the teams engaged in cognitive and social literacies at single instances. 1659 Later in the task however, both the teams demonstrated a limited engagement with cognitive and material literacy. Thus, while both the teams engaged in the task, they did not sufficiently reflect on the steps and evaluate the progress. A notable difference  was observed when the teams started data collection (49th minute and onwards). Team A engaged in all the dimensions of literacy (“C-S-M”) at single instances early in the task as compared to Team B that demonstrated such an engagement considerably later. The cross-case analysis thus suggests that although overall engagement levels with computational literacies were similar among the two teams, differences were observed in the engagement of teams with sub literacies and with literacies over the time. Our findings illustrate what diSessa (2000) conceptualized as new forms of engagement through computing which serves as the answers to our research questions. First, in understanding the student engagement (RQ1), we found a computing driven engagement and tool-centered collaboration. In the case of Team A, we observed that not only was the social engagement based on their plan (a computational artifact) (Odden & Zwickl, 2025), but their nature of engagement also presented new forms (e.g., helping a team member through demonstration rather tha n explanation). This suggests a new form of engagement through computational demonstration where textual and verbal responses are not sufficient. Similarly, in the case of Team B, the team members demonstrated new mental models that diSessa (2000) conceptualized (Blikstein & Moghadam, 2019). For instance, while wondering about the color of wire, the soil scientist in the team quickly reflected that the color of wire does not affect its functional aspect. Thus, within the scope of the task, the member focused on computational function over aesthetic. Second, in understanding the similarities and differences in engagement (RQ2), we found that early engagement with all the computational literacies was likely beneficial for Team A as compared to the high level o f engagement with only material literacies for Team B. Referring to conventional understanding of literacy as the ability to read and write, we assert that computational literacy also means engagement with all the pillars of literacy for executing computational tasks, as also discussed by Dickes and Farris (2019).  Our findings have certain limitations. First, we do not imply that students have developed computational literacy as literacy develops over time (Blikstein & Moghadam, 2019; diSessa, 2000; Odden & Zwickl, 2025) and we view the student engagement only as engagement with literacy and not demonstration of literacy. Second, we do not aim to generalize the claims; rather, our findings are situated in our context and provide insights into a relatively unexplored area. Lastly, both the teams differed in the choice of their sensors and number of team members which might have influenced their engagement. We attempted to mitigate these limitations and enhance the credibility of our findings through multiple d ata sources.  As we conceptualize computing as a literacy, our findings illustrate engagement and trajectories of computational literacies in a non-CS context.
