# Student Perceptions of Creativity Within the Social Ecosystems of Major Fields of Study

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Creativity is an important topic from political, social, and educational perspectives and is meaningful across major fields of study. Little is known about how students understand themselves as creative actors within university ecosystems, nor the ways they perceive their actions and processes as creative within their majors. By applying the five A’s model of creativity, this study positions creativity in the social and material world of the university. Findings include biases that hamper creativity and the affordances of engaging interests and choice, with implications for designing creative spaces, and academic supports for students across disciplines, empowering their future creative contributions in their fields and careers.

In the last 25 years, creativity has become an important topic from political, social, and educational perspectives as countries are concerned about rapidly changing economic and technological conditions (Newton & Newton, 2014; Sawyer & Henriksen, 2023).  In education in the US, creativity is a core component  of oft touted essential “21st century skills.” Creativity is a phenomenon that is challenging to describe. It can be inclusive of actions, processes, people, or objects, and is multisensory (Glăveanu, 2014; Torrance, 1988). Creativity has historically been considered an individual trait (Sawyer & Henriksen, 2023), a process of thinking or solving problems (Zhu & Stevenson, 2019). From that perspective, creativity is generally described as being comprised of originality and usefulness (e.g., Runco & Jaeger, 2012)  that is being expressed in the world (Sawyer & Henriksen, 2023). More recently, researchers have begun to take a sociocultural approach to understanding creativity as situated within a social context (Glăveanu, 2013; Sawyer & Henriksen, 2023). In teaching and learning contexts, creativity is considered a crucial skill. Some recommendations for teaching creativity include supporting goal setting and choice, open discussion, opportunities for both collaboration and solo work, and providing project-based challenges (Newton & Newton, 2014).  In the context of higher education, professors’ beliefs and pedagogical strategies, as well as learning environments themselves, can play a role in how college students perceive themselves in relation to creativity (Zhu & Stevenson, 2019). Despite this, little is known about how students understand themselves as creative actors  within those learning environments, nor the ways they may perceive their actions as creative. Understanding how college students perceive creativity can help empower their future creative contributions in their fields and careers. W hen people think of creativity, they often relate it to the arts (Glăveanu, 2014). Glăveanu (2014) describes this as problematic on theoretical and practical levels, as “the art bias can have serious consequences for one’s understanding of his or her own creative potential” (p.12).  Creativity is not just valued in the arts, but is important across disciplines (Sawyer, 2022). This matters because if creativity is valued across disciplines, we need to be able to understand how creativity is enacted within individual fields of study. For this paper, I take  a sociocultural approach to creativity, aimed at understanding student experience of creativity within the social ecosystems of individual major s. This paper uses  five A’s model as a theoretical framework for positioning creativity in the social  and material world of the university (Glăveanu, 2014). In the five A’s model, creativity  is operationalized by: 1) the acto r, whose personal attributes are within their social context; 2) action, where creative process is both internal ly and externally situated; 3) artifact, the product of the creative action; 4) audience, the social collaborators or observers in the environment; and 5) affordances, the material conditions for creative action (Glăveanu, 2014). Through this study, I hope to understand more about the ways creativity is perceived  and operationalized in college major fields of study  by answering the following research questions: What preconceptions do students have around creativity? How do students understand creativity within the culture of their major field of study? This paper represents a small pilot study  conducted within a centralized learning center at an R1 university in Southern California. The center has a mission to support learning and creat ivity for all students and is especially concerned with understanding and supporting students with varied learning needs. The initial stage of the study was aimed at testing protocols for a larger study around creativity within cultural contexts  among major fields of study at a university. The focus group included two stude nts: 1) Kitty, a freshman political science major; and 2) Melody, a junior communication major. Participant names are self-selected pseudonyms. 1632 The focus group was conducted in spring 2025 and took just over an hour. The protocol began with an open tinker task to support creative and critical thinking throughout the interview (Poce et al., 2019). Participants interacted with materials as material elicitation, toward making something and describ ing it at the end of the interview (Woodward, 2020).  The hope was that object creation  would support creative and critical thinking throughout the interview  (Poce et al., 2019).  Participants continued making throughout the interview as they answered questions encompassing personal history and beliefs, experiences at college and within their major, and perceptions of the professions from their areas of study. The transcript from the focus group was analyzed with a deductive approach using a priori code s (Cresswell & Poth,  2016) based on Glăveanu’s (2013) five A’s of creativity. In second round coding, t hemes around participants’  preconceived notions of creativity aros e, highlighting personal understandings of creativity, the state of the “actor” mindset and perceptions of creative “actions” when engaging with their field of study.

Preconceptions of creativity Both Kitty and Melody initially share the idea that creativity is tied to being artistic. At first, this perspective got in the way of considering themselves creative actors within their majors. Kitty shared, “I think for me, creativity is like, I took a  songwriting class last semester, and it's just like writing down random crazy things in my notes app that I'll never say to someone. I don't know, just random things like that. I don't consider myself very creative.” Because of conflating creativity and art istry, Kitty d idn’t consider herself creative. Melody takes this thinking further, describing creativity as not only artistic, but also something exclusive. She shared, “I guess the way I think of creative things are things that only a select few people cando or like, it's like your box. I just think that I see people in boxes in a way. Like you can occupy two, but I see academic, sports, and arts. I've always put myself in the academic box.” For Melody, creativity is a separate attribute from academic aptitude. Kitty and Melody both describe a personal aptitude for writing, but  both decline to describe writing as creative. For Kitty, that distinction is because she doesn’t see something that is easy, as creative. On writing, she explained, “I guess it's very second nature, I've always been told I'm good at it... I just handed in a paper last Thursday and went over it with my professor, and he was like, ‘this is so good; you have such good insights’ and I'm just thinking, I wrote this in an hour. I don't even remember what it's about. So,  I think what other people might describe as creative, I kind of describe as like doing what you have to do. ” Kitty questions whether something that comes  naturally and is executed quickly can also be creative. She also highlights her  idea that something that is required, like an assignment in a course, is not intrinsically creative. Melody has a similar perspective sharing, “I know I'm good at writing, but it's so second nature that I don't really question it. I don't necessarily feel like a M aster Builder at it because it's just like, I'm just doing it. This is what I always do.” She uses the analogy of Emmet in comparison to a Master Builder in the Lego movie, sharing that academic writing is like building a double-decker couch, but creative writing is  more impressive because, “you're doing something beyond academic writing.” This reinforces the idea of creativity being conflated with the arts and adds a perspective on academic efforts as non-creative endeavors. In academic writing, Melody shared, “it's just me putting my thoughts to words in a way. And so I guess there's nothing like, to me seemingly like unique about that.” Melody does not recognize writing her own thoughts as original. Preconceptions of creativity extended to the sociocultural contexts of their majors. As a political science major, Kitty shared, “I really don't feel like I'm in a super creative major. I think it's kind of more about passion than creativity. And kind of like determination.” Kitty’s description of political science differs from how Melody sees communication. She shared, “just acknowledging that like everyone's creative and like some aspect I think is probably important and that like, you know, degrees like communication, like give people the space to do that.” Despite critiquing herself as non-creative, Melody sees both her peers and the affordances of her major as creative. For both Kitty and Melody, the culture of their major is important to the context of their studies, their academic identity, and their perspective on creativity.

Creativity in a major field of study Despite their preconceived notions around themselves as creative actors and their participation in creative actions, both Kitty and Melody were able to identify creativity aligned with the five A ’s within their fields of study.  As we discussed the five A’s specifically, their perspective on creativity emerged as they described the more personal context of their classes and departments, and the broader context of the field as a whole. Engaging interests remains  important as they move beyond their college setting and consider the professional world of their majors. Kitty shared how she sees creativity in expressing interests and opinions in the broader field of political science. She described political expression as imperative in her field, saying, “I think about how politicians express their opinions.  I think if we have the freedom to express our opinions, we should do something about it. ” She digs into this deeper with the example of Cory Booker making a record-breaking speech on the United States senate floor in 2025. She understands his speechmaking as creative action, leading to an immediate creative artifact, the act itself. “I feel like having the passion to do something for that long and, he literally starved himself. T hat's as creative as I think you can get in my mind. I think it just show s, if you know something's important, even if, like, other people are  against you, you need to pursue it regardless of potential cost, just for the greater good.” Kitty understands creativity in the field of political science as tied to personal and representative interests, as well as freedom of expression. Choice leads to creative action Choice figures heavily in the ways Kitty and Melody describe creativity in their majors. Melody shared t hat the communication department empowers students to inform course content,  saying, “there's such flexibility from professors to make it what we want. So, it's like, if we're more interested in like X subject, let's lean into that and focus on that.”  Through collective choice around course  topics and materials, Melody sees  an opportunity for creative action. Communication students become a creative group of actors, and the syllabus an artifact. Melody also described creativity through choice of expression, or action into artifact. She shared, “I feel like [my communication school] is like a hugely creative space. I remember one class we had this huge list of different ways we could present our finals. Audience and Affordances Context is key to the social audience in both political science and communication. Kitty and Melody describe their peers and professors as their audience. Peers engage as collaborators by observing and giving feedback during presentations, debates, and group projects. In both fields, students sometimes have a chance to present actions and artifacts to the broader student body. When we shift attention to the professional field in each area, the audience changes. For political science, the audience becomes a broader community of politicians and citizens. For communication, most enter fields of entertainment and sports with a broad audience across various forms of media.

The ability to incorporate a creative actor’s personal interests, as well as the ability to choose what to learn and how to express themselves, are the affordances within  the cultures  of both political science and communication. Kitty describes the political science community sharing, “there's just like an understanding that most of us are kind of too aware of what's going on in our world.” That understanding leads to creative actions that are socially engaged and connected to topics the actors feel are important. Melody describes communication as a community “known for our lax nature,” leading to openness for methods of expression and for creating artifacts. Along with affordances of culture, there are also material and financial affordances that affect how creativity emerges. In communications, the facilities are new and full of creative spaces like media labs and sound studios for students to explore and create within. In political science, creativity  emerges despite the facili ties, which Kitty describes as rundown, and her perception that professors are overwhelmed. It’s challenging to understand the experience of creativity as it differs based on individual context. This study gives us an opportunity to consider  the ways creativity unfolds across fields of study in a university setting, and how students understand themselves as creative actors. In this pilot study, students expressed biases around what creativity is and who gets to be creative. These preconceived notions of creativity affected how they felt about themselves and about what constitutes creative action. Some of those biases, like the “art bias,” have been explored in prior studies (e.g., Glăveanu, 2014) and are unsurprising, however they shape how students enter social spaces and contexts of their major. Once Kitty and Melody considered functional rather than artistic creativity, they were able to identify creative actors, action, artifacts, audiences, and affordances within their major s, describing creativity as emerging when their interests are engaged and they are given freedom of choice. While interest-driven learning and choice are understood components of teaching that fosters creativity (Newton & Newton, 2014), this type of teaching is not always prioritized in higher education. This study emphasizes the importance of centering interests and allowing for choice based on those interests, both at the class level and within the ecosystem of a major field of study. This study is limited by its scope. There is much more we need to understand about the cultures of creativity across a university campus. Political science and communication are both professional, social science programs of study and offer just a small view of similarities and differences between the two majors. Creative actors, action, and affordances will likely vary  more widely when the study expands to include  fields of study that are more diverse. This study has the potential to help us understand the ecology of creativity in a university setting, among fields of study. Returning to the context of this study, a centralized learning center that aims to support creativity across the university, this study is meaningful for understanding cultural differences in creativity toward eventually informing the design of creative spaces, workshops, and academic supports that support students across disciplines.
