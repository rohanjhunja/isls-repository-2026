# Thinking with and Beyond the Tool: High School Students’ Use of GenAI During STEM Research Internships

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explored how high school students engaged with Generative AI (GenAI) tools during a summer STEM research internship program, and how their perceptions and responsible use of GenAI for research evolved. The study is grounded in constructivist learning theory and cognitive apprenticeship to understand student perceptions of GenAI for learning and STEM research. Using qualitative open-ended survey questions and semi-structured student interviews, it showcased how students used GenAI tools like ChatGPT and Gemini across research phases, from summarizing literature, clarifying concepts, coding, and data analysis. Students remained mindful of GenAI’s limitations, like hallucinations, while also expressing concerns about being overly reliant. As a preliminary study, this highlights the importance of developing AI literacy in high school students in an authentic, inquiry-based context. The paper also discusses the importance of designing research-based learning environments that strengthen the STEM pipeline while integrating GenAI as a collaborator and thought partner, while encouraging responsible use.

The increased integration of Generative AI (GenAI) tools like ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude in K-12 education continues to underscore the need to study how learners engage and learn with them in different contexts (Mintz et al., 2023). Some studies indicated how students are curious and cautious about using GenAI, primarily using it for brainstorming, problem solving, writing essays, and coding, while also being concerned about misinformation and over-reliance (Bueie et al., 2025). GenAI tools are seen as thought partners and collaborators, and student use is shaped by their instructors, peers, tasks, and learning environments (e.g., Klarin et al., 2024). Not a lot is known about how they engage with these tools in authentic, inquiry-based learning environments, or their understanding of responsible use in contexts beyond their classrooms. We examine high school students’ perceptions and use of GenAI tools in the context of a STEM research internship program where students conducted interdisciplinary research with mentors, while integrating GenAI tools into their activities. The internship program was developed within a research institute at a large midwestern university, in partnership with a public STEM school in the state. The 10-week summer research internship program was designed to provide students from underrepresented backgrounds with opportunities to conduct realworld, interdisciplinary research and mentorship with scientists at the institute. In this study, we investigated how 13 high school students participated in the summer internship program and used GenAI for their research tasks during the internship. The study focused on how their perceptions and use of GenAI evolved, and how mentorship and collaboration supported their responsible use of GenAI. ## Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in constructivism and cognitive apprenticeship theory to understand how students perceived and used GenAI in an informal, inquiry-based learning environment, a summer research internship program. Constructivist learning theories posited that learners actively construct knowledge through inquiry, discourse, experience, and reflection (Kalina & Powell, 2009). As active participants, they make meaning through interaction and participation with mentors, peers, and technologies. In the conte xt of the STEM research internship, it is essential to understand their perceptions and processes in a research context. This gap strongly aligns with recent studies that discussed that student use of GenAI is shifting from getting answers towards testing, comparing, and reflecting on them, indicative of developing AI literacy (e.g. Bueie et al., 2025). Drawing from 1567 constructivist theory, this study explores how students use GenAI in ethical and strategical ways to support their learning.

This study and the research internship draw from cognitive apprenticeship theory (Dennen et al., 2008), which posited that learning happens through guided participation in authentic tasks, as students engage in real-world problems with research mentors. Alongside the guidance and scaffolding from mentors, GenAI tools also provided support when research mentors were unavailable, or in addition to them. Students going to GenAI to seek help to complete research tasks and activities also aided their thinking and learning processes, as they understood the process of providing effective prompts to GenAI, evaluating and checking outputs for misinformation or bias, integrating or contextualizing them to their research project (e.g. Arora et al., 2023; Hmelo-Silver e t al., 2007). This study explores how students worked around these processes, while making choices about when and how to use GenAI in the context of academic integrity and responsible use. These two theories support our exploration of the role of GenAI as students engaged with inquiry, problem solving, academic integrity, and responsible usage within an informal, real-world learning context. Program background This study was conducted within the context of a 10-week summer research internship program for high school students, implemented within a large midwestern public university. The program was designed to strengthen experiential and inquiry-based learning opportunities for high school students from underrepresented backgrounds in partnership with their schools. The research institute partnered with a public residential school that developed an “Introduction to STEM research” course to their juniors and senior s. Researchers provided their mentorship availability, project requirements, prerequisite skills and they were matched with the students who applied for the internship through the school. Most research projects were interdisciplinary, applied STEM focused on topics related to climate change, public health, cybersecurity, with AI and machine learning being prominent in developing tools and solutions. Students participated in tasks like data collection, coding, webscraping, literature review, and data analysi s with guidance and mentorship from the researchers, provided through weekly task roadmaps, discussions with their research cohort, individual meetings, and progress checks. They showcased their work at the end of the internship as a presentation/poster.

## Participants

Thirteen high school students (rising juniors and rising seniors) participated in the 2025 cohort of the internship. Students were selected from applicants who completed the research course and expressed interest in the research projects shared by mentors/researchers.

## Research design and data collection procedures

This qualitative research study uses pre-and post-program surveys with open-ended questions and in-depth, semstructured interviews. All the research procedures were completed after the approval from the university’s IRB. Parent/guardian consent and participant assent were obtained for all students. Eleven students completed the preand post-surveys, and six students participated in the interviews, all of which were administered online.  Survey questions included “What concerns do you have while using Gene rative AI for your research tasks?”, “Describe some research tasks you completed using GenAI during your internship”. Interview questions included, “Walk me through your process of approaching a GenAI tool with a question or a task you had to work on during the internship.” “What does responsible use of GenAI in your research project mean to you?”

Using GenAI across research phases Students engaged actively with GenAI tools over the course of their 10-week internship, for various research tasks from literature reviews to data analysis. They mentioned that GenAI was very helpful in filling knowledge gaps, providing foundational knowledge about a research topic they are working on with their mentors. I had to get familiar with [topic and tool] because we were creating the graphs. Almost all students shared that they used GenAI tools primarily to seek help with coding, as using Python for data analysis or building a model or a prototype was central to all the research projects. All the students had a foundational knowledge in Python, which was also a prerequisite skill required for most research projects, and students used GenAI to seek help with writing long codes, parsing, and identifying errors. Students also discussed that GenAI helped provide them with a structure to solve their problem, a roadmap for the tasks they are working on, and guidance through data analysis. When student A shared a short overview of the tasks for the week and how they envisioned the output, “ it gave me a skeletal structure and simplified things for me because I had never done this before. The responsible use conundrum Students viewed responsible use of GenAI in STEM research as being mindful, reflective, and cautious of when and how to use it. Almost all students shared that they would not plagiarize or use AI-generated work as their own. “[I could] summarize topics for my own understanding and use it for application, but when it is doing your work for you, then that would be an integrity issue.” They discussed being careful of hallucinations and accuracy in outputs, and noted the importance of verifying information, checking codes, and verifying the resources it provides. Student C shared, “For research, I felt it was important to double-check because we are doing important work, and I do not want to be wrong. I would check almost all the information it gives.” Students also talked about the importance of working with GenAI through the process of understanding a topic or solving a problem, rather than merely requesting information and using it to complete the tasks. Students reiterated the importance of guidance and support from their research mentors. While all of them encouraged students to use GenAI for tasks like summaries, finding sources, or coding, they often provided feedback on how to navigate the tools. “ She said it is good to use ChatGPT, but you should also make sure you understand everything it is doing to generate the graphs [you need].” Students also mentioned that while they did not reach out to their mentors for every question they had during the internship, they prioritized and valued their time and when and what to seek their advice on. I do not think AI should replace going to teachers and mentors. I think the whole point of mentoring is to learn from them. However, for some smaller questions I had, I thought it was something I could research on my own. However, if it is a big question, going to the mentor is nice. It is sort of a balance between: do I want to waste their time or is it a meaningful learning experience for me to be able to talk to the mentor?

Students often felt conflicted about where the line is drawn for using AI responsibly for STEM research. Nearly all of them said that it is responsible use of AI as long as the idea comes from humans, but they also shared concerns about their work “coming off as AI.” Student F shared, “ The line is definitely blurry. There are times when I feel that using it makes me seem a little cheap. Because people have succeeded in academia before without ever needing these tools.” However, they also indicated that, taking the time to reflect on the process, learn about new interdisciplinary areas through opportunities like these internships helps them engage with these tools better. “Through the experience of doing the internship, it definitely changed and applied over to my school life and home life as well, in how I use GenAI tools and what I use them for”, student D said. This paper explored how students engaged with GenAI tools during a structured, mentored STEM research internship. Students viewed GenAI tools as collaborative partners that supported their learning and aided the completion of research tasks efficiently. Th ey also discussed being conflicted about being over reliant and the need to reflect on how and what they are using GenAI for, in a research context. Students’ use of GenAI as an interactive, collaborative partner to support their understanding of the topics, building on prior knowledge, and their strategic use of the tools aligns with constructivist theory and recent studies (Bueie et al., 2025), which described the role of student epistemic agency in engaging with GenAI. Students also shared that GenAI supplemented, but did not replace, human mentorship. They used GenAI to expand their knowledge about the research topic, get a roadmap to complete their tasks, check errors in their codes or analysis, while also reaching out to mentors to provide updates, seek specific task-related guidance, and share their project progress, while also indicating how they are using GenAI. This informs how students tried to constantly learn responsible use of GenAI, independently and through their mentors (Arora et al., 2023). Even as students reflected on over-reliance and being conflicted in blurred boundaries of ethical AI use, they voiced caution and exercised practices like code verification, seeking sources, and citations.

## Discussion & Implications

In conclusion, students saw GenAI as a partner that they need to work with awareness and strategy. This preliminary study speaks directly to the ISLS theme of “Partnering with Purpose” by highlighting how students engage with GenAI tools and research mentors as they actively learn in an authentic research environment. This shows how students learn through inquiry, problem solving, and critical thinking, while partnering with GenAI responsibly.
