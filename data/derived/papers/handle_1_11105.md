# Can Speech Ground Mechanical Reasoning During Engineering Discourse?

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Mechanical reasoning is crucial for many engineering fields, yet undergraduate engineering students struggle to understand discipline-specific formalisms from their courses that model mechanical concepts. The current investigation observed undergraduate engineering students’ speech during mechanical reasoning and the benefits of attending to students' speech for assessing their mechanical reasoning and learning. Results from discourse analysis of two undergraduate engineering student dyads enrolled in a Mechanics of Materials course revealed students’ mechanical reasoning through both course-specific and non-course-specific speech. Students generated speech that described functional or dynamic behaviors, as well as structure that described the static features. Students with highly developed formal knowledge of mathematics and physics often reason about mechanical systems in ways not captured traditional formalisms has implications for instruction and assessment.

## Introduction

For many engineers, the concept of torsion is important for describing a basic attribute of mechanical syst ems that experience forces and deformations due to twisting. Across hosts of engineering disciplines, concepts covered theoretically or practically in lab courses are fou ndational to understanding the behavior of mechanic al systems that exist in the real world. Torque refers to the action of exerting forces that perfor m that twisting. Torque, denoted by the Greek letter tau (τ)  is typically defined as a force (F) applied some distance (r) at an angle (θ). and is formally modelled by the equation τ = rFsinθ.

For example, torque is experienced in transmission shafts in vehicles or the crankshaft of a bicycle. Opportunities to express epistemological sense-making through prior embodied experiences related to torque can undergird conceptual development  (Nathan, 2022). However, torque as an abstract concept can be difficult to understand when removed from its real-world referents, resulting in numerous misconceptions. In undergraduate engineering education, instruction and knowledge assessment practices often neglect integrating the diverse ways students’ reason and learn about engineering concepts outside of formalized conventions.

## Theoretical framework

In engineering, acquired knowledge stems predominately from a formalisms first (FF) view of pedagogy, which, as noted by (Nathan, 2012), posits that conceptual meaning making and application (i.e., first-order experiences) of those concepts depends upon first acquiring mastery of mathematical and scientific formalisms (i.e., secondorder experiences, see Figure 1). Laurillard (2013) describes second-order experiences as the descriptions of realworld experiences, often depicted by representations portrayed using formal, symbolic notational systems (i.e., formalisms made up of symbols, equations, diagrams, technical language, etc.) as used in academic and scientific discourse (i.e., classroom discussions, design work, write-ups, and engineering problem-solving). Formalisms are often necessary for conducting scientific work but can impede meaning making because they are often performed in the absence of real-world referents, which impedes construction of a  real-world interpretation. This can have negative consequences for a field such as engineeri ng which is charged with using the powerful formali sms of mathematics and science as the basis for designing solutions to real-world problems. For example, a student who is only shown a formal graph of material stresses (i.e., second-order) but not provided an opportunity to interact with a material under stress may not truly understand how torsion generates stra in, and might therefore fail to generate an appropriate innovation designed to avert a future p roblem. In contrast, first-order experiences are meaningful applications of concepts in practice fro m people’s direct lived experiences. For example, the simple design innovation of affixing a straight line on an object can help undergraduate engineering students to visualize deformations due to torsion, which leads students to directly perceive the strain, and express an understanding through speech and gesture that reveals how the stu dent bridges their first-and second-order understandings (Grondin et al., 2022). Therefore, a primary objective in this study is to explore the first-order experiences students employ during their reasoning.

Educational practices that  focus on  second-order experiences can result in gatekeeping that im pairs students’ abilities to meaningfully connect to concepts and develop necessary foundational knowledge that enables their ascension in academic coursework and future e ngineering career pathways (Danielak et al., 2014). Although formalisms offer tools for modeling and an alysis, they distance students from first-order perceptions and interactions they learn in the real-world. Moreover, students sometimes paradoxically demonstrate proficient reasoning using formalisms despite continuing to struggle applying concepts in practice. In engineering, students can lose their sense-of-belonging and feel isolated from other students based on how they choose to make sense of engineering concepts; these considerations contribute to engineering programs’ attrition (Danielak et al., 2014). Disciplinary practices in engineering pedagogy that integrate students’ first-order experiences and build on their natural sense-making can enhance both successful engineering practices and long-term retention in the profession (Grondin et al., 2022).

Empirically, Nathan (2012) showed that the FF view of conceptual development is misaligned with learners preferred intellectual development in STEM. Nathan (2012) also found that using curriculum materials rooted in an  FF view of conceptual development encourages a formalisms-only mindset among instructors and students. FF privileges symbolic and iconic represe ntations (see Figure 1) as a means for conceptualiz ing engineering phenomena that might not require physical application, or activate complementary ways of reasoning. Alternatively, approaches that use progressive formalization (PF) approaches can engage students in meaningful reasoning and problem solving by leveraging sensori al experiences that serve to ground their understandings of formal symbolic and iconic representations (Nathan, 2012).  Grounding refers to the process in which real-world referents (i.e., first-order experiences) permit mapping of abstractions (i.e., second-order experiences) on meaningful, real-world experiences (Barsalou, 2008).

From a grounded and embodied cognition perspective, students  engage in mechanical reasoning by simulating their sensorimotor  interactions with objects based on their real-world experiences (Barsalou, 2008). This is often revealed in their multimodal speech d uring collaborative reasoning (Goldin-Meadow et al., 1993). However, novice engineers often invoke formalized d escriptions (i.e., second-order) of systems within sociocultural and historical norms of the engineering community even when they are not fully understood. The difficu lties that arise in reasoning through formalisms  or coursespecific speech, especially in terms of assessment, leads us to qu estion how the informal conceptualizations in students’ language (i.e., their independent ways of knowing or non-course-specific speech), can be harnessed to help establish grounded foundations for engineering  instruction. Knowledge assessment practices in engineering often neglect the diverse ways students’ reason abo ut engineering concepts outside of formalized conve ntions (Nathan, 2022). Therefore, an objective in this study is to explore  the correctness of student responses via first-order (i.e., real-world experiences)  and second-order (formal descriptions of first-order experiences) accounts while reasoning about torsion-related concepts. Based on prior research in this area (e.g., Finelli  & Froyd, 2019; Palmer, 2004), w e predict  that formative assessment practices that explicitly  acknowledge students’ informal reasoning by attending to their non-course-specific speech about mechanical systems may provide novel insights about the ways students learn. c. b. a. We used this framing to investigate two research questions. Our first research question (RQ1) asks: What aspects of students' speech, expressed in course-and non-course-specific speech, are indicative of their conceptual understandings of torsion? We hypothesize (H1) that if students have unique real-world experiences that assist in defining their educational experiences, they will draw from these independent ways of knowing when producing conceptualizations through non-course-specific speech. RQ2 asks: How might observations of  students’ independent ways of knowing expressed in their non-course-specific speech complement instructional practices for engineering concepts like torsion? We hypothesi ze (H2) that careful observations of  undergraduate engineering students' discourse in  a Mechanics of Materials lab settings will provide insights, through correctly stated conceptualizations, into how they are constructing knowledge.

## Participants

A convenience sample of eight  native-English speaking undergraduate engineering students were recruited for this pilot study from the summer session of a Mechanics of Materials course offered a Midwest university. This lab-based course is typically taken by second semester mechanical engineering and civil  undergraduate engineering students, following prerequisites in se cond semester calculus and analytic geometry and su ccessful completion of coursework in statics and dynamics. Generally, the summer session of Mechanics of Materials has a low er enrollment with many mechanical and civil undergraduate engineering students engaged in summer internships or co-op opportunities. Initially, this study recruited four dyads, however, some students were absent for the class session in which the study occurred or otherwise did not fully participate. Thus, the final sample was four students grouped into two dyads (for additional demographic information, see Table 1). ## Procedure

In general, the Mechanics of Materials lab offers a hands-on learning experience to observe the concept of torsion, as well as the formal symbolic equations that model torsion behavior. For this pilot study, students first completed an online demographics survey followed by a pre-lab assessment on torsion concepts (see Figure 2). Prior to responding, as part of the pre-assessment, the lab instructor and author of this paper prompted students to explain their understandings of torsion-related conceptual questions (see Table 2) collabor atively and thoroughly. Following the pre-assessment, dyads restated their understanding of the torsion-related questions to their lab instructor before completing a lab activity on torsion. First, the instructor introduced the ADMET material testing system (Figure 3a); next students drew a straight l ine (green) along the gauge length of an undeformed  steel sample rod (Figure 3b) before finally testing the sample until failure (Figure 3c). ## Data collection and analysis

Participants' pre-lab assessments and material testing activities were video recorded and transcribed. Researchers used sociocultural discourse analysis (SCDA; Johnson & Mercer, 2019) to inductively develop classifications of speech during collaborative reasoning and analyze t ranscripts of how student dyads choose to reason ab out the conceptual questions (see Table 2). SCDA accounts for  students’ speech focused on their co-construction of knowledge (i.e., collaborative knowledge constructi on regarding the pre-lab questions) in institutional settings (i.e., classroom and lab spaces), and cultural contexts (i.e.,  undergraduate engineering education). Transcripts from this learning activity provided direct evidence of students’ insights and their emerging conceptualizations in specific learning contexts in which the activity took place (i.e., the classroom and lab spaces). Students’ reasonings were coded as either correct or incorrect regarding the torsion-related concept s from the pre-lab assessment. Correctness of students’ verbal responses were determined by the course instructor,  lab supervisor, and research team. Further, the research team determined whether their responses aligned with cou rse-and or non-coursespecific language.

## Discussion

Undergraduate engineering education frequently reli es on collecting students' written responses using formalizations to assess conceptualizations. Our findings suggest that speech provides evidence of their reasoning about mechanics in ways that are independent of traditional formalisms. Elements of students’ speech reveal ways students are simulating, or mentally re-enacting, their first-order sensorimotor experiences as they generate the ir descriptions of those experiences in support of the ir mechanical reasoning.  This evidence offers engineering educators additional opportunities to observe student reasoning and assess conceptual learning. This pilot study demonstrates that these forms of reasoning can serve as grounded foundations for expanding students’ knowledge of mechanical systems, theoretical constructs (here, the focus is on torsion), and the formal equ ations that model structures and functions in engineering.  In this discussion, we broaden the perspective by o ffering accounts of learning along with practical implications.

This pilot study investigated aspects of speech use d by undergraduate engineering students developing knowledge of torsion. For RQ1, we investigated students' speech (course-specific and non-course-specific) indicative of their conceptual understandings. Transcription analysis of video-recorded pre-lab assessments and subsequent student discussions with their lab instructor showed that students were reasoning with their classmates and lab instructor through both course-specific and non-course-specific speech (see Table 2). Formalized representations of quantitative relations, as noted  by Koedinger & Nathan (2004), can be difficult to understand and inadequate meaning making can consequentially impact  students’ problem-solving, leading to errors of omission and commission. Though that earlier work focused on algebraic thinking, Koedinger & Nathan concluded that formalized representations affected performances and that learning was impaired by the difficulty to comprehend the formal, symbolic representations and correctly apply problem-solving strategies. They found that reasoning that was independent of formalisms (e.g., using invented problem-solving strategies such as guessand-test) enabled  students to apply their personal experiences while also contributing to their emerging understandings of novel algebraic concepts (e.g., what a variable was). In this engineering context, our findings suggest that students equally applied course-specific and non-course-specific speech in their conceptualizations, in support of the first hypothesis (H1).

For RQ2, we explored how attending to students’ ind ependent ways of knowing can complement traditional, lecture-based instructional practices for engineering concepts li ke torsion. Students’ speech was equally aligned with non-course-specific speech. This suggests that reasoning using  non-course-specific speech potentially eased students' ability to comprehend  novel and abstract concepts. It is possible that in order to understand course-specific terminology used during engineering instruction that students may need to relate their first-order experiences described through non-course-specific speech to the more technical and formal language. Students’ attempts to mimic their instructors by using course-specific speech even when it is not well understood, as in the excerpt from P3 when reasoning about frac ture mechanisms between ductile and brittle materia ls, may occur because they believe that it demonstrates an adequate understanding of t he content. Thus, the educational practice that all students use professional vernacular and language when approaching new engineering concepts may not meet the learning needs of some students. In our results, we observed students confusing bend ing-and torsion-related stresses where the crux of these types of s tresses is a force acting at a distance.  There is often crossover between the formalized symbols that represent different quantifiable entit ies that in turn cause confusions (Redish & Kuo, 2015). Thus, it is incumbent on educators to clarify possible sources of these differences in reasoning. In our case, recognition of students' current understandings of torsional-and bendingrelated stresses (represented, in part, as forces acting at  a distance) and mitigat ing their confusion and misconceptions. In support of our second hypothesis (H2), we found evidence that students appl ied their prior experiences to describe concepts. As learners accrue knowledge in pieces (DiSessa, 20 17; Vosniadou, 2013), allowing novice students to include the  non-course-specific language expressing their  prior experiences can be valuable as students develop conceptual understanding as well as helping them adopt the discourse practices that are integral to becoming professional engineers.

Although this study reports discourse patterns emer ging in students' situated speech during mechanical reasoning in the authentic settings of engineering classes and laboratories, there are some limitations we must address. First, as a case study with a small sample  size, we are limited to a non-generalizable qualitative assessment. Thus, we propose a theoretical justification for the evidence we have presented and justifications for our hypothesis and why these patterns might trend in a data set from a larger sample. Fortunately, we are currently processing data from a larger-scale in-situ intervention (N=189) with students from 15 sections of an engineering lab on torsion  that was designed based on our findings from this p ilot study. That dataset will provide a richer, more expansive view of these qualitative discourse practices, as well as the statistical power that can support quantitative analysis to substantiate the current findings and their generalizability. This, in turn,  may allow us to identify pedagogical techniques for fostering successful conceptualizations and applications of knowledge. Lastly, the demographic heterogeneity of this larger data set will better represent a broader population of students t o inform undergraduate engineering education researchers and improve learning outcomes in classrooms. This current study focused on undergraduate engineering students’ emerging conceptualizations o f torsion as revealed  through speech. Insights into students’ emerging conceptualizations may also be revealed through students’ gestures. In a previous study of a Mechanics of Materials lab, Grondin et al. (2022) noted that students gestured in a variety of different ways to represent concepts of torsion. These gestures represent ed the structural properties and the dynamic behavior of the engineering object being tested. While it is possible that students’ confusions between torsional-and bending-related stresses may be  due to their  spatial abilities (Newcombe et al., 2013; Tversky, 2003; Tversky, 201 1), instances of discordant gesture s (i.e., mismatches between gesture and concept) signified when students were amidst reconceptualizing their own prior ways of understanding torsion and a readiness to learn (Goldin-Meadow et al., 1993).  Future work will focus on finding ways to accommodate multiple modalities of students’ communication by attending to gesture, speech, and spatial ability for improving instruction for reasoning about stress development in three dimensional mechanical systems.
