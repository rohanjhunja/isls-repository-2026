# Learning to Thrive and Be Well in Times of Injustice: Teachers’ Approaches to Histories of Police Violence and Resistance

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While debates rage about the proper role of schools in addressing histories of racism and injustice, we have few examples of how teachers across school districts navigate the teaching of such histories during times of ongoing injustice. Using sensemaking theory (Spillane, et al, 2002) and research in the learning sciences on the political dimensions of learning (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Philip, et al. 2018), I explore the enactment narratives of teachers tasked with implementing a mandated curriculum on police violence. I argue that teachers’ experiences of navigating the tensions and contradictions of teaching histories of police violence in the wake of the murder of George Floyd and many others, as well as their understanding of their Black and Brown students as deserving of protection and care, led teachers to develop a specific set of learning goals focused on thriving and well-being. Their implementation approaches that linked relational, affective, and political learning illustrate possibilities to engage in learning histories of harm and resistance while also imagining and enacting just futures in the here and now.

## Background

From 1972 to 1991 Jon Burge, a Chicago police comma nder, led a program of abuse and torture of over 10 0 African American people on the city’s south side.  Decades of struggle led by survivors resulted in an historic and comprehensive reparations package. For the first ti me a major U.S. city would use its public school system as a vehicle to reckon with a history of systemic racism  and police violence.  The RWC is a mandated district-wide curriculum designed to engage students in the 8th and 10th grade. This curriculum traces the history that led to the systematic violation of human rights perpetrated by Jon Burge and the brave resistance of survivors and families. The RWC is not the first attempt to explore and reconcile painful history in the United States, however it is unique in that its development-the result of decades of organizing by activist and  community members-was written through a collaborative process between the city government, the public school district, torture survivors, activists, scholars, and the teachers ’ union. At the time of writing, the curriculum has been imp lemented with tens of thousands of students over the course of the last seven years across diverse school and neighborhood contexts. Civics education and learning for justice The RWC expands on mainstream civics education approaches by centering the lived experiences of ra cialized and otherwise marginalized youth, which are often ignored in civics curricula (Clay & Rubin, 2019; Cohen, et al, 2018; Mirra & Garcia, 2017) and whose histories and experiences with U.S. democracy and the U.S. nation state are frequently flattened or erased (Busey & Walker,  2017; El-Haj, 2009; Rodriguez, 2018; Sabzalian, 2019). Further, the RWC provides opportunities for teachers to engage students in important and ongoing conversations about how their city will memorialize such histories and construct avenues of repair and just futures. Sociocultural theories of learning help to illuminate the ways in which who participants are, where they are situated (in terms of identity, history, and po wer), what they aim to accomplish together, how processes of teaching and learning unfold matter (Philip, Bang, & Jackson, 2018). If we conceive of our classrooms and schools as mini polities (Flanagan, 2013) where we practice  and imagine just democracies and where students’ a nd their families’ experiences and perspectives are valued, then we need to begin to look at the relational  and social dimensions of learning within and across contexts. That is, we will benefit if our scholarship is atte ntive to the pedagogical practices and curricular tools that tea chers create and take up; the histories, identities, and ways of knowing of the participants; and the sociopolitical  contexts that mediate student meaning making and w orld building. Recent scholarship on the political dimensions of learning have challenged scholars to examine the ways that narrow constructions of disciplinary learning and knowledge have constrained not only our designs  for learning but the tools we engage with in order to “see” learning (Warren, et al, 2020). When students' experiences as political actors and meaning makers are recognized and seen as resources for learning, we see opportunities for expansive learning about the social and political world (Mirra & Garcia, 2022; Vossoughi, 2014). Within the civics field, there has been increasing attention to how youth voice and cultivating inclus ive classrooms with warm relationships between educators and students can support more meaningful learning where students feel empowered. Yet despite equity centered initiatives in districts ac ross the country seeking to bolster Social Emotional Learning (SEL), critics have pointed to the challenges of instrumentalist a nd race evasive approaches that ignore the impact of systemic oppre ssion on learners and focus on “fixing” children ra ther than the systems that create the unjust conditions they are asking young people to survive  (Jagers, Rivas-Drake, and Williams, 2019). Instead, h ealing centered practices must be  integrated into social justice teaching practices, particularly for marginalized youth, because just as health and well-being are defined as more than the absence of disea se, justice is more that [sic] the absence of oppression.…Building hope  among youth of color in urban schools requires that educators rethink what really is impo rtant and how healing, and well-being are critical social justice ingredients to reach young people (Ginwright, 2014). Advocates of Transformative SEL,  steeped in ongoing partnerships with educators in s chools, communities and youth organizing settings, argue for practices  that make space for young people to recognize and h eal from the harm caused by systemic injustice while also actively working to dismantle those systems.

## Study design and methods

The study presented in this paper is part of a larg er project to understand teachers experiences of implementing the RWC.  This study, launched in partnership with the Chicag o Public Schools Department of Social Science and Civic Engagement (CPS-SSCE) examined the perspectives of teachers in a district with a great deal of racial, political, and socioeconomic diversity during the l ate winter and early spring of 2021-a time of heightened discussion of the role of anti-Black racism and white supremacy in policing and in education following the murder of George Floyd and the so-called “racial reckoning” which followed in its wak e. Through in-depth interviews with 34 racially diverse CPS teachers who taught th e Reparations Won Curriculum (RWC)  in distinct types of school neighborhood contexts, I sought to understand the ways in which teachers’ identities, contexts, and their understanding of their students shaped implementati on. Specifically,  this study asked: 1.) How did teachers conceptualize the learning goals of the RWC and the  relationship of the RWC to their broader learning goals for students? How did teachers describe enacting the RWC (curriculum and pedagogy) to meet those goals? 2.) How did teachers describe their sensemaking around the implementation environment of the RWC? How, if at all, did teachers describe enacting the RWC (curriculum and pedagogy) to be responsive to their specific implementation environment? And 3.) What was the relationship, if any, between teachers ' conceptualizations of the learning goals and the implementation environment? If and ho w did teachers’ sensemaking around these two aspect s of curriculum implementation shape their enactment of the RWC? Taken together, RQs 1 and 2 enabled me to explore patterns across teacher implementation approaches and identify distinct contextual factors that seemed to be salient for particular approaches. By looking across teachers sensemaking about the imp lementation environment and their learning goals, I was able to consider the role these played in how  teachers took up the curriculum with their students, RQ 3.

Just as the RWC is a tool implemented within a cont ext and brought to life in interaction, this study benefitted from an approach to interview ing as a pedagogical encounter (Vossoughi & Zavala, 2020). Thus, the design of the interview itself intended to create the conditions for expansive and generative co-thinking to occur. I also drew upon archival records available to teachers at the time along with my own knowledge of the curriculum and sociopolitical context as a convener of the curriculum development working group, editor of the RWC, and a long time educator within the district. While these  experiences positioned me as an “insider” in some respects, my role as a scholar from an institution with a his tory of extractive research as well as my own posit ionality as a white, queer, Jewish woman, former CPS teacher and mother of CPS students from a working class backgro und undoubtedly shaped not only my interactions with teacher participants but my analytic lenses as well. Analysis My approach to the research design as well as analysis  draw on interpretive ethnographic research, emphasizin g the importance of taking seriously the meaning pers pectives of study participants. This approach requi res the perspective that teacher participants are not stati c in their own thinking and practice. As such, my a nalytic approach recognized teachers and interviewer as engaging in a process of “becoming” where our ideas, questions, and commitments continue to evolve and change over time.  These layers of complexity required a careful analytic approach that was attentive to the meaning perspectives of teachers and what they wrestled with in their practice. During my data analysis, I developed and tested key assertions through systematic analysis of interview transcripts using a constant comparative method (Glaser & Strau ss, 2017). I analyzed forms of teacher sense-making both about how they conceptualized the goals of the RWC (RQ1), the implementation environment (RQ2) and beg an to identify important em ergent findings and patterns which seemed to be sha ping their enactment (RQ3).  I was also attuned to the ways in which the political context showed up, or not, in teachers’ narrations. As you will see in the findings section, I carefully attended to di screpant cases and shared, where applicable, forms of disconfirming evidence. Finally, my analysis was en hanced by the use of archival records and my contex tual knowledge of what was happening at the time of launch and across the four years of implementation in the district and city. Keeping both the patterned and the discrepant in view allowed me to develop a more adequately complex picture of this phenomenon.

To understand how the RWC lives and breathes in a c lassroom, I take the approach that the curriculum and its embedded pedagogies are tools (Cole, 1998) that teachers use within a particular time and cont ext. Teachers are not simply deliverers of content; they are complex people who not only shape how stude nts engage in the material but who are themselves engaged in their own learning processes as enactors of curriculum. In order to investigate how teachers' understandings of curr icular learning goals and their particular contexts  shape how teachers approach curriculum implementation in thei r contexts, I dr ew on two bodies of literature  to form my conceptual framework. I look to sensemaking theory (Blignaut, 2008; Coburn, 2001; Spillane, Reiser, & Reimer,

2002) in the context of curriculum implementation to unde rstand how teachers interpret and enact curriculum

policy in K12 settings. Further, I bring literature from the learning sciences on the political dimensions of learning (Esmonde & Booker, 2016; Politic s of Learning Writing Collective, 2017; Varelas, Martin,  & Kane, 2013), into conversation with sensemaking theory to expand our analytic capacities as we explore teacher implementation of justice oriented curriculum.

## Findings

Learning goals for a racialized political world Teacher participants in this study believed that in addition to teachi ng the specific history of the RWC, the curriculum provided an opportunity to prepare stude nts with the resources and knowledge they would nee d to move in the racialized political world, as they con ceived it. I identified four overarching categories of learning goals that were centered on helping students interpret and act as members of their communities and their society. I found that teachers’ learning goals for their particular students in their particular contexts shaped how they used the RWC. Thus, two different teachers, teaching two different sets of students in two different community contexts might take the same curriculum and conceive of the learning goals for their students in very different ways; ways that may have been quite consequential to student l earning. I found that these variations in learning goals and enactment were tied to how tea chers conceptualized the sociopolitical context as well as the identities of their students and themselves within that context. Teache rs developed learning goals based on what they perc eived to be important for their students to take from the RW C and the lessons they believed it could provide to  their students within the broader sociopolitical context.

Teachers in this study described a range of learnin g goals including goals related to specific academi c skills like reading primary sources, using evidence to support argumentation, and improving academic writing. However, more often teachers made both explicit and  implicit connections between learning goals for the RWC, their students, and the racialized political world. Therefore, I focus on learning goals that teachers developed that seemed to be directly related to how teachers conce ptualized the sociop olitical context and their students' positionality and power within that world.  I calle d these, collectively, learning goals for the racialized political world because, as teachers discussed their learning goals  for students, they often referred to both the spec ific racialized identities of their students as well as how they hoped engaging in the RWC would support their students in making sense of and acting in a political world that was shaped by racism and inequality. I identified four distinct categories of learning goals that were focused on helping students learn to engage in the racialized political world: Analyzing, Navig ating, Change-Making (1) and Thriving/Well-Being. Teaching for thriving and well-being in times of injustice Teachers who described learning goals within the Thriving/Well-Being category were largely focused on providing learning opportunities that would provide students with the tools and strategies to weather the injustices of a racialized political world in ways that would be restorative and affirming of their humanity.  These teachers were concerned with  helping students develop some of the internal strat egies and habits of mind and heart to withstand the harms to young people brought on by the racialized political world and, importantly, to experience possibilities of thriving and well-being despite of and as a form of resistance to the impacts of police violence, racism and injustice in their communities.

Teachers who talked about learning goals within the  category of Thriving/Well-Being often made a connection between the “heaviness” of the RWC with the events taking place in the world outside of the classroom (i.e.. police violence, COVID-19 pandemic). Their approaches to Thriving/Well-Being were often focused on addressing the toll both might take on the students' mental health. The kinds of learning goals that I coded within this category included things like affirm (13 teachers), learn how to cope (3 teachers), have hope (4 teachers), process (4 teachers), we believe you (4 teachers) and  heal (3 teachers).  For some students who had family members in jail or who had experienced police viole nce, teachers described giving trigger warnings, id entifying ways that students could take care of themselves, a nd holding talking circles to process when things “ got deep.” For example, Ms. Starr a first year white teacher w ho identified as an activist talked about the strain that neighborhood violence was having on her Puerto Rica n and Black students. Three of their school’s 700 students had been killed in a single school year.  When the media released police body cam footage of a 13 year  old CPS student, Adam Toledo, who was shot and killed by po lice, Ms. Starr described the weight of these event s on the children in her classroom and her disappointment that many classrooms did not seem, in her opinion, to ma ke space for students to process and understand these feelings. Many of the teachers who talked about Thriving/WellBeing learning goals also mentioned stopping class and fo regoing planned lessons in order to open space to recognize and make sense of the complex emotions and feelings that came up for students. Rather than s ee such pauses as a detour, these moments were understood by teachers to be a necessary part of learning. It is important to remember that while the story of the RWC was one that survivors ultimately perceived to be a victory, the ongoing acts of police violence that these teachers and their students witnessed (necessitating the kinds of pauses that Ms. Starr took)  during the implementation of the RWC created contra dictions that were challenging for teachers. I share the story of Ms. Tina Smith, a veteran, African Ame rican, special education teacher at an elementary school in a heavily police d, Black community.  Like many of the teachers  in this study, Ms. Smith grappled with how to protect and care for  students whose lives she felt were at the very cen ter of the discussions around the RWC and its relationship to the state of racial justice in Chicago today. In he r view, the RWC represented an opportunity for students to learn about histories that were relevant to their lives and that she believed they would find interesting and empowering. However, while she was generally supportive of th e curriculum, she expressed reservations about teaching it to the students sitting in her classroom given the realities of ongoing police violence.

Ms. Smith taught the RWC once during remote learnin g in the 2020-21 school year and described the inconsistency between the messages of the RWC and t hose of the sociopolitical environment that she and  her students were navigating. Ms. Smith said, [after doing this unit] if you keep seeing on the news people keep getting k illed, and I just learned a unit about reparations and how I want the police to act and how I'm going to make all this change and social justice and nothing ever hap pens, so it's just like looking at me as the teacher like, "Well, what are you teaching me for? Nothing's going to change. Why are we doing this?" And you're still considered not a human being, your life is not of value. So, I don't know how you would really assess the kids on this becaus e it's so much bigger than that than these little assignments that you want them to do, because ultimately you want to see change …..So, I don't know. What did they learn? I'm going to env ision this world and this dream that realistically may never happen for them?

The pain in Ms. Smith’s voice was palpable as she d escribed the contradictions in teaching the RWC in the aftermath of the killing of George Floyd. She was p articularly concerned that her students with specia l needs would be especially vulnerable to police  violence so it was important to her that they learn  about the Jon Burge torture scandal and their rights to not be abused. While she felt the curriculum was important for stu dents, in the wake of the murders of George Floyd, Breonna Taylor, and Adam Toledo, Ms. Smith and others were unsure how to hold the pain of ongoing police violence with th e possibilities that the Reparations Now campaign a nd the RWC curriculum held.  There were ethical and pedago gical challenges for Ms. Smith  and many of the teachers that I interviewed. They  grappled with what it meant to teach students to ce lebrate collective action for justice when they were witnessing the very same kinds of injustices on the streets and on social media. ## Finding hope in struggle

For some teachers, the antidote to the lack of hope  they perceived was to help students identify conne ctions to current organizing efforts to address the ongoing i ssues of police harm. They believed that if student s saw there were people in their communities actively fighting to make change, this would provide them hope that they were not alone and that there were others who believed j ustice was possible. Along with stories of resistance, these teachers wanted their students to understand that the struggle was neithe r quick nor easy and that it required collective action which would inevitably result in setbacks and mistakes. Ms. Wallace, a veteran Filipina teacher invoked the teaching of abolitionist activist  and theorist Mariame Kaba that hope is a discipline. She said, “you have to have that hope because if you don't have th at hope, what else do you have left?” Through these, teachers like Ms. Wallace emphasized and extended the curriculum toward stories of resistance and political possibility in ways that refused to allow pedagogies of despair to  overtake opportunities for students to experience hope and empowerment.

Joy and beauty as antidotes to despair While the more common strategies to counter hopeles sness or despair were through stories of struggle, some teachers put their focus on building spaces within the classroom itself where students’ humanity was r eaffirmed. In these places teachers wanted students to be able to take a breath. For some this meant integrating time for play and silliness; time to “just be kids.”  For one tea cher, this meant also drawing on histories of beaut y and achievement within their communities. Ms. Wright, a  novice biracial teacher who was new to Chicago said she was tired of the idea that to be culturally relevan t teachers had to focus in on stories of pain and t rauma.  After teaching the RWC in the fall of 2020, Ms. Wright ma de a sudden shift. She recounted, “I'm like, wow, t his is a downer. This is sad as hell. So, I literally taught  about the Harlem Renaissance next. Just Black exce llence, you know?”  While she recognized that this might not make sense from a chronological perspective, the move to teach about “Black excellence” was meant to serve as an a ffirmation and a space of sanctuary.  Black people did not have to always be defined by their pain, and importantly, their stories did not have to exist as a constant reminder of or response to oppression.  Ms. Wright prioritized the Harlem Renaissance because she wanted students to see themselves in relation to “excellence and contributions and creativity and intellectual thriving and all of that.” Embodied practices of well-being Several teachers integrated embodied practices of well-being such as yoga and meditation to attend to the social and emotional well-being of their students within the classroom context. Other teachers talked about incorporating breathing exercises and art making. Ms. Meeks, a Bl ack, mid-career teacher who was herself radicalized by the killing of Mike Brown in Ferguson, MO,  described one day which really shaped her thinking about the kinds of practices and learning goals that mattered for her students. After watching the video of the killing of Dante Wright, an unarmed Black motorist who was killed by a white  police officer, Ms. Meeks described having to take an “emotional day.” She said it was too  difficult it was for her to process, let alone have  to explain these things to her students. Ms. Meeks consulted a friend who was a doctoral student studying brain trauma and PTSD for ideas on how to care for herself. The friend suggested th at Ms. Meeks try guided meditation. It was so effective for herself that she decided to bring the guided meditation to try out with her students who liked it so much they asked her to post it on their classes google website so that they could access it from home.  She said that the meditation was “just telling them, ‘You’re important. You matter. Your life is important. No one is saying that your life isn't important, no one can tell you that your life isn't important.’ It's just those affirmations and hearing that over and over, it did wonders for them and for me.” For Ms. Meeks, these weren’t just tools to survive during moments of stress and pain, she also wanted to teach them these strategies to move forward. She wanted her students to carry forward guided meditation and mindfulness so that they could feel empowered in spite of the messages from the sociopolitical environment that told them their liv es didn’t matter. By integrating meditation, Ms. Meeks was linking a pedagogy of wellness and care with a lear ning goal that would allow her students to have the ir own toolkit to be well in the racialized political world.

Building community trust and shared vulnerability Some teachers worked to shift the relational qualities of the learning environment by developing relati onships of trust and vulnerability with students, creating sha red spaces of processing and healing. The RWC was designed to integrate talking circles throughout the unit. H owever, for many of the teachers who described the importance of community trust and shared vulnerability, these circles became a permanent feature in their classrooms throught the year.  When I asked Mr. Wright, the teacher whose words opened up this paper, why he invested so much time in talking circles he said, “ I mean, that's how you get students to open up if y ou want your students to really be engaged in the curriculum. No, I understand it's all healing, right?”  Like many of the participants, Mr. Wright reasoned that for students to be able to access the  RWC, identify connections between the RWC and thei r lives, and entrust their questions and stories to their fe llow classmates and teachers, there would need to b e a space where participants could be vulnerable with each other. T o participate fully, teachers felt that students needed to trust that they would be treated with care. These t eachers also saw themselves as participants in the learning environment, often learning from the students and sharing in experiences of shared vulnerability and mutual care.

## Discussion

An important part of learning to thrive and be well in the racialized political world for this group of teachers was about tending to the wounds that injustice has wrought on individual and collective levels while still creating the space for their young people to experience love, care, and the possibilities of freedom. I situate the teachers I have learned from in this study  within the long history of educators, grandmothers,  aunties, and scholars who have attempted to build learning environments that embrace and emp ower students, even under the most difficult and dehumanizing constraints. These educators wanted their students to  experience new forms of relationality, joy, and hope. They not only described providing respite from injustice, but they were also in many ways planting the seeds for worlds to come. The teachers whose voices  I lift up in this paper take  up these commitments by prioritizing protection and care for young people in times of injustice. They invite us to stretch our understandings of what it means to do justice centered work in sch ool contexts, and they raise new questions about th e kinds of supports teachers need.

Scholars and educators have documented the long and deep histories of libe ratory and fugitive educational practices within Black communities (And erson, 1988; Givens, 2020; Walker, 1996) when state  and private run institutions of learning failed to serv e and in most cases actively worked to dehumanize a nd marginalize. Like the classrooms teachers I described here were trying to build, Black fugitive educational spaces are sites where  the violence of white supremacy and anti-Blackness are contested, where students and their teachers experience relief from dehumanization and oppression,  and importantly, participants have the space to stretch toward freedom  (ross, 2020). Similarly, Indigenous scholars and educators point to the many ways that resurgence in Indigenous knowledge systems and ways of being provide space for youth and their families to live in their full humanity. Simpson (2016) describes resurgence as “a different way of living in the world. It changes us. Engaging in different processes and practices c hanges not only those involved but the outcome. Res urgence is an emergent and generative process” (Simpson, 2016, p. 27). Together, this long history of building educational sanctuary (Espinoza, 2009) points to the ways that marginalized communities have and continue to conceptualize and enact learning as humanizing and dignity conferring social activity (Espinoza & Vossoughi, 2014). The insistence on educational dignity and rights build upon existing and collective ways of knowing and being and extend efforts to bring forth more just w orlds. For those interested in studying and buildin g fugitive spaces of learning, these teachers offer experiences that show how mandated curricula like the RWC can serve as a support and opening for stretching our pedagogies.  The range of practices teachers developed for students are not only important for what they wanted students to learn about the racialized political world, but also because their designed learning environments offered altern ative ways for how we might be and learn together toward more just worlds.  These enactment narratives offer opportunities to challenge the ways that the academic and the social and emotional are positioned in opposition t o each other rather than conceptualizing them as in tegrated. They show how matters of the  heart and matters of the mind are both essential to  our theories of change and learning. Put another way, how we learn together can teach us a lot about the kind s of worlds we are building toward. What we learn about resistance and collective action can also help us to heal our spirits, create classrooms of thriving and well-being, and strengthen ourselves for change-making.
