# A “Co-Design Intervention” with AI analytics to Support the Academically Productive Talk of Title 1 School Math Tutors and their Students: A Comparative Interrupted Time Series Analysis

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines whether a co-design-based professional learning intervention for tutors, supported by an AI feedback tool, influenced students’ Academically Productive Talk (APT) within a high-dosage algebra tutoring program in Title 1 schools in an urban U.S. school district. Using a comparative interrupted time series (CITS) design, we analyzed 9,037 tutoring sessions over the 42 weeks of one academic year, comparing tutors who participated in a nine-week co-design process with those who did not. Tuto rs in the intervention co-designed an AI interface called the “Talk Trees”, visualizing student talk patterns to guide student reflection on collaborative discourse. Results show short-term, positive shifts in students’ relating to academic ideas of their peers following the intervention, with modest, non-significant gains in instances of students making claims and providing reasoning. These findings highlight co-design’s potential to foster dialogic pedagogy in the context of boundary-crossing professional learning. They also demonstrate how CITS methods can evaluate AI-mediated interventions in authentic educational contexts.

Dialogic learning environments depend on students’ opportunities to engage in extended, reasoned discourse with one another. This is an interactional process central to Academically Productive Talk (APT) (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015) and Dialogic Teaching (Alexander, 2020). Despite decades of work establishing the importance of discourse in learning, implementing dialogic pedagogy at scale remains challenging. Educators must attend simultaneously to content, interactional flow, and equity of participation; they often receive little support for interpreting and responding to student talk in real time. Artificial intelligence (AI) tools that can analyze discourse hold promise for helping educators notice patterns of participation and learning (Demszki et al. 2024). However, to meaningfully shape classroom practice, these tools must be designed and implemented as part of broader curricular activity systems  including: professional learning, instructional materials, and teacher –student interactions (Roschelle et al., 2008; Vahey et al., 2013). Prior work by Bush and Brown (2024) describes a co-design process between researchers and tutors in the Saga tutoring program, this led to the development of a prototype AI feedback interface called the Talk Trees, that visualized patterns of student and tutor talk, drawing on designs from the author’s work in the Institute for Student and AI Teaming (Bush Tayne et al., 2026; Breideband, Bush et al., 2023). Through iterative collaboration in a co-design process, tutors developed routines for using the tool to reflect on and improve collaborative discourse practices. They also engaged in iterative design of the AI feedback interface. Initial design studies (Sawaya, Bush Tayne et al., 2026) demonstrated strong engagement and positive perceptions, questions rem ain about whether such professional learning efforts translate into measurable changes in student discourse. The present study addresses this question through a comparative interrupted time series (ITS) analysis of tutoring session data collected across an academic year. We examine whether students of tutors who participated in the co-design intervention exhibited changes in APT that differed from those of students whose tutors did not participate. Specifically, we ask: Did the co-design intervention produce an immediate change in students’ APT, and did post-intervention trends in APT differ from those observed among control tutors and during the pre-intervention period?

By connecting co-design–based professional learning with longitudinal discourse analytics, this study contributes new empirical evidence on how participatory design processes can support the enactment of dialogic pedagogy in scalable, high-dosage tutoring contexts.

## Theoretical background

This study draws on two complementary perspectives: Academically Productive Talk (APT) (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015) and Dialogic Teaching (Alexander, 2020). Both emphasize that learning emerges through reasoning, justification, and building on others’ ideas. Within these frameworks, we focus on APT dialogue acts (turns of talk) known to be correlated with high-quality mathematical instructional environments: students articulating claims, providing evidence, and relating to their peers’ reasoning (Jacobs, Thomas, Engle and Bush, 2025). These dialogic moves serve as observable markers of epistemic engagement, presenting evidence of both social and cognitive conditions where learners actively construct shared understanding, engage in critical thought, and take up one another's ideas as resources for thinking. From a sociocultural perspective, fostering dialogic instruction requires that educators themselves engage in learning processes situated within communities of practice (Rogoff, 1994). Educators’ abilities to notice, interpret, and support student discourse develop as they engage in professional learning environments that make their own teaching visible for reflection. This study situates such professional learning within a co-design model, in which researchers and practitioners collaboratively design, test, and refine tools and pedagogical practices (i.e. routines) in authentic contexts.

Co-design is increasingly recognized as a promising approach for bridging research and practice, as it positions educators as active designers of pedagogical tools and routines rather than recipients of externally developed interventions (Penuel et al., 2007). It can cultivate ownership, contextual adaptation, and relevance by aligning design goals with practitioners’ lived experiences. However, while prior work has demonstrated that codesign fosters engagement, motivation, and shared understanding among e ducators, there remains a need to demonstrate if and how these processes lead to measurable, sustained shifts in instructional practice, particularly in relation to dialogic teaching and APT. Much of the literature focuses on the design process itself rather than its downstream impact on student learning or teacher enactment (Voogt et al., 2015). Consequently, questions persist about if co-design-based professional learning produces observable, positive changes in classroom talk. We conceptualize the co-design intervention in this study as a boundary-crossing activity (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011) linking the domains of research, technology design, and tutoring practice. Co-design has proven effective at bringing tutors and researchers together to develop representations of classroom talk to make students’ reasoning visible and actionable (Brown & Bush, 2024). This process positioned tutors as designers of their own professional learning, rather than passive recipients of training, allowing them to refine components of the design to ground them in personally relevant dialogic principles. Their co-design work, as is common for most co-design studies, did not explore the impact of collaborative design on sustained pedagogical transformation. So, we follow a similar co-design methodology as the one described by Brown and Bush (now Bush Tayne), focus on new AI-powered pedagogical interfaces, and add in the distinct goal of understanding the impact of similar types of design on long-term educator practice.

Together, APT and co-design offer a framework for understanding how professional learning mediated by AI-generated feedback might shape tutors’ discourse practices. By operationalizing dialogic moves within an AI feedback system and examining their longitudinal trajectories, this study seeks to connect the micro-level dynamics of student talk to the macro-level influence of professional learning on pedagogical practice, contributing empirical evidence to a field that has often theorized, but rarely measured, the pedagogical impact of co-design.

The study took place in partnership with Saga, a non-profit organization providing high-dosage, small-group algebra tutoring in under-resourced urban schools. All tutors followed a shared curriculum and instructional model emphasizing collaborative mathematical problem solving. Within this system, a subset of tutors (n = 6) participated in a nine-week co-design intervention (Sawaya et al., 2026) focused on developing and refining an interface that would help them interpret AI-generated discourse visualizati ons and show those visualizations to their students to support their engagement with APT. The remaining tutors (n = 24) served as a comparison group, continuing regular tutoring practices without exposure to the co-design activities. The Talk Tree co-design professional learning model tested in this study has a simple logic model. Tutors engage in frequent (weekly) professional learning meetings with researchers where they are given agency to develop and refine tools. These tools are AI powered interfaces and routines for using those interfaces that focus on student and tutor discourse patterns relating to APT. We theorize that tutor’s perceived agency in creating/adapting these tools combined with analytic insights that would have not been possible without AI, will lead to changes in pedagogy, particularly increased metacognitive reflection, that result in increased instances of students’ APT. These changes are built on the theory that by presenting AI-generated data as a growing tree, it would promote both tutor and student wondering and reflection that was intrinsically motivated and less prone to 428 negative self-talk and gamification than a graph or pie chart. The tree displays were accompanied by student-facing sentence starters, an accessibility support that has been shown effective in promoting student metacognitive reflection about AI-generated discourse data (Bush et al. 2025; Bush Tayne et al., 2026, Breideband et al., 2025). The co-design intervention During the intervention, tutors engaged in two synchronous one hour collaborative sessions and eight weekly 20minute professional learning (PL) sessions with researchers. These sessions used iterative co-design methods (Brown & Bush, 2024) to adapt the AI interface for local use and to meet the needs of their specific students, workplace and workflow. Tutors explored discourse visualizations representing their own recorded sessions, tested conversation prompts with students, and reflected on how visualized feedback could support equitable participation. The AI interface displayed tree-like displays of student and tutor talk, called talk trees, with options to view recent sessions, adjust date ranges, and select conversation prompts aligned with APT principl es. Early in the intervention, the tutors would be given talk trees by the research team and they would work together during weekly one-on-one meetings to construct slides to present to the students that used these trees, sentence starters, and a legend for what the leaves and flowers on the tree represent. ## Data and measures

The dataset included approximately 9,037 tutoring session transcripts from across one school year (42 weeks). The AI system automatically annotated dialogue acts, identifying instances of students: (1) making a claim, (2) providing evidence, and (3) relating to peers. These counts were aggregated by week for each tutor, resulting in a time series of weekly average APT dialogue acts per session. The recorded audio tutoring data was first converted to text using automatic speech recognition (ASR). The resulting transcripts were then segmented into individual utterances using RoBERTa (Liu et al., 2019), a transformer-based language model designed fo r sentence-level classification tasks. To enhance contextual accuracy during segmentation and classification, RoBERTa processed each utterance within an eight-sentence 429 preceding and one-sentence following context window, allowing the model to capture meaning in the broader flow of conversation (Jacobs et al., 2025).

From these processed transcripts, we constructed a panel dataset summarizing weekly measures of student talk-move behaviors. The analytic dataset is organized at the tutor-week level, with one observation per tutor per calendar week. The panel spans the 2024–25 school year, beginning the week of September 2, 2024, and ending the week of June 16, 2025. The sample includes 30 tutors in total, 6 who received the Talk Tree training (treated) and 24 who served as controls, yielding 743 tutor-week observations overall. ## Analytic approach

A CITS design was employed to examine whether patterns in use and change in use over time (slope) of APT differed across the tutors who participated in the co-design intervention and those who did not both before and after the intervention. The “interruption” point occurred at week 31, marking the onset of the co-design intervention. ITS models compared the slopes and intercepts of weekly APT trajectories between the co-design and comparison groups before and after the intervention, assessing both immediate and sustained effects. Analyses were conducted at the tutor level, controlling for variability in session frequency. This CITS approach compares within-tutor changes in student talk-move outcomes for treated tutors, those who received mid-year training, and control tutors who did not. Although treated tutors completed their training over a two-week window (March 17–31, 2025), we use March 31 as a single common interruption point across all treated tutors. Because the analytic sample includes only six treated tutors rather than a full implementation cohort, adopting a unified cutoff date avoids the estimation and weighting complications that arise with staggered adoption (Baker et al, 2025). Aligning all tutors to a shared intervention week concentrates statistical power, simplifies the assessment of pre-trends, and yields a more interpretable post-intervention contrast within calendar-week fixed effects.

For Students Making a Claim and Students Providing Evidence, the estimated effects point in a consistently positive direction. The immediate post-cutoff level shifts are 1.92 (SE = 1.71) and 0.88 (SE = 0.79), respectively, with small and statistically indistinguishable slope differences thereafter. Given that tutoring groups produced, on average, about 13 student claims and 3.5 instances of evidence per session, these post-cutoff increases are potentially meaningful, suggesting that the intervention may have strengthened opportunities for students to articulate and substantiate their reasoning. However, the estimates are accompanied by large standard errors, a reflection of the limited number of treated tutors (n = 6) and the use of clustered inference at bo th tutor and week levels. As a result, while the direction of the coefficients is encouraging, the study lacks sufficient statistical power to make precise causal claims about these patterns. For Students Relating to Another, the estimates indicate a clear and meaningful improvement in students’ dialogic engagement following the intervention. The immediate post-cutoff level change is 2.70 (SE = 1.54), marginally significant at the 10 percent level, while the post-cutoff slope difference is effectively zero (–0.03, SE = 0.34). Considering that students typically engaged in about five such moves per session, the observed increase represents a substantial short-term gain in peer-oriented discourse.  Although the effect does not persist as a continuing upward trend, its magnitude and statistical significance provide credible evidence that the intervention successfully shifted the quality of student talk toward more interactive and socially connected r easoning. The results of this CITS analysis suggest that the co-design-based professional learning intervention had a shortterm, positive pattern on students’ dialogic engagement in the Saga tutoring context. Students whose tutors participated in the co-design sessions showed an immediate increase in Relate to One Another moves, suggesting greater community-oriented discourse after the intervention. While the trends for Make a Claim and Provide Evidence were directionally positive, they were not statistically precise and did not exhibit sustained growth over time. The combination of descriptive divergence between treated and control tutors and modest statistical precision underscores both the potential of and uncertainty about this professional learning co-design model. Short-term shifts in dialogic engagement 432 The pattern of results show an immediate post-intervention increase followed by stabilization. This reflects how tutors may have internalized the most accessible aspects of the dialogic-pedagogies the co-design professional learning was designed to support. The Relate to Another move captures moments when students explicitly acknowledge, respond to, or build upon one another’s contributions. In dialogic pedagogy, such moves represent a shift from teacher-centered initiation-response-evaluation patterns to s hared, collective meaning-making (Alexander, 2020; Mercer et al., 2019). The observed increase suggests that the intervention  helped tutors prioritize relational aspects of student talk, encouraging students to listen, respond, and connect ideas. At the same time, the absence of statistically certain and continued growth in reasoning-oriented talk (claims, evidence) suggests that deeper pedagogical and participatory shifts may require longer, more sustained engagement. Prior studies of dialogic tea ching indicate that while teachers can relatively quickly adopt surface-level interactional routines (e.g., asking students to respond to peers), developing sustained reasoning practices requires iterative reflection, modeling, and feedback (O’Connor & Michaels, 2019). The short time span and uneven attendance among tutors in this study likely constrained such development. Co-design as boundary-crossing professional learning From a theoretical standpoint, the findings align with our framing of co-design as a boundary-crossing activity (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011). The intervention served as a meeting point for the domains of research, technology design, and classroom practice, where tutors and researchers jointly examined representations of student talk generated by the AI feedback tool. These representations, visualizations of talk moves, turn-taking structures, and collaborative sequences, functioned as boundary objects, making complex discourse patterns visible and discussable across professional communities (Star & Griesemer, 1989). Through this process, tutors not only learned to interpret AI-generated feedback but also contributed to refining the feedback mechanisms themselves. This recursive relationship between design and enactment is central to co-design’s promise as professional learning: by participating in the creation of tools, tutors develop deeper conceptual understandings of pedagogy and data interpretation (Penuel et al., 2007; Brown & Bush, 2024). The observed rise in relational talk may thus reflect tutors’ evolving understanding of the social nature of learning, an understanding mediated by their engagement in boundary-crossing design work. However, the limited scale and duration of this intervention highlight a persistent challenge in the co-design literature: while educators report increased reflection and agency during design activities, few studies provide evidence of sustained pedagogical transformation or measurable shifts in student outcomes (Voogt et al., 2015). The current findings contribute to this gap by demonstrating short-term discourse changes detectable through quantitative analysis, but they also reinforce the need for longitudinal, community-based co-design approaches that extend professional learning beyond episodic participation. Methodological reflections Methodologically, this study demonstrates the feasibility of applying comparative interrupted time series (CITS) to analyze the pedagogical effects of AI-mediated professional learning. The ITS design, commonly used in policy evaluation, offers a way to es timate intervention effects using naturally occurring longitudinal data without requiring random assignment. In this case, the CITS approach enabled comparison between treated and control tutors across nearly an entire school year, leveraging approximately 9,000 session transcripts. The results support the parallel-trends assumption, as pre-intervention slopes were statistically indistinguishable between groups. The observed post-intervention divergence strengthens the argument for a causal association, even if the small sample size limits power. This illustrates the trade-offs inherent in early-stage design research: rich contextualization and strong theoretical grounding often come at the expense of statistical precision. Future studies might address this by scaling the co-design model across multiple districts or cohorts to increase sample size and support hierarchical modeling of variation in tutor participation and implementation. CITS also offers a model for how the learning sciences can study interventions that unfold in complex systems over time. When combined with AI-based discourse analytics, it provides a powerful tool for tracking micro-level learning interactions within macro-level change processes. This methodology is particularly relevant for examining AI in education, where interventions often operate continuously and adaptively rather than as discrete treatments.

Design constraints and professional learning dosage The intervention’s dosage and timing offer further insight into the patterns observed. Four of the six treated tutors participated in multiple sessions before the analytic cutoff at week 31, suggesting that the estimated postintervention jump may understa te early gains. The conservative placement of the intervention marker likely smoothed over some of the initial increases evident in the descriptive data. Conversely, the limited professional 433 learning after March due to scheduling conflicts, turnover, and the voluntary nature of participation may explain the plateau that followed.

Addressing equity and AI bias An additional consideration for interpreting these results concerns the reliability and equity of the underlying AI systems. The talk-move data analyzed here were generated through automated speech recognition (ASR) and dialogue-act classification pipelines. As recent work shows (Ezema et al., 2025), ASR models to transcribe talk (that is later processed for APT by different models) often perform less accurately for Black speakers, potentially undercounting contributions by Black tutors and students. Fine tuning done by Ezema et al. did help improve the ASR but the bias in these, and all AI-powered, models must still be considered. Even if the bias occurs at the ASR level and not the semantic processing level, bias here candisproportionately recognize the APT contributions from White students, and miss brilliant contributions of Black youth that deserve to bloom on the trees. This bias would mean the intervention and the methodology to study it would be misaligned with Saga’s goals of promoting equity and access. Further work on model bias here is recommended. This limitation does not invalidate the trends but emphasizes the need for responsible AI auditing in learning analytics. Future analyses should incorporate model confidence scores, fine-tuning for accent and dialectal diversity, and human validation samples. From a design standpoint, integrating opportunities for tutors and students to critique AI outputs can transform these biases into learning moments, extending the boundarycrossing concept to include human–AI collaboration as a form of professional learning and epistemic agency.

## Implications for design and research

Despite its limitations, this study contributes several insights for the design of AI-mediated professional learning. First, short and data-driven co-design interventions can yield measurable discourse improvements, especially in relational aspects of clas sroom talk. Second, sustaining these gains requires extending co-design beyond tool refinement and moving toward ongoing pedagogical inquiry with cycles where educators continuously test, interpret, and reinterpret routines for tool use in authentic environments. Third, the CITS methodology demonstrates how the learning sciences can integrate design-based and quantitative paradigms to evaluate complex, real-world interventions. Future research should pursue three directions:

1. Scaling and replication: Expand the sample of tutors and contexts to test generalizability and enable

multi-level modeling.

2. Sustained professional learning: Develop year-long co-design communities where tutors engage in

reflective cycles of inquiry around AI feedback and design routines and interface versions to meet their specific needs.

3. Equity-focused AI design: Incorporate participatory auditing of AI systems to ensure accuracy across

demographic groups and support critical AI literacy among educators. This study offers cautious but promising evidence that co-design-based professional learning can influence the quality of student discourse in AI-supported tutoring contexts. The short-term gains in relational talk suggest that even limited co-design participation can help tutors foster more interactive, collaborative discussions which are key indicators of dialogic pedagogy. At the same time, the absence of sustained effects underscores the challenge of translating design participation into durable pedagogical transformation. The study also unsettles the notion of co-design’s primary affordance being the act of designing, and suggests further study to explore if the participants’ perception of design agency, or if agency around local and personal adaption, could lead to the same benefits to learners.

Theoretically, these findings extend the view of co-design as a boundary-crossing practice that links research, design, and instruction. Empirically, they demonstrate how longitudinal analytics and CITS methods can reveal subtle, time-sensitive impacts of professional learning. Methodologically, they model how data-intensive, AI-mediated contexts can be studied responsibly and rigorously. While it comes with tradeoffs, future work in this area could explore research designs that attend to within-tutor variation that samples multiple baselines across tutors, with staggered rollouts or within-tutor crossover designs. Ultimately, realizing co-design’s transformative potential will require scaling these efforts through sustained, equity-focused professional learning communities. Doing so will not only deepen educators’ engagement with dialogic pedagogy but also ensure that AI systems in education reflect, and amplify, the full diversity of human thought and voice.
