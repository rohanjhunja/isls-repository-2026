# Rethinking Differentiated Instruction: Lessons from the Pandemic for Today's Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Our study examines teachers’ experiences with Differentiated Instruction (DI) during the pandemic, focusing on teaching modality and student choice. Using interviews with ten teachers, we found that asynchronous formats supported individualized differentiation, while synchronous formats posed challenges. Increased student choice emerged as a key practice, prompting reflection and perspective shifts among teachers. Grounded in student choice literature and Mezirow’s Transformative Learning Theory, findings inform DI research and highlight the importance of reflection in teacher education.

## Introduction

Not all students are alike, and a one-size-fits-all approach is often considered limiting. Differen tiated Instruction (DI) has been widely recognized as a strategy to ad dress diverse student needs and learning styles by tailoring content, process, product,  and learning environment (Tomlinson, 2001, 2014; To mlinson & Imbeau, 2010). Nevertheless, DI is broad, multifaceted, and inhere ntly complex, making its implementation challenging  (Dixon et al., 2014; Tomlinson, 2003), and its use in classrooms limited (e.g., Letzel & Otto, 2019). While the differences between synchronous and asynchronous online instruc tion have been well-documented—highlighting the strengths and challenges of each format (Hrastinski, 2008; Murphy et al., 2011) —little is known about how DI specifically plays out acr oss these two modes. This is a notable gap; especially given the distinct demands and possibilities each modality presents. Additionally, much of the DI literature f ocuses on teacher-level factors— such as motivation, self-efficacy, and assessment practices (Kalinowski et al., 2024; Smale-Jacobse et al., 2019)— with comparatively less attention given to student-level factors, particularly student choice and agency. Implementing DI during typical in-person instruction is often constrained by systemic  and individual barriers such as limited time, lack of one-on-one interaction, insufficient training, standardized curricula, and rigid grading structures (Hartwig & Schwabe, 2018; Mengistie, 2020). The COVID-19 pandemic temporarily disrupted these norms, creating space for teachers to adapt, experiment, and reflect—while navigating different teaching formats. Experiences varied across synchronous and asynchronous modes, and for many, these shifts prompted a rethinking of DI’s possibilities, especially around  student choice. With this backdrop, we seek to ans wer two research questions: how did the modality of online instruction shap e teachers’ experiences with DI, and how did they incorporate student choice, if at all, into their differentiation practices?

## Conceptual framework

Our conceptual framework brings together three core ideas: Differentiated Instruction (DI), student choice for DI, and Transformative Learning Theory.  DI involves tailoring content, process, product, and environment to students’ readiness, interests, and learning profil es, with the goal of supporting every learner (Tomlinson, 2003; Sousa & Tomlinson, 2018). It is based on the idea that teaching should respond to learner differences rather than treat all students the same (Tomlinson, 2001), and draws on Vygotsky’s view that learning is most effective when instruction matches a student’s developmental level  (Subban, 2016). We focus on student-centered DI, using the terms student choice  and student agency  interchangeably to highlight how autonomy and activ e participation improves learning. Research shows that when students have in put into pacing, tasks, or assessments, they are more engaged and take greater ownership of their learning (Brennan, 2021; Kanevsky, 2011; Ryan & Deci, 2000). Lastly, we use Mezirow’s T ransformative Learning Theory (1997, 2018) to under stand how the pandemic acted as a disorienting event that prompted teachers to adapt, rethink, and revise their approaches to DI—especially in ways that center student choice.

## Methodology

This study builds on our previous work presented at  the 2024 ISLS Conference (Raj et al., 2024). Using an interpretivist approach, we explored secondary science teachers’ experiences during the COVID-19 lockdown in Spring 2020. Recruited via purposeful, convenient sampling, we Zoom interviewed (45–120 minutes) ten teachers from socioeconomically diverse schools in a New England state. Although the original study focused on teaching and assessment during emergency remote teaching, th emes related to Differentiated Instruction (DI), student choice, and transformative learning emerged organically. We present four illustrative cases that reflect broade r patterns across participants. Data were analyzed us ing Braun and Clarke’s (2006) thematic analysis, co mbining initial inductive coding with deductive refinement based on our conceptual framework (for a detailed account, see Raj, 2022).

## Findings

To address our first research question—how the modality of online instruction shaped teachers’ experiences with DI—we found that asynchronous formats made differentia tion more manageable, while synchronous formats introduced new challenges. Mia and Myra, who primarily taught asynchronously, described how remote learning opened up more room for individualized instruction. Mia shared, “I think we shifted into a more holistic approach. And, you know, definitely a more creative learning, individualized sort of opportunities, and the remo te learning definitely lends itself off to some of that kind of  more individual instruction.”  Myra added, “When students are working through assignments at different levels and  different approaches, it doesn’t really matter —your neighbor’s doing their own thing anyway. So, it becomes less about leveling and more about personal progress.” In contrast, synchronous instruction limited real-time responsiveness. Teachers struggled without inf ormal cues like body language. As Hazel expl ained, “I felt like differentiation is something I had a b etter handle on in the regular classroom setting. Teaching to a screen was completely different… it came down to one-on-one teaching during office hours, which wasn’t always effective.” Our second research question examined how teachers incorporated student choice into their differentiated practices. Findings suggest that the  pandemic prompted many to reflect on their routine s and experiment with increased student choice. Heather described how one AP student led her to rethink her approach: “He couldn’t just do the worksheets and turn them in, like that was not going to work for him… I ended up coming up with a completely different semester for him… but in the end, he produced a fantastic project, got a great score on his AP exam, and everything worked out perfectly.” Reflecting on the experience, she said, “It showed me the opportunities I might miss if I stick to keeping everything the same for everybody. I’ll be more comfortable making bigger shifts for kids in the future. ” Similarly, Hazel noted the impact of flexibility:  “I’m definitely going to continue being more flexible with timelines… offeri ng more choice on assignments. Even when grades wer en’t attached, students were more likely to complete tasks when they had more choice.” She added, “Student choice is a strategy I’ll keep going forward—more flexibility, more opportunity for students to show success.” These examples show how teachers began to see stude nt-centered differentiation, grounded in choice and flexibility, as both practical and powerful.  We did not find any disconfirming evidence for thes e findings, though it is important to note that these were emergent themes; our interviews did not directly probe these research questions, but rather they surfaced through participants’ responses.

## Discussion and conclusion

This study underscores how teaching modality affects teachers’ experience with DI, how student-level factors like choice matter, and how the pandemic fostered reflective teacher learning. Teachers in synchronous settings struggled to make real-time adjustments, largely due to limited access to informal cues. This points to a need for teacher education programs to consider the specific  challenges of synchronous online environments when designing DI training. In contrast, asynchronous formats offered teachers more flexibility to offer individualized support and feedback. These strengths suggest that elements of asynchronous teaching might be valuable in both online and in-person classrooms. However, balancing such individu alized practices with teachers’ overall workload remains a practical concern, and teacher t raining must address how to implement these strateg ies sustainably.

Our findings also contribute empirically to the growing literature around DI (Idrus et al., 2021; Yuen et al., 2023), calling for deeper engagement with student-level factors. Student choice, which emerged organi cally in our data, played a key role in shaping how teach ers differentiated during the pandemic. These insig hts align with self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci,  2000) and recent work emphasizing the role of stude nt agency in fostering motivation and engagement (Brennan, 2021; Scarparolo & MacKinnon, 2024). Lastly, this study adds to the growing recognition of the pandemic as a mom ent of reflection and transformation in education. The shift to emergency remote teaching created space for teac hers to question existing practices and explore new  ones. In doing so, some discovered the power of student-centered strategies like choice and flexibility. These shifts reflect the potential of experiential learning to catalyze teacher growth, as described in Mezirow’s Transform ative Learning Theory (1997, 2018). As such, teacher education programs should continue creating space for reflective dialogue, helping educators make sense of their experiences and carry forward what matters most.
