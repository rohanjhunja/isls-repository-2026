# Operational Routes: A Construct for Characterizing Intermediate Learners’ Cognitive Navigational Strategies

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As novice learners advance past the introductory stage, they must coordinate their prior and intuitive knowledge with increasingly abstract information. This assimilation process is especially important in physics as unobservable and counterintuitive topics enter the curriculum. We share three case studies of upper-division physics students working with Lagrangian mechanics to showcase their reliance on and confidence in procedural and mathematical knowledge while navigating this conceptually challenging topic. We introduce an ontological innovation – the operational route – to describe this pattern of behavior and discuss its implications for educational research focusing on intermediate learners.

## Introduction

In the domain of physics, problem-solving has been a popular locus for research (Maries & Singh, 2023). Within this body of work, common topics of interest includ e the relationship between conceptual and mathemati cal reasoning (Uhden et al., 2012), the use of generali zable strategies over well-defined problems (Williams, 2018), and the trust students place in mathematics vs. intuition (Yavus, 2015). At times, this work has yi elded generalizable (but physics-specific) frameworks for understanding the ways phy sics students can interact with problem-solving strategies (Gifford & Finkelstein, 2020). Much of  this work has dealt with secondary or introductory college students, possibly due to the relatively large class sizes compared to upper-division physics courses. As such, much less work has specifically investigated intermediate learners (Burkholder et a l., 2020; Caballero et al., 2015).   Furthermore, little work has been done to characterize intermediate knowledge st ructures under generalizable theories, physics-specific or otherwise. Without these theoretical lenses, studie s of intermediate physic s learners  remain scattered and disconnected from each other and isolated from other domains in educational research. In this work, we introduce a formal construct for describing a generalized pattern of behavior to systematize research on the transitional knowledge structures that exist between novice and expert.

## Theoretical framework

This work builds on the theoretical foundation of knowledge in pieces (KiP; diSessa, 19 88), which posits that learners accumulate a large wealth of atomized knowledge elements from their experiences before they ever enter a classroom. The naive network formed from these el ements is highly sensitive to contextual nuance and usually lacks global organization, which explains why novices may simultaneously hold contradictory causal explanations for somewhat-unconnected phenomena. According to KiP, the goal of early instruction is to refine this knowledge network such that elements are activated appropriately and reliably in their relevant contexts. diSessa’s foundational monograph (1993) introduced a particular class of knowledge ele ments, called phenomenological primitives (p-prims). P-prims describe the intuitive, sub-conceptual patterns which underpin causal mechanisms, such as how “closer means strong er” can be observed in explanations of sounds, scen ts, and heat (Hammer, 1996). The clarity of p-prims as a class has allowed researchers to utilize the category for fur ther theory building, either by observing previously documented p-prims in new contexts (Arnell et al., 2023; Barth-Cohen, 2018) or by identifying new p-prims like “you can’t make something from nothing” (Prather et al., 2017) or “rotating bodies bulge outward” (Arnell et al., 2022b). Beyond p-prims, KiP theorists have produced a wide range of ontological innovations (diSessa & Cobb, 2 004) to expand the theory’s lexicon, such as coordination classes (diSessa & Sherin, 1998) and naturalized axioms (Philip, 2011). With each new addition, researchers candescribe knowledge systems and their components with greater precision. Due to KiP’s heavy focus on naive knowledge, the bulk of KiP work has been concerned with young learners, introductory classes, and novices. While KiP theorizes that the revisions observed in these early stages must inevitably continue as novices transition into experts, proportionally les s work has sought to explicate this process beyond its initial stages. Our present work references one ontological category which was first observed in this transitional zone  of intermediate learning: instructional landmar ks (Arnell et al., 2022a). Instructional landmarks are axiomatic rules picked up by students during their classroom instruction. These landmarks have a strong central node  around a well-known physics term or phrase (such as “inertia”), a long with a loose organization of related knowledge elements. Students treat these rules as authoritative – reasoning is only deemed sound so long as it aligns with the rule. Students use the landmark to orient themselves while brainstorming, checking any propos itions against the landmark as a form of conflict resolution. Instructional landmarks are somewhat unique among KiP ontologies, in that they are introduced through instruction and not present in naive knowledge systems. Therefore, they can provide a pilot construct fo r understanding the transitional stages of knowledge between novice and expert. Our work builds on this foundation by introducing a second construct for describing the transitional knowledge states of intermediate learners.

## Methodology

The data which we report here were gathered as part of a larger research project called DynamicsLab. The eventual goal of DynamicsLab is to create a suite of online simulations for exploring archetypal problems from upperdivision physics courses. One of the project’s curr ent objectives has been to create a simulation of a bead strung onto a spinning wire hoop for use in Classical Mech anics courses. The “bead on a spinning wire hoop” i s a relatively common problem for examining a topic known as Lagrangian mechanics (Taylor, 2005, p. 260). Lagrangian mechanics is a challenging subject, even for seasoned physicists. Thankfully, the results of this work do not necessitate an in-depth understanding of the concept. Nevertheless, w e will provide a summary here for reference. In Introductory Physics courses, students are introduced tonewton’s laws of motio n, which can model simple phenomena and only require algebra  to construct and manipulate (though, some calculus  can be helpful to understand the derivation of the models). However, using the Newtonian approach becomes far more challenging (perhaps even untenable) in complex cases, such as when air resistance is considered. The methods which can handle these complex phenomena require an understanding of calculus and differential equations. Once physics students have completed these math courses, they may take Classical Mechanics and finally be introduced to one such method:  Lagrangian mechanics. The Lagrangian function (or simply, the Lagrangian) is defined as the difference between the kinetic energy and the potential energy of a system as measured from any generalized coordinate system. The Lagrangian’s utility comes from its interaction with a generic differential equation known as the Euler-Lagrange equation. When the Lagrangian is combined with the Euler-Lagrange equation, they allow students to construct equations of motion – the same type of output produced by Newton’s laws. Once mastered, the Lagrangian becomes a powerful tool for intermediate physics students to examine and analyze a much larger variety of more complex phenomena than would be possible with Newton's laws alone. Students who had recently completed the Classical M echanics course at a large university in the Intermountain West region of the US were invited to participate in clinical interviews to provide feedback on the new DynamicsLab simulation. From the 12 students in the course, 3 a greed to meet with the lead author for roughly 1.5 hours each for individual, semi-structured interview s. First, the students explained their current understanding of the Lagrangian topic. Afterwards, they worked through the bead-on-a-hoop problem on pen and paper. Finally, the students engaged with the DynamicsLab simulation through guided exercises and gave their feedback. The data for this current work comes mainly from the initial exploratory discussions. The inter view transcripts were coded using a grounded approach (C orbin & Strauss, 2008) in accordance with the KiP-aligned framework of knowledge analysis (diSessa et al., 2015), which places the structure of participants’ knowledge at the focal point. Through an iterative process of ob serving points of interest, schematizing the observ ations, and systematizing the resulting codes (Barth-Cohen et al., 2023), patterns of student behavior a nd response became evident. Given the small number of participants, we  also drew heavily upon “explanation building” case  study analysis (Yin, 2014, p. 147). We alternated between  studying each case and revising our theoret ical construct to progressively hone the details until a stable description had been reached.

## Case study #1: Youssef

Youssef worked as a math tutor and was, unsurprisingly, quite confident in his ability to work with mathematical expressions and perform derivatives or algebraic manipulations. Youssef commented that he sees the Lagrangian as an “analog of finding the relative maximum and minimum of a function,” and later reiterated “I tend to view it mathematically.” At several points, he even referred to the process simply as “the formulation.” When asked about the utility of the Lagrangian, Youssef stated that “it helps us develop equations of motion for dynamic systems, and so it’s a framework for analyzing systems compa rable to using a Newton’s Second Law framework.” So me tension with the Lagrangian was brought to the surface when Youssef was asked to describe how the Lagrangian equations relate to the physical system: I do my best to understand the mathematics, knowing  that it produces this description of dynamic systems. But I don't know, it's not quite i ntuitive to me how this Lagrangian relates exactly to the system itself. I mean, it is based o n the energy of the system, and we know the Lagrangian is the kinetic energy minus the potentia l energy. Why it's the difference in those energies, I have no idea. In class, when that was f irst introduced, we deduced through a discussion, well, Lagrange must have said it has to be this way to satisfy Newton's second law. And so, that's also not intuitive to me… it is a little mysterious to me. Elsewhere, Youssef compared the Lagrangian to a related physics topic, the Hamiltonian, saying “we’ve spent just a couple lectures on Hamiltonian formula tion, which is more intuitive because it’s the sum of that potential and kinetic energy. So, you’re just looki ng at the total energy. That makes a lot more sense.” Despite this conceptual detachment, Youssef placed a large amount of trust in the Lagrangian. In fact, when the Lagrangian was compared directly tonewton’s laws, Youssef revealed: I view it as kind of equal tonewton’s second law, but I view the Euler-Lagrange equation as perhaps a bit more useful in a mathematical sense… it’s this functional analog of an optimization, and that means that it could, I mean,  I haven’t tried this, but I imagine it could potentially be applied to other problems that aren’t dynamic systems. Youssef proposed one such scenario: “if we take the  simpler case, just the optimization of a function, how does that apply to the real world? And I think it’s easy to see that it does… some function descri bes, like, hours studying, hours working versus productivity, and we could see there’s a clear maximum or minimum, there’s a clear optimization of it.” Notably, the m athematical process which Youssef was connecting to  the real world here was not the Lagrangian, but rather funct ion optimization in a generic sense (though, given Youssef’s understanding of the former as an analog of the lat ter, his conflation of the two is understandable). Throughout the interview, Youssef never provided a specific ex ample of a physical situation where Lagrangians wou ld be utilized. Instead, his suggestions were always non-specific, such as systems where “energy is conserved.” On two occasions, Youssef enumerated a list of step s which constitute the Lagrangian process. In the first, he listed “choosing generalized coordinates… writing down the Lagrangian… [and] solving that.” The next time, he included a preliminary step to “dra w a picture…[then] determine the generalized coordi nate and formulate and solve.” Youssef indicated that the initial steps were the most challenging, saying “just writing down your Lagrangian from the start, I think that’s the hardest part for me” and late r “the hardest part for me, it’s just choosing the generalized coordinates.” While these statements may appear to contradict each other, it could be argued that choosing the generalized coordinates is  a part of writing down the initial Lagrangian equa tion. Additionally, both stated hurdles come before the “solve” step, in whi ch Youssef lump ed together the rather diverse set of mathematical operations: partial der ivatives and time derivatives, algebraic manipulati on, and finding solutions to a differential equation.

When asked how he would go about assessing the validity of his final answers, Youssef conceded that it was “an important step” and indicated: Most of the systems that we’ve looked at, one can get an intuitive sense of what the particle or object we’re looking at, how it’s going to move. And so that’s normally how I double check, often just plugging in specific points or initial c onditions and saying, well, I would expect it to be at rest under these conditions… but not always. If the equation turns out to be more complex, I can’t always picture that in my head – what the coordinate is, how it’s changing over time.

## Case study #2: Cait

Cait sees herself as an atypical physics student. W hile she has worked as a TA for several introductor y physics labs and a few astronomy courses, she identified herself as “more of an engineer when it comes to physics.” Cait mentioned that she had her own methods for solving certain problems, and that “everybody in class did not like me doing [that] because I do it very wrong.” In fac t, Cait utilized one of these alternative methods d uring the interview when she encountered a roadblock finding the velocity vector (which was needed to describe the kinetic energy for the Lagrangian). She took inspiration fr om her experience approximating the length of a pat h across the surface of a sphere from its longitudinal and e quatorial components. This prompted her to construc t a right triangle, which enabled her to visualize the normal  and tangential components of the velocity. Even th ough this method was successful, Cait was still apologetic and said, “I probably shouldn't have used it in the interview because it's completely wrong.” When first asked about Lagrange’s equations, Cait s tated “I’m not entirely sure what they are, but I d o know what they do, and that’s finding these equatio ns of motion.” Cait recognized a strong connection between the Lagrangian and Newtonian approaches, saying “it  seems like just a different mathematical way of do ing the same stuff. You know, all of classical mechanics was looking for those equations of motion, and this i s the same exact thing.” Later, she described the Lagrangian as a “weird way of mathematical thinking.” Cait was aware that there was some connection to the real world, since “it deals with different types of energies,” though she admitted “honestly, I have no idea how they got [the equatio n].” Although her instructor had attempted to expla in the derivation of the Lagrang ian, Cait lamented “it wasn’t nearly as connected a s I would have liked… he related every step back to the physical world, and I never understood them more than when I was in that class,  but the second I got out and tried to  do my own homework, it kind of dissipated.” This al so wasn’t the first time the connection between physics and the real world was challenging to see: There's a couple of things in non-mechanics courses that have been that way, such as the gamma factor in special relativity, where I'm not really sure where it came from. They'll always tell you it's some sort of geometry, but I'm not good at geo metry. But in mechanics, everything feels a little bit more intuitive. That's the world that we live in. Even the Hamiltonian in its not-energy form feels more solid. But I just can't seem to connect this one. Despite these conceptual shortcomings, Cait had a c lear idea for the operations required for the Lagrangian: “First and foremost, you have to deal with your generalized coordinates… the second step would be writing down the potential and kinetic energies in those generalized coordinates... and then you just combine those and you get your Lagrangian, and then you can plug it into Lagrange’ s equations.” Regarding these equations, Cait asserted “I know how to use them” and that they had a clear order: the Euler-Lagrange equation comes “after you have the Lagrangian, and when you’re looking fo r equations of motion.” In this sequence, Cait felt  that the initiation was the most difficult part: “I think fi nding the generalized coordinates themselves is def initely the hardest part…the rest is just partial derivatives or second order differentials.” Cait relied on a variety of heuristic confirmations  to check her answers, but felt that her methods ha d limits, especially as the problems became more diff icult: “If it's a less complex problem, then it's p retty obvious if you have the wrong answer by just  drawing a picture, doing free-body diagrams, intuition, stuff like that... but if it's a much more complex or abstract one, I'm not really sure how to determine if it's correct.”

## Case study #3: Alena

Alena had the good fortune of joining a research pr oject, which had her learn related Classical Mechanics top ics about one month ahead of when her course reached La grangians: “when I actually went into that subject in the class, I was already kind of like, oh yeah, I remem ber reading about this… he was even having me check  out the same textbook we used in class…I kind of had a month's head start on this subject.” Even still, the material wasn’t without its challenges, as Alena noted “some of it tripped me up.” To Alena, the Classical Mechanics course was a repr ise of the Introductory Physics material through a more rigorous mathematical lens: “we learned a lot about that stuff in first year physics, but then to  go back and use more heavy math and go through the proofs and s ee how we get to this point, I think it was worth i t.” In line with this, Alena described the Euler-Lagrange equation as “more math” than physics. Furthermore, Alena felt that the Lagrangian became more useful “when it comes to a lot more complex systems.” She elaborated: I remember we did an example using Lagrange’s equat ions… that becomes very complicated to solve with Newton’s equations. But, if you can u se the energy of the system… you can use those instead of your actual coordinates, and you would still get the answer… [the Lagrangian] helped us figure this out way simpler than Newton’s method. When asked if she could connect the Lagrangian to the physical reality in these more complex problems, Alena responded “it can be harder for sure.” She of fered the counterexample of projectile motion to identify a phenomenon that was easy to intuit, and while she didn’t elabo rate any further on her feelings specific to the Lagrangian, she pivoted to a different domain in physics to encapsulate her experiences with non-intuitive topics: The quantum mechanical world is so beyond what we c an really understand from our perspective, right? I can see a ball getting launch ed into the air pretty easily in a 3D space… take the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, how you can't necessarily know its p osition or its momentum at the same time… all that kind of stuff w ith quantum mechanics, that's a world that's like, that's heavy. That's hard to visualize.

Alena gave the clearest description of the procedur e for using Lagrangians: “you take your kinetic energy, subtract your potential energy, and then af ter that, you plug them into the Euler-Lagrange equations, which are a set of differential equations that,  once you put in your variables, will come to a diff erential equation in your coordinates that you can solve for an equation of motion.” Later, she also stated “the first step is just, kind of draw what’s going on.” Alena felt that the mathematical operations in the latter steps “tend to be not too bad.” Instead, it was the start of the problem that once again posed the greatest challenge: I wouldn't even know where to start, necessarily. I  find that usually to be my problem when it comes to physics problems in general, homework, it’s like, where do I even begin? Like, what's the starting step? If I can get the starting step, usually I can intuit my way towards the answer… you give any physicist (who at least understands the topic) the starting stuff, you tell them how to start it, they can pretty much finish it, usually, because they know the steps. Alena admitted that this hurdle was so persistent, she had to seek outside help on several occasions: I'd look some stuff up online to just see what other people were doing. I never liked looking up answers… I never liked doing that. It's definitely a feeling of integrity for me… I want to know what I'm doing. It's just the starting place has be en such a h iccup for me, I need to see what other people are doing to start… I was looking up solving the first step. When dealing with classical problems, Alena felt th at her sense of intuition was sufficient to determi ne if her solution was correct. However, this confiden ce waned as the complexity of the problems rose. Ev entually, Alena returned to the reliability of mathematics fo r confirmation: “using just intuition, I feel like if you're trying to get a rigorous proof that your answer is correct, it won't take you all the way. You need to have s ome kind of backup with that, mathematically.” While Alena’s de scriptions of the Lagrangian continue to lean more on procedural knowledge than conceptual knowledge, she  made nearly no mention of any confusion regarding the origin or derivation of the Lagrangian. This may be due to her early exposure to the precursory topics through her research, or it may be that any gaps in her interna l explanation of the process simply didn’t concern her as much as they had the other two participants. Instead, Al ena spent far more time recounting the trouble she faced when setting up physics problems, Lagrangian or otherwise.

## Discussion

Youssef, Cait, and Alena displayed many similaritie s. They each describe d the Lagrangian as a primar ily mathematical tool. They showed confidence in both the Lagrangian’s reliability and their own capacity to use the method (at least, once they got the ball rolling). Each participant articulated the stepwise procedure for navigating the problems, singling out the initial steps as the  most troublesome. Youssef and Cait both commented on the counterintuitive nature of the Lagrangian, though A lena only referenced non-Lagrangian topics to describe a similar feeling. Finally, all three participants fe lt that solutions to simpler problems could be chec ked on pure intuition, but that the usefulness of intuition dropped off as problems became more complex or abstract. Together, these shared behaviors outline a novel construct fo r characterizing how intermediate learners interact  with problem-solving procedures acquired during formal instruction. During the coding process, the confidence the parti cipants displayed in the Lagrangian method was reminiscent of the authoritative attitude students have shown toward instructional landmarks (Arnell et al., 2022a). This prompted an inquiry as to whether the patterns  in our data could be candidates for new instructio nal landmarks. Our participants' use of the Lagrangian method did rely on a clear central idea obtained du ring instruction, but the divergence emerged when analyz ing how the participants used the Lagrangian. Instructional landmarks were characterized by overarching rules w hich defined an acceptable region of solutions duri ng freeform sensemaking sessions; additionally, the landma rks gave students a litmus test for evaluating the validity of proposed solutions. In contrast, the patterns seen here were constrained, linear processes toward an explic it goal which was known from the start. Here, their primary  purpose seemed to be a recipe of sorts, giving ste p-by-step instructions applicable to this one species of problem. Due to the pattern’s similarity with instructional landmarks, we chose to continue with the analogy of orienteering (Golden et al., 1987), a sport in which competitors must navigate a path between a specified start and end point while visiting intermediate locations. As there are a multitude of possible paths, orienteers use the term “route” to refer to any one particular path which c onnects the two chosen locations. Different routes may vary their choice of trail, but all routes must visit th e course’s intermediary locations along their way. Using this analogy, we introduce the construct of operational routes and outline a preliminary set of characteristics:

1. Ordinal List: An operational route consists of a list of procedura l steps, which follow a specific order.

Optional steps may be included, and learners are fr ee to customize the smaller-grain moves which accomplish each individual step.

2. Destination: Operational routes lead to known outcomes. Some routes may lead to general solutions and

can be transferred across domains, while others maintain domain specificity. The chosen destination will inform which operational routes are relevant for the chosen task.

3. Reliable: Operational routes are inerrant methods which amass support through frequent use and

empirical confirmation. So long as the learner choo ses the correct operational route for a problem and executes it without mistake, the solution will be reached without question.

4. Trailhead: The starting point of an operational route involves the most agency as learners separate

relevant information from background noise and identify conceptual signposts. Decisions made here will have cascading effects throughout the subsequent steps of the route.

5. Difficult Terrain: While intuition may play a supportive role in the na vigation, operational routes’

usefulness lies in their ability to guide learners through challenging cognitive tasks which likely co uld not be traversed on intuition alone. As problems be come more abstract or complex, the necessity of an operational route increases.

The intermediate students’ use of Lagrange’s equati ons provides an archetypal example for an operational route. The ordinal list consists of 1) choosing a generalized coordinate, 2) finding the k inetic and potential energy terms in that coordinate, 3) apply ing derivatives to the Lagrangian to plug it into t he Euler-Lagrange equation, and 4) manipulating the resultin g equation to find a comprehensible equation of mot ion. At various times, each participant included drawing a picture as a preliminary step, but  the inconsistency of its inclusion suggests that it is not necessary for com pleting the overall task; in contrast, the core ste ps cannot be skipped or reordered as each one requires the compl etion of the preceding action. At the same time, th ere is flexibility in how these goals are reached. Cait employed unique strategies during her problem-solving, but these were ultimately in service of writing the Lagrangian – a task to which Youssef and Alena were also beholden. The expectation from all three participants was that solving the Lagrangian would provide them with the equations of motion for a particular system. This clear destination leaves little room for any uncertainty as to when the Lagrangian would come in handy. Furthermore, the Lagrangian is introduced in the classroom as a centuriesold technique that has been proven effective: so long as the mathematical operations are performed correctly, the Lagrangian will produce equations of motion in the end. These factors frame the Lagrangian as highly r eliable, even to the point of being unquestionable (at least, in its ability to produce useful results). Youssef, Cait, and Alena all agreed that the initia l steps of the method – choosing which generalized coordinates to use and writing down the kinetic and  potential energies in those coordinates – were the most challenging. Alena’s lament of “where do I even begin” captures the feeling in a manner no doubt familiar to any educator. The difficulties that plague this figurat ive trailhead appear to arise from the large amount  of freedom and choice inherent to such early steps. For the co ordinate, students can technically pick any referen ce point. However, some coordinates will be much better suite d for the problem: their origin and direction will align with other factors in the problem space, greatly simplifying the subsequent mathematics. Thus, it takes a bit of practice to develop an intuition for which coordinate will b e easiest. Without this guiding sense, novices may have to use trial and error to determine the most suitable coor dinate system. Furthermore, the construction of the  energy equation is anything but trivial. Aside from knowing how to mathematically represent the various forms of energy, learners must also identify how many ‘reservoirs’ of energy exist within the system – and then, somehow, confirm whether or not they missed any. Once again, many n ovices will have to restart problems after they dis cover that a necessary portion of the energy equation was either incorrectly modeled or missed altogether. Later steps in the Lagrangian are far more deterministic; the output f rom the previous step becomes the input in the next, and the tasks shift from being generative to following a clear mathematical progression through known operations. These characteristics drastically reduce the agency requi red to reach the destination from that point and re inforce the reliability of the method.

The Lagrangian’s mathematical sophistication grants  it the power to grapple with far more complex phenomena than Newton’s method. In fact, the comple xity of many of the archetypal Lagrangian problems rendered them conceptually obtuse for some of our p articipants. Without the clear instructions provide d by the Lagrangian method, the likelihood of the students f inding equations of motion for the ‘bead on a hoop’  scenario would have been near impossible. Nevertheless, all three participants demonstrated their capability to  complete an example Lagrangian problem regardless of any con ceptual troubles. It should be noted that we do not  believe that a lack of conceptual underpinning is a definin g characteristic of operational routes; such an ass ertion would need to be confirmed across multiple domains, but e ven so, our anecdotal experience leads us to believ e that operational routes exist that have robust ties to t he conceptual mechanisms of physical phenomena. Ins tead, the takeaway should be that operational routes may allow students to access and explore challenging problem-solving spaces ‘ahead of the curve’ or before their conceptual understanding has developed to match. Operational routes such as the Lagrangian play an i mportant role in intermediate students’ learning by providing a clear scaffolding for the actions requi red to solve a class of problems. In naming this co nstruct and describing its core attributes, we contribute to th e continued expansion of the ontological lexicon in  the learning sciences and promote deeper studies of the transiti onal structures of knowledge that emerge in interme diate learners.

Future directions Together, instructional landmarks and operational r outes indicate that intermediate learners are attem pting to assimilate abstract classroom facts and procedures into their developing knowledge structures. At time s, these new pieces may be confusing or even run counter to their intuition. The manner in which intermediate l earners address and potentially resolve these cognitive conflicts is an important area of study for educational researchers; a richer clarity of intermediate knowledge could ca rry implications for upper-division curricula and educational tools. With our introduction of operational routes as a construct for identifying (and eventually comp aring) intermediate learners’ problem-solving strategies, we hope to empower educational researchers who  are developing such instructional materials with a clearer picture of their students’ cognitive behaviors. There are several opportunities for continued resea rch in this domain. First, it will be necessary to examine how instructional landmarks and operational  routes interact, if at all; their shared axiomatic  nature suggests some level of synergy, but their ap parent divergence between free-form and linear cognitive exercises warrants further analysis. Second, these intermedia te constructs need to be situated within the larger  KiP theory. This can be accomplished by inspecting their relati onship to prior and intuitive knowledge elements, c omparing their characteristics to other KiP ontologies, and tracking their continued development into higher le vels of expertise. Third, axiological questions must be raised about these constructs; it is one thing to observe intermediate learners and characterize their navigational strategies, but it is another to ask whether or not the patterns seen are desirable. For instance, operational routes may be extending learners’ reach beyond what would normall y be accessible from thei r still-developing conceptual foundations, promoting cognit ive challenge and growth. Alternatively, the unquestioned nature of such rule s may be contributing to epistemological beliefs th at cast scientific knowledge as a collection of objectively  true stat ements that override all objections. Careful consideration will be required to tease out the man y nuanced effects and influences which may take pla ce in the complex dynamic system of intermediate knowledge.

The knowledge structures of intermediate learners are in a peculiar state of flux as intuition and normative science knowledge must be brought into accordance. It is a critical goal of educational research to understand the ways in which these learners incorporate non-intuitive information – equations, laws, procedures, abstract models, and the like – into their knowledge system. To this end, ontologic al categories such as operational routes can enable the comparison of disparate literature acros s domains. In time, such work could identify interdisciplinary similarities, reveal common templates, and formulat e generalizable postulates to better understand the complexities of knowledge in transition.
