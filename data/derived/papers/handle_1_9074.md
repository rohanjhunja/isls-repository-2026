# Testing Simple Instructional Models to Promote the Spontaneous Transfer of Inquiry Strategies in New Contexts

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Science simulations enable students to conduct rapid experimentation. Many students conduct sub-optimal experiments, even after instruction in effective inquiry. Consequently, they may not discover the underlying scientific regularities. A study with 8th-grade students compared the effectiveness of three instructional models and a control condition. Students completed a pre-test inquiry task and a transfer task using simulations, and we analyzed changes in their inquiry patterns. The results indicate that the most effective instructional model showed students an effective inquiry strategy, acknowledged the effort of using that strategy, and explained that they should resist the pull of the more common ineffective strategy. There was also a strong main effect of SES in student inquiry strategies use. Results provide initial evidence that adding a cue to resist the lure of a poor strategy improves transfer over simply explaining the right strategy.

## Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the need to prepare children to be independent learners. Ideally, students would be equipped with the strategies to self-direc t their learning given a task. While this goal is n ot new (Meichenbaum & Biemiller, 1998; Schunk & Zimmerman, 2007), the pandemic amplified the learning challenges for students who were otherwise accustomed to stron g teacher and parental learning guidance (United Na tions [UN], 2020; United Nations Educational, Scientific, and Cultural Organization [UNESCO] et al., 2020). Despite many heroic efforts, the reality is that many stude nts needed to learn on their own. This situation cr eated high demands on students and their families as well as learning loss for students who did not possess the strategies to self-direct their learning (Engzell et al., 2021; Patrick et al., 2020). Inquiry is one-form of self-directed learning. Mo dern standards ask students to collect, analyze and interpret data, as well as form hypotheses (Nationa l Research Council [NRC], 2013). Learner success of ten depends on having a set of strategies to help handle the potential complexity of a system or a task (Stender et al., 2018). Of particular interest here are inquiry stra tegies to help gather evidence that can reveal the underlying systematicity of a scientific phenomenon exhibited in a scientific simulation. During the pandemic there was an explosion in the use science simulations as a tool to learn science in the classroom, e.g., PhET Interactive Simulations use has increased in some countries by 500% (Wieman et al., 2008). One potential inquiry strategy for making headway i n many physics simulations is to find a base unit that can equate different types of quantities. Figu re 1 provides two simple instances, which we also u sed in the current investigation. In Balancelet, students need to find the relative weights of the  colored objects. In Tuglet, students need to find the relative pulling-force of the players. The four examples in Figure 1 include setups used by students while exploring these simulations. The two examples in the first row display the correct u se of the base-unit strategy. In both simulations, Balancelet (balance scale) and Tuglet (pulling cart), students are using one element on one side and are adding two or more of another element to find the ratio among them. The examples in the second row display a typical but ineffective  strategy. In this case, students keep adding eleme nts in an unorganized way to find the “balance” point.

If a goal is to make independent learners, then ins truction should prepare them to use the strategies on their own, often in a context that differs from the original context of instruction. For instance, if students learn the base-unit strategy for Balancelet, they will ideally use this strategy for Tuglet. How can we teach inquiry strategies that equip students to transfer those strategies (B ransford & Schwartz, 1999; Day & Goldstone, 2012)? For promoting inquiry in science, one instructional approach is modeling, which we will call the Show Right model – SR). When modeling, teachers show the correct use of a strategy. For example, when running a lab experiment, a teacher can model a base-unit strategy for using th e balance scale:  If I want to find the equivalence between oranges and limes, I need to keep one orange on one side and keep adding limes until the scale balances. When teachers show students how to conduct a practice, t hey may later use the practice while doing the acti vity (Boekaerts & Corno, 2005; Chen & Klahr, 1999; Klahr & Nigam, 2004). ICLS2022 Proceedings 823 © ISLS Figure 1 Instances of Two Types of Setups Use by Students in the Simulations Balancelet                                                      Tuglet Base-Unit Strategy (Right strategy) Common Ineffective Strategy (Wrong strategy) Another instructional approach is to combine showin g the correct strategy and adding an explanation, which we will label – SR+Exp. For instance, an example might be: “Keeping constant one side of the scale allows me to find the weight equivalence between fruits. Finding the ratio helps me to better predict what the scale will do when I put many lemons and oranges on it.” Both SR and SR+Exp are good approaches. Our study examines whether we can increase spontaneous transfer by als o alerting students to their natural but competing and often ineffective strategies. We hypothesize that strategy instruction can enhance transfer if it shows and explains the right strategy, and goes further to show students a common ineffective strategy and alert them to recognize when they start using the common ineffective strategy. T his way, students’ natural tendency to use the inef fective strategy becomes an internally generated cue to use  the target strategy instead. We will call this the  Expanded model.

The Expanded model approach added two elements (i) showing the ineffective (wrong) strategy and (ii) using the wrong strategy as an internal cue to overcome the demands of the cognitive effort. Students may be cued to transfer the right inquiry strategy intonew env ironments by previously highlighting incorrect solu tions (Clifford, 1984; D’Mello et al., 2014; Limón, 2001). When teachers share wrong approaches to solve an exercise, students see behaviors they want to correct and avoid (Manalo & Kapur, 2018; Vosniadou et al., 2001). Therefore, if students find themselves in a situation in which  they are using a poor strategy, they may recognize  it. Additionally, effective inquiry strategies may requ ire more cognitive effort at first, at least compar ed to the common strategy of just changing things to see what happens. In the Expanded model, we also communicate to students that the common ineffective strategy may b e easier at first than the target strategy, but tha t in the long run, the taught strategy will lead to more success in finding key patterns. A factor that can affect students as they become in dependent learners is family SES (socioeconomic status). Prior research has shown that students fro m low-SES backgrounds have lower GPAs and standardi zed test scores (Reardon, 2011; Reardon & Bischoff, 2011). One contributing factor might be that schools don’t focus enough in preparing independent learners. For examp le, in the United States, high school students from  lowincome backgrounds showed a learning loss of 0.4 st andard deviations after 12 months of remote instruc tion during the pandemic (Agostinelli et al., 2022). The  same pattern has also been found in other countrie s such as The Netherlands (Engzell et al., 2021). Therefore, we wanted to examine whether we saw less inquiry st rategy patterns or different gains from instruction between students from two different SES family backgrounds. We designed an experiment to compare treatments of  no instruction (control group), SR, SR+Exp, and Expanded. In session one, students explore with a simulation (baseline measure), and shortly afterwards receive a brief video that uses one of the instructional mo dels. Two weeks later, students complete a transfer  task. We hypothesize that students assigned to the Expanded model will display a higher use of the taught inquiry strategy in the transfer task.

## Participants

One hundred and nine students from two schools loca ted in Bogota (Colombia) participated in the study.  One institution is a public (run by a private organizat ion) school where students (N = 59) come from a low  socioeconomic (SES) background. The second is a private school with students from a high-SES background (N = 50). The science teachers in both schools facilitated th e activity. This study was approved by the Universi ty’s Institutional Review Board. Parents provided passive consent and students provided active assent. Design The study design is composed of a control group plus three instructional models of inquiry instruction. Students assigned to the control condition do not receive the treatment video. In the first instructional model, Show Right (SR), a video shows students the right strategy. The second model is SR+Exp (Show Right + Explanation), where the video’s instructor not only tells students the right strategy, but also explains the reasons why the right strategy is good. The last model is Expanded, which includes both components of the SR+Exp model and adds a brief component telling students a common wrong strategy and an explanation about why they might be inclined to use the wrong strategy even after they know that the right strategy is better. Students in both schools were randomly assigned to a condition. Randomization occurred within class. We created one short video for each condition (<5 m inutes). The videos are the key element of the intervention. All the videos feature the same teach er who follows a format used by YouTubers. Main messages are delivered using visual supports such as icons, images, and reinforcing text. The right strategy included in the videos is a particular strategy to learn the rules controlling a system, in this case, finding the equivalence between different types of units (e.g., how many red coins (base coin) equal a green coin?). Procedures Figure 2 Graphical Representation of Studies’ Procedures Studies occurred in the second half of 2021 and stu dents in both schools were attending in-person instruction. In school A (public), students used the computer lab and in school B (private), they used  laptops or tablets in the classroom. During the study, students in both schools used headphones. When needed, we provided ICLS2022 Proceedings 825 © ISLS technical support to the teachers or the students (in school B, teachers shared the technical support session link with some students). Teachers followed a script to introduce and execute the study. Figure 2 presents a graphical representation of the procedures. On day 1, students provided assent and watched a short video with instructions on how to play Balancelet. Then they had five minutes to play with the simul ation. We used this measure as a baseline of student inquiry. Once time elapsed, students watched the assigned condition video. They then took a post-test assessing their inquiry strategy knowledg e (for space restrictions we don’t include or repor t this evaluation measure in this paper). On day 2 (two we eks later), students played Tuglet for a maximum of 15 minutes. Again, they received short textual instruc tions about the interface. For Tuglet, the instructions did not state a specific task, though it becomes apparent that the inquiry goal is to predict whether any given configuration will go right, go left, or result in a tie. Tuglet served as the transfer task. The overall duration of the study, spread over two sessions, was approximately 60 minutes total.

## Measures

We used choice-based assessments (CBAs) to measure students’ inquiry processes (Schwartz & Arena, 2013). These CBAs (Balancelet and Tuglet) provided process measures about students’ inquiry  processes. In a CBA students are free to make choices about how to learn during the assessment, and we measure those choices directly from the logfiles of their actions. Balancelet provided the baseline while Tuglet the transfer measures.

## Taught (Right) Strategy Process Measures

Balancelet and Tuglet are CBAs about finding balance and equaling forces (see Figure 3). Balancelet uses weight; students need to learn to predict when a scale balances. Tuglet is about pulling; students need to learn to predict which team will win or tie when pulling a rope. Balancelet assesses students’ exploration strategies to learn when a scale balances. Balancelet has a simple interface (Fig. 3-a): A scale, an eraser, a timer, and three types of coins. The coins have three different weights: red (weight = 1), blue (weight = 3), and green (weight = 6). Students stated learning task is to be able to predict what the scale will do for any combination of coins put on the scale – balance, tilt left, tilt right?

Tuglet is an analog of Balancelet. Students’ task is to learn how to predict whether the blue or red team will win or tie in a tug-of-war. Tuglet characters come in three “strength” sizes: small (pull level = 1), medium (pull level = 2), and large (pull level = 3). In Tuglet, students do not receive a specific task, but they h ave the opportunity to run experiments every time they miss a question in the challenge room. Students can exp eriment in an explore room, and when they feel ready, they can move to a challenge room where they need to answer eight increasingly difficult questions in a row to win. I f they make a mistake, they are sent back to the ex plore room where they can experiment or return to the challenge room at any time. Figures 3b and 3c show the explore and challenge rooms.

Setups run by the students in both CBAs are recorde d and then extracted as logfiles. The clickstream data is later processed by classifying the setups p erformed by the students into meaningful categories. For the purposes of this paper, we only classified setups that displayed the taught strategy. The use of the unit equivalence (taught) strategy is calculated by counting each instance in which a student added elements when trying to isolate a specific unit (e.g., 1 red medium player on the l eft and 2 blue small players on the right). In esse nce, students got credit when they did not mix multiple sizes on either side of the scale or rope. We also count up the total number of setups run by the students in each CBA. The measure can be used as a raw frequency of strategy use, or it can be divided by the total number of actions  to control for the total number of explorations. I n this paper, results are reported using the latter option. Stude nts could choose not to explore in the transfer tas k (Tuglet). ## Discussion and Conclusion

This study directly measured whether students use a unit-equivalence strategy and whether instruction increases the transfer of this inquiry strategy. In our sample, we find that SES makes a difference in inquiry strategy process. One possible explanation for this result is that pr ivate school students engage in more inquiry. Looki ng at classroom size, science teachers in a private school have an average of twenty-two students, whereas those in a charter school have around forty. From an instructi onal perspective, given the attention inquiry instr uction ICLS2022 Proceedings 828 © ISLS demands of teachers, promoting inquiry may be easie r with a smaller group of students, therefore, may happen more often for high-SES students.

We find that school type makes a difference in in quiry, but does instructional condition? The contro l condition students had a chance to complete Tuglet without receiving instructions. Yet, these students displayed a high percentage of inquiry strategy use in the transfer task in comparison to the other conditions. To offer some possible explanation it might be worth separating s tudents by SES given the strong effect of SES on students’ inquiry strategy use. For the high-SES students assigned to the control group they might not needed the instruction, and if you look at Table 1 average percentages of taught strategy use, they look similar among high-SES students. In low-SES students is possible that instruction didn’t provide enough cues for spontaneous transfer and maybe instead confused them. The Expanded condition students had the opportunity to problem solve and then receive an instruction containing both right and wrong strategy use and explanations about why it is good to use the right strategy and cognitive effort of the right strategy  to cue students to transfer intonew settings. Ove rall, it seems that students in the Expanded condition do better than the students in the control condition. Instruction that cues students when learning an inquiry strategy seems to support inquiry strategy transfer tonew contexts. Students in the other two conditions, SR and SR + Exp, fell in between, perhaps because individual diffe rences are driving the effects in these latter conditions or because t hese instructional models are not as effective for promoting inquiry strategy transfer tonew settings.

The interaction between SES and condition was mar ginally significant. Results show high-SES students already know a lot about inquiry and are more prepared to learn inquiry. Even the simple form of instruction (SR) worked for high-SES students. In the case of low-SES school, students assigned to the Expanded condition show the highest transfer of inquiry strategy tonew contexts. It is possible that for students with low inquiry exposure, cueing them to recognize when they are using a wron g strategy and acknowledging the cognitive effort, better prepares them to use inquiry strategy in new contexts.

Finding instructional models that promote the use  of inquiry strategies should be helpful to students as they move forward in their science learning. This s tudy provides initial evidence that instruction tha t alerts to common ineffective strategies can promote the transfer of a taught strategy. By hypothesis, pointing out that “just adding things without a plan” is a poor strategy cues students to recognize when they are doing that behavior and reminds them to use the right strategy. Future stud ies will determine whether showing a wrong (ineffec tive) strategy vs. wrong strategy with effort cue are key to promote spontaneous transfer. Here, we examined a single strategy – find equivalence to a base unit. There a re multiple inquiry strategies, such as the control-of-variables strategy (CVS). If we teach CVS to students using t he expanded condition, would students spontaneously  use CVS in new contexts? Subsequent studies will look at the effectiveness of the Expanded model for teaching other inquiry strategies.
