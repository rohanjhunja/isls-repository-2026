# Developing Thinking Routines Instructional Design Principles

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The concept of making thinking visible and thinking routines (TRs) have been widely adopted in teaching practice and shown to yield positive learning impact in previous research. However, a gap exists between the high-level theoretical principles and practical application. Thus, our aim is to bridge the gap between theory and practice by developing and testing the thinking routines instructional design principles of (TRIDPs) through three design-based research (DBR) iterations in a course on social issues from English movies at a Taiwanese university. We aligned the 3-phase DBR model and conjecture mapping for a systematic evaluation, depicting a detailed design history and trajectory of TRIDPs evolution. Our research has produced six TRIDPs. The iteration process also furthered our understanding of both learning process and outcome in a TRs-embedded classroom. We believe our TRIDPs and revised conjecture map offer useful model for those interested in TRs-embedded instruction.

Key words: thinking routines, design principles, design-based research, conjecture mapping Is making thinking visible enough?

The concept of making thinking visible and thinking  routines  (TRs) have been widely adopted in teaching, demonstrating positive learning outcomes in previou s research. Project Zero of  Harvard Graduate School of Education has promoted the concept of making thinki ng visible and proposed many TRs to be used in teaching practice to scaffold students’ thinking and enculturate thinking dispositions in the long run (Ritchhart et al., 2011). TRs, comprising simple questions or prompts, guide stude nts to externalize their thinking, engage in thinki ngrich discourse, and make thinking moves such as con necting ideas and considering multiple viewpoints. Our overarching educational goal is to incorporate teac hing of thinking into curriculum with adoption of T Rs. However, while making thinking visible seems to be an unquestionable guiding principle in teaching practice, its generality may hinder its testability in local cont exts (Sandoval, 2004). On the other hand, previous research on TRs mainly focused on the intervention effects of TRs as learning tasks but provided little insight into furthering the theoretical understanding of the design princip les (DPs) for incorporating TRs at a curricular level. Thus, we found an urgent need to rethink the TRs usage by ensuring coherence among curricular components, inc luding course aims, learning tasks, materials, assessment,  etc. We aim to propose a TR design framework  and design principles to bridge the gap between the high-level theoretical principles and practical applicat ion. This study examines the development of thinking routines desig n principles (TRIDPs) across three design-based research (DBR) iterations, asking: How may we develop thinking routines design principles? Alignment of DBR and conjecture mapping to capture the evolution of TRIDPs We utilized DBR to iteratively refine instructional and research design and employed conjecture mapping to trace the trajectory of TRIDP evolution. As clarified by Barab and Squire (2004), in DBR, “the design is conceived not just to meet local needs, but to advance a theoreti cal agenda, to uncover, explore, and confirm theoretica l relationships” (p. 5). The merit of design lies not in theory testing but in refinement or development of theory (Edelson, 2002), and in our case, the establishment and refinement of design principles. However, the refinement of design through multiple iterations may become too messy for a systematic review. Therefore, McKenney and Reeves (2012) proposed a 3-phase model of educational design research to visualize the design procedures, which includes three main phases: (1) analysis and exploration, (2) design and construction, and (3) evaluation and reflection. Then the final design product is the maturing inter vention and theoretical understanding. Edelson

(2002) also suggested considering design procedure, problem ana lysis, and design solutions in design decisions.

McKenney and Reeves’s model, along with Edelson’s view in design decisions, assists us in documenting  and evaluating the design procedures. We gather  inspiration from literature review and problem analysis in Phase 1, depict design solutions including both instructional and research design in Phase 2, and uncover key findings and emergent problems in Phase 3, all of which become sources for adaptation in the next iterative cycle. While the DBR 3-phase model serves a useful guide for iterative cycles, w e still need a tool to evaluate how our TRIDP guides intervention design, so we used conjecture mapping. Conjecture mapping was proposed by Sandoval (2004, 2014) to bridge the gap between the generality of high-level theoretical design principles and the specificity of embodied conjectures  in particular contexts. He explained that “embodied conjecture is a conjecture about how theoretical propositions might be reified within designed environments to support learning” (Sandoval, 2014, p. 215). We adopted conjecture mapping  to specify how the theoretical principle of making thinking visible and the derived TRIDPs are embodied in local instructional design and ev aluate whether the embodiments lead to predicted mediating processes and achieve intended outcome. With our purpose of capturing evolution of TRIDPs in a longer trajectory through multiple iterations, we found a need to align DBR cycles and conjecture map ping. Sandoval (2014) admitted that conjecture mapp ing itself alone may not be a good representation of mo vement in a longer trajectory. Therefore, we then referred to Wozniak’s strategy of aligning 3-phase DBR and conjecture mapping (Wozniak, 2015). Wozniak explained that the development of conjecture mapping occurred in Phase 1 and 2 in a DBR cycle, and the evaluation of conjecture mapping is done in Phase 3, which then leads to revision in the next iteration. With this alignment, findings from revision of conjecture mapping and TRIDPs can inform the iterative design of DBR, and vice versa. This way, we can conduct a more systematic review of the refinement of TRIDPs.

## Research methodology

In this section, we will present the research conte xt, the design of conjecture maps, and DBR iterations. Due to the iterative nature of data collection and analysis, we will discuss data collection and analysis in the findings and discussion sections.

## Contexts and participants

We conducted this research in a n English as foreign language  course on social issues from English movies at a Taiwanese university. The study involved three iterative cycles, each con ducted within the same course, taught by the same instructor, and spanned over three semesters in three different classes. Iteration 1 included 12 students, Iteration 2 had 20, and Iteration 3 had 24. The course was a 3-credit semester course, with classes meeting for three hours each week, lasting for 18 weeks. It was  delivered in English, and most students were capab le of effective self-expression and discussion. We introduced four Engli sh movies rich in social issues and guided students in analyzing the plot, characters, and social themes. In Taiwan's educational system, there's an emphasis on teaching thinking in the core curriculum, but in tegrating it with the curriculum remains a challeng e. The transmission model of teaching is still prevalent, and students have limited opportunities to develop their thinking skills. Therefore, our overarching goal is to make thinking visible and create a thoughtful classroom. How TRIDPs guide instructional design and evaluation of conjecture mapping We used conjecture mapping to develop and refine our DPs (Figure 1). We first defined our high-level theoretical principle as: Making thinking visible assists articulation and appropriation of thinking and thus deepens learning. From this, three initial DPs were derived: 1) DP1: Personally relevant or controversial topi cs of discussion will activate engagement of thinking; 2) DP2: Collaborative work and discourse will provide venue where learners can scaffold and deepen one another’s thinking; 3) DP3: Thinking routines will guide students to externalize their thinking and take thinking moves  (TMs). DP1 highlights the importance of personally conne cted topics in that they can engage students’ in deeper thinking (Dinsm ore & Fryer, 2023). DP2 conforms to sociocultural view of learning and importance of social community in thinking enculturation (Resnick, 1987). DP3 stems from Project Zero’s application of TRs, which shapes the core of teaching intervention design. We selected four TMs aligning with course nature: building explanations and interpretations, making c onnections, considering different viewpoints, and reasoning with evidence. Then we followed these DPs to sketch our initial conjecture map. Figure 1 Initial Conjecture Map to Guide TR Instructional Design These DPs guided our design of the embodiments and helped us predict and evaluate both process and product of design. As shown in Figure 1, The embodiments show how our initial DPs are translated into instructional design in three aspects: tools and materials, tasks and participant structures, and discursive practice. Take tools and materials for example. Four social issues movies were introduced, and TR gu idelines were given to assist students in using TRs. Whiteboards were u sed to help students externalize and visualize thei r thinking. DP2 and DP3 played a significant role in shaping task design and discursive practices, promoting both individual and collective thinking following TRs. In the media ting processes, students collaboratively produced m ovie analyses and engaged in group discussions. To test our conjectures, we collected evidence to assess (1) design conjectures, determining if they led to student art ifact creation and observable interactions, and (2)  theoretical conjectures, evaluating whether intended outcomes w ere achieved. Both expected a nd unexpected findings informed the adaptation or creation of new design conjectures, enhancing our understanding of designed learning, which, in turn, informed revisions to the conjecture map.

## Data collection and analysis: Systematic review of TRIDPs evolution

Alignment of the 3-phase DBR model and conjecture mapping enabled us t o have a more systematic review of both design and outcome. Conjecture mapping provides a useful guide to decid e what data to collect (Wozniak, 2015). Also, in DBR,  revision of data collection and adaptation of measu rements are inevitable for better responsiveness to the changing dynamics of designed  learning (Cobb et al., 2003). Therefore, we evaluated and adapted (1) the design conjectures (the intervention design), (2) the theoretical conjectures (the intervention effects), and (3) data collection and analysis. For example, problems arose in Phase 3 of Iteration 1 led to problem analysis in Phase 1 of Iteration 2, followed by the revised design, including the conjecture map, DPs, task design, and revised data collection. The revised design constructed our implementation i n Iteration 2. And the same evaluation process was applied in Iteration 3. Even tually, through iterati ve implementation and evaluation  in various contexts, generalization of TRIDPs were produced.

The analysis results (reported in Chen & Jan, 2023) showed that the intended outcomes were achieved. Students were able to take expected thinking moves to analyze social issues by following TRs. This indicates our initial DPs led to desired mediating processes and intended outcomes. How evaluation in Iteration 2 informed revisions in Iteration 3 The positive results reported previously confirmed that the revised conjecture map worked well in Iter ation 2; however, new problems emerged from our reflection o n Iteration 2. As is common in DBR research, while our understanding of learning in the designed environment grew through research iterations and ongoing analysis, we also had to adapt to naturalistic contextual factor s. With our increased understanding of mediating pr ocesses through students' reflections and joint TR products, we still required more evidence of how mediating processes were connected to the embodiment of TRs and the nor m of group collaboration. Thus, we revised guidelin es of self-reflection in Iteration 3 by asking students to ref lect on experience of learning in groups and using TRs, looking for emergent subthemes of these two predefined themes. On the other hand, we acknowledged the need for in-class interaction data. In the first two iterations, we couldn't collect observable interaction data l ike video or audio due to pandemic restrictions. The Taiwanese government enforced indoor mask-wearing and distancing, making audio data collection challenging. However, by Iteration 3, with the mask restriction lifted, w e could collect audio data more effectively.  Thus, in addition to previously collected data such as students’ writing and joint products, we planned to collect students’ self-reflection with revised guidelines and interaction recordings.

Data analysis results from Iteration 3 pointed in two directions for DPs and conjecture mapping revision. First, the emergent themes of group learning in stu dents’ self-reflection and the analysis results from group interaction strengthen the idea that group collabor ation plays a critical role in scaffolding thinking  and learning. Thus, we want to make a further revision to the intended outcome IO2 in our conjecture map as: Thinking routines and group discussion scaffold and deepen students’ thinking. Second, the emergent theme of classroom e thos echoes our overarching educational goal of making t hinking visible and transforming the transmission m odel of teaching into a thinking-centric one. It aligns with DBR's features of estab lishing a different clas sroom ethos (Brown, 1992) and changing the educational environm ent (Bradley & Reinking, 2011). This leads us to pr opose a new DP: Collaborative work and discourse will help create a classroom ethos and a sense of community where members support one another's thinking.

## Conclusion and implications

Our alignment of the 3-phase DBR model and conjecture mapping brings three  key contributions  in theory and practice. First, it enhances our theoretical understanding of learning processes and outcomes in TRs-embedded classrooms. While Ritchhart et al. (2006) recognize d the importance of TRs as a classroom cultural for ce, they acknowledged the ir limitation of insufficient student views. In contra st, our three iterations collected data, including mediating processes and outcomes, offering valuable insights from students' self-reflection and in-class interactions regarding TRs' impact on their learning experiences. Second, the alignment of the 3-phase DBR model and conjecture mapping allows us to trace a detailed design history and the evolution of TRIDPs. Our systematic problem analysis and testing across contexts  culminated in a generalized set of DPs and a compre hensive design framework. Edelson (2002) stated that a very important goal of DBR is “developing useful, generalizable theories” and that “a design framework is a generalized desig n solution” (p. 114). We acknowledge that DBR is hi ghly contextualized, but it also helped us respond effec tively to contextual factors. We believe that our TRIDPs and the revised conjecture map can serve as a useful de sign framework for those interested in incorporatin g thinking routines into curriculum design in similar contexts. Third, the emerging themes of learning new ways of learning and classroom ethos open new research avenues. Our teaching model significantly differs from tradition al ones, suggesting that TR-embedded curricula can be a transformational force in classrooms. However, we also encountered some limitations in our methodology. Conjecture mapping helped envision how DPs manifest at the instructional level, but curricular-level DPs (e.g., DP4-6 regarding curriculum alignment) did not  integrate well into the original conjecture map. The associations between curricular-level DPs and intended outcomes weren't adequately represented. Future studies should adapt the model to include curricular elements and their outcomes.
