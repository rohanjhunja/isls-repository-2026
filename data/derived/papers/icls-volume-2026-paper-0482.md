# Complex Science, Simple Tools: A Pocketable, Low-Cost Lab for Sophisticated Experiments in Middle-School Science

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Although advanced science tools are central to modern research, K –12 students rarely access them due to cost and complexity. Using microfluidic stickers as a design case, this paper explores how such technologies can be repurposed for constructionist “hands-on” science education. By simplifying fabrication, drawing on cultural forms, and enabling hands-on experiments with liquid flow and diffusion, the stickers illustrate pathways to make advanced science tools accessible through everyday making.

While advanced scientific instruments are central to advancing contemporary science, they remain largely inaccessible in K-12 education (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2021; Gericke et al., 2023; National Research Council, 2012). Barriers, including high cost, technical sophistication, and physical fragility, have limited the extent to which advanced laboratory experiments can be integrated into classroom practice (Fajrin & Hakim, 2023; Fitzgerald et al., 2019; Ndihokubwayo, 2017). This study explores how scientific devices can be reimagined as affordable and accessible tools that enable students to engage with more advanced scientific phenomena in everyday learning environments (Sotério & Blikstein, 2025). Guided by Papert’s (1980) constructionism and Freire’s (2005) critical pedagogy, we approach science learning as an emancipatory practice that centers students’ agency. Constructionism informs our emphasis on hands-on, artifact-based exploration, while critical pedagogy guides our focus on accessibility and expanding who can meaningfully participate in scientific practices. Specifically, we investigate how low-cost and portable implementations of microfluidic stickers, which represent a more familiar cultural form to K–12 learners (Horn, 2013), can support exploration of fluid flow, diffusion, and colorimetric reactions. By making these processes tangible and manipulable, we aim to broaden opportunities for inquiry-based science learning. As an initial contribution, we present the embedding of microfluidic stickers into accessible kits as a pathway to expand where and how science learning can take place. “School Science” and “Real Science” Science education research has long noted differences between what students encounter in classrooms and the practices associated with scientific work (Duschl & Grandy, 2007; Larkin, 2020). In addition, in many school contexts, science is presented through structured lessons and laboratory activities that follow predetermined procedures (Abrahams & Millar, 2008). While these approaches support curricular goals and classroom manageability, they represent only a narrow slice of how scientific knowledge is constructed (Bell, 2005). Scientific practice, in contrast, involves working with uncertainty, formulating questions, and revising ideas through iterative experimentation (National Research Council, 2012). Papert (1980) observed that children’s exploratory thinking may, in some respects, resemble the reasoning found in scientific inquiry more than the forms of science typically offered in schools. This suggests that opportunities for authentic engagement may be constrained by how school science is framed. Rathe r than positioning school science and scientists’ science as opposing domains, current scholarship seeks to create connections by designing learning environments that introduce core features of scientific practice in developmentally appropriate ways (Manz,  2020). These features include modeling, explanation, argumentation, and engagement with phenomena, each of which can be adapted to classroom contexts (National Research Council, 2012; Manz, 2015). Given these challenges, we frame this study not as an attempt to replicate professional science in schools but as a design effort to create tools and environments that invite learners into the intellectual work of “scientists’ science.” Bridging school science and more complex scientific practices requires more than refining instructional techniques; it calls for rethinking the very forms of participation and material tools that constitute science learning environments. In design-based research, diSessa and Cobb (2004) describe ontological innovation as a form of theoretical contribution in which new constructs, entities, or forms of participation are introduced to reframe how learning can be understood. Rather than focusing solely on improving instruction, ontological innovation expands what researchers and educators consider to “exist” in a learning environment. Building on a constructionist 2874 (Papert, 1980) and critical pedagogy tradition (Freire, 2005), we approach educational design not as the production of classroom materials but as an opportunity to reimagine what counts as participation in science. In this sense, our microfluidic stickers may not simply act as instructional tools, but may signal new ways of positioning scientific instruments within everyday classroom materials, creating opportunities for learners to approach science as generative and exploratory.

Design approach of microfluidics stickers The miniaturization of experimental systems has long been a focus in scientific research, aiming at producing laboratory functions at a small scale through lab-on-a-chip technology and combining precise fluid control with analytical capability (Whitesides, 2014). These systems are typically introduced in higher education to study fluid dynamics, chemical analysis, and diagnostic applications (Whitesides, 2006). At the K –12 level, portable microfluidic materials hold potential to support foundational science topics such as mixtures and separations, pH, fluid flow, diffusion, and simple colorimetric reactions. These tools can make abstract concepts more tangible for younger learners, although little work has explored adapting such technologies for classroom us e. To make microfluidics more accessible, we transformed standard laboratory chips into microfluidic stickers (Figure 1), integrated into Lab-on-a-Book, a constructionist paper-based portable science toolkit designed to expand where science learning can occur (Sotério & Blikstein, 2025). As shown in Figure 1, we replaced rigid laboratory substrates with thin vinyl cut using a relatively low-cost consumer-grade vinyl cutting machine, producing a flexible, sticker-like format. These channels supported capillary-driven flow, mixing/splitting,  and diffusion when dyes were introduced from different inlets. Beyond color gradients, the same layout can host simple chemistry experiments, such as placing vinegar and baking soda solutions in opposing inlets to track acid-base reaction fronts, or compa ring how water, oil, and soap travel at different speeds to explore viscosity. By shifting from rigid hardware to a flexible sticker format, the design invites tactile tinkering and supports pump-free flow, even on curved or bent surfaces. Stickers function as familiar cultural forms associated with play and personalization, helping to position microfluidics as approachable and open to tinkering (Horn, 2013). While such controlled flow experiments would typically demand costly lab equipment, in this sticker format, they can be carried out with a few drops, making them easy to embed into book pages and scalable for classroom use.

## Implications and next steps

This early-stage design case illustrates how microfluidic devices in laboratories can be reconfigured into sticker-like formats for K-12 learners to access scientific tools in familiar material forms (Horn, 2013). While preliminary, these prototypes suggest that reimagined instruments may function not only as instructional aids but as tentative invitations into epistemic practices such as inquiry, modeling, and iterative testing. This aligns with Freire’s

(2005) critical pedagogy by creating opportunities for students to question, challenge, and actively participate in

knowledge construction. We recognize that classroom engagement differs from professional scientific work; however, our aim is to open pathways for learners to engage with scientists’ science, where knowledge is constructed, tested, and revised. For the next steps, we will bring sample pages and microfluidic stickers to ground the poster in concrete classroom artifacts and prompt reflection on their pedagogical use. By situating these materials within a research-driven design framework, we emphasize theory, educational use, and implications for school practice. Important challenges remain, including material durability, curricular integration, and the need for pedagogical support to guide classroom use. This work is part of a broader effort to create a portable Lab-ona-Book with varied page formats, from chemical reaction sheets to microfluidic inserts (Sotério & Blikstein, 2025). Our shift from rigid materials to stickers supports accessibility,  lowers cost, and uses familiar cultural forms to broaden inquiry and agency in school science.
