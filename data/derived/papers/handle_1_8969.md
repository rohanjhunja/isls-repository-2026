# Navigating Making Space: Attending to Multiple Learning Pathways in Science Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Over the past decade, science education has been pushed to an inflection point. What was once a given of scalable standards-based science education is now being reimagined as adaptable models for teaching science through collaborative student-led sensemaking. This tension of supporting individual students’ ways of knowing and being in coordination with canonical STEM practices has teachers caught between a paradigmatic binary. The field of the Learning Sciences provides comprehensive knowledge about the need for opening up diverse learning pathways in the classroom, yet little is discussed about what to do once those learning pathways are made visible. In this symposium, the authors consider how teachers “make space” for student contributions in classroom-based science education, and consequently, analyze what happens after it is made.

## Significance and contribution

The works collected here highlight multimodal practices in which teachers use representations (Ainsworth et al., 2020), scaffolds (e.g., Tabak, 2004), and other mediated actions (Wertsch, 1993) to support meaningful learning experiences (Schwartz et al., 2020) wherein student s’ culturally relevant (Ladson-Billings, 2014) know ledge, skills, and dispositions lead collaborative sense-making about science phenomena. These acts of “making space” redistribute epistemic authority and agency (Miller et al., 2018) towards diverse ways of knowing and being in ways that move beyond the constraints and expectations of a narrow, standards-based STEM pipeline (Cannady et al., 2014). With a goal of supporting the inclusion—and the growth—of diverse ways of knowing and being in classroom sense-making, this symposium explores multiple approaches to teaching in which educators worked to bring out and validate students’ ideas and prior ex periences for collective problem-solving and context-relevant learning experiences. Since teachers are navigating complex interactions and relationships with students, we also consider moments where teachers’ moves missed makin g-space opportunities as a way to implicate future professional development and science teaching; espe cially within pedagogical moments that present a gr eater potential for students to make visible, share, and enact their prior experiences. In our different analytic viewpoints, we offer examples of approaches, tools, and activities where teachers trusted students (and their families) to lead sense-making experiences. When teachers acknowledged that students offered important ideas with which to work, they challenged the singular STEM pipeline narrativ e and set up emergent trajectories for transforming  how learners “do science.” Organization of the symposium Following an introductory talk that frames the session, each author or authors will describe their work and explain how it expands the paradigm of making space. Our discussant will synthesize the works and connect these pieces to the field in a broader sense, then conclude with questions for the audience to consider. This session will use 90 minutes accordingly: (1) 7-minute introduction and framing (Chairs); (2) four 12-minute talks from author(s); (3) 15-minute synthesis of the work and connection to t he field (Discussant); and (4) 20-minute conversati on with the attendees based on discussant-posed questions.

Paper 1: Making space for heterogeneity in phenomenon-centered science learning Ashlyn Pierson, Danielle Keifert, Sarah Lee, Heather Johnson, Adam Bell In the past decade, the call to start “from [students’] curiosity about what they see around them” (NRC, 2012, p.

11) has led to the development of phenomenon-centered science curricula (e.g., Edelson et al., 2021; K rajick et

al., 2011; Reiser et al., 2021) that encourage stud ents to co-construct knowledge by asking questions about a phenomenon, then exploring a subset of questions th at align with a pre-determined “storyline.” Through  this process, students develop shared understandings and  “knowledge products” (Berland et al., 2016, p. 8).  These curricula foreground an image of science-as-converg ence, bringing together students’ diverse ideas to create a shared explanation. While convergence represents important aspects of disciplinary science, it backgrounds other (messy, nonlinear) images of science. Moreover, it creates tensions between accountability to standards and fully empowering students to engage in “felt” inquiry—fee ling puzzled and exploring questions of interest to  them (Keifert & Stevens, 2019). For instance, in these convergence-seeking curricula, “it is likely that not all students’ questions can be fully taken up” (Reiser et al., 2021, p. 818). In response, we ask instead: how can foregrounding an image of science-as-heterogeneity make space for students to genuinely lead inquiry? Framework Research provides countless examples of science as a messy, non-linear process that is shaped by human  and material agency, offering an image of science-as-heterogeneity (Gooding, 2006; Latour, 1993; 1999; Pickering, 1995). Rosebery et al. (2010) argue that such heterogeneity is fundamental to K-12 science learning, showing that words like “heat” can take on multiple meanings. Building on this work, we see heterogeneity not only as a starting point for asking questions, but also as an opportun ity to empower students to lead inquiry that is dee ply felt (Keifert & Stevens, 2019). By valuing the multiple co-existing ideas that students see in a phenomenon, researchers and educators can attend to the generative and disciplined questions that students propose. ## Methods

We present a case (Yin, 2014) that illustrates how a third-grade teacher, Jack, used a heterogeneity-s eeking approach to make space for students’ questions and ideas in a phenomenon-centered unit about magnets. Data include video-recorded lesson observations and post-observation interviews (to triangulate observation s with Jack’s intentions and reflections). We used interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to analyze how Jack made space for students’ questions and ideas, and w e used co-operative action (Goodwin 2018) as a lens  to understand how he brought together multimodal representations and materials in service of these goals.

## Findings and Implications

We describe how Jack used a heterogeneity-seeking a pproach to make space for students’ questions and i deas. We focus on a unit anchored with a video: a reporter showed that a car parked at the bottom of “Magnetic Hill” would roll backward uphill in neutral (in fact, an optical illusion). Though the phenomenon is not caused by magnets, Jack explained in an interview that invest igating these (false) claims could allow students t o explore concepts like magnetic fields, attraction, and repulsion. Jack encouraged each student to create and test their own theory about what happened. He provided students with materials to model their theory, and he used questioning to help students refine their theories. In this way, though the unit was centered on a single phenomen on, Jack invited students to bring new phenomena to the grou p and relate these phenomena to ongoing inquiry (Co uld a strong, underground magnet from an asteroid pull the car? Could the Earth’s core and magnetic field pull the car?). Rather than choosing one or two ideas for the students to investigate as a class, Jack encouraged each student to test the theory that “is most true to you.” Through questioning, Jack gave voice to the material agency that pushed back on their theories—he repeatedly asked students “what do [the materials] represent?” and “how does that help you prove your theory?” For example, when a student claimed the asteroid theory worked because he could hold a magnet above a toy car to pull it up a ramp, Jack  responded, “but the asteroid is under the road.” J ack’s case offers one example of how a heterogeneity-seeking approach can allow students to genuinely lead inquiry in their classrooms. With this case, we offer a new paradigm  for phenomenon-centered instruction that foregroun ds heterogeneity (and epistemic authority) rather than  convergence. We propose that reframing phenomenoncentered instruction in this way is one step toward valuing and leveraging students’ affective experience in science (e.g., Keifert et al., 2018; Jaber & Hammer, 2016) to support generative disciplined science learning. Paper 2: Family Engagement and the Kindergarten Classroom Michelle Salgado Research has continued to call for family engagemen t in science education, particularly from culturall y and linguistically diverse families (Barton et al., 200 4; NRC, 2012; Schwartz et al., 2017). Family engage ment is well supported by research which indicates the positive impact on student achievement and influence over gaps in opportunities afforded to children in school set tings (Sheridan & Kim, 2015; Henderson & Mapp, 2002).  Further, family engagement is not only possible but essential to young children’s engagement in science and engineering disciplines (NRC, 2021). The purpose for this study will be aiming to contribute research to the field of science education as it relates to designi ng learning environments that support family engage ment in scientific practices of observation and obtaining, evaluating and communicating information. Research Questions: 1. How do families engage in school dire ct scientific activities that support scientific pr actices? 2. What are the design elements of such activities that promote meaningful engagement by families? Framework In this study I attended to a situative perspective  (Gresfali & Greeno, 2008) where learning occurs wi thin the activity systems utilized by social worlds and actively provides opportunities for families to engage and participate. Authority figures in the classroom, actively proble matized the assumption that participation and under standing are solely attributes of the student occurring in s chool (Gresfali et al., 2008) and instead, views students and families as epistemic agents capable of co-creating a collaborative classroom culture (Stroupe, 2014). Using this perspective allows for a shift in how this teacher-researcher partnership opens spaces where we consider how we view, frame, and design with children and families for disciplinary specific learning.

## Methods

A collaborative design study (Cobb, Confrey, diSess a, Lehrer, & Schauble, 2003) was conducted with kindergarten students over the course of four years. In this paper I present the two cases from year two and year four cohort and examine a set of family engagement activities which were selected using purposeful sampling ICLS2022 Proceedings 1693 © ISLS (Merriam, 2009; Patton 2015). This video-based research study was conducted in a title one elementary school. The school’s demographic composition: 80% Free and Reduced Lunch; 23.3% Special Education; 42.4% Transitional Bilingual; 46.4% Hispanic/Latino, 19.6 % Asian, 4.4% Pacific Islander, 8.2% African Americ an, 9.8% white. The selected multilingual classrooms ha d 24 and 27 students respectively, with over half o f the students coming from immigrant or refugee families. Families regularly brought lunch to their children and ate with them in the classroom.

Building off of previous scholarship on family engagement and scientific practices in elementary grades, I analyzed how families engaged with the activities which occurred both at the school and outside of the school in the neighborhoods. I drew out emergent codes to provide additional explanatory significance for the design elements of the family engagement activities and th e responses of the families within those activities. After coding transcripts I looked for themes and patterns  in the coded data that corresponded to specific de signed elements present during particular activities. Part icular combinations of conditions generated by the coding scheme and emergent themes led to opportunities tha t were more likely to result in meaningful family engagement with scientific practices.

## Findings and Implications

The findings indicate the importance of design elements that promote accessibility, purposeful engagements, and meaningful learning experiences in which students and their families participated and provided contributions back to the classroom for disciplinary specific use. Thr ough the analysis we learned that family engagement  was an essential component to supporting diverse and robust science discussions within the classroom as well as enabling children to receive feedback and engage in scientific practices both within and outside of the classroom walls. In designing for family engagement three key findings emerged, 1) Ongoing, relevant, and bidirectional communication supports the inclusion of families in to disciplinary specific content, 2) Designing for activities which extend on everyday family events such as neighborhood walks and playground visits supports accessibility and inclusion, 3) Family science symposiums were de signed for communicating information, investigation s, celebration, and inclusion into their child’s acade mic endeavors. This design framework afforded stude nts and their families the opportunities to take advantage of a series of learning engagements where they coul d make diverse, intellectual connections between their ide as, experiences and scientific concepts. Designing for family engagements is important to highlight as our field continues to understand how to support children more equitably from diverse communities in engaging in meaningful science learning within elementary science classrooms. Paper 3: Opening up or closing down space for equitable sensemaking during disciplinary engagement Soo-Yean Shim, Christina Krist This paper explores conditions that led an experien ced science teacher to make space for equitable sci ence sensemaking. The call for shifting science educatio n from students “knowing about” content toward stud ents “figuring out” how/why the natural world works has emphasized the importance of providing students wit h opportunities to engage in epistemic practices of science and take active roles in constructing knowledge (NGSS Lead States, 2013; Schwarz et al., 2017). One of the expectations is that this can promote equitable sensemaking in science classrooms, as students can build on their own ideas, personal experiences, and diverse ways of making sense of the world in the knowledge-building proces ses. However, disciplinary practices in science cla ssrooms do not guarantee equitable sensemaking opportunitie s for students, especially if students’ everyday id eas and experiences do not obviously align with teachers’ disciplinary expectations.

## Framework and methods

We observed that an experienced teacher, who had pa ssion and capacity to be responsive to students’ id eas, sometimes opened up, but other times unintentionall y closed down space for students’ equitable sensema king while facilitating their disciplinary engagement. We explore the conditions and contexts that led this  teacher to open up space for students’ ideas, experiences, and ways of thinking, especially when she tried to support students’ disciplinary engagement. The teacher, Nadine, was a middle school science teacher who had 15 years of teaching experience and multiple years of experience designing and enacting NGSS-aligned curricula and instruction. Her school, located in the US Midwest, served a population of ethnically and socioeconomically diverse students. We used data from point-of-view observations (Sherin et al., 2011) with Nadine over two instructional units. Nadine wore a GoPro camera and tagged moments while teachi ng in which she was making a decision in the moment about whether and how to pursue student ideas. Nadine was interviewed immediately after teaching to reflect on ICLS2022 Proceedings 1694 © ISLS those moments. We identified episodes of equitable sensemaking—where students’ everyday ideas and experiences were substantively taken up as part of the classroom community’s disciplinary engagement—a nd qualitatively identified conditions (e.g., context of the lesson, what prompted Nadine’s decision-making) that led Nadine to open up or close down space for equitable sensemaking. Due to space constraints, we present one such episode.

## Findings

Episode: Pressin g for mechanistic reasoning.

This episode occurred during a class discussion to design an experiment to investigate what yeast (as a model single-celled organism) needs to grow and reproduce. The curriculum materials explicitly noted that students would likely already know that water and salt are n ot “food” (carbohydrates, proteins, or fats), based  on their learning in previous years. However, when Nadine asked her students to suggest what they thought yeast would need to reproduce, many of them said water. (Later, some students also said salt, but here, we focus on the class discussion about water.) In response, Nadine said, “So you’re saying water,” and students immediately responded with ideas and everyday experiences: “Because we need, that’s why we drink a lot of water,” “People di e when they don’t drink water for three days,” “Our body has a lot of water.” Instead of correcting students by saying that water is not food, Nadine decided to approach this by pressing for students’ mechanistic reasoning about the role of water. She asked students what cells and water were made of, and students said, “molecules” and “atoms.” This connection appeared to prompt an “a-ha” moment for many students. It reminded one student of her experience making bread and putting yeast in water as part of this process, which she shared eagerly (“Oh, that’s  why you put in like, the bread, the dough stuff, when you p ut in water, it goes up!”). Through discussion, the  class coconstructed three mechanistic arguments specific to yeast: that 1) yeast makes gas through chemical reaction when it meets water, 2) yeast absorbs water like a spong e, and 3) the bread rising is evidence of the repro duction of yeast. The first two arguments in particular were mainly led by students, as they actively built on th eir personal experiences (e.g., “It [the gas]  caught up in fresh bread, then all the steam comes  out.”). Importantly, this conversation also led to students’ request to include an experimental condition that included yeast + water alone (in addition to yeast + sugar + water and yeast + salt + water). Two conditions that led Nadine to make space.

In this episode, Nadine decided to make space for students’ mechanistic reasoning, instead of foregrounding the authority of “canonical scientific knowledge” about what counts as food. This decision was a diversion from the curriculum, and based on her interview, was prompted by two major conditions. First, Nadine saw that students were highly engaged with the idea that yeast would need water for growth and reproduction based on their personal experiences. She said: “I felt pushed to change it [spend more time on the discussion about water], because of the class’s needs. They [students] were really set on water.” Second, Nadine herself was not satisfied with the declarative knowledge that “water/salt is not food, ” and is therefore irrelevant to cell growth, as ev idenced by own scientific wonderings during the interview: “And then I was thinking, ‘Ok, well, water’s made of molecules. If we go down that path of molecules, yeah, water molecules.’ So, I asked that, and... oh, the salt, too. That was interesting, they were like, “Yeah, we need salt.”... Do I need to worry about kids figuring out that  salt is not food? No, they can easily look at the food label.... But then, sometimes, I’m tempted in my head to think, ‘Well, it must just have something to do with how our body  functions. We must need it.’” Here we see Nadine recognizing that students’ intuition—that water is necessary for cell growth and reproduction—was not unreasonable, despite its contradiction with the information in the curriculum materials.

## Implications

We argue that this space for expansive sensemaking provided students with richer opportunities for multifaceted disciplinary engagement than had she continued to p ress for “correct” knowledge. At the same time, the se rich opportunities rest in the familiar tension between canonical correctness and responsive teaching. These tensions can oftentimes lead teachers to position disciplina ry practices as in opposition to students’ everyday reasoning and intuition: they are used to push back upon, diminish, redirect, or oppose students’ everyday ideas. Instead, we saw Nadine use students’ everyday ideas as the substance upon which disciplinary practices were deployed. First, Nadine’s own continued sensemaking about water during her interview suggests that she recognized how students’ everyday ideas were asking for a more complex accounting of what cells need to grow, rather than a misconceived one. Second, Nadine’s continual presses for mechani sms in students’ reasoning reflects her recognition  of the disciplinary legitimacy of mechanistic reasoning as important scientific work in and of itself. Finally, we propose ICLS2022 Proceedings 1695 © ISLS that the fact that their reasoning about water could be empirically tested right away through the class experiment attenuated any worries about students eventually figuring out the scientifically correct explanation. Paper 4: “We need to step it up—we are basically the future”: Latinx young women doing chemistry Jasmine Nation, Hosun Kang This study explores three Mexican American girls’ experiences mediated by a 10th grade chemistry curriculum co-designed by researchers and teachers in a long-term research-practice partnership. We show how the curricula and teacher’s facilitation expanded students’ opportunities to contribute in collective sense-making in a classroom community. Additionally, we uncover how these students constructed stories about themselves and science based on their interactions with peers and the teacher.  The research questions that guide the analysis are:  1) In what ways did the Mexican American girls draw from their  everyday experiences and ideas in relation to the focal phenomenon in high school chemistry? 2) How were th eir everyday experiences and ideas taken up and incorporated into the classroom learning context? 3) How were these students positioned by others in t he classroom, and how did they position themselves in relation to science? Framework We draw upon the notion of ‘figured world’, a socia lly and culturally constructed space, that outlines  what it means to participate, local definitions of science, and who is considered a science person (Calabrese Barton et al., 2012; Holland et al., 1998). Our analysis is rooted in classroom social encounters since relationships with others shaped learning opportunities, experiences, and per ceptions of self for the three girls: Natalie, Juli ana, and Briseida. Tuning into interactions helped us consid er what experiences and types of knowledge (“histor y-inperson”) were brought to the learning context, and how discourse was shaped by many factors beyond any individual.

## Methods

This study employed qualitative case study methods (Merriam, 2009) and narrative analysis (Clandinin e t al., 2007), to examine: (a) class session videos, (b) student artifacts (worksheets, models, assessments), student surveys, (c) interviews with the teacher about focal students, and (d) exit interviews with students. We analyzed each class session four times, approaching the video from four lenses (figured worlds of individual, small group, whole group, and intersection of storylines). We created event logs for each class, a unit event map, and interaction logs for each case student, recording every comment or question and whether the teacher or peers ignored, rejected, teased, or validated their experiences and ways of communicating. When we traced individual discourse over time, we paid attention to the interaction between the in dividual and collective storyline such as when a pe er built on their ideas or their discourse shifted the focus of the class discussion.

## Findings and Implications

Our analysis revealed how diverse modes of particip ation and opportunities to leverage cultural ways o f being created porous boundaries between everyday science and chemistry class. We documented students drawing from connections to loved ones; previous class knowledge; personal interests; local geographical and climat e knowledge; and core values such as climate activism. Additionally, the diverse activities including sharing stories, drawing models, and creating a PSA facilitated self-expression through sense of humor, imagination/creativity, and home languages and informal discourses.  We also found the  students’ discourse was taken up and incorporated in three consequential ways: 1) epistemological recognition 2) cumulative contribution 3) across figured worlds (classroom and beyond).

Recognition within the rich disciplinary context, or epistemological recognition, was key to how students’ ideas were incorporated into the class storyline. R ather than a general “thanks for sharing” or “great  job”, experiences were validated for their connection to the science content and how they propelled their unit forward in terms of answering the overall question of “Why are wildfires worse now than 100 years ago?” Contributions also occurred across time, creating a cumulative ef fect. The teacher referenced students’ ideas from p revious classes or other small groups, making connections a nd building out understanding. All three girls made connections based on their “history-in-person” that  were sustained across multiple classes. These rele vant experiences were almost always shared in their small group first, expanded by peers’ ideas, and then presented in front of the whole class, where the teacher provide d public validation and connected to previous conte nt. The explanations articulated the importance of these id eas for all students and positioned students as aut hentic ICLS2022 Proceedings 1696 © ISLS contributors through altering the direction or propelling the collective storyline forward. Opportunities to move between the figured worlds of the classroom (i.e. s mall group to whole class) were essential to formul ating and refining complex ideas, and ultimately helped the girls develop agency in science. Despite starting with different interests in and academic records in science, they all showed meaningful engagement with science and reported the unit as th eir favorite. Juliana, who was a “high achiever” in conventional science classrooms and expressed confi dence in her science abilities, initially appeared uncomfortable with the format of this chemistry class. She did not participate much in either small group or whole class discussions until midway through the unit. In contrast, Natalie and Briseida, who had struggled more with previous science classes, immediately shared their fire stories and models with the whole group and continued to show high involvement throughout the unit based on the opportunities to integrate everyday science. Na talie leveraged her rich experiences from out-of-school science activities and childhood fascination with weather, while Briseida built new connections and interests and a commitment to “step it up” because “we have to-we a re basically the future, you know...I think it really impacts me because it has to tell us that we have t o step up and make a change to make our place better.” This work provides both practical and theoretical implications on how to support diverse Latinx students in high school science, through equitable enactment  of the NGSS and recommendations for educators and policymakers (Cantu, 2012). Specifically, this study expands the knowledge base of culturally relevant pedagogy by articulating two critical dimensions—consequenti al ways of leveraging students’ everyday experience s and recognizing their epistemological contributions. We  advocate for more focus on how students leverage t heir cultural resources and on how teachers facilitate agentic collaborative sensemaking in science.
