# Implementation of Dialogic Teaching: Exploring Teachers’ Perceptions, Attitudes, and Practical Challenges

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research interest in discursive strategies for orchestrating vibrant classroom interactions has burgeoned. Few studies, however, have given details about teachers’ perceptions and attitudes. This paper reports on an on-going study that explored elementary and secondary school teachers’ perceptions and attitudes towards dialogic teaching and the challenges they encountered in the implementation. We recruited three elementary and middle school teachers from different subjects in China to participate in the video-cued interview. The results showed that some teachers with professional vision on dialogic teaching, though attitudinally in favor of this pedagogical approach, tended to be reluctant to adopt it for fear of losing face or being challenged by their students. In parallel, students’ learning ability, learning efficiency, and willingness to participate in class may obstruct teachers’ efforts to unfold classroom instruction in a dialogical way. These findings help enrich our understanding of the effective implementation of dialogic teaching.

## Introduction

According to sociocultural perspectives (Vygotsky, 1978) and situated learning theories (Brown et al.,  1989), classroom discourse is viewed as a vital role in in fluencing student learning (e.g., Resnick et al., 2015). Special emphasis has been put on the fruitful classroom dialogue, which is construed as a key feature of effective teaching and learning. Against this backdrop, a range of theories on dialogic teaching have been proposed to help teachers orchestrate productive classroom discourse (e.g., A lexander, 2017; Mercer & Littleton, 2007; Michaels et al., 2008; Pehmer et al., 2015). Dialogic teaching is a newly advocated pedagogical approach that engages students in the joint construction of knowledge by encouraging them to share their ideas with others and to int eract with the thoughts of peers.

Although scholars have long endorsed classroom dialogue (Howe & Abedin, 2013), dialogic and vibrant teacher-student interactions are rare in authentic classrooms (Chen & Chan, 2022; Nystrand et al., 2003). In fact, teachers face many obstacles when they attempt to adopt dialogic pedagogical approaches as a legitimate means and successfully implement them in their classrooms  (Chen et al., 2020; Resnick et al., 2018). In one sense, dialogic teaching skills are developed gradually (Clarke et al., 2016; Rapanta et al., 2021; Sedova, 2017); however, teachers are not given adequate time to acquire the  necessary knowledge and skills to organize classro om discourse (Resnick et al., 2018). According to some researchers, lacking theoretical strategies and not being aware of the precious functions of language may be hindering teachers (Ruthven et al., 2017). On the other hand, even if teachers realize and value the virtue of dialogi c teaching, their attitudes towards adopting this p edagogy are crucial, in particular when faced with intensive teaching tasks and fixed learning objectives. Despite this, there is little research that delves into the teachers’ perspectives on dialogic teachin g. Therefore, this study aimed to explore teachers’ perceptions and attitudes toward dialogic teaching through videocued interviews, which could enhance our understanding of implicit teaching practices. Video as a vehicle for teacher reflections With the emergence of learning sciences, video rese arch has become increasingly popular (e.g., Chen, 2 020; Gröschner et al., 2018). As exemplified by Sherin’s  studies on the video club (Sherin, 2007; van Es & Sherin, 2010), video serves as a tool not only for capturing classroom interactions, but also for teacher reflection. Teachers can watch and discuss the videotaped lessons together and thus reflect on their own teaching practices. Teachers’ perceptions of teaching phenomena can be discerned through their interpretation of the teaching behavi ors demonstrated in video recordings. As Goodwin (1994) pointed out, individuals with a professional vision perceive and interpret events in a particular way.

Further, video-cued interviews have been developed and introduced into education research to further enhance our understanding of the implicit cultural practices of teaching and learning (Xiao, 2018). While watching video clips of other teachers’ lessons, the videos can be used as interviewing cues to stimulate the t eachers’ relevant thoughts and reflections as insiders. Therefore, it is a promising avenue for researchers to tap into teachers’ perceptions and attitudes, thereby contributing to their professional development. (2) What are the challenges involved in implementing dialogic teaching?

## Participants

In this on-going study, we are recruiting participa ting teachers from various subjects and grades in d ifferent regions of China, including Henan province, Jiangsu province, and Shanghai municipality. As a pilot, three female teachers were invited to participate in the intervi ew, including Ms. Min (elementary school mathematic s, from Jiangsu), Ms. Li (middle school Chinese, from Henan), and Ms. Gao (middle school science, from Shanghai). All three teachers have three years of teaching experience, and they each hold a master’s degree in education.

## Data collection and analysis

The video-cued focus group interview method was used to collect the data in this study. In this research method, we could gain insight into respondents’ perceptions  and attitudes towards an event or issue, as well a s their experiences and the meaning of their behaviors. The  video-cued interview allowed for impromptu discuss ions among the interviewees, which provided a collective  exploration and interpretation of the issues. Such  an interview would even break the shackles of the researchers’ predetermined questions. Three steps were involved in this interview: First, the researcher stated the purpose of the interview and described the basic characteristics of dialogic tea ching to clarify interviewees’ understanding. Next,  the three teachers were shown a video clip of approximately ten minutes due to time constraints. The video clip we chose was from a fifth-grade math classroom in a public elementary school in Shanghai, China. In the video, the teacher was demonstrating how to calculate the volume of a rectangular object. After discussions among researchers and experts, this videotaped lesson was identified as a  dialogic teaching style. Finally, we invited the t eachers to evaluate the teaching style in the videotaped clip and to share their perspectives related to their own experiences. Approximately one hour of audio from the interview was transcribed verbatim. Teachers’ attitudes and perceptions of dialogic teaching, as well as the im plementation challenges they faced, were noted in t he transcription. These initially marked themes were then grouped into broader thematic categories. Finally, in order to answer research questions, we further scrutinized the interview transcript connected to these themes.

## Findings

Teachers’ perceptions and attitudes Three teachers commented unanimously that the teach ing practices presented in this video clip tended t o be student-centered and dialogic. They think this teac her has attempted to delegate as much responsibilit y for discourse as possible to the students. Ms. Min comm ented, “The questioning culture is good in this class. Afte r answering the question, the student would ask other  pupils if they agreed with his or her answer. And the next evaluator would state his/her position before giving reasons.” In fact, the video showed that misconceptions were corrected during peer interactions. Furthermore, when a child’s statement was incomplete, “appropriate reminders and corrections by teachers could help this child develop a deeper understanding.” The teacher’s comparison of the two students’ ideas “actually gave many other children a hint” to awaken their attention. Ms. Li echoed Ms. Min and pointed out that this type of thinking exercise and expression could help students transfer their skills to other areas. Taken together, these comments by the interviewed teachers implicitly reflected their perceptions and claims about dialogic teaching, such as “say more”,  “agree/disagree”, “explain others”, and “revoice”.  These results demonstrated that teachers have a certain level of professional vision. Though all three teachers favored dialogic teaching, they also acknowledged the difficulties of its implementation. Interestingly, the reasons for agreement and the concerns about the difficulties of implementation were both related to high-stakes testing (such as e ntrance exams). The interview transcript revealed t hat exams serve as a baton that requires teachers to deliver content with quality and quantity, but also examine s students’ higher-order competencies that need to be nurtured in dialogic, interactive instruction. Challenges teachers face in implementing dialogic teaching In addition to several objective factors (e.g., intense teaching tasks and busy schedules), the interview transcript revealed that teachers were hesitant to adopt dialo gic teaching due to several factors such as students’ learning ability, learning efficiency and their willingness to participate in classroom discussions. She said, “For students who are able to concentrate, the interactions make sense.” Ms. Li continued, “Otherwise, you will always find the same group of students raising their hands in a large class.” Even though all three teachers acknowledged the importance of previewing before classes, the unequal knowledge reserves between teachers and students were identified as a  potential constraint. ## Conclusion and implications

This study explores teachers’ perceptions of, and attitudes towards, dialogic teaching, and to identify the barriers that hinder implementation of this pedagogy. Despit e recognizing the virtue of dialogic teaching, the findings indicate that teachers have difficulties executing it effectively due to personal reasons and other co nsiderations related to students.

This work provides a glimmer of evidence regarding the reasons for teachers’ reluctance to adopt dialogic teaching. The success of innovative pedagogies in the classroom hinges on the teachers. Thus, teacher professional development entails more than just equipping teache rs with teaching strategies; it requires that teach ers be attitudinally, cognitively and emotionally fulfilled. Our on-going research is digging deeper into the influencing factors with a larger sample of teachers.
