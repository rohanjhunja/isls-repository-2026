# Disciplinary Design and Design Thinking in Scientific Inquiry

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Design thinking (DT), strategies from engineering and business for designing, is gaining traction in education. However, little attention has been paid to how design heuristics and techniques may be domain dependent, particularly in cases where the designed artifacts address epistemic goals in disciplinary communities. In addition, authentically engaging in DT strategies may be challenging in classrooms where outcomes are focused not on students’ novel solutions but acquisition of canonical content. This paper describes why we might anticipate DT to be generative outside of engineering and business contexts, and why we might anticipate that disciplines will have unique strategies for design as well. We present one case of a design generated by undergraduate students in scientific inquiry, with a focus on how design problems emerge and challenges and successes students meet in designing solutions.

## Overview

Design thinking (DT) (Brown, 2008) is receiving increased attention in education in general (Razzouk & Shute, 2012), and in STEM education in particular (e.g., L i, et al, 2019; Goldman & Kabayadondo, 2016; Chin, et al, 2019). While models of design thinking exhibit heterogeneity, they all share an emphasis on methods for creating solutions to complex, real-world design problems fo r which there is no one “right” answer. The approac h is promising for a range of reasons: the strategies of  design thinking, as Chin, et al., (2019) point out, may help novice designers avoid “tendencies that can inadver tently interfere with effective design.” In additio n, design thinking in education positions students not as reproducing known solutions to acontextual problems, but as active participants in the construction of novel solutions  to genuine problems sensitive to context. And whil e design thinking is an approach developed in engineering and then business (Razzouk & Shute, 2012) with the go al of improving designs of products for consumers, all disciplines engage in some form of design (e.g., Li, et al, 2019). Scientists, for example, could be considered unique ly adept designers of scientific models, experiment s, and representations, to name but a few artifacts produced in the process of scientific inquiry. At the same time, it’s reasonable to expect that design thinking may require adaptations, as strategies for generating and evaluating designs may look quite di fferent in different disciplines. For example, the emphasis placed on a “user-centered, empathy-driven approach” (Goldman & Kabayondo, 2019) is not as relevant to  the experimental designs a scientist creates in a lab where the user is the scientist. Prototyping may look quite different for experimental designs where a rough design is often good enough. And constraints, often construed as material, financial or temporal, may instead be driven by dis ciplinary aesthetics, conventions and conservation laws. So while DT points out the importance of explicit atte ntion to how disciplinary artifacts are designed, i t is also the case that disciplines have unique techniques and at tending to those is critical for developing design thinking in settings outside of engineering and business. The broad research agenda behind this paper is articulation of what disciplinary design thinking in science (particularly physics) looks like. In science education research, a range of construct s make contact with DT. “Developing models,” “constructing explanations,” and “obtaining and com municating information,” from NGSS represent design ed artifacts. And these practices position students as designers: developing, constructing and obtaining. And so the strategies for meeting those standards may point towards design thinking processes that are productive in science. But in most instantiations, these artifacts are not  students’ designed solutions to complex problems o f science (e.g., Stroupe, 2014). Instead, curricula o ften expect that the students will recapitulate the  “disciplinary core ideas” of NGSS. This seems almost inescapable. As Li, et al (2019), note, “design activity … can be easily perceived as creating new terms and procedures, act ivities often viewed as belonging to professional mathematicians and scientists, not students.” This stands in contrast to an engineering course, in whi ch it is not just anticipated but expected that design thinking will generate truly novel des igned artifacts; science courses expect students to converge upon sanctioned solutions, as highlighted by Miller, et al (2018).

## Novel design in scientific inquiry: Background and methods

The study takes place in a semester-long junior/sen ior undergraduate course on science research for fu ture secondary STEM teachers (Atkins & Salter, 2015, Atk ins Elliott, Jaxon & Salter, 2016). The broad goal of the course is engagement in scientific inquiry, which w e define as the pursuit of coherent, mechanistic mo dels of ICLS2022 Proceedings 881 © ISLS natural phenomena (Hammer & van Zee, 2004). We generally start with a puzzling phenomenon (e.g., a springdriven clock) or question (e.g., is every color in the rainbow?), and, through mediated class conversations, small group work and individual work, make progress towards modeling the phenomena. Over several iterations of the course, materials and designs made with those materials emerged as a critical, and undertheorized, aspect of students’ activity. For the purposes of this research, we consider design in science as designed solutions to open-ended problems that arise in the pursuit of mechanistic models of natura l phenomena. And we define design thinking for science as novel practices and strategies that support powerful and epistemically appropriate designs in response to those problems. Finally, the types of design chal lenges we are interested in are those that are emergent from and in service of students’ epistemic  goals. While designs need not be physical artifacts, for this paper we will limit our discussion to those. We ask the following questions of these artifacts: (1) When do opportunities for design in science emerge in students’ scientific inquiry? (2) What challenges do students face in developing solutions to those problems? and (3) What “design thinking” strategies lead to prod uctive design solutions in light of those problems? In this paper we focus on the first two questions, and make conjectures for the third.

## Research methods & definitions

Data: All class sessions are taped, artifacts and student work and lab notebooks are photographed, and field notes collected. To characterize extended periods of desi gn thinking, we begin by identifying novel and comp lex designed artifacts, as described below, and then constructing design narratives around those objects. Identifying designed artifacts: identifying a novel designed artifact is a gray area; even copied designs often contain some differences. For this study, we were interested in characterizing designs that are prolonged, intentional, novel designs by students in pursuit o f scientific problems. We began by purposeful sampling, based on field notes and images, ultimately identifying 14 designed artifacts for closer analysis. Design narratives: For these artifacts, the researchers developed, through field notes, images, and video, narratives of the designs. The narratives considered: initial design problems students sought to solve or goals for the artifact, its relationship to our ongoing modeling of phenomena, designed solutions, iterations and outcomes. Designs in Scientific Inquiry Below we highlight one example of design from the course: a design that is a novel, student-developed solution to an open-ended, emergent problem that also highli ghts tensions and challenges for productive design.  Due to space limitations, we do not offer detailed moment-by-moment interactions that produced the designs, i nstead offering an overview of the driving questions, challenges and revisions that took place. Corrugated surfaces: a series of designs to select between models Context: This semester, the course focused on energy, beginning with the observation of a Gauss Gun: a simple arrangement of metal ball bearings and strong disk magnets. When an incoming ball is released, it acce lerates towards the magnet, strikes it, and an outgoing bal l is ejected at great speed. The sudden appearance of kinetic energy is surprising and begs explanation, and offers a rich context for modeling energy for a range of disciplines (Atkins Elliott, Sippola, & Watkins, 2018; Atkins Elliott, et al., 2019). One student group argued that the magnet itself mus t supply the energy to the outgoing ball, and thus predicted that the ejected ball would leave at a higher speed than the incoming ball. Other groups disagreed. With a skeptical audience for their claim, the first group sought to determine the two speeds. Here, the design question of how to measure the speed of an object that gains and loses speed very rapidly became the core activity of their group over multiple weeks.

Designs: This group began by using a slow motion setting on their phone camera. However, the data was too blurry to make any claims; better cameras did not solve the problem. They then decided to slow down the “reaction” in a viscous fluid (syrup) and record this. While the reaction was slower, it was qualitatively quite different. They attempted to have the incoming ball fall just before striking the magnet and determine the speed from its trajectory, but the attraction was too great and the ball would not fall, but stick to the magnet. Motion detectors brought in from the undergraduate physics lab were not sensitive enough. The group then thought to roll the balls over a ridged surface and listen for a sound-anticipating that the audible “prtrtrtrtrtrt” sound would indicate speed and answer the question. After trying to use readily-available evenly-ridged surfaces (computer cds), which were not loud enough to analyze, they strung fishing line across a track at regular intervals, but the ridges were too far apart to capture changes in speed. This inspired them to 3-d print ridged surfaces.

The sound was not audibly different, and so the students went to a recording lab on campus. One student processed the waveform of the audio file, expecting that the peaks of the waveform would represent the ICLS2022 Proceedings 882 © ISLS ball striking a ridge, allowing the group to calculate the time between strikes and therefore the speed of the ball. The waveform, however, was more complicated than expected, and peaks did not always represent the striking of a ridge. By this point, the other two groups in the course had convincing arguments against this group’s model (relying on an argument from conservation of energy), and the group abandoned their efforts.

## Findings & future directions

Opportunities for design in scientific inquiry In the brief case presented above, and in most of the cases selected for our study, we notice the following relationships between scientific inquiry and design opportunities that address the above, When do opportunities for design in science emerge in students’ scientific inquiry?

1. Dissent can present a rich opportunity to engage in design. When students developed different

models of phenomena, they readily recognized that empirical evidence could serve as an arbiter, and turned conceptual problems (“which model best describes this phenomenon?”) into design problems (“how do those models differ in observable ways, and how do I create that observation?”). We expect that it is not only the curiosity and/or confusion generated by competing models that drives students’ engagement and persistence in the design (e.g., Watkins, et al, 2018), but social interactions as well: there is a genuine and skeptical audience for the claims they develop. In this way, the grasp of practice that Ford (2008) identifies, wherein scientists are constructors and critiquers of claims, is not imposed but emergent from the activity. Students are genuinely skeptical, even critical, of their peers’ ideas and experimental designs -- driven by, and speaking to, that skepticism and critique -- offer a relatively objective arbiter.

2. Designs themselves can generate novel design “sub”problems. Nascent designs often contain

components, some of which require their own (relatively self-contained) designs. In the example above, we see students who have the design goal of measuring speed with a slow-motion camera, engaged in creating a version of the experiment submerged in syrup. Later, the design problem was how to print a ridged surface.

3. “Failed” designs generate novel design challenges. Designs, of course, frequently do not fully

work as anticipated. As Pickering (2010), notes, the world can offer “resistance,” refusing to participate in anticipated ways, and requiring accommodations by the scientists in what he describes as the “mangle of practice.” In this “mangle,” we see students needing to change course, with novel designs or problems. The need to arbitrate between models became a need to measure speed, which was revised to be a way to measure sound. And designs frequently do not fully work as anticipated, with components requiring re-design. Challenges encountered in addressing design problems When we consider the second question, What challenges do students face in developing solutions to those problems? we note the following:

1.  Invisible content knowledge demands: In the case presented here, students' designs would be

strengthened or facilitated (or even obviated) by additional content knowledge. And while we value developing content knowledge in authentic contexts, where students’ goals require developing the ideas, we note that students themselves did recognize their lack of knowledge (e.g., that white paper does not function as “nothing” but is reflecting the light back through the filter).

2. Lack of familiarity with common tools or designs: Seeing students struggle with long-solved

problems (e.g., a ballistic pendulum for speed measurements), and contrasting this with their use of familiar tools (e.g., a 3-d printer), highlights the value of introducing tools, particularly ones that can be flexibly used in a range of scenarios.

3. Lack of necessity for experimental designs: Again, in the case presented here (and in several

others) we note that there are strong scientific arguments to be made that would obviate the need for an experimental design. Frequently we see students readily turning what could be basic conceptual problems into complex empirical ones.

Disciplinary design thinking in science We now offer some brief speculation on design thinking for science-- particularly in experimental design. First, we anticipate a tension, as noted above, between supporting students’ own designs and building familiarity with standard practices that facilitate more complex designs. We also anticipate that attending to the role experimental design plays in selecting between models will require attention to statistical reasoning from data, which has not been a part of our course. Finally, we note that students often quickly engaged in constructing designed solutions when theoretical solutions (e.g., conservation of energy), which do not require additional empirical data, may be more efficient. Having more extended experience in design throughout their science ICLS2022 Proceedings 883 © ISLS education may have prevented some of the theoretically and experimentally “clumsy” mistakes that students made in these and other design scenarios. It also positions students as designers of scientific ideas, who create authentic and unique solutions to scientific problems.

At the same time, we note that “failed,” unnecessa ry, or inefficient designs are not synonymous with unproductive activity or a lack of progress; they can point students toward important theoretical ideas, reify those ideas in important ways, or strengthen connections between theory and evidence. Moreover, as noted in Pickering (2010), failed designs are common in science and part of the rich mangle of practice. Constructs in the math and science education literature, such as productive struggle (Warshauer, 2015), productive failure (Kapur, 2010), and invented science (Russ & Berland, 2019) highlight this distinction, and suggest further research to better articulate the value of engaging in design in scientific inquiry and identifying potential benefits and drawbacks of students’ failed designs.
