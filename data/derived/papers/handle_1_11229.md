# “Gotta Learn to Hide Your Mistakes:” Troubling Failure Through Its Interactional Formation Within Enduring Struggles

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
There is a growing interest in how learners experience mistakes and failures as opportunities for learning. We analyze the interactional achievement of one mistake surfacing between two youth and the robot over forty-five minutes on a high school robotics team. We conceptualize mistakes to be the interactional accomplishments of problem formulation (Lave, 1988). We then consider how the accomplishment of the mistake in our case can be viewed as what Holland and Lave (2001) called local contentious practice where struggles are mediated between intimate forms of identity development and competing forms of institutional oppression. For the two youth on the team, these intersecting struggles come to matter when the mistake has the possibility of keeping them from having a complete robot at the competition. We posit that the sociopolitical implications – and historical underpinnings – should inform how we consider youth can and should learn from mistakes and failures.

## Introduction

Competitive high school robotics teams are aftersch ool places of goal-oriented making and tinkering for youth. Previous work articulated as them authentic to scientific practice (Verma et al., 201 5). Youth consistently make mistakes in all parts of team production as they learn the skills, participation routines, and knowledge required to perform the everyday tasks.  Building on our previous work studying the social, political, and material developments as part of youth learning on the team (Hennessy Elliott, 2020; Spruil, et al., 2022), in this study we articulate the social construction of one particula r mistake that surfaced in the work of one high sch ool robotics team (Turing Robotics) that was part of a larger Di gital Video Ethnography.  Telling and analyzing the story of this mistake’s becoming troubles the broad depictions of the learning opportunities failures afford learners. In the Learning Sciences, there is a  growing interest in how learners experience mistake s – and their subsequent failures – focused on learning environment design. Scholars have examined failure  in physical computing (e.g., Fields et al., 2019), programming (e.g., DeLiema, et al., 2023) or mathematics reasoning (Kapur & Bielaczyc, 2012). This literature seeing bugs, mistakes, or moments of  failure as learning opportunities (i.e., Kafai, et al., 2019) is often underpinned by constructivist theoretical stances (Papert, 1980) that inform facilitating learning environments which provide learners’ with opportunities to wrestle with failures in both conceptual ideas and physical materials. Kapur &  Bielaczyc’s (2012) work on designing for productive failure in mathematics classrooms has informed learning environments where  teachers allow for students to struggle with parti cular questions before supporting them to find to correct  way to approach them. Gomoll et al. (2022), in another example, highlight the importance of framing failures as constructive for students us ing engineering design to develop human-centered robots in a project-based learning environment. They argue that instruc tors and peers supporting learners to frame failures as short-term moments in the process of “productive success” (Kapur, 2016). Other scholars have articulated the possibilities of learning from failures when facilitators ask learners to engineer physical computing systems that do not work for the ir peers to debug (Fields, et al., 2019).  Further, in previous work colleagues and I (Colin) (Hennessy Elliott, et al., 2024) have articulated the role of teachers in supp orting students using physical computing to develop the sk ills to approach debugging their systems when they don’t work as intended.

DeLiema et al. (2023), capturing learners’ heterogeneous experiences of failure and the wide array of scholarship attuned to failure’s role in learning, frame failures as “an event in the learning process that participants consider to be a breakdown or impasse en route to a goal” (p. 4). They summarize that moments of failure are multifaceted opportunitie s that depend on how learners – and their teachers – approach them. Building on the recent focus on learners’ experience of failure, in this paper we focus on the interactional formulation of a mistake as having histories and developing futures intertwined with the sociopolitical stakes for the learners. We see mistakes as interactional development where an act, an artifact, or a process become perceived as the source of a failure. In other words, mistakes are the interacti onal accomplishments of problem formulation  (Lave, 1988) produced through a dialectic relationship between externalizing goals (i.e., get this thing to work) and producing the setting of those goals (i.e., uncovering what matters in making this thing work). At the same time, there is an emerging field of cri tical interaction and microgenetic analysis (Philip & Gupta, 2020),  considering the co-creation of power dynamics and learning at interact ional scales. This has included, for example,  foci on the brilliance of young children in shaping  family cultures and their learning practices (Keifter, 2021), the relational and land-based aspects of learning (Marin, 2020), practices of desettling science education (Bang & Marin, 2015), and the eth ical and ideological learning in engineering classr ooms (Philip, et al., 2018). Further, other scholars hav e analyzed what it takes to design STEM learning en vironments that forward education justice by supporting youths’ critical science agency (Schenkel & Calabrese Barton, 2020) and rightful presence (Krishnamoorthy, et al., 2023; Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2019) in wa ys that interrogate the historicized and relational nature of learning.

In this paper, we use Interaction Analysis (IA; Jordan & Henderson, 1995) of video data to examine how one mistake in the engineering of the robot came to  matter to sketch mistakes as interactional accompl ishments (Goodwin, 2018) and map their emergent sociopolitic al and historical underpinnings (Vakil, 2020). We see this mistake as an example of the complex nature of mistake-making on the team and its relation to youth’s learning.

## Conceptual framework: Mistakes as interactional accomplishments

Rather than treating mistakes as singular problems to be discovered in the course of activity (i.e., t he thing that went wrong in constructing this bracket for the rob ot), we understand mistakes to be the interactional accomplishments of problem formulatio n (Lave, 1988) produced through a dialectic relations hip between the activity at hand (i.e., get this thing to work) and producing the setting of those goals (i.e., uncovering what matters in making this thing work).  In Lave’s conceptualization, settings a re dialectically constructed in relation to activity. In other words, within some material-built environment, settings are experienced by participan ts in particular ways based on the activities in which they are engaged (activity shapes setting). At the same time, their activity is shaped by what is available in the buil t environment and how they are experiencing it  (setting shapes activity). In the context of this case, the setting for our focal participants included the material space of the shop, the robot arm with which they were working, and the tools available for building it. It was also elaborated by the mentoring relationship between Jav and Santiago, th e shifting activity of identifying and fixing the m istake, and the broader activity of building a robot that will be successful in competition. Simultaneously, the activity at hand was constituted by not only the material conditions  of the shop and the holes cut into the bracket and  robot arm, but also Jav and Santiago’s dynamically unfolding experience of the setting. Within this relationship, Jav and Santiago were mak ing sense of what the mistake actually was  – formulating the problem, which informed what it was  that needed to be fixed. In this way we see how mi stakes are interactionally produced. In this shifting dialectic between setting and activity, Jav and Santiago, in interaction, made sense of what exact ly the mistake was, then took appropriate next step s to resolve it. As the mistake took on new meanings, the setting and activity took on n ew meanings. New features of the setting took on sa lience (e.g., the orientation of the holes on the bracket vs. the orientation of components already installed  on the arm), and the activity took on new goals (e.g., rotating the bracket vs. reinstalling components of the arm). Engaging in new activity and concomitant goals then provided ne w resources for formulating the mistake, thus editi ng the setting. It is in this way that we understand mista kes to be interactionally accomplished  through constant editing of the activity and it’s setting.

Further, we consider how the accomplishment of this  mistake can be viewed as what Holland and Lave

(2001) called local contentious practice, or “the [site] of complex mediations between intimate, inter iorized

practices of identity, on the one hand, and on the other, …race and class relations, the rights and op pression of nationally marginalized ethnic populations, gender inequalities, and competing forms of capitalism” (p. 20-1). In everyday local practice, they argue, broad historic al institutional struggles play out-not in a deterministic way, but in ways that shape how  individuals may author themselves in social practic e. In our analysis we understand Jav and Santiago’s identity practices  (being and becoming specific kinds of youth)  and the local practice of accomplishing this mistake to be in reciprocal relation with the historical struggles of Black and Latine youth and their under-resourced, low-status schools.  Especially within the time crunch of impending comp etition, the dynamically unfolding shifts of setting and activit y in the accomplishment of the mistake we analyze was a realization of the broader historical struggle, and at the same time what Holland and Lave called a “struggle over the future” (p. 27). In this way youth are “histori cal actors” developing possible futures (Gutiérrez et al, 2019) within enduring personal and institutional struggles.

## Settings and methods

Turing robotics: The setting of the mistake Jav and Santiago were participants on their high sc hool robotics team (called Turing Robotics, a pseudonym). In another study, youth – interviewed by youth researchers – share that they felt much more comfortable making mistakes in Turing Robotics than as student in thei r high school classrooms (Hennessy Elliott, et al., 2024). Jav, a 12th grader at the time, identified as a mixed-race young man of Brazilian and Portuguese decent. Santiago, a 9th grader at the time, identified as a Latine young ma n. Turing Robotics was housed in Turing High School, an engineering focused magnet school in a midsized city in the north Atlantic region of the United States. The team was exclusively comprised of youth of color from a variety of cultural diasporas often associated with Black and Latine youth. They also identified as from their city – one that continues to be positioned as poor and lacking. At the time of the mistake profiled in this paper, Tur ing robotics was also amidst a transition to being more lead by young women and youth who identified as gender non-binary (see Spruil, et al., 1, 2022; Hennessy Elliott et al.,

2024) from being a much more masculine dominated team i n previous years (see Hennessy Elliott, 2020). The

coach, Mr. Merkur, was the second coach in the team ’s 20 year history. Mr. Merkur, a White cis-gendered man, was also an engineering teacher at Turing High.

Turing Robotics had participated regionally in an international robotic s competition for decades. In the competitions, which shift in rules and strategy each year, teams were given two months to design and build a robot that can perform in matches.  Competitions privileged competition and cooperation  through a structure of teams participating in multiple matches, three minutes long, that consisted of two three-team alliances that competed for points and rankings. Therefore, competitions were w here teams’ ro bots – and in turn their design, build, and programming abilities – were on display. And they were spaces where members  of the team had to engage with their peers from other teams, as well as judges who  gave out awards for performance in things like des ign, engineering, and cooperation.

In their region, Turing Robotics  were a team that had some historic success in competitions but still struggled to overcome the negative perceptions of t heir city and, in turn, their team  undergirded by racism and classism. Youth on Turing Robotics often talked to each other (and Colin) about the particularly stark comparisons between their robotics team’s resources and those f rom neighboring White, affluent communities, reveal ing overlapping structural inequities, which we conside r as part of the historical struggles in our concep tual framework. During ethnographic data collection including video recordings and interviews, some participants on the team talked about being victims of racial profi ling at competitions (e.g., “I was followed around a lot at the snack bar, probably because I’m Black”) and feeling that their peers from other teams di d not expect much from them because “we’re from [city].”

## Methods

Guided by interaction analysis (IA) practices (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) data were coll ected as part of a threeyear digital video ethnography (Goldman-Segall, 1998) of Turing Robotics with an emergent focus on understanding youths’ participation trajectories on the team and, in turn, how learning happened. For generating the video data used in this paper, I (Colin) used a GoPro camera that Jav wore on his head and s upplementary digital video cameras capturing other parts of the teams’ practice. We selected this case for analysis – depicted in 45 minutes of video recording Jav and S antiago coming to realize there is a mistake and that cannot be fixed by reorienting the parts of the robot – because it is illustrative of the ways mistakes happen and  how they can shift youths’ orientations to the work  of the robotics team and each other. Further, the slowness with which the mistakes comes to be proved to be fruitful in analyzing the roles of enduring struggles in the local contested terrain of their everyday practice. Our analysis focuses on the first 17 minutes of this case where the mistake is interactionally produced. In the rest of the 45 minutes, Jav and Santiago bring the mistake to their coach and work out next steps, which we identified in analysis as a “post-segment” using it to confirm analysis of the mistake formation.

We primarily rely on video analysis (Derry et al., 2010) to understand the unfolding nature of the mistake formation. Our analysis  followed methodological traditions of IA  using iterative cycles of analysis. Cycles consisted of developing and revising transcription, collective viewing between authors, analytic memoing, collective analysis with other researchers, and written analysis. Through the iterative process, we defined and redefined the boundaries of segments – noting and negotiating where the activity, setting, and positioning of the mistake shifted. Attending to the needs of critical interaction and microgenetic analysis (Philip & Gupta, 2020), in our case, to trace powered histories and struggles  against oppression, we drew on other ethnographic d ata including fieldnotes and semi-structured interviews generated around the time of video data collection. We also reviewed video recordings from the week leading up to the mistake formation and at the competition after. During the focal competition year, I (Colin) formally interviewed Jav three times and Santiago – with his peers – twice and maintained regular semi-structure interviews.

From “Hiding your mistake” to “gotta tell [coach]” The mistake’s trajectory over 45 minutes is a story of shifts in activity and settings as Jav and Santiago navigated multiple attempts to make the bracket, as constitut ed, work with the robot’s arm. Our analysis started  with the vignette that opened the paper when Jav attempted t o put the newly cut bracket (with motor attached) o nto the robot’s arm. He struggled to get a nut on one of th e two bolts around the protruding motor, seemingly realizing that the motor was not positioned correctly. Beginn ing the first segment of our analysis, we see the m oment of the nut dropping – as viewed through a GoPro on Jav’s headcam – as a point of departure from the activity of getting the bracket on the arm  to one of fixing the mistake. Jav and Santiago both reified the shift in activi ty by sharing “Do not tell me I just did something very s tupid” (Jav) and “You might have to take the motor out” (Santiago). Jav, a bit later, articulated the need for Santiago to “learn how to hide your m istakes,” further positioning the mistake as something easily fixable. As Silvis et al, (2022), analyze in the context of Kindergarten robotics, these moves signify that the arm (and sub sequently the robot) are worthy of sociotechnical care. We identify this first shift as the first segment of o ur analysis. Across each of the segments for analysis – chosen to coincide with a bidirectional shift in setting and activity – Jav and Santiagonegotiated their developing relationship to the bracket as well as their develo ping relationship to each other. They moved from po sitioning the mistake as something to be hidden easily, to something to be h idden with a bit more work, to something that needed a more time-intensive fix like recutting the bracket.  Further, Jav’s positioning of Santiago as a support rather than a contributor to the work of hiding the mistake and Santiago’s consistent bids to contribute show their developing relationship as a mentor – mentee pairing.

In the end – through attempts to detach the motor, flip the brac ket, reorient the motor, and others – Jav, Santiago, and the bracket concluded that the bracket needed to be fixed or remade in so me way.  In other words, the mistake was interactionally produced through sh ifting the activity from one to hide the mistake in  a setting where the bracket was just oriented incorrectly, to  an activity of  fixing the mistake more publicly in a setting where the holes cut on the bracket was not going to work in any configuration. It became a mistake that could not be hidden. In our case, the near-future competition, -- and its’ undergirding personal and institutional struggles – impacted the ways that Jav and Santiago oriented to  the positioning of this mistake.

The cascading attempts to hide the mistake across t he 5 segments of analysis were punctuated by shifts  in tone and pace, indicative of the pressure to get th is part of the robot completed as quickly as possib le. Further, considering how Jav and Santiago positioned who had access to the mistake suggests a pressure to get things right rather than learn from their mistakes. We identified two major sources of evidence for the  ways that the contentious practice of the mistake’s becoming was interleaved with overlapping historical struggles: 1) increasing pacing and more serious tone and 2) limi ting who has access to seeing and knowing what the mistake was.

When Jav needed to shift the activity and broaden t he setting to realize that the mistake could not be  hidden as initially thought, his tone shifted to be much m ore serious and less playful. When Santiago, for in stance, tried to joke with him in Segment 4 Jav repeatedly told h im to shut up. Further, from segment 2, onward, Jav  spoke increasingly fast and said things like “you gotta work fast here” (Segment 2) which Santiago repeated later “work fast man” (Segment 4). These are indications of the  pressure Jav felt to get the mistake hidden quickly – and the increasing need to work and speak fast (and less) to get it done. The impending pressures were amplified because their team had fewer youth participants than many of the large teams they competed against from large suburban communities. Fewer participants and fewer resources  meant they could not, for example, usually bui ld two different versions of the competition robot.

Jav also indicated the need to limit who had access  to the knowing what the mistake was. First, he slo wly brought Santiago on board with the process, only sharing what they were going to do (e.g., flip the wrist, take the motor off) rather than what the problem might be (u ntil segment 5) even as Santigo made several bids f or alignment and asks for explanation. Further, twice (Segment 3 and Segment 4) Jav wonders where Coach Merkur is and indicates that he wants to keep this from hi m. In this, who has access  to the mistake is a powered relation that Jav takes control of. Later, when he shares with the coach what the issue was and how he thinks t hey should fix it, they both talk about the lack of time they have that day and, implicitly, before the competition. This struggle to hide the mistake from the coach is linked to a struggle to hide the mistake from ot hers at competitions and the need to be seen as successful at competition.

Further, as mentioned above, in other instances in my time with the team youth talked about the material and social consequences of anti-Black racism and other forms of discrimination they  endured at competitions (e.g., being followed around the concession  stand and accused of possibly stealing because of w hat they looked like). At the same time, Jav described going to competitions as their “opportunity to learn how to talk to White people,” when talking tonewcomers who were going to their f irst competition that year. Speaking as a light ski nned, mixed-race, person, and around two White adult authority figures (Coach Merkur & Colin), this comment gestured more to the struggle against racial injustices that the competition represented for Jav (and the team) rather than it actually being about just learning to talk to folks  from another race and/or culture. He was inculcati ng them into the struggle against racial injustice that the team, and their city, represented. In this way, to be seen, as a team, as equal to their White, more affluent, peers was situated in the larger politics of racialized power in STEM education (Vakil & Ayers, 2019). And it represents a bid in t he struggles over the future of how they – and their city – can have developing engineering identities.

## Sociopolitical implications of mistakes as interactionally produced

The interactional accomplishment  of the mistake surfaced contentious practice caused by  the time crunch Jav, Santiago, and their coach were feeling. But the tim e crunch – and it’s resulting social pressures – were mediated by larger struggles associated with the competition including struggles to be seen as valued youth, engineers, and participants in the robotics competition community from marginalized social positions of being Black and Latine youth – and young women and gender non-binary youth for others on the team. In this way, mistakes in learning environments are not neutral but shaped by their po sitioning within institutional struggles, identity development, and the connective tissue between them  (Radke, 2021). This means, to some degree, making mistakes can be either moments that discipline youth out of STEM (Hennessy Elliott, 2020; McGee, 2020) or “moments of rightful presence” (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2019).  In addition to making spaces that are “emotionally safe for students to make mistakes” (Donaldson, 2019, p. 536), the po wered nature of mistakes and their resulting failur es, therefore, means that our conceptions of the role of mistakes in learning must consider their historical trajectories and sociopolitical implications. We must, as designers of learning spaces, explicitly consider the history-inpersons perspective on how learners interactionally produce mistakes. As an example, Robotics competitions are deeply  imbedded within the military industrial complex, often sponsored by defense contractors and other military-connected corporations. These connections are part of the “imagination battle” (Benjamin, 2024) that extend particular kinds of STEM and engineering practices that embed racial injustices (Benjamin, 2019). In these STEM learning spaces, t here are competing notions of what equity and justice in STEM learning looks like (Vossoughi & Vakil, 2019). Having such connections also signals possible ideological learning on what engineering can and cannot do in relation to human lives (see Philip, et al., 2017). Competing in such engineering spaces extends representational and respectability politics in the  face of histories of injustice and oppression against racialized groups, and women and gender non-binary folks (SenguptaIrving & Vossoughi, 2019). The sociopolitical implications in contentious practice should inform how researchers and educators consider youth can and should learn from mistakes that have larger competitive stakes. For Turing Robotics, as coaches and mentors,  we might explicitly engage with what mistakes can feel like and mean  as the competition gets closer and the robot needs to be b uilt. In other learning spaces, this means identifying where personal and institutional struggles emerge for learners and finding ways to d evelop a communal sense of solidarity around learning from mistakes that also address their social, political, and personal consequences. Access to the ability to make the kind of  mistake Jav and Santigo (and others) made and learn from it is important. But considering the racial – and interconnected economic – politics of STEM education (Vakil & Ayers, 2019), means accounting for the ways these mistakes candraw heavily into institutionalized and personal struggles against oppression. Further, it implores the growing field of scholars researching failure to find ways to consider how youth and children’s  interactions with failures in STEM lea rning environments are part of historic and current struggles for justice, identity development, political learning. As Silvis et al., (2022) posits this might mean we might question what makes certain technical  objects “social actors worthy of care,” (p. 3). Wh at, then, might it look like to design  learning spaces for failures that include organization of solidarities in struggles for a more just world? To think with Jav’s words at the start of this paper: instead of youth having to learn to hide their mistakes, it might be about helping youth imagining new ways to learn from them in service of their struggles for the future.
