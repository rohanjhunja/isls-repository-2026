# Profiling Self-Regulated Learners in Flipped Project-Based Learning: A Person-Centered Analysis in Higher Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While flipped project-based learning (PBL) shows promise for developing self-regulated learning (SRL), students engage with these environments heterogeneously. Using person-centered analysis with 84 higher education students across four courses, we identif ied three distinct SRL profiles —Foundational Regulators (52%), Strategic Regulators (29%), and Expert Self-Navigators (19%)—each requiring differentiated instructional support. Our SRPBL framework, developed through research-practice partnership, demonstrates significant pre-post gains (d=0.61–0.83) while revealing that one-size-fits-all implementation leaves over half of students with minimal SRL development. We provide evidence-based design recommendations for differentiated SRPBL implementation targeting each profile’s developmental needs.

Modern workplaces demand autonomous learners capable of managing complex projects, yet traditional higher education provides limited opportunities to develop self-regulated learning (SRL). Project-based learning (PBL) offers authentic contexts for fostering SRL, but implementation remains challenging due to students’ heterogeneous regulation skills and instructors’ difficulty in providing adaptive scaffolding (Wu, 2024). Understanding these individual differences is crucial for designing effective learning environments. SRL strategies—goal-setting, monitoring, help-seeking—predict academic success (Panadero, 2017). Flipped classroom designs create temporal space for  SRL by shifting content outside class (Bishop & Verleger, 2013). Yet few studies examine how distinct SRL profiles interact with such environments (Tempelaar et al., 2024).

To address this gap, we developed the Self-Regulated Project-Based Learning (SRPBL) framework (Zarouk et al., 2019) through research-practice partnership. SRPBL integrates: (1) flipped preparation for pre-class autonomy, (2) authentic projects requiring sustained regulation, and (3) scaffolds for metacognitive, motivational, and collaborative processes. Implemented through Moodle to support resource access and progress monitoring, SRPBL aligns technology with classroom realities where digital tools enhance—rather than replace— face-to-face collaboration (Roschelle et al., 2016).

## Research questions

This study investigates three research questions through a quasi-experimental pretest-posttest design with 84 students across four university courses, combining quantitative analysis (paired t-tests, K-means cluster analysis

— a method that groups individuals into non-overlapping subsets based on statistical similarity — and hierarchical

regression) with qualitative data from student feedback and instructor interviews:

• RQ1: How does the SRPBL approach impact students’ self-regulated learning strategies (motivational

beliefs, regulation strategies, collaborative strategies)?

• RQ2: What distinct SRL profiles emerge among students engaging with flipped PBL, and how do these

profiles differ in their response to the intervention?

• RQ3: Which factors (motivation, collaboration, instructional facilitation, problem quality) predict SRL

development, and do these differ by profile?

This work contributes to learning sciences in three ways. First, we provide empirical evidence that integrated flipped PBL significantly develops SRL competencies (effect sizes d=0.61– 0.83, where Cohen’s d: 0.2=small, 0.5=medium, 0.8=large). Second, we identify three SRL profiles challenging one-size-fits-all implementation: over half of students (52%) showed minimal SRL gains despite the intervention, suggesting need for adaptive support. Third, we demonstrate that person-centered analysis reveals actionab le design implications —such as intensified scaffolding for “Foundational Regulators” and peer mentoring for “Expert Self-Navigators”—that average-effect studies miss.

## Theoretical framework and related work

Self-regulated learning (SRL) in technology-enhanced contexts SRL encompasses “self-generated thoughts, feelings, and actions cyclically adapted to goal attainment” (Zimmerman, 2002). In higher education, SRL competencies predict academic achievement and professional readiness (Broadbent & Poon, 2015). Yet students rarely develop SRL spontaneously—they require structured opportunities and explicit instruction (Dignath et al., 2008). Technology-enhanced environments can make SRL visible and support strategy deployment (Prasse et al., 2024). The three SRL dimensions were selected on theoretical grounds: motivational beliefs as a precondition for SRL (Pintrich, 1991), regulation strategies as core processes following Zimmerman’s  model, and collaborative strategies are not merely social activities but an SRL requirement in team-based PBL (Järvelä et al., 2013). Flipped project-based learning (PBL) as a design for SRL (SRPBL Framework) PBL creates authentic conditions for SRL by presenting ill-defined problems requiring extended coordination (Hmelo-Silver, 2004). PBL demands SRL strategy activation—making it ideal for developing self-regulation (Loyens et al., 2015). However, PBL research reports mixed results: students without adequate prior self-regulatory skills struggle with the open-ended structure (Bui & Johnson, 2024; Zheng et al., 2020). This is precisely why the integration of flipped classroom design is theoretically motivated. Flipped classroom designs complement PBL by shifting content delivery outside class (Bishop & Verleger, 2013). Critically, this operationalizes three SRPBL’s Model phases: pre-class preparation activates forethought strategies (goal-setting, planning, resource management); in-class project work activates performance strategies (monitoring, time management, help-seeking); and structured project milestones create occasions for reflection strategies (self-evaluation, adaptation). By separating content delivery from application, flipped PBL also reduces the cognitive overload that prevents novice learners from self-regulating in fully open-ended environments (Sweller, 2011). The full operationalization is documented in Zarouk et al. (2020) and Zarouk (2021).. Person-centered approaches to SRL Most SRL research employs variable-centered analysis, reporting averages that may obscure individual differences (Järvelä et al., 2013). Person-centered analysis identifies subgroups with distinct SRL patterns (Saqr et al., 2024; Wormington & Linnenbrink-G arcia, 2017). If distinct profiles exist, interventions should target profile-specific needs rather than assuming uniform support benefits all. Gap statement and study positioning Despite theoretical arguments for flipped PBL supporting SRL, empirical evidence remains limited—particularly person-centered analyses revealing who benefits and why. This study addresses three gaps: (1) examining integrated flipped PBL with explicit SRL scaffolding, (2) identifying SRL profiles beyond average effects, and

(3) incorporating instructors as research partners. Our mixed-methods design triangulates self-report

questionnaires and qualitative interviews.

## Research design and platform implementation

We employed a quasi-experimental pretest-posttest design within design-based research methodology (McKenney & Reeves, 2018). Four courses were redesigned following SRPBL principles with five instructor-partners at a Portuguese polytechnic during Spring 2019. Each 8-week course followed flipped PBL: stude nts accessed content via Moodle pre-class, then worked face-to-face in teams. Instructors adapted projects to their disciplines: business model canvases (Economics), corpus-based genre (Tourism). Full course design is provided in Zarouk et al. (2020) and Zarouk (2021).

## Participants

Of 120 enrolled students, 84 (70%) completed both questionnaires (M_age=22.3, 69% female). ## Measures: SRPBL questionnaire

We adapted the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire  (MSLQ) (Pintrich, 1991) and Online Self-Regulated Learning Questionnaire  (OSRLQ) (Barnard et al., 2009) for flipped PBL, appending validated scales for instructional facilitation and problem quality (detailed below), creating a comprehensive questionnaire: the SRPBL Questionnaire (α=0.82–0.91), available at: https://shorturl.at/vu479 The instrument measures three primary SRL dimensions:

• Motivational Beliefs:  (11 items; α_pre=.83, α_post=.91): intrinsic/extrinsic goal orientation, self-efficacy, control beliefs.

• Regulation Strategies: (27 items; α_pre=.84, α_post=.89): metacognitive processes before/during/after

learning (13 items), time management (4 items), environment structuring (4 items), persistence (6 items).

• Collaborative Strategies:  (9 items; α_pre=.82, α_post=.90): help-seeking (6 items), peer learning (3

items).

Figure 2 SRPBL Questionnaire Structure 1040 All items used 5-point Likert scales (1=Never to 5=Always). Both questionnaire waves were delivered via Moodle’s survey module during scheduled in-class sessions: the pre-test was completed in the first class of week 1, the post-test in the final class of week 8, ensuring supervised completion. Internal consistency was good-toexcellent across all scales.

## Supplementary measures (post-test only)

To examine contextual factors influencing SRL, we administered two additional scales at post-test, adapted from validated instruments and reviewed for content validity with all five instructor-partners:

• Instructional Facilitation: (13 items; α=.82): course design & organization (4 items), facilitation quality

(6 items), direct instruction (3 items). Adapted from Arbaugh et al. (2008) and DiDonato (2013).

• Problem Quality: (18 items; α=.83): clarity (2 items), clues/keywords (2 items), familiarity (3 items),

interest (4 items), collaboration triggers (3 items), reasoning stimulation (4 items). Adapted from Sockalingam et al. (2012).

Qualitative data Qualitative data comprised open-ended post-test survey items and semi-structured interviews with all five instructors, analyzed thematically to contextualize quantitative findings. We conducted three complementary analyses to address our research questions:

• RQ1 (Impact): Paired-samples t-tests compared pretest-posttest scores on each SRL scale. We calculated

Cohen’s d effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals for meandifferences. Assumptions (normality, no outliers) were verified through Q-Q plots and boxplots; Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirmed results for scales with slight non-normality.

• RQ2 (Profiles): K-means cluster analysis grouped students by their SRL gain scores (posttest minus

pretest) across nine indicators: motivational beliefs and eight regulation/collaboration subscales. We determined optimal cluster number (k) through elbow method examination of within-cluster sum of squares and silhouette coefficients, then validated with distance-between-cluster-centers metrics. Hierarchical regression (described below) informed variable selection for clustering.

• RQ3 (Predictors): Hierarchical multiple regression tested which factors predict SRL regulation strategy

gains. Each subscale = mean of constituent items; composites = mean of subscales. Predictors entered in four blocks: Model 1 (motivational beliefs gains), Model 2 (+coll aborative strategy gains), Model 3 (+instructional facilitation and problem quality), Model 4 (+prior online experience). Regression assumptions verified via histogram and P-P plot.

All analyses used SPSS 27 with α=.05. Given our interest in practical significance, we emphasize effect sizes and confidence intervals alongside p-values (Cumming, 2014). The nested structure of the data (students within instructors within courses) was not  formally modeled due to sample size constraints; this is acknowledged as a limitation addressed in the Discussion.

RQ1: Impact of SRPBL on SRL strategies Strongest effects The largest effect emerged for Environment Structuring (d=1.17): students reported substantially improved ability to create productive study spaces after SRPBL. This reflects the flipped structure’s weekly pre-class demands, which required independent management of home study environments —a recurring demand traditional courses do not create (Zarouk et al., 2020). Help-seeking (d=0.83), During-Learning Strategies (d=0.73), Time Management (d=0.69), and Motivational Beliefs (d=0.56) also showed strong effects, indicating students became more comfortable requesting assistance, better monitored comprehension, scheduled work effectively, and developed task value perceptions.

## Profile validation and significance

One-way ANOVA confirmed the three clusters differed significantly on overall SRL gain (F(2,81)=30.08, p<.001, η²=.43), with all pairwise differences significant (p<.001). Prior online experience and demographic variables did not predict cluster membership (χ²<5.2, p>.10), suggesting profiles reflect SRL readiness rather than background characteristics.

## Key findings

Model 2 (motivational + collaborative gains) explained 46% of variance in regulation strategy development (R²=.46, p<.001), with both predictors significant: motivational beliefs (β=.34, p<.001) and collaborative strategies (β=.47, p<.001). Adding instruct ional facilitation, problem quality, and prior online experience contributed minimal additional variance. Students who gained motivation and collaborative skills also developed regulation strategies.

Interpretation + connection to profiles The strong collaborative-regulation link (β=.47) makes theoretical sense: PBL requires coordinating with teammates, necessitating time management, persistence, and monitoring —precisely the regulation strategies we measured. The null effect for instructional facilitation requires two complementary interpretations. Statistically, a ceiling effect is evident: students rated facilitation consistently high (M=3.7/5, SD=0.4), yielding near-zero variance that cannot predict individual SRL differences (Pearson r=−. 057, n.s.; regression ΔR²=.019, p=.239). Conceptually, the global facilitation measure captured quality but not profile-specific fit: Foundational Regulators needed more structured guidance than provided, while Expert Self-Navigators thrived on the same level. The null finding does not imply facilitation is unimportant—a single global indicator cannot detect the differential effects that profile-sensitive measurement would reveal.

This study examined SRPBL’s impact on SRL  development in authentic higher education contexts, moving beyond average treatment effects to identify distinct learner profiles. Three key findings emerged: (1) SRPBL significantly improved most SRL dimensions with medium-to-large effects, validating the pedagogical integration of flipped classroom and PBL structures; (2) students clustered into three profiles differing dramatically in their SRL gains —Foundational Regulators (52%) showed minimal benefit, Strategic  Regulators (29%) improved moderately, and Expert Self-Navigators (19%) made substantial gains; (3) motivational and collaborative development predicted regulation strategy growth, while instructional quality perceptions did not. These findings challenge one-size-fits-all implementation and suggest adaptive scaffolding keyed to learner profiles.

## Finding 1: SRPBL effectively develops SRL

Interpretation of significant gains The moderate-to-large effects for help-seeking (d=0.83), during-learning monitoring (d=0.73), time management (d=0.69), and environment structuring (ES) (d=1.17) provide empirical support for flipped PBL as an SRL-fostering environment. These gains compare favorably to standalone SRL interventions (meand=0.63; Dignath & Büttner, 2008), suggesting authentic project contexts may be more powerful thandecontextualized strategy teaching. The (ES) effect reflects the flipped approach’s structural demands: stude nts were required to organise their own pre-class study sessions weekly, creating repeated occasions to develop this regulatory competency. Interpretation of null/weak effects The unchanged before-learning and weak after-learning effects warrant discussion. Our qualitative data suggest these reflect implementation choices: predefined objectives reduced opportunities for goal-setting (before-learning), while structured milestones  replaced post-hoc evaluation (after-learning). Future iterations might explicitly build goal-setting and evaluative reflection into project workflows.

## Finding 2: Three profiles require differentiated support

Foundational Regulators—the challenge The most concerning finding is that over half of students (52%) made negligible SRL gains despite SRPBL participation. Foundational Regulators entered with low self-regulation capacity and, without targeted support, struggled to benefit from the open-ended structure. Qualitative data revealed cognitive overload (Sweller, 2011): novices simultaneously navigated unfamiliar PBL format, course content, and novel SRL strategies. Their modest help-seeking gains (M=0.13) suggest they learned one concrete strategy but didn’t generalize. Design Implication: Foundational Regulators need explicit, structured SRL instruction embedded within projects: explicit teaching of planning strategies with templates, guided monitoring with instructor-led reflection prompts, 1044 and gradual release as students internalize strategies. Rather than assuming students will “pick up” self-regulation through project experience, instructors should front-load SRL instruction. Strategic and expert profiles “Strategic Regulators”  (29%) represent the “responsive” middle: they entered with moderate SRL skills and improved meaningfully with standard SRPBL scaffolding. This group likely benefited most from the current implementation—projects challenged them appropriately, and facilitation was sufficient. Their moderate gains suggest they’re on a developmental trajectory toward expertise with continued practice. “Expert Self-Navigators” (19%) might be under-challenged by uniform scaffolding. Their substantial gains — particularly in help-seeking and peer learning —suggest they not only mastered SRL but became collaborative leaders. Yet they represent a minority.

Design Implication:  Adaptive SRPBL could leverage “Experts” as peer mentors for “Foundational” students, benefiting both: Experts consolidate their SRL through teaching, while Foundationals receive near-peer modeling. This requires intentional team composition and role assig nment, which research-practice partnership could iteratively refine.

## Interpreting regression findings

The motivational-regulation link (β=.34) reflects classic SRL theory: students must want to self-regulate before deploying strategies. SRPBL’s authentic problems likely increased task value (“this matters”), enhancing motivation which enabled strategy use.

Research-practice partnership: “Partnering with purpose” This study exemplifies “Partnering with Purpose” through authentic research-practice partnership. Instructors weren’t research subjects but co-designers who identified which content could be flipped, designed discipline-authentic projects, and provided context for interpreting findings. This enabled studying SRPBL under real-world constraints rather than idealized lab conditions. Partnership creates tensions: instructors prioritized course objectives over research purity, introducing implementation variabil ity. Yet this variability is precisely what produces generalizable findings —SRPBL works in messy, authentic contexts where adaptations are inevitable, and implementation fidelity matters less than responsive facilitation.

## Limitations

Several limitations warrant mention. First, single-institution, one-semester design limits generalizability (Erdogan & Senemoglu, 2017). Second, self-report measures may inaccurately estimate strategy use (Veenman, 2011). Third, we did not assess academic achievement outcomes. Fourth, quasi-experimental design limits causal inference. Fifth, the nested data structure was not formally modeled; future studies may should employ multilevel modeling (HLM). Finally, instructor experience varied across courses. This work advances learning sciences through three contributions. First, integrated pedagogical design— combining flipped classroom, PBL, and explicit SRL scaffolding—significantly develops SRL competencies in authentic higher education contexts (d=0.61–0.83), comparable to standalone SRL interventions. Second, personcentered analysis reveals that 52% of students gained minimally despite positive average effects —reporting only averages would mislead practitioners into believing SRPBL works for everyone. Thi rd, we contribute validated SRL profiles with theoretically meaningful labels and evidence-based design recommendations. For practitioners, this study offers concrete implications. Instructors should: (1) assess students’ entering SRL capacity through brief questionnaires or early project performance, (2) provide differentiated scaffolding keyed to profiles (intensive for Fo undationals, moderate for Strategics, peer-mentoring for Experts), and (3) explicitly teach SRL strategies rather than assuming students will discover them. The SRPBL framework offers a blueprint, but implementation requires adaptive facilitation informed by ongoing formative assessment. This study’s findings motivate our next phase: integrating learning analytics into SRPBL  framework to enable profile membership identification and prediction.
