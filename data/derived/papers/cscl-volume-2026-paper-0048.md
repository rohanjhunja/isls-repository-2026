# Self-organizing Interdisciplinary Team in Generative AI-Supported Collaboration Context

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We report two-year design-based research (2024: N = 14, 2025: N = 19) on interdisciplinary research practice by doctoral students supported by generative AI (GenAI). The purpose was to explore necessary support s for interdisciplinary team formation by investigating how learners self-organized teams in this community. In both years, participants formed five teams to prepare interdisciplinary research proposals. Their video recordings data were analyzed. Findings revealed that AI-enabled exploratory collaboration experiences functioned as a collaboration history to build students’ networks for the final team decision. We further identified three boundary-crossing patterns (e.g., Wide-Range Explorers). T hrough these diverse interactions, the community-level interdisciplinary distance converged toward a balance between novelty and mutual understandability. A m ore nuanced understanding of interdisciplinary team formation process in GenAI-supported setting, as well as the development of a visualization system displaying interdisciplinary distance, will benefit interdisciplinary team formation process.

While today’s complex societal problems demands higher education to provide interdisciplinary learning, several critical questions  remain (Cai et al., 2025; Markauskaite et al., 2024). One of the m is interdisciplinary team formation, requiring  to seek collaborators outside of their academic networks  (Daniel et al., 2022). Team formation has been commonly  led either by instructors or by students. For example, mechanical and civil engineering students collaborated to solve a real industrial engineering problem  (Bani-Hani et al., 2018) through random team assignment by the instructor or by students themselves. Another line of s tudies has provided technological support for team formation. It includes genetic algorithms  or evolutionary algorithms, where students’ knowledge, their learning styles, among others are used for grouping  (e.g., Akhrif et al., 2021). When applying i n highly interdisciplinary contexts, two challenges can arise. First, predicting interdisciplinary collaboration fit in advance is a difficult task because participants have limited information about each other. Second, existing technological supports (e.g., genetic algorithms) can hardly work for the lack of any mutually comparable knowledge levels as a basis for grouping. Therefore, in our design-based research (DBR) project across two years, we attempted to provide  learners with information that can be used for team formation through GenAI. Specifically, our students explored the communities through collaborati on experiments, self-organized their own teams, and co-created interdisciplinary research proposals, with GenAI available to support them at every stage of the process. Therefore, b y investigating how learners form their interdisciplinary teams in our GenAI-supported context, we aim to provide insights into designing and understanding more suitable team formation support in the future. ## Theoretical framework

This study adopts the Complex Adaptive Systems (CAS) framework (Dooley, 1997; Holland, 1992)  to conceptualize interdisciplinary team formation as an AI-augmented process of self-organization. Within CAS, multiple interacting agents generate emergent system-level structures through local interactions without centralized control. In our context, stu dents function as agents who scan their classroom environment, engage in conversations, and construct interpretive and action-oriented schemas regarding potential collaborators. Through distributed and adaptive partner-selection processes, interdisciplinar y teams emerge as socially negotiated outcomes of ongoing interaction. GenAI serves as an informational augmentation mechanism by projecting potential collaborative themes and expanding locally available decision-relevant information. Although team formation remains grounded in human interaction, AI-supported interaction may influence the trajectory of emergent team formations. This augmented CAS perspective shifts analytical attention from static individual attributes to relational dynamics, enabling examination of how conversational exchanges, adaptive decisions, and AI-mediated information jointly contribute to the emergence of interdisciplinary teams in classroom settings. 334

## Method

Our DBR (e.g., Brown, 1992) was conducted in 2024 and 2025 as three-day workshops. The workshop design was refined based on our aforementioned pilot study (Naganuma et al., 2024; 2025; 2026). This DBR focuses on parts of  the design conjecture concerning the relationship between embodiment and the expected mediating process toward team formation as outcomes.

## Participants and contexts

Fourteen doctoral students at a national university in Japan participated in 2024, and 19 students in 2025, representing various departments from natural sciences to huminites. This study complied with ethical guidelines. The workshop’s goal was to develop high-quality interdisciplinary collaborative research proposal s. It comprised eight 90-minutes sessions with four main activities. After the orientation (Sessions 1-2), students were assigned to teams and asked to judge the promisingness of AI-generated themes twice as exploratory collaboration experiments (Sessions 3-4). Second, they talked with others through four rounds (three 15 min-intervals and final round) to decide on the final team members (Session 5). After each interval, they rotated to talk with different students. Then, five teams were in 2024 and five teams were formed in 2025.  Third, teams created research proposals (Sessions 6-7), and finally, each team gave a 10-minute presentation (Session 8). For RQ1, to investigate the implicit effect of incorporating collaboration experiment  activities (Sessions 3 & 4), we used team composition record from the  collaboration experiment  activities and the final team s. We then visualized the team member transition s from two collaboration experiment activities to final team to investigate the implicit effect of collaboration experiment activities. RQ2 focused on the team formation activity (Session 5), so video-recoding data of students’ movement during the team formation process were collected. We then developed an interdisciplinary distance score adapted from the disciplinary affinity measure by Takahashi et al. (2023). The score was defined as 100 minus the affinity value between two disciplines. Since affinity is asymmetric,  there are two distance values for each pair. For example, since the affinity from Neuroscience to Medicine is 58.2550 while the affinity from Medicine toneuroscience is 3.2110, the corresponding interdisciplinary distances are 41.7450 and 96.7890, respectively. Calculations of the scores were based on 26 Scopus disciplines, excluding the General category. We analyzed the scores at two levels to capture both individual and collective aspects of interdisciplinary interactions. For individual-level, we considered only the distances from their discipline to those of their partners, the values of which reflect the individual’s interdisciplinary communication effort.  For instance, if Member A talked with Members B and C, we averaged scores of A to B and A to C to quantify Member A’s communication effort. After the calculation, to examine the  individuals’ interaction patterns toward team formation, we conducted hierarchical cluster analysis (Ward’s method) on the interdisciplinary distance scores  after eliminating missing values.

For team/community level, in each team, we calculated directed distances among all discipline pairs. For example, with three members (A, B, and C), six pairs exist (A to B, B to A, A to C, C to A, B to C, and C to B). Their average gave the team-level interdisciplinary distance scores. Then, we calculated the average of all teams’ scores to represent the community-level interdisciplinary distance and also computed the standard deviation to capture how much the team-level indices varied within the community. This suggests that collaboration experi ment activities in Sessions 3 and 4 implicitly functioned as a collaboration history to help participants evaluate one another’s collaboration potential toward team formations.
