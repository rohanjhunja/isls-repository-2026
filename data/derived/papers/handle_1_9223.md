# Epistemic Scaffolds to Promote Disagreement Identification and Resolution for Multiple Conflicting Documents

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Resolving disagreements among multiple documents by identifying contradictions and reasoning about them is at the core of integrating information from multiple conflicting documents. This study reports the results of an experiment that examined the effectiveness of an epistemic scaffold for promoting productive epistemic discourse to reason about multiple conflicting documents to reach reasonable conclusions. The scaffold introduced possible reasons for why disagreements exist among multiple documents. The results show that the experimental groups exchanged more reasons for disagreements and referred to more types of reasons, including both those that appeared in the scaffold and their own original reasons. Excerpts of student discussions illustrate that the scaffold enabled richer discussions that considered diverse perspectives and possible reasons for disagreements. However, even the experimental groups drew their final conclusions based on only a few of the reasons they considered in their analysis.

## Introduction

People in the digital age struggle to reach reasonable conclusions about issues  due in part to the abundance of conflicting information on the Internet (Kienhues et al., 2016). The essential skill of integration requires drawing information from multiple diverse sources, understanding the issues, and reaching reasonable conclusions (Barzilai et al., 2018). However, laypeople have limited skill in integrating conflicti ng information. Laypeople are often biased by personal preferences and prior knowledge (Barzilai, et al., 2020). They do not sufficiently consider and weigh alternative viewpoints and carefully examine pieces of evidence when integrating conflicting information from multiple sources (Bråten et al., 2011). Thus, students find it difficult to integrate conflicting information (Barzilai & Zohar, 2012).

Successful integration requires both identifying disagreements (Thomm et al., 2017) and figuring out ways to resolve these disagreements (Allchin, 2011). Identifying disagreement s among multiple documents is challenging for laypeople. For example, students frequently ignore or misunderstand explicit contradictions between websites (Barzilai & Zohar, 2012). Explaining contradictions can help la ypeople understand discrepancies among multiple sources and thus may help resolving their conflicts (Barzilai, et al., 2020). However, research suggests that undergraduates generate few explanations for disagre ements when creating possible explanations (Thomm et al., 2017). They may also fail to resolve disagreements even when identifying reasons for disagreement that could help them do so (Mochizuki et al., 2022). The aim of the present study is to examine the effectiveness of an epis temic scaffold implemented in a CSCL environment that aims to foster lay students’ productive epistemic discourse when they attempt to resolve disagreements among multiple documents. The scaffold is designed to encourage students to think about why the documents disagree with each other from a variety of perspectives and how to resolve these disagreements. We present the results of a preliminary evaluation of the effectiveness of the scaffold in an experimental comparison without the scaffolds targeted at supporting reasoning about disagreements. The CSCL system and its epistemic scaffolds Mochizuki et al. (2022) developed a web-based CSCL system called EDDiE (Electronic Documents Disagreements Evaluation), in which learners analyze a set of conflicting documents th rough creating a graphic organizer (analysis tableau) to synthesize information and analyze disagreements among documents to rea ch reasonable evidence-based conclusions. The tableau is designed to provide an epistemic script (Weinberger et al., 2005) and epistemic scaffolds (Sandoval & Reiser, 2004) that guide learners’ thinking as they analyze documents. The scaffolds are aligned with Grasp of Evidence (GoE) Framework (Duncan et al., 2018), including: analyzing what the main claims, evidence, and sources are; evaluating source credibility and degree of consensus of experts cited in the documents, and evaluating evidence quality from each document. Learners draw colored evidence arrows (solid or dotted) to represent how evidence supports or contradicts each colored claim from other documents. The CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 314 tableau also guides students to generate their integrative conclusions based on th e disagreements they identified and reasoning about the disagreements. Thus, learners can form content connec tions (showing agreements and disagreements) among multiple sources and explain the disagreements among them to reach their conclusions. Figure 1 EDDiE’s Epistemic Scripts and Scaffolds including Disagreement Reason Tags in Comment Box Another important EDDiE scaffold provides disagreement reason tags (Figure 1), which introduce possible reasons for why conflicting sources candisagree, including: differences in duration, different participants, different measures, different conditions, amount of relevant evidence, better or worse methods, vagueness of terms, different interpretations of evidence, quality of experts, amount of support from experts, author trustworthiness, and bias. For instance, differences in duration  indicates that different findings are explained because some studies run for years, and others did for only months. Amount of support from experts indicates that there might be expert consensus on one side and not the othe r. The reasons derive from students’ ideas in our previous research and from reasons listed in Thomm et al. (2017). These tags are designed to prompt students’ productive epistemic discourse to reach reasonable conclusions about conflicting sources. The present study especially addresses the effectiveness of disagreement reason tags. Our past research has revealed that EDDiE’s analysis tableau enhanced productive epistemic discourse around evidence, sources, and claims among multiple documents along with all dimensions of the GoE framework (Mochizuki et al., 2022). This study focuses how lay students used disagreement reason tags to engage in reasoning about disagreements among the documents to reach their conclusions so that we can continue to re fine our design to elicit more productive epistemic discourse toward integrating multiple conflicting information.

## Participants and design

Twenty triads (58 undergraduate and 2 graduate students; 66.7% female; majors educa tion, informatics, and engineering) from two private universities in Japan participated via Zoom. Each triad was provided with five different credible documents in Japanese (217–399 words in English translation) describing different positions on different diets, their positive and negative effects, and relevant scientific studies. Each triad was asked to reach a joint conclusion on the best diet or diets based on their analysis on the documents on EDDiE. Each document was presented as a digital media or blog post that included the source informati on and descriptions of one or more nutritional studies ranging in quality, strength, and number. Each group discussion was recorded and transcribed. The discussion was preceded by an instructional video explaining the functions and usage of EDDiE. A trained facilitator was assigned to support each triad in the usage of E DDiE and facilitate the discussion. The facilitator guided students to read the documents first and then analyze e ach document, followed by identifying and reasoning about disagreements among the documents and developing reasonable conclusio ns based on their analysis; neither the facilitator nor the video explained any norms or strategie s for evaluating evidence or documents.

To examine the effectiveness of disagreement reason tags, each triad was randomly assigned (within universities) to either the experimental or control condition; the experimental condition provided disagreement reason tags as preset tags which appeared when students opened the comment box function while the control condition did not. To ensure their best collective epistemic performance, each group discussion lasted until reaching their conclusion, ranging from 90 to 135 minutes. ## Results

Table 1 presents descriptives of the five dependent measures, along with th e results of Mann-Whitney U tests (suitable for the small number, n = 10, of groups in each condition) on each variable. These results indicates that the triads in the experimental condition had richer discussions related to reas ons for disagreements and how to resolve disagreements than those in the control condition did. The experimenta l triads articulated significantly more reasons (M = 4.00) that appeared in the preset tags during their deliberations; the control tr iads articulated fewer of these reasons (M = 1.50) that appeared in the preset tags. The control triads generated a mean of 1.80 original reasons that were not among the preset tags; this was not statis tically different from the 1.10 original reasons in the experimental group. These results indicate that the scaffold promoted learners’ reasoning about the disagreements but did not constrain them from generating their original ideas  when reasoning about disagreements. However, no significant difference was observed in disa greement resolutions regardless of the types of reasons that helped the resolutions. Even in the experimental group, the number of resolutions based on the reasons appeared in the preset tags was relatively few. Table 2 displays two brief cases illustrating how students in each condition disc ussed reasons for the disagreements they identified and how the disagreement tags promoted student productive epistemic discourse. Bold italicized words in the discourse are reasons that appeared in the preset tags in the experimental groups (thus, bold italicized words in the control groups indicates reasons that were original to them but that w ere reasons among the preset tags in the experimental group). Underlined bold italicized words are the students’ original ideas for reasoning about the disagreements. The control triad (No. 5) developed sever al reasons for disagreements. However, the experimental triad (No. 7) considered more diverse reasons (including reasons appearing in the preset tags as well as their own original reasons) and examined them to  see if these reasons were useful in explaining the disagreements across documents. Thus, most experimental triads more frequently investigated and deliberated possible reasons for disagreements to derive plausible or reasonable explanations, whereas the control triads examined a lesser number of possible reasons without scrutinizing other possible perspectives.

## Discussion and implications

This study shows how the disagreement reasons tags —an epistemic scaffold —promoted productive discussion about reasons for disagreements. The scaffold also did not constrain stu dents’ generative thinking about other reasons for disagreements. However, even though the triads in both conditions develope d a variety of disagreement reasons as they worked with EDDiE’s analysis tableau, they tended to take only a few reasons into account when they tried to resolve the disagreements (cf. Thomm et al., 2017). These findings indicate that learners still need further scaffolding to ac hieve successful integration of multiple conflicting documents to reach reasonable conclusions. They need m ore support in using reasons for disagreement to figure out how to resolve the disagreements. Thus, the e pistemic scaffold may be improved by directly prompting learners to recognize relationships between the disagreement reasons, their ideas for resolving the disagreements, and their conclusions on the analysis tableau through the use of graphical objects such as arrows. This could allow learners both to consider a variety of reasons for  explaining disagreements and to use CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 316 them to reach conclusions. Further research on fostering integration of ideas from multiple conflicting documents is necessary to make proper and successful integration through the learners’ collective analysis on EDDiE.
