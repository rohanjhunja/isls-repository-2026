# Reimagining Literacy as Liberatory Learning: Centering Black Literacies in the Learning Sciences

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper outlines a research program situated at the intersection of the Learning Sciences, Literacy Studies, and Black Studies. The work investigates how racialized histories of literacy shape contemporary learning environments and how Black learners and communities design countercurricula that affirm joy, resistance, and self-determination. Drawing from archival, ethnographic, and design-based methods, this research advances a theoretical framework of bound literacy—a concept describing how literacy has historically been constrained by antiblackness—and explores what learning might look like when literacy is “unbound.” The program contributes to the Learning Sciences by positioning Black literacies as sites of innovation, imagination, and design, offering frameworks and methodologies for understanding learning as a collective, historical, and liberatory act. ## Overview

My research bridges the Learning Sciences, Literacy Studies, and Black Studies to examine how racialized histories of literacy shape learning and how Black communities design liberatory literacies in response. This paper outlines my research program on “bound” and “unbound” literacies and its contributions to theorizing learning as a site of freedom-making.

## Theoretical framework: Learning as a liberatory and historical practice

My research begins from the premise that learning is not only cognitive or social but also historical and political. Building on sociocultural theories of learning (e.g., Vygotsky, 1978) and critical traditions within the Learning Sciences (e.g., Nasir et al., 2006), I conceptualize literacy as a designed social practice shaped by structures of race, power, and history. To theorize this relationship, I introduced the framework of bound literacy (Massengale, 2024), which traces how literacy in the U.S. popular imagination has been historically “bound” to chattel slavery and its afterlives. This reframes literacy not as a neutral skill set but as a cultural construct used to delimit who counts as a learner. By examining how literacy has been racialized—particularly through associations with Black deficiency—this framework exposes how antiblackness has shaped the epistemological foundations of schooling, assessment, and learning theory. At the same time, I view literacy as a site of resistance and design. Across historical and contemporary contexts, Black people have cultivated literacies that imagine freedom beyond dominant educational systems. Aligning with Learning Sciences scholarship that centers imagination, equity, and design (e.g., Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2018), I argue that Black literacies are world-building practices that create new ways of being, knowing, and learning.

Methodological approach: Bridging historical, ethnographic, and design-based inquiry My research blends archival analysis, ethnographic fieldwork, and design-based research to trace how Black literacies emerge, persist, and transform across time. This multimodal approach positions literacy as a layered, intergenerational practice connecting past, present, and future learning ecologies. Through archival work, I analyze primary sources such as the Federal Writers’ Project (FWP) slave narratives to examine how formerly enslaved people described their learning and teaching practices. These narratives reveal literacy as both a tool of domination and a site of resistance, laying the groundwork for theorizing how racialized notions of literacy continue to shape educational discourse. Complementing this historical analysis, I conduct long-term ethnographic research with contemporary Black learners in community-based educational spaces. My dissertation centered on an informal enrichment group in the San Francisco Bay Area that fosters racial affirmation and creativity among Black children aged four to twelve. Over eighteen months of fieldwork—including participant observation, interviews, and multimodal documentation—I explored how children author their own literate identities and reimagine reading and writing as acts of joy and self-determination. Finally, I approach designbased research as a generative praxis rather than a prescriptive model. Building on insights from my ethnographic work, I developed and iteratively refined a series of Creative Literacies workshops that invite children to codesign literacy experiences through storytelling, art, and play. These workshops function as sites of learning, d esign, and theory-building, illuminating how liberatory literacies can be cultivated and sustained in real-world contexts.

Research contributions to the learning sciences This program of research contributes to the Learning Sciences in three key ways: First, my work reframes literacy as a designed and historical construct. By tracing the racialized histories that shape current learning practices, my research expands the field’s understanding of how sociocultural contexts and power relations influence learning. I understand literacy not just as situated action (Lave, 1987) but as historically sedimented design—an inheritance that both constrains and enables future learning. Secondly, this work centers Black literacies as sites of epistemic innovation. My research foregrounds the creative and intellectual labor of Black learners and communities, showing how their everyday practices constitute theories of learning in their own right. This approach aligns with equity-oriented and justice-centered movements within the Learning Sciences (e.g., Esmonde & Booker, 2017), while also contributing new conceptual tools for studying learning as a mode of freedom-making. Finally, my research integrates ethnographic and design-based methodologies. The methodological synthesis I employ offers a model for how the Learning Sciences can bridge qualitative, historical, and design traditions. This allows me to weave archival and contemporary data into narrative analyses that honor the imaginative dimensions of learning. Design-based approaches then transform these insights into practical interventions that support liberatory pedagogies.

Future directions Moving forward, I plan to extend this work in three interconnected directions. First, I will explore intergenerational literacies by examining how literacy learning unfolds across generations within Black families and communities. Preliminary findings from archival and ethnographic data suggest that literate identities are familial and relational—passed through stories, songs, and everyday acts of care. This analysis will inform a theory of intergenerational literacy learning that highlights continuity, inheritance, and imagination across time. Second, I am committed to community-engaged design. I plan to expand the Creative Literacies workshops into a pilot study that partners with educators, families, and children to co-create early literacy resources grounded in joy and self-determination. This work will translate theoretical insights into practice, producing materials that challenge deficit narratives and broaden the possibilities of literacy education. Finally, I will continue theorizing unbound literacies by extending my concept of bound literacy. I aim to explore what it means to unbind literacy from antiblackness, investigating how educational research and design can presuppose Black brilliance and creativity as foundational rather than exceptional. This work advances critical design traditions in the Learning Sciences by envisioning learning environments designed from and for liberation. Across its theoretical, methodological, and practical dimensions, my research advances a vision of the Learning Sciences that takes seriously the historical and racialized conditions under which learning occurs. By centering Black literacies as both analytic and imaginative sites, my work contributes to ongoing efforts in the field to build more equitable, humanizing, and justice-oriented learning environments. Ultimately, I aim to cultivate a body of scholarship that helps the Learning Sciences reckon with its historical inheritances while designing toward freer, more expansive educational futures.
