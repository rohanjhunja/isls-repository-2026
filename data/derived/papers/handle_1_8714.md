# Designing for Youth-Centered Experiences in Informal STEM-rich Making With Minoritized Youth During the Pandemic: Conjectures & Considerations

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The pandemic has brought an abrupt shift from in-person community-based programming at an informal weekly STEM-rich making club with minoritized youth, to one of socially-distanced, solo-work only, virtually-facilitated experience. We explore what such an abrupt pivot from in-person to virtual programming meant, including the challenges and tensions, for the design of pedagogical materials and virtual facilitation practices, when facilitators aim to maintain an asset-based, relational engagement where youth can make in agentic ways, pandemic notwithstanding.

Purpose The Covid-19 pandemic has surfaced many inequities in STEM education, especially for youth of color. Learning environments as youth have come to know them remain unstable, exacerbated by the sudden and necessary pivot to virtual platforms that youth and STEM educators have had to learn to navigate very quickly. While pandemic-related discussions around learning environments in formal classrooms have been robust (McKinney de Royston & Vossoughi, 2021), how, for whom, and to what ends informal STEM settings might pivot during the pandemic have been largely left out of the conversation. This proposal investigates the virtual pedagogical practices of informal educators in a community-based makerspace (Make Club) at a local Boys and Girls Club (BGC) that, prior to the pandemic, had operated as a sustained, weekly in-person program for youth. Our overarching research question is:  What are the design features built into the virtual pedagogical practices aimed at supporting and maintaining youth maker agency and everyday concerns amidst the pandemic? As STEM education researchers and informal STEM facilitators, we are interested in how youth and informal STEM educators, suddenly limited to virtual programming engage, pivot to continue engaging in rigorous STEM-rich making as a result of the Pandemic, including the challenges, insights, tensions and unanticipated possibilities.

## Theoretical Framework

We draw on Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) (Engeström, 2001; Gutiérrez & Arzubiaga, 2012) and youth identity (Holland et al., 2001) in learning a s guiding frameworks. Youth bring in other identiti es into the community youth makerspace and there is a plurality  and fluidity between their identities-in-practice as youth engage in any particular figured world, either in-p erson or virtual. CHAT as a framework helps us prob e youth engagement on two levels: 1) It allows us to unpack  how youth are utilizing what particular pedagogica l tools made available in the virtual programming to result in what kinds of STEM-rich making engagement; 2) it makes visible how particular dynamics within an activity system (e.g., different combinations of tool-rule-community) impact youth engagement. Overlaying CHAT with youth identity is helpful in gaining insights into what germane identities-in-practice youth are bringing to bear w hen negotiating the virtually facilitated Make club  activity system.

## Methods, Data Sources & Data Analysis

Context:  At this BGC Make Club, youth have fostered a community where they have a strong bond and sense of ownership (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2018). Group collaboration and iterative design are core features o f Make Club, for example, youth surveyed club-mates for co mmunity concerns that resulted in a semester of bui lding toys such as race cars and coding games on scratch,  that Make club youth shared with all BCG children for feedback and further refinement. However, since the pandemic, Make Club has been drastically reconfigured. As a result, by the time virtual Make Club begins at 3:45pm on a Tuesday, Make Club youth participants would have already spent an entire school-day worth of time ph ysically at the BGC, 6 ft socially distanced, with a chrome book. This was due to youth attending school virtually while physically at the BGC. Pandemic rules also dictated only solo work and virtual facilitation by Make Clu b facilitators. Data includes fieldnotes, zoom videos of Make Club sessions, 18 check in/exit surveys with youth, and educator (Worsley) made artifacts (powerpoints) that surface the pedagogical patterns related to the learning experiences of each youth, analyzed in the grounded theory (Glaser, 1992) for 7 months of 45-minute weekly programming (13.5  total programming hours). While the popup cards were the primary project, youth also worked with snap circuits. Initial Conjectures & Next Steps Through mapping CHAT triangles for various virtual sessions layered with identity analysis a recurring  theme emerged –the imbricated nature of relational repres entation with pedagogical content modeling and with community building during an isolating pandemic. Even though youth were physically together in a room at the BGC, they were isolated from one another. We mapped  the ways in which relational representation were m ade manifest in the weekly powerpoint slides. First, vi deo representation of an established, embodied pres ence was helpful in creating short videos (boomerangs) featuring Worsley, the primary Make Club facilitator who  shares identifiers with the youth. These videos were used as a direct result of the shift to virtual facilita tion. It was important and meaningful for the youth to “see Ms. T show us”. Short videos featuring Ms. T’s hands “making” included doing particular pop-up folds, showing the trickiness of working with flimsy copper tape, explaining her social reasons for designing a particular pop card. This way, we were “there” with the youth demonstra ting and modeling the technical steps as we engage in dialog ue with them in real-time, through zoom. In our yea rs of experience as STEM facilitators, youth have also co nsistently remarked of the lack of representation o f Black people in making videos or STEM videos one could lo cate online. To exemplify relational representation  we intentionally populated the slides with Black people, characters, and cultural references as well as pictures of BGC staff and ourselves. We are analyzing how these representations impacted how various tools (including physical materials like copper tape, scaffolding activity sheets showing different types of circuitry from simple to parallel, other art-based materials) were taken up by youth as they navigate the activity system, in connection with which salient identities. For example, many youth were experiencing personal loss in their families to Covid-19, which brought somber designs to the cards. We are further  investigating how youth-centered design for virtua l programming could facilitate needed caring during stressful times as such.
