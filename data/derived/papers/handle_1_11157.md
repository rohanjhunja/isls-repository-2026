# Cultivate Research Literacy: High School Students Work with Community Partners to Investigate Real-World Challenges

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research explores educational designs for engaging high school students in authentic research in collaboration with a network of professional communities. The design framework features an interdisciplinary community-based approach to research experiences. Students' research activities were supported by a collaborative community, which included a team of teachers, classmates who worked on diverse topics and challenges, and professional community partners. The data collection included students’ research journals, records of student meetings and presentations, interviews with the teachers and students, and an online survey of the community partners. The findings revealed how students navigated the unfolding research processes to address real-world challenges with the support of their teachers and community partners. Based on the findings, we discuss designs for cultivating students’ research literacy.

## Introduction

Recent education reforms highlight the need to enga ge students in authentic knowledge practices by whi ch realworld researchers investigate the world and solve complex problems (National Research Council, 2012; OECD, 2018). Integrating authentic research practices in high school education may help increase student engagement and develop high-order competencies that are needed for college succ ess and future careers. In this research, we explore educational designs for engaging high school students in authentic research  in collaboration with a network of professional communities.

Living in a rapidly changing world with ever-emerging challenges, society members must learn how to navigate complex problems, messy information, and d iverse ideas in order to position their own roles to make productive contributions (Pendleton-Jullian & Brown, 2018).  In this context, education needs to prepare students as critical knowledge consumers as well as knowledge builders and producers, who can work with an open world of information and ideas to develop creative soluti ons to challenging problems. Research skills and ex periences used to be only valued by students who are interest ed in academic careers. In the new environment, all students need to develop a skillset and mindset of research, which become even more essential in the age of generative AI. Thus, we propose the concept of “research literacy” as a goal of education for all learners. Research literacy may be defined as an epistemic and cultural practice to understand how research works, participate in research practices with various partners, use research as a n intellectual tool to address knowledge gaps and solve problems in professional and personal settings, and communicate research outcomes with various audiences and contribute to the larger social practices and discourse. Students must understand that research goes beyond hands-on work with materials, data and instruments and participate in the full process of research (Bird et al., 2023). Research on integrating research experience in K-12 has been limited to science/STEM education. The prominent model for student research apprenticeship s is student-expert partnerships in which students engage in “guided participation” or “legitimate peripheral pa rticipation” in a scientific community of practice (Aydeniz et al., 2011; Hellgren, 2019; McGee et al., 2018; Melton et al., 2022). However, most programs only invite students to shadow the experts or follow predefined procedures to collect data for a larger project or dataset (Bird et al., 2023; Sadler et al., 2010). As  culminating activities, students present their research in a group event (e.g., poster competition) (Aydeniz et al., 2011; Hellgren, 2019; Hsu & Venegas, 2018). Such activities may only help students gain a shallow and narrow research experience in a recipe-like manner, focusing on how to perform specific research techniques. Future educational efforts could benefit from an expanded conceptualization of research. To cultivate authentic research literacy in an open and interdisciplinary community, it is important to understand how members navigate people and ideas  in dynamic ways  to carry out ever-deeper research, including how they interact with real-world professionals across boundaries (Bird et al., 2023). In this project, we design and analyze student research practice in light of theories of Knowledge Building (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006) and community-based learning (Rogo ff, 2014).  The Knowledge Building (KB) theory offers a framework for engagin g students in knowledge-creating practices guided by a set of principles, which highlight authentic problems a nd real ideas, collective responsibility for commun ity knowledge, and epistemic agency for continual idea improvement (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006).  Students engage in collaborative discourse for knowledge bui lding, which may include interactions between different knowledge communities (Zhang et al., 202 0). Enriching the notion of community-based k nowledge building, Rogoff (2014) identified “learning by observing and pitching in ” as an authentic learning mechanism. Children participate in the ongoing endeavors  of their communities (and families), with expectations and opportuni ties to contribute based on  their interests and skills. The social organization of the shared endeavors involves collaborative engagement as an ensemble with flexible leadership, which allows learners to “take initiative along with the others as everyone fluidly blends their ideas and agendas at a calm mutual pace.” (Rogoff, 2014, p. 74). Thus, engaging students in authentic research requires creating a collaborative community in which members take personal agency and collective responsibility for advancing shared knowledge while contributing to one another’s learning.  Students work with their peers and professional partners to advance shared research goals and contribute to advancing the knowledge of their community. As t he community advances knowledge together, member s develop their own personal interests, knowledge, skills, dispositions, and ide ntities as learners and researchers. Through reflect ing on their  participation in research, s tudents develop epistemic cognition (Barzilai & Chinn,

2018) about the nature and processes of research, including epistemic beliefs, understandings, and values

regarding knowledge and knowing and ways of communicating and contributing. The current research aims to explore educational designs for engaging high school students in authentic research. Building on the above literature review, our design features a community-based, interdisciplinary design for authentic research experiences, which center s on sustaining students’ creative work with ideas th at can make a positive impact on their local communities and th e larger world. While the multi-year design-based research is underway, this paper presents our analysis of the first iteration of this project. Our research question investigates how students engaged in research-based processes to address  real-world challenges with the  support of their teachers and community partners.

## Method

Classroom contexts This multi-year design-based research project took place at Tech Valley High School  located in Albany, New York. Each year, three teachers work with a class of 12 th-grade students to carry out the Innovative Solution s project. The teachers specialize in English, arts/business alliance, and special education, respectively. The current study analyzed data collected in the first iteratio n of this project during  2022-2023 using an interactional ethnography approach (Green & Bridges, 2018). A total of 29 students (17-to-18-year-olds) enrolled in this research class, and, among them, 19 granted the permission for us to analyze their learning data. A community-based, interdisciplinary design for authentic research experiences The student-driven research was kicked off in mid-November 2022, when the representatives of 11 commu nity partners came to the classroom to present their “Bu siness Pitches”: authentic challenges or problems t hat the organizations needed to address, open to multiple s olutions. For ex ample, two staffs from Red Cross presented the challenge: How do we, the Red Cross, create a s ocial media campaign to attract high school student s and recruit them as volunteers? Students indicated pers onal interests and options. Those interested in the  sa me challenge (community partner) formed a team. Member s of each team then worked with one another and wit h their community partner in the next 8 weeks to carr y out various research activities. Students generat ed ideas, wrote proposals  of research, and conducted surveys, interviewed target partici pants and people in their local communities, and carried out field observations. The student teams had in-person and/or online meetings and ongoing email exchanges with their community partners. They organized their  research by using planning documents, online websites and databases of research papers, and other specific tools relevant to their research (e.g., digital design software). Short whole class meetings were held each week to  share progress and challenges and plan for the next steps. The teachers provided guidance and support to help address their needs, s uch as by providing a template for recording their journey of  research and using rubrics to set up expectations for major works.  As the research product, each team drafted a research report and received feedback from their peers and partners. In mid-January 2023, a student-partner conference was organized. Each team present ed their research and solutions, with the community partners serving as listeners and discussants. Data sources and analysis Our data sources included (a) classroom observation s (18 lessons in total, from November 10, 2022 to J anuary 13, 2023), (b) teacher meeting observations (Novemb er 14, 2022 and January 17 2023), (c) observations of student/community partner meetings, (d) vi deo/audio recordings of major small-group work, whole-class discussion and their rehearsal/final presentations,  (e) teacher lesson documents (e.g., innovative sol ution rubric, writing scaffoldings, team pacing chart), (f) each team’s online folder which in cluded team research notes, meeting notes, team contract, annotated bibliograph y, proposal paper,  final paper, presentation slice s and so on,

(g) a community  partner survey, and (h) student and teacher intervi ews. Each teacher interview took

approximately 45 minutes, focusing on the teachers’  design and support, student participation, and the  support from the community partners. A total of 13 students volunteered to participate in an interview about their research experience, including how they managed and participated in the research process, what they had learned, and what kinds of support they received. A total of 7 (out of 11) community partners responded to our online survey t o reflect on their mentoring and support and evaluate students’ participation. Our data collection and analysis used an interactional ethnography approach (Green & Bridges, 2018), which offers a logic of inquiry for investigating what le arners do and construct in a temporal sequence of e vents as viewed through multiple levels of analysis. This  method was selected to address our  need to understand how student research work unfolded over time across different social and physical settings, including personal research, small group work, online/face-to-face meetings with partners, on-site visits, and whole class discussions and presentations. Three researchers observed the research class  on a weekly basis and created a rich documentation of the learning events, which were indexed based on time. The data organization provided contextual information about each major activity observed, t ime and topic of the research activities, highlight s of student and teacher participation, and sources of data recorded. We the n reviewed the major events observed to construct a  whole storyline representing how the Innovative Solutions  project was initiated and implemented to support s tudents’ research processes taking place across the various settings. We coded students’ major researching goal s, ideas, events, and inquiry activities, attending to students’ collaborative engagement w ith their peers and par tners to blend their ideas and interests that drove their research agendas.  The analysis further traced temporal connections among the different inquiry activities. Doing so allowed us to create an “intertextual we b” (Green, & Bridges,

2018) over time, which involved multiple and meanin gful relationships/interactions, multiple direction s and

phases of students’ research experiences.

To deepen the whole picture view of student researc h, we further analyzed two research teams, as “telling cases” (Mitchell, 1984), each representing  a rich research project conducted by a group of students with a community partner. The two teams worked on two  different areas of research: (a) Team A with two students who worked on a marketing-related challenge proposed by a major retailer company  (i.e., how to better engage Gen Z customers?); and (b) Team B consisted of thre e students who worked with a semiconductor company to generate learning designs that could help young lea rners develop understandings and interests in the field of electronics and semiconductors. The two teams were analyzed to understand their knowledge-building interactions that led to notable research outputs.

## Findings

A whole picture view of student research experience Our data analysis helped understand and unpack the key facets of students’ research experience. As a core theme emerging from the initial analysis, the teacher and  community partners worked with students to co-configure a niche for students’ research work in the  professional world of practices. The co-configured niche helped to develop meaningful expectations and roles of authentic research that matched with students’ knowledge, experiences and interests. From their freshman year  in school, the students learned a variety of skills and techniques for conducting research project s, such as teamwork, different strategies of note ta king, and collaboration. The Innovative Solutions project required students to work with external part ners for a longer period of time and take more responsibility for managing their research processes. The t eachers hoped that students would use the skills they had learned duri ng their high school years to plan, pace, manage, create, communicate and present an inquiry project that add ressed a complex  authentic challenge. When inviting community partners to propose authentic challenges for students, the teachers suggested that the challenges should be at an entry level (not requiring advanced techni cal expertise), open-ended (with multiple solutions to  allow creativity), realistic, and important to the organi zation. The challenges proposed reflected these tho ughts. Six of the 11 partners (e.g., Red Cross, A-Mart, WMHT digital media) posed challenges related to m arketing and outreach among younger generations.  Other partners proposed challenges related to civic engagement, videogame design, STEM learning design, design of a summer program on entrepreneurship, etc. Students p layed their part in selecting challenges based on their interests, communicating with their community partners to understand their needs in order to define research goals and directions for their teams. The above negotiation process led to the cocreation of a specific research plan in each team. The planned research worked out productively for mo st of the teams. While for other teams, the balance between being realistic versus being creative/open-ended did not work out easily. In those cases, students felt that thei r resea rch was too narrow focusing on marketing and not so meaningful or engaging to them personally.

The second aspect of student research experiences pertains to the learning of research skills as tools for progressive problem solving and continual idea impr ovement. Driven by the challenges received, student s made efforts to understand the nature of the problems in context. To support their search of practical solutions, students also identified what they needed to understand and engaged in conceptual knowledge building that informed their practical problem solving. For example, the Red Cro ss team spent time understanding what Red Cross doe s and why it is important  to the society. They realized that Red Cross is not only about bl ood donation but provide s a wide range of services. This expanded view helped the students envision how Red Cross may engage high school and college age students. Aligned with their problem solving and idea development as the central focus, students further learned and applied specific research skill s, such as how to design and analyze surveys, write re search report for an authentic professional audience, and give presentations. T he teachers played an important role in nurturing a disposition toward research as an ever-deepening process (like “digging a rabbit hole ”), with new information found continually informing deeper ques tions. At the same time, they also monitored and su pported students’ needs of research skill building, such as  by offering tips for conducting team meetings and designing presentations, building on what students had learned across the high school years. Another important aspect of student research is to develop epistemic understandings of research as a social practice as they carried out specific resear ch works with their community partners, and to envi sion opportunities and identities  for themselves to become researchers in formal and informal ways. In the final interview, students commented on their new insights  in what counts as good research. Their comments hi ghlight the importance of cross-validating information sources as opposed to simply using Google search to find a quick answer. They further realized that research is a never-ending and messy process, and that research is an important tool integral to what people do across different pr ofessional settings. Beth (a student working with A-Mart) reflected on her understanding of research: “If you're doing under 30, 45 minutes of research, you're probably not researching enough… having a basic understanding of  what you need to do, and kind of just making sure you don't give up once you have like a just an answer. You need to go like that extra step. Because if you  take that extra step, you might find that you turn around and  like you turn around the corner by taking that ext ra step, and there's actually another mile to go. So it's import ant to just kind of go the extra lengths to underst and and comprehend and just kind of explore.” Such notions of research open up spaces for students to develop researchrelated roles and identities in continual research efforts, including considering research as part of what they do in future college study and personal lives.

## Discussion

The existing practices to integrate research experience in schools are often limited to STEM fields, expecting students to follow prede fined procedures to collect data for a larger science project or dataset (Bird et al., 2023; Sadler et al., 2010). This study explored creating an open, community-based context  that support s high-level research processes across a wide range of authentic settings. Our data analysis revealed how students navigated the unfolding research processes to address  real-world challenges with the  support of their teachers and community partners. Beyond hands-on work with data and tools, students participated in the full cycles of research processes as a cultural and epistemic practice. Based on the findings, we discuss supportive designs for cultivating such research practices—research literacy—for all students.  First, educational efforts to cultivate research literacy need to create school-community connections that open spaces for  students to participate in the collaborative endeavors of the local or professional communities (Magnussen & Hod, 2023; Roggof, 2014), with authentic challenges identified from the communities driving student research engagement. Teachers, community partners, and students may work together to co-configure a niche for student  research in the authentic context. Second, students enact agentive roles in positioning their research efforts in the authentic context, including defining research questions and goals based on their interests and expertise, negotiating research plans with their team members and partners, and ref lecting on their research processes for continual i dea improvement.  Deep research processes involve progressive problem solving and finding (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006), with new problems identified as progress is made, opening new spaces of inquiry. Third, to support effective research and problem solving, students may access in-context skill training and guidance on how to use specific research methods and tools (e.g., survey). Research skills can be learned and practiced along the way for authentic purposes. Finally, it is important for students to engage in ongoing reflection on their research experiences, including both productive and unproduc tive moments, to develop epistemic understandings o f knowledge, knowing, and ways of contributing (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018).  Students in this study reflected on their research as an ever-evolving journey of inquiry supported by multiple s ources of information and expertise. Research was seen as an integral part of people’s work and life. Such  notions of research may enhance students’ agency and identity as researchers.

The findings shed light on  new possibilities to extend student knowledge building to an open world, with students working alongside real-world professional to tackle authentic challenges facing the local and global communities. Based on the findings, our teacher-researcher team co-designed the second iteration of this project. More detailed data analyses are conducted to examine student-partner interactions and document the teachers’ practices for supporting student deep engagement in research.
