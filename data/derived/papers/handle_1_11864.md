# A Complex Systems Perspective on Educational Makerspaces: Leveraging Mixed Methods for Enhanced System Insights

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Makerspaces are complex systems composed of various interacting parts and actors, including materials (physical and digital), learners, and educators. Complex systems have properties and qualities that are useful for researchers to study collaborative learning in this environment. Towards that end, we present a mixed methods approach for analyzing group understanding and network-level properties using video data exclusively. Our research design combines two complementary methods: qualitative interaction analysis and quantitative sociotechnical network analysis. The latter is an extension of social network analysis. We combine these methods to identify challenges in educational makerspaces, such as access to resources and curriculum integration. The main challenge identified with our complex systems perspective is rapid build-up of complexity in making activities, which is manifest as nested structures in the network analysis and lack of shared understanding in the interactions analysis.

## Introduction

In small classroom groups, a network of connections starts to form once a group receives an assignment. Initially simple, the learning environment becomes increasingly complex over time due to actors’ actions and interactions (Chen & Crilly, 2016). Observing this process helps identify challenges that hinder group participation and shared knowledge.

We argue that complex systems theory provides a use ful perspective to describe network dynamics (Arrow, McGrath, & Berdahl, 2000) —how networks expand, evolve, or decline based on member actions and interactions and complex system qualities. Davis & Sumara (2006) propose a set of  qualities needed for a phenomenon to be complex, including: 1) self-organizing in multiple levels, 2) interdependence; 3) bottom-up emergence; 4) nested and recursive structures; 5) inherently stable entities yet adaptive structure s; and 6) a desirable lack of equilibrium. The levels include individual actors characterized by their utterances and moves, individuals in groups solving a problem or a science task, and a higher level where individuals and tools are nodes of a graph illustrating the relational structure. Development and change can occur within and across these levels. Nested or recursive structures exist in two forms: recursive communication (Hilpert & Marchand, 2018) and artifact subassemblies (Simon, 1996). These are  manifested through repeated exchanges in verbal behavior and physical or digital creation, respectively. Suc h structures can lead to complexity build-ups, which, in a welldesigned process, serve as a platform for further development known as stable intermediate forms (Simon, 1996). Conversely, they can also become a source of difficulty and inequity, which is the focus of this paper. Multiple levels of analysis are needed to understand these systems methodologically, and we draw on previous research in computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) and mixed methods research that consider micro-level interactions and the macro-level patterns that emerge (e.g., de Laat et al., 2007; Hmelo-Silver & Azevedo, 2006; Litherland & Mørch, 2021). In our case, this approach helps us understand the process leading to scientific understanding in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM). Therefore, we ask the following research question (RQ): How can a mixed m ethods research design investigate access to resources and the integration  of makerspaces into school curricula by examining talk and technological artifact creation in collaborative learning from a complex systems perspective? Previous work Educational makerspaces, often characterized as collaborative environments where students can engage in handson learning through creating, tinkering, and proble m-solving, have gained significant attention in recen t years. Research has shown that makerspaces can enhance stu dent engagement, creativity, and STEM education outcomes (Halverson & Sheridan, 2014). Martin (2015) emphasizes the role of educational makerspaces in promoting collaboration, critical thinking, and problem-solving skills among students, which are essential for their future careers. Kajamaa and Kumpulainen (2020) highlight the multimodal knowledge practices (e.g., discussing and making), underscoring the diverse and reciprocal nature of these learning activities. Despite these promising benefits, researchers also note several challenges,  such as ensuring equitable access to resources and  integrating makerspaces into traditional school curricula (Mørch et al., 2023; Vossoughi & Bevan, 2014). CSCL researchers have been involved in complex syst ems research for a long time despite not always referring to these systems as complex. For example,  both Koschmann (1996)  and Fjuk & Ludvigsen (2001) explore how many interconnections among learners, t ools, tasks, and context can lead to complex interdependencies. Riikonen, Seitamaa-Hakkarainen, and Hakkarainen (2020) contribute to this understanding by examining how discursive and materially mediated aspects influence collaborative making processes, highlighting the complexity inherent in CSCL environments.  Our work builds on the previous work coordinating t wo modalities of collaborative learning in physical makerspaces (e.g., Kajamaa & Kumpulainen, 2020; Rii konen, Seitamaa-Hakkarainen & Hakkarainen, 2020) but uses a different methodology.

## Methodology

Our work uses a similar mixed methods design to Sch oonenboom (2019), which is informed by an education al goal like ‘discursive positioning.’ However, our approach is different in the sequence of combination of methods and in its novelty (new method). First, our research design incorporates elements from embedded and convergent parallel designs (Cresswell & Plano Clark, 2011). Second, it  is guided by the dynamics of a sociotechnical classroom system; informed by the objective of equitable access to resources and curriculum integr ation (Vossoughi & Bevan, 2014). The methods were integra ted using sociotechnical network analysis (Litherland & Mørch, 2024) as the quantitative approach. This approach provi des a broad view of the network, helping to identify network dynamics, nested structures, and key figures. Following this, we use interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to understand how access to resources and knowledge plays out through interactions. We obtained all our data from video recorded classroom interactions (Derry et al., 2010) and transcribed the files using speech-to-text tool Whisper (OpenAI, 2022), generating three interaction tables (talk, references, and part-of) from which relational network files were (partly) converted using GPT 4.o (OpenAI, 2024). Sociotechnograms is a new type of display that enhance traditional SNA sociograms (Freeman, 2000) by including nodes for non-human actors and incorporating 'part-of' and 'reference' link types. We anticipate that it will be possible to develop these data points directly from transcribed video files in the not-too-distant future. We computed structural properties of selected sociotechnical networks, such as degree  centrality, diameter, network density, and the number of nodes. For deeper analysis of process data, we employed interaction analysis (Derry et al., 2010; Koschmann, 1996; Stahl, 2006),  to examine learning activities as they unfold ed moment-bymoment, focusing on talk, turn-taking, and artifact use (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). This analysis was correlated with network-level information in a reflexive integrated analysis discussion that we demonstrate by an example.

## Findings (data and analysis)

We have reproduced the structural context of a 2½ m inute critical group interaction episode of a three-hour workshop where three students created a burglar alarm using Microbits (microcontrollers), programmed with the MakeCode programming environment, and materials like cables, tape, and aluminum foil. The assignment was part of a physics class with curricular goals focus ed on electricity, programming, collaboration, and creativity. It aimed to combine subject-specific learning with the development of domain-general skills, a common practice in educational makerspaces (Mørch et al., 2023). The selected video data is representative of the themes we investigated; it illustrates complexity, high engag ement, and revealed challenges pertaining to partic ipation and curricular learning goals. We analyzed network level data and interaction data, and then brought them together. ## Discussion and conclusion

Based on our analysis utilizing complex systems theory, we gain insights into how elements within digital learning environments interact and self-organize over time. We address ed five complex systems qualities (Davis & Sumara, 2006).

The self-organization, including nested and recursive structures  highlights how students frequently develop their own structures and problem-solving routines. For instance, students constructe d subassemblies of the burglar alarm, including the code to control it  and the digital components for its assembly. Additionally, the pair-programming pattern (driver-navigator) emerged as an interactional structure in vented by the students through interaction, represented as a dense set of links between actors and tec hnical components in the sociotechnograms, each holding specific weights and  values; these are complementary representations. Understanding how these patterns emerged can illuminate strategies to improve collaborative learning and provides means for better scaffolding through different angles (de Laat et al., 2007; Vossoughi & Bevan, 2014). Interdependence is evident in how students' actions and interaction s influence each other and are influenced by prior component assemblies (created structures), both technological and interactional. These include design artifacts (Simon 1996)  and recurrent communication. They “talk back” to the actors, a phenomenon best understood through higher-level analyses, such as network analysis operating on a broader time scale (de Laat et al., 2007;  Litherland & Mørch, 2021). Facilitators’ interventions also unders core how external inputs can recalibrate the system’s dynamics, facilitating a smoother progression towards learning goals at the lower levels. Bottom-up emergence is evident in how students gradually progress toward more complex tasks in their collaborative learning activities, as shown in our brief episode. The process of gradually acquiring new knowledge through troubleshooting, facilitation, and the creation of technical artifacts by assembly and control of the alarm device reflects a tiered approach mirroring the pro cess of gaining competence in interdisciplinary sci ence disciplines (Hilpert & Marchand, 2018; Hmelo-Silver & Azevedo, 2006; Jacobson, 2001). Teachers (in our brief episode, a participating res earcher) must navigate the tension between providin g stability, such as clear structures and goals, and adaptability, like responsive support based on student needs. Striking a balance enables students to leverage eme rgent interactions while being guided toward educat ional objectives (Davis & Sumara, 2006; Halverson & Sheridan, 2014; Hmelo-Silver & Azevedo, 2006). This is a goal that was partially achieved in the group we observed.

In summary. This study underscores the importance of viewing makerspaces through the lens of complex systems theory. By employing multi-level and combined analyses to examine the interplay between students’ interactions, the network dynamics, and the facilitator’s role, we gain a nuanced und erstanding of the learning process. Leveraging mixed methods for managing complexity wil l not remove all hurdles but  offers promising avenues for further work. There are numerous opportunities for both research and practice in this area.
