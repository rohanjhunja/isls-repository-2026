# Developing Assessment Systems for Engagement, Learning, and Transfer Using GenAI and an LMS

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Developing effective assessment “systems” is challenging for many educators. Learning management systems streamline administration and scoring, but creating quality items that support engagement, learning, and transfer demands expertise. Meanwhile, Generative AI has introduced new integrity challenges. We present design principles and examples from research on an online graduate course on educational assessment, demonstrating ChatGPT's capacity for creating high-quality formative and summative items. These include proximal items (from specific resources) and distal items (aligned to standards). ChatGPT can generate quality item pools that capture disciplinary nuances, supporting transfer, validity, and credibility. This approach aligns formative and summative assessment functions, reducing construct-irrelevant variance and promoting "generative" learning that transfers widely.

## Introduction

“Everyday” assessments, designed by educators, are widely used in online and in-person classessvia LMS platforms. Creating them is time-consuming and requires assessment and subject-matter expertise (Stadler et al., 2024). This paper offers strategies to help educato rs use generative AI (GAI) tools to generate formative and summative LMS-aligned items, saving time and reducing integrity r isks linked to GAI.  Three challenges make assessment design difficult: transfer (how learning carries over), validity (evidence supporting learning claims), and credibility (trust in assessments). Effective systems must align instruction with formative and summative tasks to foster generative learning (Engle, 2006) while avoiding “teaching to the test” (Messick, 1994). LMS platforms like Canvas and Google Classroom have supercharged  online administration and scorin, whileGAI has raised major academic integrity concerns Focus, claims, and prior research This paper presents design principles and examples from design based research of a graduate-level online course in educational assessment. We explore the synergy between generative AI (especially ChatGPT-4), modern LMS platforms, and innovative assessment strategies (Hi ckey & Zuiker, 2012; Hickey & Harris, 2021). Ground ed in situative learning and transfer theories (Greeno, 1998; Engle et al., 2012), we offer steps for using GAI to identify content, create formative and summative items, and ensure "alignment" to support transfer. We make three claims: First. GAI’s ability to answer most items increases the ne ed for integrity safeguards; Second, GAI makes it far easier to align formative and summative items by selecting from pools rather than manual generation; Third: GAIpowered self-assessments can boost mastery, reduce grading pressure, and deter cheating. While many suggests portfolios and performance assessment alternatives (Ghapanchi, 2023), these can be equally vulnerable to GAI misuse and place heavy scoring burdens on instructors. We align with scholars who argue that well-constructed selected-response items can still support transfer, so long as they don’t dictate instruction (Messick, 1994). Conventional formative assessments, despite thei r promise, often fall short and risk distorting instruction (Bennett, 2011). Our review revealed growing interest in GAI-generated assessment items (Baidoo-Anu & Ansah, 2023), but few explore using GAI for s elf-assessment. The most relevant item creation article is Indran et al.’s (2023) “Twelve Tips”  and Schaper’s (2024) work on GAI-generated outcomes and test items. A central challenge facing assessment “systems” is that formative items must resemble summative items enough to engage learners but differ enough to foster transfer (Dolin et al., 2018). While some theorists emphasize narrower assessment  purposes (NRC, 2001), we emphasizes b roader functions, like conformity and transformation, along with formative and summative  (Hickey & Pellegrino, 2005). This allows the same item to serve multiple purposes—for instance, summatively assessing understanding while formatively assessing transfer & achievement. Creating items for these functions requires time, assessment expertise and disciplinary knowledge. GAI helps by offering “cheap variation” (Mollick,  2023), rapidly generating multiple item versions. This allows educators to focus on deeper elements like validity and credibility-areas GAI can't yet handle.

## Methods

The insights shared here are based on a long-running design-based research program developing assessment principles grounded in situative theory. The first phase introduced "multi-level" assessment to align learning across formal levels (Hickey & Zuiker, 2012). The s econd phase added principles from productive discip linary engagement (Engle & Conant, 2002) and expansive fra ming (Engle et al., 2012), resulting in a comprehen sive online assessment model (Hickey et al., 2020). A persistent challenge is item creation workload. Examples in this paper come from an online graduate course, Assessment & Learning, developed by the first author (since 2008) and completed by the seco nd author. The course includes three modules —Practices, Principles, and Policies. Most recently, in response to GAI security concerns, we expanded the self-assessments in each assignment and reduced the value of the exams associated with each module.

## Results

We present our our results from five elaborations on using GAI as seven initial design principles that capture what we learned.

Elaboration #1. Create proximal assessments with GAI Proximal assessments focus on course-specific learning over several days. In Assessment & Learning, each assignment now includes open-ended self-assessments and selected-response self-checks. Each item comes with expert explanations, prompting students to try answ ering from memory, consult resources if needed, and  then compare with the expert response. For instance, Figure 1 sho ws two items generated by ChatGPT 4.0 from  the textbook chapter. After uploading the chapter and giving an initial  prompt, ChatGPT identified key topics and created multiple versions of such items, each with clear explanations. Following Indran et al. (2023, #11), we improved item s by refining prompts —for example, asking for more detailed explanations. GAI-generated items work especially well with LMS tools  that offer automated feedback and scoring. This enables a streamlined, i nstructor-free assessment system that fosters generative learning of key ideas. Our first two design princip les are: (1) use GAI to create proximal self-assessments from course materials, and (2) leverage LMS automation to manage assessments. Elaboration #2. Learn about transfer, validity, and credibility with GAI Another insight is that generating item pools helps  educators explore transfer, validity, and credibil ity. Figure 1 shows how recognition-level learning can weaken alignment. For example, j ust glancing the the formative feedback on grade-equivalent scores should help students recognize the (similar) correc t summative answer. In contrast, the standard deviation item requires deep er understanding and knowledge transfer. Still, bot h formative items align well enough with their summative versions to sustain engagement (Nolen, 2011). ChatGPT responded easily to our prompts to use different language and examples to ensure transfer from prior feedback. Several professional development webinars and works hops have convinced us that most educators can quickly learn to use GAI for generating, selecting,  and aligning items in this manner. Our next two design principles are (3) use GAI to create pools of proximal formative and s ummative items and select aligned pairs, and (4) leverage GAI-generated items to explore and understand transfer, validity, and credibility. Elaboration #3. Assess transfer with distal assessments and GAI Our third insight inverts Schaper (2024)—rather than using GAI to generate learning outcomes, we use it to create "standards-oriented" distal assessments that measure transfer tonew contexts. These items align with broad standards but remain curriculum-independent, helping evaluate generative learning. Wh ile some argue multiplechoice tests don’t capture transfer (Bransford & Sc hwartz, 1999), we side with those who caution that portfolios and performance tasks candemand excessive feedback and introduce variability (Messick, 1994).

Using external professional standards, we prompted ChatGPT 4o to generate difficult best-answer multiple-choice items with detailed explanations. We generated distal far transfer items after repeatedly prompting for plausible distractors and “make them harder.” These items demand deeper understanding beyond surface-level recall—difficult for most educators to write alone. Our fifth design principle is use GAI to create distal items that promote transfer from formative feedback..

Elaboration #4. Respond tonew integrity concerns for online summative exams ChatGPT 3.5 disrupted our efforts to create “semi-secure” time-limited exams. Previously, we found textbook item bank items on Chegg, prompting us to rewrite questions to be unsearchable within two minutes. Until 2022, engagement scores correlated over.5 with exam resu lts, and time stamps showed high scorers weren’t us ing the full time—suggesting minimal searching. In 2023, we found that answers could be searched in under two minutes for 60%, 55%, and 16% of items across three exams—similar to ChatGPT 3.5’s scores (55, 52, 38). Later versions scored much higher: GPT 4.0 (85, 75, 82) and GPT 4o  (95, 86, 88), close to top student scores. We conc luded these exams alone no longer produced valid evidence of achievement or transfer. As a result, we reduced exam weight to 10% and shif ted points to self-assessments and self-checks, promoting learning over cheating. We also agree wit h agencies like Australia’s TEQSA (2023) that unaid ed assessment remains important. Yet, virtual proctori ng is often costly and discriminatory and in-person exams present logistical challenges. Thus (6) prioritize assignments and self-assessments over summative exams, and (7) use digital or in-person proctoring only when necessary. Elaboration #5. Provide an alternative response to GAI-driven cheating Our updated framework aligns learning across these levels:  A. Informal immediate-level feedback: Instructors give contextualized feedback on expansively framed assignments that encourage students to explore cont ent through personal and diverse perspectives (Engle et al., 2012). These public (to the class) assignments help deter GAI misuse and free instructors to deepen discussio ns through reflective engagement. B. Proximal self-assessments: Ungraded open-ended self-assessments follow each assignment. In Assessment & Learning, they are required (submitted as Canvas Ungraded Surveys) but  not scored, helping students check understanding b efore reflecting. C. Informal reflections: Students briefly reflect on different forms of engagement after each assignment. These reflections deepen understanding and shape future participation. They serve both formative and summative functions in awarding completion credit. D. Proximal self-checks: Multiple-choice self-checks offer a final review of key ideas. These ungraded checks include detaile d feedback and are required for assignment submissi on. E. Formal distal exams: Time-limited, auto-scored LMS exams test transfer. Their limited score  weight, alignment with instruction, item difficulty (targeting 80% av erages), and time-per-item constraints reduce cheating incentives. Poorly performing items are replaced based on statistical analysis.

## Caveats and conclusions

Concerns about GAI bias toward non-dominant races, communities, and identities have dr awn significant attention (e.g., Ferrara, 2023). Though we haven’t encountered stereotyping in our GAI-generated items, everyday assessments, unlike standardized tests, often lack bias reviews. While beyond our scope, our approach to selecting from GAI-generated item pools may support justice-oriented efforts like Randall (2023).  One takeaway is that effective use of GAI still requires understanding content, item formats, and assessment principles like transfer and validity. GAI has great potential to help educators  build this knowledge while designing items, and th is paper offers initial support for such learning. A second conclusion is that while integrity concerns are rea l, many responses fall short. Yet, GAI’s ability to generat e aligned assessments may outweigh these risks —especially when assessments are integrated into seamless systems that make it hard to cheat without  learning. Further research is needed to confirm our assumptions. We v alue ongoing work in this space and hope others exp and on our examples and design principles. Ideally, GAI wi ll usher in a new era of m ore credible, valid, and efficient assessments that also reduce burdens on educators.
