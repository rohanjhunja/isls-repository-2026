# Healing Metabolic Rifts Through Bridges of Conviviality: An Exploration of Urban Gardening Practices in Indian Cities

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Diverse, interconnected socio-ecological issues beset societies with an increasing urgency to respond in meaningful, substantive ways. However, the abstract, temporally stretched, and geographically diffused and distributed nature of the issues presents a significant cognitive challenge, thereby diluting the moral imagination necessary to nurture transformative changes directed at collective wellbeing. Using the context of urban spaces in Indian cities, this study describes the ‘metabolic rift’ created in food systems through opaque, efficiency-driven systems of industrial agriculture and modern supply chains while exploring alternate practices that lay the ground for civic participation. Drawing on Illich’s idea of ‘tools for conviviality’, the paper describes a qualitative study with urban farming practitioners to argue that food gardens can scaffold affect-based, collaborative spaces based on social, emotional, and physical feedback offered through plant-care practices. Such initiatives can provide crucial grounding to challenge dominant narratives and build a praxis of relational wellbeing based on pluralistic epistemologies.

Urban food systems: Broken and unsustainable By 2050, more than 70% of the global population is expected to live in cities, with the fastest expans ion and growth of metropolises projected in the Global Sout h (UNDESA, 2018). The unprecedented rate of urbaniz ation in the past century has significantly reoriented la nd use patterns, employment opportunities, supply c hains and energy demands. Following the conventional linear m odel of resource extraction and use, most cities ar e seen as ‘one-way resource sinks’ wherein natural resources and energy mined from far-off areas are used to sustain urban areas (Yang & Yang, 2022). Moreover, cities continu e to generate massive amounts of waste, and degrade d materials, thereby causing a ‘metabolic rift’ due t o the imbalance between input and output of resourc es (McClintock, 2010). The concentration of resources and people, also make cities particularly vulnerable to increasingly common climate risks and associated socio-ecological issues of pollution, water and food insecurity, distress-induced urban migration and so on. Food systems offer a tangible manifestation of the imbalance created due to the industrial crop production techniques and supply chains that have made possible storage, transport and consumption of food over thousands of miles. However, engaging with ecological issues requires an ‘unsettling’ of the dominant, oppressive ways of being and knowi ng (Bang, 2020). The ontological shift demands an u rgent need to pay attention to the particular histories, contexts, and perspectives underrepresented in the education discourse to create alternate imaginaries that are not dominated by superficial technocratic visions o f “fixing” a problem. This study focusses on urban food systems as a context to illustrate the ways in which the conventional arrangements create an estranged and exploitative r elationship with land and labour, and draws on expe riences that can help reimagine one’s relationshi p with food. More broadly, the paper explores proce sses that can shape agency and enable actions to broaden moral horizons  of collective wellbeing. Linking the act of consuming food to the conditions under which food is grown and bro ught to our plates requires a systemic way of think ing about the human –nature relationship.  Recognizing, and nurturing, rich urban ecosystems a midst cities presents numerous challenges, as well as opportunities. Stud ies on urban food systems, however, have a regional  bias of countries from the Global North, with relatively less research focused on cities of the Global South, despite their rapid expansion (Rao et al., 2022).

‘Conviviality’ as a framework to explore urban garden interactions The philosophical use of ‘conviviality’ as a term was most famously employed by Ivan Illich (1973, p.24) to refer to ‘autonomous and creative intercourse among perso ns, and the intercourse of persons with their envir onment’, with ‘intercourse’ referring to diverse interaction s and engagements. Deeply critical of the industria l technology and underlying value processes of alienation it ent ails, Illich described convivial tools as ideas and  actions that allow “each person who uses them the greatest opportunity to enrich the environment with the fruits of his or her vision” (p.25). Given that much of modern civilization is a product of tool-making and associated technological processes that are co-shaped by socio-economic models and cultural practices (for instanc e, agroecology vs industrial farming, or, small-scale renewable energy vs fossil fuels), convivial processes require a critical analysis of the technology in question. Vetter  (2018) for example, suggest a criteria for a democraticall y controlled convivial technique. These are five levels: a) relationshi p quality: What kind of interactions does the techn ology promote? Does it encourage or discourage knowledge/  resource sharing? b) accessibility: Is the technol ogy accessible to everyone? Is the knowledge used to cr eate the technology open-access? c) Adaptability: Can the technology be adapted to local contexts o r can only be used at a large, uniform scale? d) Bi o-interaction: What are the potential risks of the technology, and does  it promote ecosystem flourishing (or atleast not h arm it). e) resource intensity: What resources are consumed by the technology, and how sustainable is the use? Adhering to convivial principles emphasizes the connections and  interdependencies sustaining life, and includes no n-human agency and wellbeing as an integral part of human f lourishing. As a physical practice, it refers to a deep and sensory engagement with  the surrounding landscape (Hamilakis, 2017). Such i nteractions don’t ignore the inevitable tensions, conflicts and trade-offs involved in relational sustenance; instead, recognizing the interdependencies allow for a fuller unde rstanding of relational wellbeing. Kavedžija  (2021) argues that while traditional psychological studies of wellbeing analyze factors and outcomes from individual to community l evel in a series of cascading impacts, social and relational wellbeing could be better understood by moving away from the individual as a focal point. The process and pr esence of convivial structures might help in gainin g newer insights. While many studies have studied urban gardens as ways to engage with questions of food access, security and sustainability, understanding the potential of such spaces to nurture socio-political perspectives of shared wellbeing merits further research. The participator y culture in these initiatives, especially from the  view of expanding notions of sustainability, is a less unde rstood phenomenon. Grassroots community initiatives such as farming can play a role in subverting common notion s of cities as being ecologically barren, and far r emoved from 'Nature' (which is thought as only existing in  pristine places). These initiatives help reaffirm various kinds of ecological relationships, which are otherwise op aque, made invisible by industrial modes of product ion and consumption. These are projects that can grow into larger ideas and practices, which were not original ly envisaged. Such a gradual expansion creates further avenues for participation by a  wider group of people (rather than a small, invested group). Understanding the 'h ow' and 'what' of such hands-on engagement  in and through community practices can help urban environmental ed ucators desig n better collaborative learning and action initiatives. This project thus sought to explore the following l ines of inquiry: 1) What forms of interactions incorporate aspects of interdependence in urban gar dening practices?  2) How do such practices engender perspectives on conviviality amongst the practitioners?

## Research context and approach

The study was motivated by the need to contribute to ongoing dialogues and policy discussions on transformative educational experiences beyond the silos of classro oms and strait-jacketed syllabus. Based on her previous experiences of volunteering at community farms and facilitating school gardens  (Dutta & Chandrasekharan, 2018), the author sought to explore urban gardening practices as sites of recreation, l ivelihood, well-being, pedagogy and even resistance. Thus, using the oppor tunity of a grant1, the study aimed to foreground the voices of practitioners and understand the scope, motivati ons and challenges  in sustaining food gardens across a few Indian cities. In the spirit of co-creating knowledge through the active  participation of the people interviewed, emphasis was given to particular histories, plurality of voices and geographical contexts. The approach involved interviewing diverse practitioners 2. Dimensions such as the potential interviewees’  background, experience in growing food, positionality and  geographical context  were considered. Based on mutual availability, we interviewed 15 people from seven cities through reaching out vi a various public WhatsApp groups consisting of farming enthusiasts. Ethical protocols of seeking consent and voluntary participation were fo llowed as approved by the Institutional  Ethics Committe e. The transcribed interview data was thematically ana lysed to identify patterns, structures, and relations in the data  (Boyatzis, 1998). Rich descriptions of data were co nnected back to the research questions, to provide an interpretation of specific points of interest such as motivation and expanding sphere of actions. Since many themes were closely associated to certain artifacts/ practices, interpretation involved going beyond the description of an occurrence, and relating it to underlying events (Ospina & Dodge, 2005).

## Findings

The interviews provided insights into ways in which  participants began seeing their agency in farming-related activities. A ‘personal transformation’ was generat ed by the perspectives embedded in the practice, and these views were affirmed through peer feedback (Hards, 2 011). This led to the farming practice being unders tood in relation to wider environmental issues, such as foo d miles, seed sovereignty, water usage and increasi ng local biodiversity. These observations are supported by recent views of  sense-making, as inherently relational, and as a core component of social cognition (Jaegher, Paolo & Gallagher, 2010). These views propose social interactions as enablers of the sense-making process, which simultaneously shape normativ e ideas regarding the world. In a similar vein, Ramstead, Veissière, and Kirmayer (20 16) argue that social learning constitutes immersio n in local contexts through what they describe as 'regimes of attention' that direct humans to engage with the en vironment in specific ways. Specific themes illustrating these aspects are discussed as follows: Material scaffolding through emerging affordances Respondents described how their interactions with d ifferent materials to make compost, collect biomass, grow seasonal edible plants or engage in plant care helped them expand their notions of sustainability, labour, care and community engagement. As one participant remarked, “I use whatever waste is generated in the house and  sometimes make liquid ferments to use as a spray or add to the compost. I think whate ver we are regularly using should be easily and locally available. I haven’t found good quality cow dung, so I have dev eloped my own recipes for nutrient mixes. Each plant respo nds differently, and I am always learning that way.”(VN) Visceral experiences of activities in the garden seem to engage a wider spectrum of senses and their combinations, which allows one to attend to previously ignored fe atures of the environment. Respondents commented on  how they had begun paying more attention to the weather, even as artificially controlled environments have  become the norm, as they could see it impacting plant growth and health. Such diverse and consequential acts of noticing were mediated through their connection with the plants and the immediate surroundings. Broader engagement with resources Many respondents described related practices that c ould be understood as a broadening of sustainabilit y perspective owing to recognising the interrelated c onnections constituting ecosystem wellbeing. For in stance, recycling grey water for gardening purposes, and replenishing soil fertility through various methods of composting and mulching were often mentioned. Through these ac tions, connections between various environmental phenomena gained significance for the practitioners.

Physical, social and cultural feedback – Convivial relations Most participants described feeling motivated throu gh tangible positive feedback, in terms of harvest or encouragement from peers. The idea of being able to grow food in limited spaces, even in small quantities, seems to have helped participants actively seek groups and practices to help them sustain the effort. Furthe r, the possibility of forming and strengthening social bon ds through shared actions acted as a motivation to participate in similar activities. For instance, a participant described how people organised themselves to collect dry biomass from various localities and kickstarted a civic movement to prevent dried leaves from going to the landfill. Another connected her food growing experiments extending in to her profession as a chef, wherein she had taken to introduce people to fermented food as part of her m enu, as a way to reduce wastage and incorporate mic robial connections into the customers’ pa late. As evident in the narratives, the experience of tog etherness attached to social interaction and affiliation not only motivat es individuals to seek pleasure in social interacti ons, but also works to strengthen social bonds. These actions are  mediated through the artefacts of practice, which act as tangible media for shared interactions.

## Discussion

Participating in practices of growing edible plants  provides volunteers with a visceral sense of ecolo gical relationships manifested in the form of plant care,  pest-predator interactions, seed saving, composting, mul ching and so on. These actions create a ‘coagulative’ pra ctice (Dutta & Chandrasekharan, 2018) – a set of actions that generate an understanding of the interdependence of  elements in the environment, such as the symbiotic relationship of microbial systems and a garden, and  the need for biodivers ity for a healthy ecosystem. The term ‘coagulative’ captures the quality of the practice wherein seemingly different actions are integrated.  This coagulation gradually leads to amorphous ideas becoming more substantive and actionable. For instance, the need for nutrients led to the realization of nurturing healthy soils and reimagining their connection with organic waste. Such close interactions led practitioners to deeply reflect on the methods of garbage disposal in their communities. Growing food thus cr eates various associations between elements that ar e systematically compartmentalized in an urban set-up, in terms of production, consumption and waste disposal. By paying close attention and unearthing such interdependencies, individuals began to partic ipate in acts of care and are motivated to deepen the ir relationship with the “cared-for” environment based on the response. Building a meaningful relationship with the environment requires immersive, sensorium-based experiences, which generate the rich, mor al imagination necessary to think and act in ecologically responsi ble ways. Given the atrophied and opaque nature of socioecological relationships in cities, facilitating su ch rich experiences and understanding the challenge s in acting upon them is a promising and urgent area of researc h. In this project, we explored growing edible plants as a practice that allows one to embody the reciprocal relationships embedded in the health of the land, soil and living beings dependent on it. Based on our findings we argue that urban gardening can be an important way to motivate people to re-establish connections with the ecosystem. As a tool  for conviviality, urban farming at personal and community level allow for social bonding, free shar ing of knowledge and resources, is less resource-intensive owing to local scales of practice, and provides a l ens to develop a critical perspective towa rds food technologies that operate in ignorance of, or against communal w ellbeing. The barriers experienced only underscore the need of such initiatives to find infrastructural and pol icy support. Within the larger narrative of transit ioning to sustainable food systems, such grassroots practices constitute significant nudge points to alter behavior and actions at a community level. The socio-political emphasis on care and reciprocal relations hips manifested in convivial practices form a generative platform of transformative change. As conventional educational pedagogies based on linear, arbitrary and reductionist models of the world fail to nurture the perspectives, skills and values required to engage with an uncertain future, the need to draw o n local wisdom and imagination rooted in meaningful encounters is a vital task for educators. Instead o f drawing boundaries around formal educational spac es, active efforts to include diverse learning environments, a nd practitioners as sources of grassroots knowledge  must be undertaken. This study offers an arc of such practi ce networks that could in turn support educational initiatives directed towards embodied forms of collective wellbeing.
