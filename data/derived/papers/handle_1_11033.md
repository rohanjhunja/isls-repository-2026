# Connecting the Dots: Expanding on What is Scaffolded in Inquiry-Based Lab Approaches for College-level Physics

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Scaffolding is essential for inquiry approaches, yet little is known about how it is designed into inquiry based labs (IBL) in science courses. Using a conjecture mapping framework, we describe the design progression of scaffolds in a two-year design-based research IBL physics curriculum. This highlights how and why changes were made to the intervention, and reveals what it took to align the design to the learning processes to ultimately improve learning outcomes.

## Introduction

Research in science education investigates the appl ication of inquiry-based approaches in laboratory courses  — Inquiry Based Labs (IBL)  — to overcome the challenges of teaching scientific thinking. IBL aims to promote development of experimental skills and  critical reasoning, providing students with opportu nities to connect theoretical concepts with practical lab work, somet hing that traditional labs typically do not do succ essfully (Holmes, 2015). Inquiry instruction is distinct fro m discovery learning and  makes explicit the central role of guidance, identifying scaffolds and scaffolding as essential to the approach (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2007). Inasmuch, it can be characterized as preparing students for autonomous decision making. To date, few studies have examined the process of designing and validating the scaffolds built within IBL instruction — Holmes and colleagues being the exception (Holmes, 2015; Holmes et al., 2017). Our study was a two-year design-based research (DBR) aimed at investigating the nature of scaffolds required to develop scientific reasoning among students in a college-level physics IBL curriculum. We report on our design pro cess and provide practical findings for science edu cators about the types of scaffolds needed to support the types of knowledge outcomes associated with higher education scientific reasoning. In addition, we describe the systematic process used to identify the mediating roles of graphs as the artifacts produced from the data transformation activity.

## Methods

The IBL study was carried out through three DBR cyc les over a two-year period. Each cycle involved designing for the same 15-week Waves and Modern Physics college course, taught by the same instructor, but each with a different cohort of students. To account for the va riability across cohorts, we utilized a standardize d instrument specifically developed for assessing students’ prog ression in scientific reas oning to establish a baseline for assessing the effectiveness of the IBL approach across the differen t groups — the process and instrument are not described here. Data were collected from various so urces, including artifacts from the teacher (assign ments instructions) and students (lab reports, assignments, etc.), documentation from design-team meetings (teacher reflections, notes, etc.), interviews with both tea chers and students, as well as classroom observatio ns. Written artifacts underwent qualitative content analysis, while graphical artifacts were coded based on criteria such as the suitability of the choice of fitting function param eterization for the data (e.g., linear, power-law). The conjecture mapping technique (Sandoval, 2014) was used as a fr amework to explain the design process and iterative refinement of our IBL curriculum. It provides a clear framework for educational design research, separating design from its implementation and enables systematic eval uation of the fidelity of the learning design and t he effectiveness of the intended learning process, improving both application and knowledge. Designing the IBL and iterating the scaffolds This IBL intervention involved four modules, each a imed at enhancing students’ scientific reasoning sk ills and integrating new physics concepts, culminating in a comprehensive design pr oject — design of a musical instrument. The curriculum was modeled on two instr uctional programs: (1) PBL labs at Harvard Universi ty (Espinosa, et al., 2019); and, (2) Learning by Desi gn (LBD; Kolodner, et al., 2003) for inquiry-based science curricula. The former structured our conceptual con tent of the labs. The latter prov ided an instructional pattern for the modules: (1) a “mess-about” lab, exploration of scientific methods; (2) a science experiment lab, application of rigor in scientific procedure; and (3) a project lab, justification of design decisions based on data. Principles used for the designs of the initial scaf folds (Cycle 1) were based broadly on social constructivist theories: preparation for learning, modeling and coaching of cognitive processes, engag ement in collaborative activities that afford discursive p ractices, and the promotion of metacognition. These  principles manifested in scaffolds such as semi-guided checklists and rubrics, designed to promote cognitive prompting. Additionally, practices within the messing-about phase that prime students to recall  their conceptual and procedural knowledge thereby preparing them for the  decision making that is a major component of inqui ry instruction. This phase also generated both planned  and unplanned failures within a “safe” space, ther eby affording the developme nt of curiosity and authentic discursive episodes a mong students, often deepening their use of the concepts associated with scientific meth ods and formulation of questions for the teacher th at showed growing insights and integration of knowledge. Last ly, scaffolds designed for the end of modules used prompts for self-explanation, modeling self-regulatory processes and promoting the development of metacognitive skills. Despite this extensive scaffolding, this first cycl e produced mixed results. While positive gains were observed in the trajectory of students’ scientific reasoning, the qualitative coding of their artifacts and lab reports showed that they were lacking in the recruitment of some conceptual and procedural knowledge. While they could answer questions correctly on a questionnaire, thei r practices ignored the need for reliability in the ir data gathering. Furthermore, when interpreting their data, the students opted to conform to the expected theory without realigning it with the graphical evidence. In this instance, it demonstrated a clear inability to correctly identify the nonlinear relationship between the dependent and independent variables. Cycle 2 incorporated these findings, but learning o utcomes remained unchanged. Cycle 3’s design focused exclusively on adding tasks components that were likely to better mediate the learning of the conceptual and procedural knowledge associated with data trans formations (production of graphs) and analysis (con necting the dots). While a graph sense-making activity had been added to Cycle 2, this tim e, we adjusted the pacing and focused explicitly on the graphing of variable rela tionships. We reinforced the c ognitive prompts related to the concept of reliability throughout the modules, havi ng identified in the analyses performed that we had  likely removed them prematurely in the earlier iterations  — i.e., we slowed the fading of the scaffolds. Lastly, we built scaffolds that guided students through each of the procedural decision-making tasks, such as selecting variables, identifying constants, and determining plot types. During this process, students were prompted to recall and apply prior learning to differentiate between the linear and nonlinear relationships.

## Discussion

There is much yet to be learned about the designing  of scaffolds for IBL instruction. Compared to K-12 inquiry, higher education science inquiry labs need to design mediating processes that promote the understanding of more than what is typically described as scientific thinking. Scaffolds need to promote l earning how to transform data into appropriate representations and relate these r epresentations to scientific models, which is essen tial for drawing conclusions. This two-year DBR revealed gaps between th e initially conceived scaffolds and these desired learning outcomes. Using conjecture mapping, this paper explains our systematic evolution of s caffolds that allowed us to come closer to achieving the des igned outcome stated. In doing so, the study provid es both practical insights for IBL research and confirms the value of a design framework.
