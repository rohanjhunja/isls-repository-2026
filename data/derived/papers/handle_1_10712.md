# What Youth Know: “Knowledge in New Pieces” and the Uncovering Nuanced Intuitive Thinking about Everyday Complex Systems

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Youth today learn, play, and live in inextricably digital and analog worlds that are becoming ever bigger and more connected. Accordingly, the intuitive knowledge youth generate in navigating and authoring those worlds might evolve similarly, perhaps in ways that could inform their formal education. Building on foundational theories in conceptual change, we present pilot findings of a broader research program that studies what and how youth know about complex systems based on their everyday lived experiences. We document intuitive knowledge about topics such as supply and resource chains, globalization, and industrial agriculture. Beyond simply documenting youth’s modern intuitive knowledge pieces, this line of research could inform the design of educational activities and curricula that leverage youth’s intuitive knowledge in preparing them for 21st-century life.

## Motivation

The past few decades have brought a dramatic increa se in youth’s everyday exposure to complex systems in the form of new technologies, natural phenomena, and so cial interaction. Example technical systems include networked, internet-enabled, real-time processing devices that are inextricably woven into yout h’s physical and social lives and make learning the mechanisms of da ta collection, organization, and processing as real  a consideration as learning to drive. Modern discours e provides other examples of com plex systems as conversations about climate change, resource manage ment, global commerce, and inequity are increasingl y “in the air.” The worlds that youth inhabit, navigate, and author are at the same time both larger and mor e interconnected than ever before.

Rooted in the theoretical tradition and Knowledge in Pieces (diSessa, 1993), we study how 8-to 14-yearold youth’s everyday experiences might generate ove rlooked knowledge “pieces,” proposing that they might constitute a new category of intuitive knowledge fo r today’s youth: Knowledge in New Pieces (KiNP; Bli kstein & Rosenbaum, 2023). Experiences with viral internet  content, for example, might help youth understand exponential growth before high school, when such co ntent is typically taught (Common Core State Standards, 2010). Based on conversations around recycling or c omposting, youth might think about resource conserv ation and energy cycles before middle school (NGSS Lead S tates, 2013). Other elements of modern cultures mig ht equip youth with prior conceptions about disciplina ry topics that could support their developing compe tence in those areas. Standards and curricula may need updating to honor these new “knowledge pieces” and reflect them in students’ formal learning. Our work is guided by  the following research question: What and how do today’s youth know about life-relevant complex systems based on their everyday, out-of-school exposure to such topics? Theoretical framing This project has its main theoretical roots in Know ledge in Pieces (KiP), which among other strengths, accounts for the diversity and contextuality of intuitive kn owledge. KiP’s phenomenological primitives (p-prims) capture the small bits of intuitive knowledge – “students’ commonsense, everyday ‘prior conceptions ’” (diSessa, 2018)

– developed in daily life, about how things work (diS essa, 1993). Examples from math and physics include

knowledge “pieces” like “more effort begets greater  results,” “forces act in balance,” and “multiplica tion makes numbers bigger.” Importantly, learners cannot simply be asked to “forget” typical ly unproductive p-prims, as misconceptions have traditionally been treated (Smith III et al., 1994). Learners may not even be awar e that they regularly use a p-prim, and a given p-prim may continue to prod uce logical results in certain settings. KiP also describes how concepts develop into coordination classes  (diSessa & Sherin, 1998). As learners’ conceptual understanding matures, the ways in which they notic e concept-relevant features and make inferences and determinations based upon them become more aligned.  For example, a novice might make different inferen ces and determinations based on different, even concept ually irrelevant features, but with expertise, sali ent features, inferences, and determinations  align. Modern youth inhabit complex techno-social worlds vastly different from those of 30 years ago, and new models of cognition have similarly emerged (e.g., Hutchins, 2000; Varel a et al., 1991). Accordingly, we seek to account for these ne w resources w ithin the conceptual change literature by documenting the intuitive knowledge in new pieces  (KiNP; Blikstein & Rosenbaum, 2023) available to to day’s youth.

In studying youth’s experiences and learning in the ir complex techno-social worlds, we also build on research that studies how people in general (Turkle, 1984) and children in particular think with and a bout technology (e.g., Danovitch & Severson, 2021). Spec ific topics include children’s understanding of the  internet (e.g., Brodsky et al., 2021), their use of voice as sistants (e.g., Oranç & Ruggeri, 2021), and their t rust in the authority of search engine results (e.g., Tong et a l., 2022). Distinct from this previous work, we foc us less on documenting youth’s technology practices and skills  or theorizing about the role of various technologi es within youth’s lives. Instead, we use the intuitive knowledge gained from those pr actices as a springboard for formal, disciplinary learning, as tested through activity design and evaluation.

## Pilot methods

We have run three rounds of pilot interviews, collecting and analyzing several hours of video data from interviews with seven youth aged 8-14 (1). Based on convenience sampling, all participants lived in major metropolitan areas. Of these seven participants, we selected three to share as examples of the rich, nuanced thinking youth develop in the course of their modern techno-social lives. We assigned a pseudonym to each participant. Lasting roughly 45 minutes each, our semi-structured interviews addressed topics in youth’s t echnosocial lives. We based the interview protocol on th e first author’s 10+ years of informally conversing  with youth about their thoughts on technology and iterated the  questions slightly between rounds. The particular vignettes shared below emerged in response to the following two prompts: ● When you buy a t-shirt, what happened for that t-shirt to be made and get to you? ● At a family dinner, a child left food on their plat e. Their parents complained that the child was wast ing food, but the child replied, “No, I am not wasting. If we put it in the compost bin, it will turn to fertilizer.” What would you say to this child?

Interviews were conducted via Zoom, automatically t ranscribed, and verified by the second author. Auth ors collaboratively and thematically coded (Braun & Cla rke, 2006) the resulting transcripts to identify vignettes of interest. Criteria for these vignettes include the participant addressing a phenomenon at more than on e level of detail and presenting ideas that they had not learned in a formal school setting.

## Pilot findings

We present three vignettes from participating youth  to illustrate the nuanced ways they talk about com plex phenomena not yet covered in school.

Timo, aged 11, indicated an awareness of complex re source and supply chains as well as of the unintended environmental consequences of industrial workflows and consumption. Considering the origins of his t-shirt, he first identified a number of steps in the production process: designers who conceive the idea, plantations where cotton is grown, factories that make fabric, factories that make the shirt and print it, the sto re where it’s sold. Asked to consider the environmental impact of each step, Timo elaborated: So most factories, I don't know if it's true, there 's a lot of pollution. And not only that, but let's assume, how much water am I going to have to spend for all the plantations? … you have deforestation with these areas.... There's something I've learned. Every time you're going to be doing good, you're also going to do evil. Because to plant cotton, you have to spend water. … I think there's also something that factories use a l ot …. which is not renewable, so it's also an impact because the elements that are non-renewable produce more pollution. … People who work in factories do not work so long, they retire faster because of that. Timo identified a number of environmental consequences across the production process, including deforestation, water use, pollution. He also identified the human impacts of working with harmful materials (“they re tire faster because of that”). Timo summarized these issues in an intuitive sense of ethical balance: “every time you’re going to do good, you’re also going to do evil.” His resp onses suggest a nuanced set of knowledge pieces abo ut the trade-offs of industrial activity and well-being that could inform formal studies of ethics and economics. Flavia, aged 13, also offered detailed considerations of the underlying global forces and human impact s of the clothing industry. She mentioned that such work “probably happens in places like China, India … because Americans don't have time for that.” When asked to elaborate, she added: it’s not like you see it in other countries where s uch a high amount of the labor force is like factory workers. Also, the US minimum wage is different than in other countries. For example, a minimum wage in China is very different than in t he US. And also factory work in China or India or places where there's a lot of population, factory work is one of the more salvageable options. And also very available. It’s good, or may be not good work, but it's work that can provide enough.

Asked why she thought minimum wages weren’t higher in those countries, Flavia explained: the people who are actually in control in those cou ntries are often the ones with more money who are less willing to pay more. Or there’s some p laces that don’t even pay their people … The work that then gets shipped off to different co untries and then turned into things that we use every day. The country is completely dependent on that. Though not in these terms, Flavia’s reasoning inclu ded considerations of the cost of living, wage thef t, labor relations, and global economic dependence. Her resp onse demonstrates awareness of social, political, a nd economic dynamics that could inform formal studies of macroeconomics and international relations. As a final example, Tyler, aged 12, expressed a nua nced understanding of the ethics of agricultural and farming practices at work in the United States, including their historical evolution, which she credits to her casual watching of many ‘commentary videos’ on You Tube. When discussing composting, Tyler described an imal factory farming as unethical and cruel. Asked to el aborate on why factory farming exists in the first place, Tyler responded: we have developed technology, whether they became outdated or is still used today, like spears, stuff like that.… We made mammoths go extinct durin g the Ice Age, but they were such a reliable source of material-food and hides for clothing and tusks and such. And  they’re also so, they were renewable. …it's always just kind of in our nature to hunt and survive whether at the expense of this thing or this thing… And since we have so much technology, modern technology, we can capture and contain our modern cows and such, we can now do said hunting easier. And it may not be the most ethical, but it's still a way to keep agriculture alive and keep our food sources up.

Tyler contextualized factory farming within a histo ry of humans hunting animals for food and materials  as well as within the technological and societal changes that led to factory farming. Her explanation suggests an intuitive understanding about ethics, evolution, and ecosystems on which later formal studies could be built.

## Discussion and future work

Our preliminary data underscore the nuanced ways that youth reason about complex systems based on their daily exposure to them. We note that the topics addressed by our interview protocol are typically not included in school curricula for children of our participants’ ages. A dditionally, some of our participants overtly menti oned online content (e.g. YouTube) as an information source. We see these finding s as resonant with research on connected learning in which, through community-supported pursuit of personal interests, youth lear n knowledge and skills that can support their academic, civic, or career development (Ito et al., 2013). Similar to connected learning researchers, we also argue that formal curricula that acknowledge and reflect youth’s everyday knowledge and reasoning pr actices could more effectively bridge intuitive kno wledge with formal disciplinary learning. Connected le arning researchers have identified the link between  everyday knowledge and academic, career, or civic engagement  as both a weak point (Ito et al., 2018) and a plac e where formal education can contribute (Esteban-Guitart, 2016). Interpreting our findings in  this light, we agree that educational designs and curricula could precisely a ddress youth’s missing knowledge pieces that are ha rder to glean in the course of everyday life. Consider the intuitive idea documented above that humans were responsible for mammoth extinction. This idea makes sense given current conversations about human-caused climate change and extinctions. To move this intuition toward disc iplinary norms, we could design a population simula tor that accounts for both habitat loss and hunting behavior s, enabling learners to explore scales of impact to  which they have less everyday exposure. More broadly, our interest in the curricular implications of modern youth’s intuitive knowledge contributes to efforts to fill the gap between everyday and formal knowledge. Intuitive knowledge gets generated in specific contexts, thus inheriting the  limitations of such contexts. Despite the novelty and sophistication of the knowledge pieces mentioned by students, transcending their limitations and connecting these pieces in more productive ways will likely require new and more sophisticated pedagogical strategies and learning environments. We believe that our preliminary findings justify fu rther, systematic study of youth’s intuitive knowledge about these topics and considerations of how future educational designs and curricula might reflect and honor that knowledge in preparing students for life and civic participation in the 21st century.
