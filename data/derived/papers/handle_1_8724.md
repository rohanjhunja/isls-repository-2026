# Towards Automated Tracking of Affect: Testing the Use of Continuous Self-Reports and Multimodal Metrics

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The affective states of students while studying outside of class hold rich information about how students are doing in a course and how instruction could be improved. In this paper, we test the co-occurrence of webcam-based nonverbal metrics with self-reported student affective states during an instructional video. Preliminary results suggest that low-level, selfreported affect can be a promising ground truth data for research, and that nonverbal metrics created from webcam streams can be associated with this self-reported affect.

## Introduction and setting

The affective states of students while studying outside of class is generally unknown to instructors – However, this information can help instructors better support student needs. With this motivation in mind, we collect two data sources an introductory data science course that could help track student states: continuous self-reports of affective states, and multimodal data via a webcam-based data collection tool, EZ-MMLA. The system does not save video data, and gives students access to their data (see Schneider, Hassan & Sung, 2021, for more details). From the x, y position of the eyes, ears, nose, and upper body joints, four known cues of affect are detected by simple rule-based algorithms: head tilts and nods (e.g., Lee & Marsella, 2006), zoom-ins (e.g., Zhao et al., 2020), and hand over face (e.g., Behera et al., 2020). Then, we perform statistical analysis  to check for the existence of links between self-r eports and multimodal metrics. Reports tended to congregate around points (circled in dotted lines), indicating moments in the video that students commonly find interesting. While feeling ‘inspired’ is rarer, the patterns match expectations as they seem to be reported only during times where interest was high. Students rarely reported that they were bored or frustrated. We suspect these emotions may have been underreported, given prior research that documents the prevalence of boredom during learning (Baker et al., 2010). Visual inspection also showed the existence of clusters for “aha” and “confused” reports, and a pattern of confusion preceding “aha” moments. For the nonverbal metrics, chi-squared tests of independence indicate that all four metrics have significant associations with affective states (nodding: χ²(3, N = 2410) = 37.23, p <.001; tilting: χ²(3, N = 2410) = 13.67, p =.003; hand on face: χ²(3, N = 2410) = 19.56, p <.001; zoom-in: χ²(3, N = 2410) = 69.57, p <.001). Boredom and frustration were excluded due to suspected underrepresentation, and subsequent violation of the size requirement for the chi-squared test. Cramér’s V, a measure of effect size that corrects for sample size, indicates that only nodding and zoom-ins are substantially associated with affective states (Cohen, 1988). As expected, we observe nodding much more than chance when students felt interested, and less so than chance during confusion. Students also zoomed in much more than chance during states of confusion, and less than chance with reported interest and ‘aha’ moments. ## Conclusion

We investigated the use of continuously self-reported affective states and nonverbal multimodal metrics for tracking the affective state of learners outside of the classroom. Coherent patterns within and between emotions indicate students are using the button as intended, and that continuous self-reports could be a promising way to collect ground truth data for research. However, further investigation is needed to ensure that students are reliably reporting states, particularly for negative emotions. Additionally, based on these self-reports, we were able to discern two promising non-verbal metrics for automatic tracking of student affect, nodding and zooming in. These metrics were most consistently tied with two emotions: interest and confusion. These prevalent affective states are noteworthy for their relevance in supporting students, e.g., knowing when students are interested and bored to update the learning material. In future investigations, we plan to expand and refine nonverbal metrics to gain more accurate proxies of student affect. Ultimately, our goal is to track previously invisible student states outside the classroom with the help of automatic, non-intrusive multimodal metrics.
