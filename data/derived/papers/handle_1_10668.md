# Recognizing Cherry-Picked Data in Scientific Information: Epistemic Challenge toward Understanding Comprehensive Evidence

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
An abundance of everyday scientific information circulates with insufficient scrutiny and often contains inaccuracies. This can be challenging for laypeople since sources frequently lack systematic integration of evidence, opting instead to cherry pick specific data in a study to bolster their claims. To address this challenge, we conducted an experimental study to investigate how undergraduates perceive and respond to cherry-picked data and when they become attuned to the potential omission of some data by sources. The analysis revealed that laypeople had difficulty recognizing cherry-picked or hidden data, even when provided with cues. When students are made aware of the potential presence of concealed data with an explicit warning and a vivid example, their reasoning aligns more closely with people exposed to all comprehensive data. We discussed the pressing need to develop an educational program to foster students’ awareness of cherry-picking data for their scientific information literacy in a post-truth era.

## Introduction

Scientific information, including health information, is usually disseminated through online platforms and social media. A substantial portion of the scientific information circulates via these channels often undergoes insufficient vetting and is rife with inaccuracies (Lewandowsky et al., 2017). For instance, climate change is denied, and the risks associated with vaccination ha ve been often exaggerated by selective use of the scientific literature and evidence that supports preconceived stances (e.g., Ranney & Clark, 2016). This practice, known as cherry-picking, involves deceptively choos ing unrepresentative stud ies or data that support their positions while concealing a larger body of contradictory evidence (Levitan, 2017). Laypeople frequently experience challenges in discerning cherry-picked evidence in online information problem-solving (Oura et al., 2022); they fail to recognize cherrypicked evidence without very explicit signals that authors have cherry picked evidence. Therefore, educators in the contemporary digital era need to support students in developing epistemic vigilance (Gierth & Bromme, 2020), which includes the ability to recognize cherry-picked data or evidence. This critical skill empowers individuals to verify that evidence is comprehensive rather than selective. Diverse research corroborates that people should prefer positions that are supported b y a full range of relevant evidence (e.g., Duncan et al., 2018). On issues related to health, this includes consider ing the full range of relevant health outcomes when considering the ef fects of a disease or a health policy. To illustrate,  media sources that advocated for an early return to conventional normal lifestyles during  the COVID-19 pandemic emphasized data related to  hospitalization and mortality rates  but sometimes omitted information about longcovid (e.g., potential long-term disability or organdamage)—yet citizens ought to consider the full range of health effects including long-term disabilities into their judgments. Understandably, laypeople do not have the time or expertise to meticulously review and evaluate an extensive array  of evidential data from numerous scientific studies (Duncan et al., 2018). But individuals in this post-truth era at least can become sensitive to whether important data is being withheld when they make judgments about health and other issues. However, researchers have not yet enough investigated the extent of laypeople’s sensitivity to cherrypicked data or data that is concealed from view when making epistemic judgments. Thus, our research endeavors to address the following two research questions: (a) Do laypeople notice and take into consideration the fact that data have been selectively presented (on immediate health effects of a disease—death and hospitalization), while other important data (on long-term disabilities) are not reported?  (b) After reading different explanations about the importance of considering long-term health effects, do they become more aware that relevant data on such long-term effects have been omitted from the presented data and take this more into consideration? The answers to these questions can provide information to guide the development of educational programs that enable students to consider the full range of relevant data when making epistemic judgments.

## Method

We developed two documents (A and B) as professional medical weblogs that described a fictious new avian influenza virus called B3N. In Document A (ranging from 257 to 438 words when translated into  English, according to the conditions), a virology professor provided an overview of B3N’s transmission, basic reproduction number, and various health effects. Document B (ranging from 77 to 114 words when translated into English, featuring a table showing data as evidence) was written by another professor of microbiology, who presented data on diverse health effects of B3N with a brief call for public health preparations based on the outlined risks. This data reported low risks of immediate health effects due to the B3N infection (that is, deaths and hospitalizations) but a relatively high risk of potential long-term aftereffects on the heart, liver, and l ungs (explained below in detail). Both professors were affiliated with prestigious universities and argued for the necessity of considering a full range of possible health effects without clearly claiming a specific position, concluding “we need to act in accordance with these risks” (Document B) in every condition. In some conditions (explained below), Document B selectively presented the data while concealing long-term harms to health. There were four conditions, which varied according to the contents of Documents A and B, as described below:

(1) “All data presented” condition: I n this control condition, we investigate d how lay people evaluated

comprehensive data explaining the risks of B3N infection. Document A outlined the transmission and health effects  of B3N (described above), emphasizing the importance of considering a full range of effects, not just immediate illness but also longer-term aftereffects. Document B pr esented comprehensive data, ranging from low risks of hospitalization (0.08%) a nd death (0.01%) attributed to B3N infection to a significantly higher risk of long-term organ aftereffects within 6 months  (8.22%). The concluding message called to be prepared in accordance with the risks (as explained above).

(2) “Cherry-picked data without any cues ” condition: This condition was to assess  whether lay people

could discern cherry-picked data without any guidance; we presumed this task would be highly challenging for them. In this condition, Document A was the exact same  as that presented in the “all data presented” condition. On the other hand, Document B selectively presented only data on immediate health effects, highlighting low rates of harm to humans  (in hospitalizations and death) to argue that it was not even necessary to restrict social gatherings. In sum, this document omitted the data presenting a significantly higher risk of the long-term health aftereffects. But the concluding text echoed the necessity of measures in accordance with the risk (with the exact same sentence as appeared in the first condition).

(3)  “Cherry-picked data with a cue ” condition: In this condition, Document A, with the same text as the

aforementioned conditions, additionally detailed  long-term health effects that should be taken into consideration, encompassing immediate acute disease, hospitalization, death, and long-term health consequences such as damage to the lungs, other organs including the brain, and cellular damage leading to cancer later in life. Document B presented the exact same data (i.e., the cherry-picked data) and text as provided in the “cherry-picked data without any cues ” condition. Our presumption was that the additional information about the longer-term aftereffects  in Document A could work as a cue for laypeople to detect cherry-picking, because Document B in this condition did not present any data related to the longer-term aftereffects that were mentioned as important in Document A.

(4) “Cherry-picked data with a warning and a vivid example“ condition: In this condition, Document A

included a warning message about the mistake of sol ely considering death and hospitalizations, along with a vivid example of polio ’s serious health consequences (which caused not onl y 1% of patients' deaths but also left 3% of them paralyzed),  in addition to the exact same text provided in the “cherrypicked data with a cue” condition. In contrast, Document B in this condition still presented the cherrypicked data (i.e., solely focusing on hospitalization and death) and the t ext, exactly akin to the second and third conditions. We presumed that the warning message and the vivid example would be  potent enough for laypeople to recognize the selectively chosen and unreliable nature of the data in Document B.

The study was conducted in an online introductory education course at a public university in Japanduring the spring semester of 2022. Participants were 155 undergraduate students fluent in reading Japanese. A ll materials were provided in Japanese. Students were randomly assigned to  one of the four conditions described above, and they engaged in all procedures individually. All participants first answered a pre-survey in which they rated degrees of their agreement with restricting social gatherin gs or with precautious wearing masks to avoid new bird flu (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). Then they read versions of the two documents that corresponded to their assigned condition and rated (a) their B3N hazard assessment, (b) their degree of agreement with the necessity of personal or/and social measures (wearing masks or/and restricting social gatherings), and (c) the author trustworthiness of each document. Each rating was made on a 7-point Likert scale.

## Results

Results are presented in Table 1. We performed one-way ANOVA to assess differences in each rating across the conditions. The pre-survey data indicated no significant differences among the conditions concerning the variables and means near the midpoints of the scales  (Need for personal measures: F (3,151) = 0.33, p =.802; Need for social measures: F (3,151) = 1.02, p =.384). In contrast, we observed significant differences in most of the postreading measures. On post-reading measures, there w ere significant difference s observed between the conditions in the ratings for B3N hazard assessment (F (3, 151) = 6.37, p <.001, η2 =.11), the need for social precautions measures (F (3, 151) = 3.66, p =.014, η2 =.07), and the author trustworthiness of both documents (Document A: F (3, 151) = 3.52, p =.017, η2 =.07; Document B: F (3, 151) = 12.11, p <.001, η2 =.19). There was also a marginally significant difference in the rating of the need for personal precautionary measures (F (3, 151) = 2.20, p =.09, η2 =.04). The results of Tukey’s multiple comparisons for these significant differences (excluding the last marginal variable) revealed that students in the “all data presented” condition (p =.003) and the “cherry-picked data with a warning and a vivid example ” condition (p =.001) provided higher ratings to  the hazard assessment compared to  the “cherry-picked data  with a cue ” condition. This pattern also held for the need for social precautious measures, with the “all data presented” condition indicating significantly higher ratings for the need for social precautionary measures (p =.00 1) than the “cherry-picked data  with a cue” condition, and the “cherry-picked data with a warning and a vivid example ” condition exhibiting marginally significant higher ratings as well (p =.059). In terms of the trustworthiness of the authors, Document A received significantly lower rating in the “cherry-picked data with a warning and a vivid example ” condition compared to the “cherrypicked data with a cue” condition (p =.009), while Document B was rated significantly lower in the “cherrypicked data with a warning and a vivid example” condition than in the other conditions (p <.001 for each). ## Discussion and conclusion

As summarized in the bar graphs in Figure 1,  students in the “cherry-picked data without any cues ” and the “cherry-picked data with a cue” conditions rated the B3N hazard significantly lower because of the lack of data regarding the long-term aftereffects in Document B. In contrast, those in the “all data presented” and “cherrypicked data with a warning and a vivid example ” conditions could assess the hazard relatively higher. In the “cherry-picked data without any cues ” condition, in which no information (beyond their own possible prior knowledge) was available for the students to recognize the potential for cherry-picking, it is not surprising that students focused primarily on the low hospitalizati on and mortality rates, leading them to perceive B3N as relatively safe. However, the “cherry-picked data with a cue” condition presented intriguing results — despite students being prompted to consider long-term aftereffects as a cue for possible cherry-picking in Document A, they remained predominantly fixated on the hospitalization and mortality rates presented as cherry-picked data in Document B. This narrow focus appeared to impede students’ ability to engage in comprehensive reasoning and fully contemplate the potential existence of concea led long-term aftereffects as d ata. On the other hand, it is notable that students in both the “all data presented” and “cherry-picked data with a warning and a vivid example” conditions exhibited similar patterns in their ratings of the post-reading measures. This result suggests that the warning message with a vivid example, which activated epistemic vigilance as same as our previous study (Mochizuki et al., 2021), had the effect of aligning students’ reasoning processes in a more appropriate manner. This effect was akin to the situation where students were provided with more comprehensive data to consider the issues. These results indicate the challenges students faced in discerning selectively presented (cherry-picked) data, even when provided cues to consider other relevant data might have been omitted. It highlights the inherent difficulty that l aypeople experience when they encounter selectively present  data, particularly when presented with only a few cues  among relatively coherent documents that conflict only implicitly. More specifically, the results suggest a tendency among laypeople to overlook the presence or absence of data related to the longer-term effects of a disease unless such long-term effects are made salient with a vivid example. Our recommendation for designing learning environments to promote scientific information literacy includes supporting students in learning to consider such implicitly conflicting information and cherry-picked data. Exposure to explicitly conflicting information often prompts laypeople to generate explanations for the disagreements, even though they may typically fail to notice conflicts in a text (e.g., Stadtler, et al., 2013). In addition to learning how to resolve explicit conflicts, they should learn to recognize cherry-picked data by using information presented across multiple documents to determine what data is needed for comprehensive evaluation. Digital information environments encompass such complex and uncertain scientific inf ormation sets, and laypeople should enhance their skills in noticing limitations in the information they encounter daily through rich experience with a variety of situations.
