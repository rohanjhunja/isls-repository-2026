# Resilience and Passion Forged Through Culture and Autonomy: Redesigning Tabletop Games in Middle School

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explored the potential of redesigning games as a culturally sustaining pedagogy that promotes autonomy for middle school students. We collected data through research notes, videos, and interviews in two 7th-grade classrooms. Comparing two cases, we show the complex interplay between culture, autonomy, passion, and resilience. The cases exemplify how game redesign allows students to take ownership of learning and how students can infuse their cultures into their learning, creating personally meaningful artifacts. This personal connection fuels a passion for their game, whereas the independent nature of the redesign project encourages students to assume responsibility for their learning enhancing resilience. While these cases demonstrate the positive impact of game redesign, future studies should focus on strategies for fostering shared passion and commitment among diverse student groups engaged in joint activities.

## Introduction

Tabletop games go beyond play, offering students a unique learning opportunity by engaging them in des ign practice (Bastani & Kim, 2022; Kessner et al., 2021). (Re)designing games is an interest-driven design practice that places a strong emphasis on tailoring educatio nal experiences to individual learners (Kim et al.,  2015). This approach focuses on meeting the specific needs of learners, enabling them tonegotiate their identities and express themselves in personally pleasurable ways. In the context of these design practices, learner-generated designs are treated as dyna mic artifacts in the process of being created and r efined, reflecting the ongoing journey of the learners themselves (Kim et al., 2015). In this pap er, we discuss a study that brought the tabletop ga me redesign approach to the 7th-grade humanities and science classrooms and explored how game redesign allows students to take ownership of learning and how students can infuse their cultures into their learning. Culturally sustaining learning repositions diverse learners as catalysts for interdependence and their cultural and linguistic differences as the basis fo r understanding the world and creating novel and pl uralistic outcomes. The term culturally sustaining requires t hat our pedagogies be more than responsive to or re levant to the cultural experiences and practices of young peo ple—it requires that they support youth in sustaining t heir cultural and linguistic competence while simultaneo usly offering access to dominant cultural competence (Paris, 2012). Redesigning games can act as a culturally su staining pedagogy (Bastani et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2021). Culturally relevant games have the potential to bring historical and cultural contexts to disciplinary thinking (e.g., mathematics; Bayeck, 2018; Bastani & Kim, 2022) whi le helping learners position themselves as confiden t contributors and their culture and language as important resources in the learning settings. Autonomy in learning involves students taking control of their learning process beyond merely following instructions (Holec, 1996; Boud, 2012). It is often  misunderstood as solely self-directed learning outside the classroom, but it can also be nurtured within structured classroom and social learning contexts (Haffner & Miller, 2011). Promoting autonomy fosters motivation and ownership, requiring projects to offer flexibility aligned with each student's interests and competence (Lysne et a l., 2023). Redesig ning games as a culturally sustaining pedagogy offers such flexibility, promoting culturally relevant and personally meaningful opportunities for learner autonomy, and potentially enhancing students' passion and resilience within a structured classroom environment. In essence, passion is highly cherished, self-defining, and a strong attraction to an activity that holds deep personal meaning, requiring a significant investmen t of time and energy (Vallerand, 2012). In learning, passion can be highly motivating and instigate actions (Ser in, 2017). For an activity to be considered a passi on, it is necessary that it holds personal meaning and is int egrated into one's identity (Vallerand, 2012). We s uggest that redesigning games can be a source of passion for students. Firstly, students have the autonomy to make decisions on the theme, backstory, rules, and other pertinent  aspects of game redesign. These decisions are pers onally meaningful to the students and could enable learners to use their cultural resources in their game ideas (Bastani et al., 2023). The students’ cultural background, as p art of their identities, is kept intact and serves as an asset for their game development decisions. Passion also allo ws people to engage in deliberate practice repeated ly and relentlessly so that over time, they improve their skills in the activity (Vallerand, 2012), and it ca n serve as a source of resilience and perseverance.

## Research design

We took a design-based research approach, focusing on reflective inquiry into an innovative learning environment in a naturalistic setting (Brown 1992; Collins et a l., 2004). As part of this larger design-based research study on redesigning games, the current iteration was conducted over five months with a pproximately one hundred Grade 7 students and four science and humanities teachers  in an urban middle school in a culturally and ling uistically diverse neighborhood of a Western Canadian city. Tw o cohorts of students with a common science teacher  but different humanities teachers were grouped into smaller teams of three to four students. These teams were tasked to redesign the game, Forbidden Island (Leacock, 2010), within their humanities and science classes. We selected this game due to its emphasis on teamwork and strai ghtforward gameplay that is accessible to Grade 7 s tudents, while still offering strategic depth and engaging m echanics. Students worked on the redesign project b orrowing some curriculum ideas from both science and humanit ies while following the Tabletop Game Redesign proc ess and its workbook (Play Design Learn Group, 2023). T he students’ conversations and workflow were record ed either on action cameras or iPads during their game rede sign process. One of the researchers from the team was physically present in the room, actively observing,  engaging in discussions with the students, and cap turing photographs. We reviewed, transcribed, and analyzed  these observations to study each group's game rede sign process.

Within the scope of this paper, we delve into the e ngagement of two students: Grace, from a team comprised of four girls, originating from diverse cultural backgrounds, including Sudan, Cameroon, Lebanon, and Canada, and Jimmy, from another team composed of th ree Canadian boys. Grace’s team’s game centered on the theme of colonization and incorporated small rule c hanges, while Jimmy's game was rooted in a fantasy world brimming with monsters, featuring many changes in the redesign.

## Findings

We present Jimmy's and Grace's game redesign cases to illustrate the passion and resilience fostered b y this project. Both teams redesigned the same game and fo llowed the same workbook guide. They, however, were  in different classes, had dissimilar team dynamics, and created unique final games. On the surface, Jimmy and Grace had very different experiences, yet several similar ities within this process cannot be overlooked: Bot h students' cultural identity significantly influenced their ga me redesigns, and the project fostered autonomy and ownership of learning, ultimately sparking passion and resilience for game creation. As evidenced by the numerous conversations with his  team members and the researchers, Jimmy was deeply embedded in the Canadian teenage media cultu re of TV shows, movies, and video games. The three students in the team had many side conversations, c ommon language expressions, and moments of play tha t demonstrated their preoccupation with these ideas and their embeddedness within the current youth culture.  Under this cultural umbrella, Jimmy redesigned the game to suit one of his interests: monsters. His childhood fascination with dinosaurs ignited a love for monsters, fueling his passion for movies like "Godzilla" and games like "Monster Hunter." This passion carried through to his game r edesign, where his monster-themed creations, as depicted in Figure 1a, resonated with his teammates, united by their share d youth culture. The project fostered his cultural connection, transforming his initial enthusiasm for monsters into a deeper passion for game design. Ownership played a pivotal role in fueling this passion. Jimmy took the lead from the start, meticulously crafting a binder filled with ideas, rules, drawings, and the game board itself. This personal binder represented the game's heart, Jimmy's dedication, and creative vision. Though committed to involvin g his teammates, Jimmy's vison of creating a perfect game  clashed with subpar contributions. And although h e yearned for collaboration, his strong attachment to the game's quality drove him to work solo. While all the team members enjoyed playing the game, only Jimmy felt a sense of ownership over  the redesign process. It was unmistakably Jimmy's game, a fact acknowledged by everyone, including the teacher, teammates, and most notably, Jimmy himself. Ownership and leadership in the game redesign project contrib uted to Jimmy’s notably higher resilience compared to his groupmates (Edwards et al., 2016). His dedication t o the redesign project extended beyond the classroo m as he frequently worked on it at home. Jimmy never voiced  complaints about this additional effort, and despi te facing distractions from his peers and receiving limited s upport from his teammates, he displayed perseveranc e and tirelessly worked on the game.

Sometimes, you don’t have money for boat and stuff… Grace's heartfelt story about her friend's uncle's migration journey—a story deeply rooted in her cultural identity as a recent immigrant —resonated with her teammate. Grace's game redesign,  fueled by her experiences and cultural background, exemplifies how the project empowers students to express themselves. Likely grappling with the realities of war in her home country and potent ial challenges as a new Canadian, the game offered Grace a safe outlet.  It allowed her to envision positive outcomes, explore possibilities, and share her experiences through a creative medium. Grace's dedication to this deepl y personal project was remarkable. Despite having E nglish language difficulties, she tirelessly created cards, crafted rules, and managed tasks throughout the  months-long process. She was enthusiastic throughout, persevere d even when her teammates faltered, and was immense ly proud of her final product, evidenced by her eagern ess to share the game with her father during “Games  Night” (Figure 1b).

Similarly to Jimmy, Grace assumed ownership of the game, making key decisions regarding the backstory and the game's objectives. Despite taking  input from Shaza, Grace was the driving force behi nd the game, motivating her teammates to work on it, even when their enthusiasm waned. On one occasion, when she shared her idea for the game's backstory and objective with the teacher, the teacher suggested she cross-check her explanation with what the girls had previously written in the backstory document. Grace promptly reviewed it and was visibly perturbed to find that Shaza had proposed something different. Without hesitation, she erased Shaza's input and replaced it with her own. This ownership was further confirmed when, in Grace's absence, the  other team members hesitated to progress, demonstrating their depen dence on her guidance. Ownership of the game redesign process empowered Grace to take charge, fo stering her passion and unwavering dedication throu ghout the project.

## Discussion and conclusions

Passion is a powerful attraction to an activity wit h profound personal meaning, deep value, and a sign ificant role in shaping one's identity, thereby requiring a substantial dedication of time and energy (Vallerand, 2012). Jimmy and Grace both demonstrated  passion for their game redesign and this passion, i n turn, bolstered their determination when facing challenges throughout the  process. The project, as a culturally sustaining p edagogy, was deeply meaningful for Jimmy and Grace, who inco rporated their personal interests in the game redesign and embraced and integrated their cultural contexts int o their ideas. In addition, Jimmy and Grace greatly  valued the game redesign process and its outcomes, as Jimmy to ok responsibility for crafting the game elements to  ensure their quality standards were met, while Grace showcased her pride in the project du ring "games night" with her father. Both students exemplified learner autonomy and leadership within their teams, ultimately keepi ng them focused and motivated to persist. Finally, both Jim my and Grace demonstrated exceptional dedication to  the project, investing significant time and effort into  it, with Jimmy meticulously crafting the game and Grace persisting in her work ethic despite her teammates'  lack of enthusiasm, showcasing remarkable resilien ce throughout the process.

The complexity of game redesign became apparent wit hin the two cases. Game redesign serves as both a culturally sustaining pedagogy (Paris, 2012) and holds significant potential for fostering learner a utonomy (Boud, 2012). These attributes enable students to align their personal interests and assume contro l over their learning, igniting a passion within them, which, in  turn, drives their dedication to the process, allo wing students to achieve success in an authentic learning environ ment. This feedback loop can promote the resilience of all students involved.

The advantages of the game redesign project were evident in Jimmy and Grace's cases, yet not all students displayed the same level of initiative. Thus, a lim itation of these instances of game redesign was the  partial engagement of students in taking ownership of their learning. In both cases discussed, the game did not evolve as a good collective endeavor but rather as pursuits of two dedicated students. It would be valuable to explore ways to generate interest and passion among the entire g roup to collaborate toward a shared objective with mutual interests and dedication. Future iterations of this  design-based research study could explore methods to facil itate such collective engagement.
