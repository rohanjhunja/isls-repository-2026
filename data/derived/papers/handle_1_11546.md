# Learning to Serve: Building a Research-Practice Partnership Informed by Ethnic Studies to Improve Higher Education for Asian American and Pacific Islander Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Federal Asian American and Native American Pacific Islander Serving Institutions (AANAPISI) funding is intended to serve the racialized needs of Asian American and Pacific Islander (AAPI) students in higher education (HE) in the United States. This requires institutions, and practitioners within them, to learn to move beyond colorblind norms in HE and offer race-conscious programming. We report on how a research-practice partnership (RPP) between an AANAPISI program and a research team organized learning to ensure the AANAPISI program responds to the racialized contexts and needs of AAPI students. We share three core practices in the partnership that organizes learning: building on Asian American and Ethnic Studies, leveraging interdisciplinary expertise, and engaging in multi-level sensemaking.

Objective and conceptual framing Goals of AANAPISIs: Serving AAPI students AANAPISIs are minority-serving institutions (MSIs) that receive funding from the US Department of Education (DE) to build programs, systems, and practices that, “improve and expand their capacity to serve Asian Americans and Native American Pacific Islanders and low-income individuals” (ED, n.d.). Garcia and colleagues’ (2019) offer servingness as a framework that seek to advance both academic (e.g. GPA) and nonaca demic (e.g. critical consciousness) outcomes that recognize the students’ racialized positions in HE. Becoming serving AANAPISIs AANAPISIs often do not have existing practices to serve AAPI students. This necessitates a developmental view of AANAPISI programs in which learning is paramount  to becoming serving institutions.  Such learning is complicated by racist ideologies and practices in HE that misrecognize the needs and  racialized experiences of AAPI students (Poon et al., 2016; Squire, 2016). As such, there is a need for research that points to how learning servingness can be achieved given these constraints.

RPP as a mechanism for learning to serve Our RPP (Coburn & Penuel, 2016) was informed by participatory design methods (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) that integrate knowledge-theoretical and practical-among participants, articulate shared outcomes amon g partners, and engage in the co-design and study of interventions for iterative  improvement. We viewed RPPs as political and racialized endeavors (Vakil et al., 2016) with the need to make visible participants’ politicized and racialized goals, sensemaking, and conjectures related to the RPP’s shared endeavors (Ahn et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2022). Contribution of this paper In this paper, we advance the methodology of RPPs a s a collaborative learning mechanism towards racial ly just institutional change. We share how we adapted the broader methodological approach by integrating concepts from Asian American studies (Prashad, 2006)  to respond to the aforementioned constraints to ser vingness in AANAPISIs.

## Study context

The RPP was formed between researchers from two res earch-based higher education institutions and faculty co-PIs of an AANAPISI grant at a third institution to support the implementation of the new AANAPISI gran t. The AANAPISI is situated within a large public universi ty system in the northeastern part of the United St ates, with a diverse AAPI population that comprise over 25% of the student bo dy. All participating faculty members had training in Asian American or ethnic studies, with additional expertise in disciplines including English, education, and research methods. In its first year and a half,  the RPP team met on a monthly basis to engage in p rogram designs, strategize data collection, and discuss ongoing findings. Data We collected qualitative data which included RPP team meeting notes, on-site observations, staff interviews, and RPP artifacts, such as research instruments. 14 research team meetings took place between January 2023 and July 2024 ranging from 60-90 minutes in length. We also conducted on-site observations of several program sessions offered to students by the AANAPISI program, and co nducted three AANAPISI staff interviews focused on examining the AANAPISI efforts and the RPP from the perspectives of AANAPISI staff members. Analysis We analyzed the data to build a deep case study (Yi n et al., 2012) that examined how an RPP, as a coll aborative learning effort, organized itself to learn to serve  AAPI students in an AANPISI. We were interested in how such learning mechanisms responded to the racialized socio-historical and institutional contexts. We identified moments where the RPP participants conceptualized A ANAPISI and RPP practices in relation to the aforementioned constraints to AANAPISI program deve lopment, and  examined these  processes in relation to narratives in the interview data to develop core categories.

## Findings

Asian American Studies as Epistemological and Ontological Foundations A shared understanding of Asian American studies, a nd a commitment to its ethos, played an important role in directing the RPP. We drew from perspectives in Asian American studies that point to HE institutions’ historical resistance to the: a) intersectional experiences with varied assets and needs; b) expansion of legitim ate outcomes for AAPI students; and, c) opportunities for learning that validate AAPI students’ racialized experiences (Prashad, 2006). Our RPP responded to these insights by  collecting data on students’ racial identity format ion and critical consciousness, disaggregating such data by ethnicit y, and examining this data through critical framewo rks that consider how institutional systems might be producing problematic outcomes. Interdisciplinary Use of Research RPP utilized tools from a broader set of discipline s to advance its learning. This included Nguyen’s research on AANAPISI policy and practices (Nguyen, 2020; Nguyen, 2023), Ngo’s expertise on quantitative methods focused on program designs and student outcomes (Ngo et al., 2018), and Lee’s expertise in the learning sciences and RPPs (Lee et al., 2022; A hn et al., 2024). These disciplinary expertise were pu t in conversation with core understandings in Asian American studies, and used to inform RPP activities. Multi-Level Framing Following research that situates racial justice work at the intersection of multi-level contexts (Diem et al., 2022), the RPP situated its learning at the intersection o f policy, the institution and its structures, the p rogram design, and student experiences. Decisions about the RPP st emmed from sensemaking on how these factors inform one another in the local context. The RPP unpacked, for example, how the AANAPISI program might be designed to nurture additional opportunities for student leadership in ways that filled institutional gaps.

## Discussion

We shaped our RPP through a deep knowledge base of Asian Amer ican studies, the pragmatic, interdisciplinary use of theory and research methods to drive learnin g, and careful attention to the multi-level educational context in which our work takes place. These components of our RPP inform the ways race consciou s policy in HE is implemented to serve students who have historically been minoritized in HE.
