# Disruptions, Dissent, and Discontinuities: What Tensions in Intergenerational Learning Dynamics Reveal About Learning Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper describes family member participation in an informal learning setting called Family Create Nights. With attention to the design of equity oriented STEAM learning experiences, we argue that looking at moments of tension or disruption in families’ work together may reveal important needs and desires of learners. Our analysis reveals the ways families positioned themselves in terms of expertise, created collaborative arrangements for working together, and asserted creative control through their project creation. Findings point to the importance of holistically attending to families learning needs, to highlight parents' own curiosities as well as the roles of youth as facilitators and educators within their families.

## Introduction

In recent years equity oriented researchers and ed ucators have increasingly turned their attention to wards the design of programs that engage families in learning  with creative technologies. One model for this bri ngs in families’ funds of knowledge through storytelling for culturally responsive computation learning (Roque, 2018; Tzou et al., 2019). As researchers' exploration of this type of family learning is relatively new, there is a need to better understand families’ social organization to support the design of pedagogical and facilitation practices..

In this paper we look at family members’ participat ion in the Family Create Night (FCN) workshop series aimed at creating joyful and personally mean ingful experiences with technology for families his torically marginalized from computing spaces. In this learning context we asked: When did tensions or disruption s arise in families’ collaborative work together, and how d id families negotiate them? Through our analysis we identified moments of disruption or indications of tension as families made projects using creative technologies. We propose that these moments reveal the needs and desires of learners in ways that can inform program design and the development of facilitation practices. In l ooking at tensions and disruptions, we saw curiosit y and a desire to make creative contributions from both you th and adult learners, and argue that parent’s lear ning must be centered along with those of youth in these collaborative learning environments, Theoretical Approach The social organization of informal learning contex ts is key to understanding how participants learn t ogether (DiGiacomo & Gutiérrez, 2016; Rogoff et al., 2016).  In this short paper, our analysis focuses on the s ocial organization between family members in FCN, which w e understand as joint activity. Joint activity posi tions youth and adults as, “active co-participants in kno wing and learning” (Vossoughi et al., 2021, p.215, citing Rogoff, 1994). Each family has their own culture of  interactional arrangements and values that all mem bers contribute to shaping, and “while practices may be sedimented, they are nonetheless reified in particu lar moments of interaction and potentially transformed in those same moments”(Keifert, 2021, p.267). This means that facilitators and designers must attend to how the design of collaborative learning environments m ight contribute to reifying or reshaping families’ cultural practices. In the learning sciences, disruptions, tensions or breakdowns have been used to understand collaboration and joint work through theories of bo undaries and infrastructure (Akkerman & Bakker, 201 1; Penuel, 2019; Penuel et al., 2015). We take up thes e theories to see families as their own cultural co mmunities with their own sets of cultural tools, roles, and p ractices. Penuel et al. (2015) describe joint work as the negotiation of differing goals and desires for indi vidual participants within these cultural communiti es. From this theoretical perspective, points of tension or disruption can be leveraged to inform the redesign of collaborative structures, norms and activities. We define tension or disruption in the context of fami lies’ work together as: moments of negotiation that disrupt the flow of work, moments of dissent or refusal, disengagement or discontinuing work, and/or expression of an unme t desire. We do not place a value judgment on these expressions or moments of tension, but view them as places of negotiation in collaborative engagement. ICLS2022 Proceedings 1613 © ISLS

## Context and Methods

This study draws on data from an iteration of Famil y Create Night (FCN) designed to serve Spanish-spea king families who recently emigrated to the US, and held  in partnership with an elementary school in the mo untain west region of the US. In this four-week workshop series, families engage in creative computing together, using ScratchJr, a block-based programming software desig ned to be accessible for young learners. The worksh ops use a four-part structure where families share a me al, connect with their peers, make a project togeth er, and share their creations (Roque, 2016). A university p rofessor, graduate, and undergraduate students acted as both researchers and facilitators to support families to create a favorite family story. We recruited 9 families: 14 children (ages 5 to 10 years) and 11 adult caretakers. Our analysis focuses on the 5 families who attended at least 3 of 4 work shop sessions and participated in interviews. Ethno graphic data collection from the workshops included facilit ator fieldnotes, screen recordings capturing activi ty in ScratchJr, and post-workshop interviews with famili es. The research team applied a first round of iter ative coding to fieldnotes and interviews to identify ref erences to family interactions during project making. Authors then coded fieldnotes, interviews, and screen recor ding transcripts for moments of disruption or tensi on (as defined in the Theoretical Approach section above).

## Findings

During FCN workshops, families took up a range of different collaborative arrangements  in which they negotiated roles, contributions, and turn-taking. These arrangements included whole families working on one project, children working on projects individually while their parent took a hands-off role, and a parent working with their youngest child(ren), while older children paired-up to work on their own project. All families had moments where collaboration flowed; they took up each other’s ideas, embraced complementary roles, and transitioned smoothly while turn-taking. However, families also experienced moments of tension or disruption, which emerged around the positioning and negotiation of roles, creative control of projects, and physical control of the materials and tools (often concurrently).

Collaborative Arrangements and Negotiating Contributions Tensions or disruptions frequently arose when participants voiced a desire to increase their participation in a collaborative project. In these moments of disruption, we saw different styles of negotiation. Some parents, like Nina whose whole family worked on a single project, were very assertive in their establishment of turn taking across family members. In an exchange between Nina and one of her daughters, Lorena, Nina was selecting a character to represent herself, when Lorena asked, “Can I pick it?” Nina responded, “yo la escogo. < I’m choosing it.>” Lani, a volunteer from the school reinforced this by explaining to Lorena, “You chose yours, now it's your mommy’s turn.” Another family, Julia (parent), Kiara, Cal and Hamid, worked in pairs, with Julia and Kiara on one iPad, and the two boys on another. Cal and Hamid argued throughout the workshops about who got to contribute to the project. During one workshop, a facilitator attempted to demonstrate how to work more collaboratively on the iPad, saying, “Here, put it on the table so you can both do it. Set it down so you can both work on it.” The two obliged, but when they returned to fighting a few minutes later, Julia tried to intervene by inviting Hamid to help with her and Kiara’s project, saying, “Ven, tu auydame aqui Hamid. < Come, you help me over here Hamid.>”  Julia defused conflict in that moment by soliciting Hamid’s help, and reconfiguring the family’s collaborative arrangement.. These kinds of interaction were characteristic of the negotiations families engaged in as they tried to join their familial structures for collaboration with those required by the workshops. Physical control of device Tensions over contributions often manifested in a need tonegotiate who had physical control of the device, as seen in the example above with brothers Cal and Hamid. This sometimes came up when one family member tried to help another with something they were struggling with by taking the iPad from them and performing the programming action. For example, in a moment of tension between Hamid and Kiara, Kiara rejected Hamid’s help and started crying, while Julia affirmed that Kiara could do it herself, “ Dejale, ella lo esta haciendo. Ella sabe que hacer. < Let her. She knows what she is doing. She knows what to do.>” It was common to see both children and parents pushing family members hands away as they worked on projects. Sometimes this gesture was used to assert control or ownership of the activity, like when facilitator Amy observed Kiara push Julia’s hand away and say, “yo muevo las cosas! < I get to move the things!>” after Julia tried to move some characters. In other instances pushing a hand away was done in service of scaffolding learning. As one facilitator Coreina observed, “Nina would push her daughters’ hands away from the screens sometimes, which led me to actually closely observe the family’s dynamic. What I noticed was that Nina was ICLS2022 Proceedings 1614 © ISLS very intentionally doing things in ScratchJr herself, but narrating her actions and what she was making happen to her daughters. Then, once she had completed her action, she would then get Mia or Lorena to do the same thing on their own from start to finish.” This observation points to facilitators’ ability to gain significant insights about family dynamics through attention to moments of disruption. However, caution must be exercised in initial interpretations of the meaning of a gesture or action in the context of a family’s collaborative practice. Creative control Role negotiation also played out in moments of tension when both parents and kids expressed desires for creative control. For example, Julia discussed the challenge of everyone in the family, including herself, wanting to “make what I wanted to make.” Negotiations around creative choices were most often around aesthetics (ie. “No, I like the pink one more”), or continuity or percieved fit (ie. “We do not need flowers in a library”). Within aesthetic negotiations, tensions over the visual representation of family members were by far the most common. In prior analysis (Roque et al., 2021) we found that families faced limitations and tensions in how they were able to visually represent themselves in ScratchJr, especially when it came to race and body type. We found here as well, that frictions emerged as individual family members tried to assert their agency over how they were represented in the projects.

Both parents and children commonly expressed displeasure at how they were portrayed when other family members selected or edited characters that represented them. In an exchange between Lucia and her daughter Jasmine, Lucia started to select a character for Jasmine saying, “ponemos una foto Jasmine? ¿Cuál quieres? Oh, esta, esa. < Should we add a picture, Jasmine? Which one do you want? Oh this one, this one.>” Jasmine dissented, “No, esa no. < No, not that one.>” Lucia responded affirmatively, “andale pues. < Okay then>,” and let Jasmine select the one she wanted. In other instances, family members’ expressed desires about their own representation was not respected. For example, Hamid became upset when his brother Cal represented him with a cat character, saying, “Noo that’s not me.” Cal responded by selecting a pig character and saying, “Okay you can be this one,” which made Hamid more upset. Cal only agreed to change Hamid’s representation to a human character after Hamid got their mother Julia to intervene. Another form of tension related to creative control was caused by what we interpreted as discontinuities in participants’ priorities regarding their projects. We saw several instances where one family member (usually a child) wanted to take more time to work on aesthetics, while another (a parent or sibling) wanted to move on to develop other aspects of a project, often in service of completing the project by the end of a workshop. In one example, daughter Camila fixated on the selection and aesthetics of a dog character, while mother Ana said, “Corrale que tenemos que hacer todos estos, y no hemos hecho ni uno. < Hurry we have to make all of these, and we haven't even made one.>” In a related dynamic common between children and parents in the workshops, parents shut down their child’s assertions that  they wanted to continue drawing instead of working on the ScratchJr project. These dynamics may point towards children prioritizing whatever form of creative expression was most compelling to them in the moment, while parents generally seemed to be more product oriented.

Expert/ Novice Positioning In interviews, some parents discussed a dynamic where children were positioned as experts and parents as novices with technology. One mother, Marta said of her sons, “Arturo enseñó a Roberto, porque me quitó el tablet, ‘no mami, es que tú no sabes.’ <Translation: Arturo taught Roberto because he took the tablet from me, ‘No Mom, you don’t know what you are doing.>’” During the workshop that Marta described, Amy, a facilitator, had also observed that Roberto and Arturo “had taken over their mom’s iPad and deleted her project so they could restart with their own… Marta, was sitting off a little from them and was not participating actively with the boys.” In an instance during the following workshop, facilitator Amy observed a further interaction between Marta and her son Arturo. “Marta asked Arturo if he would teach her how to do Scratch this time, and he said ‘no’ that he was the expert. After a bit though, he said he might be willing to teach her some.” Even though Arturo had positioned himself as an expert (as did Marta by asking for his help), he was hesitant to teach his Mom. Later in the workshop families outlined their family story through storyboards and then translated them to ScratchJr. Marta said that because her kids wanted to draw and program their story quickly, “yo nunca traté porque ellos ya sabían. < I never tried because they already knew how to do it.>” While the positioning of youth as experts with technology did not always inhibit adults' engagement with the projects, it hindered collaborative learning between family members when youth did not take on a teaching role with their parents or pace their activity to actively engage them as collaborators.

## Discussion

While intergenerational collaboration in informal learning settings may often be positioned as joint activity, adult's learning often remains peripheral, as the lens is still focused on children's learning, placing adults in an assistive role. We found that parents were also curious learners who wanted to experiment and make creative contributions. Ignoring the presence of parents’ curiosities and desires both marginalizes parents as active learners, and marginalizes the role of youth as potential facilitators and educators within their families, who shape family culture and dynamics. In earlier iterations of FCN, we noticed this curiosity from parents and created space and time in the design of the FCN model for parents to explicitly create with ScratchJr on their own before introducing their kids to the program (Roque, 2016). Considering parents as learners is particularly important for technology-based family workshops where the expertise may be flipped from the norm of adults holding more established expertise than youth. Designers of these learning environments should consider how all family members can make valuable contributions and hold expertise in these dynamics, e.g. how to support youth as facilitators in their family and see their parents as curious learners too. Taking on a leadership role as educators within their families is something that may be modeled for youth.

While youth may have been more likely to be positioned as experts around technology, parents were more likely to take on roles structuring turn-taking and the negotiation of work together, as well as asserting their ideas about normative storytelling. Based on our findings on the challenges of negotiating turn-taking and legitimizing the contributions of all family members, initial activities that scaffold the practice of building off of each other's contributions could be beneficial. It may also be worthwhile to consider the tensions families had around normative narrative construction (ie. the continuity issue) and how expansive possibilities for world building, remembering, and dreaming together might be designed for in family’s collaborative storying with creative technologies. Lastly, our findings suggest that when representations of the self and personal identity are incorporated into family learning activities, participants may benefit from activities that norm individual family members having agency over their own representation.
