# Exploring the Path to Assessment Reform: Transforming Teachers’ Perspectives Through Insights into Students' Thought Processes

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how focusing on students’ cognitive processes can help teachers rethink assessment design. In a professional development workshop, high school teachers examined student thinking using data collected during test-taking. Their reflections provided insights that facilitated the alignment of assessments with instructional goals and deeper learning. The findings highlight the potential of using student cognition to bridge the gap between instruction and testing, ultimately enhancing classroom practices.

## Introduction

Embedding assessments into meaningful classroom act ivities has long been a challenge in education. While instruction emphasizes active engagement and deep t hinking, assessments often remain disconnected from  these goals, leading to a persistent misalignment between  teaching and testing. This misalignment stems from  a structural gap: instruction tends to focus on learning processes, whereas assessments prioritize scores or outcomes (e.g., Binkley et al., 2012). Researchers  have long advocated for a closer integration of instruction, assessment, and learning goals. The Assessment Triangle framewo rk proposed by Pellegrino et al. (2001) underscores the importance of aligning cognition, observation, and interpretation to support meaningful assessment des ign. Building on this foundation, scholars have highlighted the role of formative and transformative assessment  in promoting deeper learning and  improving instruction (e.g., S cardamalia et al., 2012; Shepard et al., 2018). However, assessments often remain isolated from classroom practice, and effective approaches to alignment are still being explored. This study suggests that focusing on students’ thought processes during  tests offers a promising way to bridge this gap. While classroom instruction emphasizes active eng agement and testing prioritizes scoring, examining students ’ thinking during test-taking allows educators to analyze both  of these through the  same lens —revealing the similarities and differences between the intend ed cognitive processes promoted in class and those actually elicited by the test. This paper presents a professional development workshop designed through a research-practice partnership (Coburn et al., 2013) called CoREF (Iikubo et al., 2022; Shirouzu et al., 2023),  a Japan-based community that brings together researchers and educators to collaboratively investigate and enhance classroom practices.

## Method

The workshop held in July 2024, brought together  18 experienced high school teachers from across Jap an, representing five subject areas: math, Japanese, En glish, social studies, and science. The 120-minute session followed a  three-step structure designed to prompt reflection and redesign of asses sment practices based on student thinking data.

In Step 1 (Sharing Current Assessment Practices), participants were divided into subject-specific groups and introduced examples of assessment tasks they currently use in their classrooms. Through discussion, they clarified the skills and cognitive processes they aimed to cultivate in students and reflected on whether t heir existing test items aligned with those intentions.  In Step 2 (Examining Student Thinking through a Jig saw Activity), participants engaged in a jigsaw-style group activity using think-aloud protocols (Ericsson et al., 1993) and dialogue data from three national assessment items. These it ems were intentionally selected to highlight potential mismatches between the intentions of the item writers and the actual cognitive processes stu dents used when answering. For instance, one multiple-choice question illustrated how students could arrive at the correct answer without engaging in the intended reasoning p rocess. This activity was designed to help particip ants recognize that correct answers do not necessarily reflect the depth of understanding they aim to assess and revealed the specific test-taking strategies students employed. The goal was to generate a shared awareness of ho w assessment items shape student thinking, sometimes in unintended ways. In Step 3 (Designing Better Assessment Tasks), participants returned to their original subject groups to revise or redesign assessment tasks. They explored how test items could be modified to better elicit t he kinds of thinking and performances that aligned with their instructional goals.  By embedding Step 2 between  the two phases (Steps 1 and 3), the workshop provided a structure that encouraged participants to critically examine  their own assessment practices through the lens of student thinking. This approach allowed for an in-depth exploration of  how expos ure to students’ cognitive processes during test-taking influenced teachers’ perspectives on meaningful and aligned assessment. Throughout the workshop, worksheets served as shared tools for documentation and reflection. In Step 1, participants recorded the competencies they aimed to develop in their lessons, the method through which these would be observed, and their initial test design ideas. In Step 3, they used new worksheets to revise or refine their ideas. All group discussions were audio-recorded and transcribed for analysis. To analyze the impact of the workshop, we first examined the Step 2 dialogue data to identify whether teachers r ecognized the limitations in test formats, such as constraints on student thinki ng or misalignment with intended competencies. Next, w e compared the two sets of worksheets to trac k how test design ideas evolved. Revisions and new id eas were inductively categorized, with Step 3 dialogue data used to qualitatively validate and expand on the categories.

## Results

During Step 2, teachers across groups recognized th at question formats can shape —and sometimes constrain— students’ ability to express their thinking. They observed instances where students applied formulas mechanically without fully understanding concepts, or where time or word limits led to superficial responses. Multiple-choice formats often encouraged surface-level reasoning, wh ereas descriptive questions fostered deeper engagement. Drawing on these insights, participants revised their test designs in Step 3. Analysis of the revised worksheets and accompanying dialogue data revealed three key insights (Table 1). These two categories were anticipated and widely observed across groups. The third and most conceptually distinct category encompassed innovative ideas that  reimagined the role of assessment itself. A notable example came from the Japanese group, which proposed allowing students to bring the full text of a novel into the test —an uncommon practice compared to conventional forma ts that rely on  short excerpts. The task required students to interpret vocabulary and kanji in context, drawing on their broader understanding of the story. Teachers noted that even with access to the text, s tudents unfamiliar with the narrative would struggl e to locate meaningful information within the time limit. This example illustrates how some groups sought to  align assessment with the depth and context of students’ learning experiences, rather than focusing on isolated recall.

## Discussion

This study demonstrates that focusing on students’ thought processes during test-taking can shift teachers’ perspectives on assessment. Teachers identified discrepancies between the cognitive skills  they intended to develop and those actually elicited by conventional test formats. For instance, they noted that correct answers did not always reflect intended competencies and that formats like multiple-choice often constrained student thinking. These realizations lead to a shift in perspective—from viewing tests as isolated checkpoints to seein g them as integrated parts of the learning process. Through this reflective engagement, teachers developed assessment ideas that more closely aligned with their instructional goals and students’ cognitive processes. Some proposals, such as linking test items with prior classroom activities or encouraging open-ended interpretation, reflected principles of formative assessment. Others went even further by reimagining the fundamental role of assessment itself.  A notable example came from a group, which proposed allowing students to bring the full text of a novel into a test. While this might seem like a simple shift—from using an excerpt to the entire text—it represented a deeper shift in the underlying cognitive model: moving from isolated recall to structural interpretation o f narrative. In terms of the assessment triangle (cognition, observation,  interpretation), this proposal reflected a new ali gnment that connected assessment more directly to the depth and context of students’ learning.  These findings suggest that when teacher learning communities engage deeply with student thinking, they are able to collaboratively develop contextually grounded and pedagogically meaningful assessment practices. Future research should invest igate how these assessment designs function in practice and their impact on student learning over time.
