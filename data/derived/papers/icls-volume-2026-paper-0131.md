# Worlding With Bees: iPad Readings and AR Explorations as Multispecies Pedagogies

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how children’s encounters with bees, mediated through different digital assemblages, opened distinct possibilities for inquiry, affect, and ethical orientation. Fifty-two Grade 5 –7 students from various schools in urban British Columbia, Canada, either read informational texts and graphics about bees on iPads or used an augmented reality (AR) Bee Explorer app featuring 3D Bee models triggered by flower recognition, with both activities taking place in community garden parks as situated child– bee–technology encounters. Afterward, students responded individually in writing about their feelings toward bees and questions they still wanted to pursue. A diffractive reading of these students’ responses, informed by posthumanist concepts of learning-with and becoming-with, traced how different child–bee–technology encounters enacted divergent pedagogical openings. Responses in the iPad engagement centered on factual knowledge, exemplifying a “learning about” orientation that positioned bees as objects of study. By contrast, the AR engagement prompted ethical and ecological questions alongside affective expressions of care, joy, and responsibility. These differences point to how AR, by staging more immersive and interactive encounters, can amplify affective and ethical orientations that remain less visible in text-based engagements. The study contributes to multispecies pedagogies by illustrating how digital technologies can invite children to think, feel, and inquire with more-than-human life.

Bees are essential for the planet’s survival, yet their decline under anthropogenic pressures has become a symbol of the fragile state of the Anthropocene (Green & Ginn, 2014; Nxumalo, 2018; Weldemariam, 2020). Beyond their ecological role, bees encourage us to rethink how children might learn to live ethically and attentively with more-than-human life in damaged worlds. In educational settings, bees are often framed through a “learning about” paradigm that treats them as objects of scientific study  or curricular themes, rather than as participants in relational pedagogies (Atkinson, 2015; Nxumalo, 2018). While such approaches are useful for conveying factual knowledge, they risk narrowing children’s learning by reinforcing human exceptionalism and sustaining divisions between human life and ecological interdependencies.

Recent scholarship foregrounds the need to cultivate pedagogies of entanglement that acknowledge humans’ interdependencies and vulnerabilities alongside other species (Atkinson, 2015; Taylor & Pacini-Ketchabaw, 2015). Building on this shift, scholars have introduced posthuman frameworks that highlight becoming-with (Haraway, 2008; Weldemariam, 2020), response-ability (Barad, 2007; Haraway, 2016), and worldings (Nxumalo, 2018). These concepts collectively advocate for pedagogies that do not simply transmit information but foster ethical opportunities, emotional attunements, and situated practices of care. This study contributes to these conversations by exploring how children’s encounters with bees, mediated through digital (iPad-based reading) and augmented reality (AR) engagements, generate different possibilities for inquiry, affect, and ethical orientation. Rather than asking what children learned about bees, we inquire how their questions and expressions signal processes of thinking and learning with bees, attuning to  their vulnerabilities, imagining responsibilities, and placing humans within multispec ies common worlds. We ask: In what ways do children’s questions and expressions during digital and AR interactions reveal differences between learning about bees and learning with bees? And what possibilities for care, attunement, and responsibility towards more-than-human life are shown through children’s responses?

## Theoretical framework

Our theoretical orientation draws from posthumanist and postqualitative orientations that foreground entanglement, emergence, and relational ethics. Drawing on the common worlds framework (Atkinson, 2015; Taylor & Giugni, 2012), we understand children’s engagements with bees not as opportunities to acquire knowledge about a separate “natural” world, but as encounters that open possibilities for learning with more-thanhuman others. This orientation challenges human centric traditions of environmental education by attending to shared vulnerabilities and interdependencies that cut across species boundaries. 1139 Building on this shift, we also draw on Haraway’s (2008, 2016) notion of becoming-with and Barad’s

(2007) concept of response-ability. To become-with bees is to acknowledge that children and bees are mutually

implicated in one another’s lives, shaping possibilities for both learning and survival. Response-ability is understood here not as individual agency but as the capacity to be affected and to respond within multispecies assemblages, cultivating ethical sensitivities that emerge through entanglement rather than through mastery. Finally, we draw on Nxumalo’s (2018) theorization of worldings as pedagogical practices that story the messy, situated relations of children, educators, and bees in damaged worlds. Worldings highlight how encounters with bees can become matters of concern that unsettle anthropocentric assumptions and foreground relational, affective, and embodied forms of response.

Fifty-two Grade 5 –7 students from various schools in urban British Columbia, Canada,  participated in two different bee-encounters at community garden parks.  One group (n=26) read an eight-page iPad booklet with informational texts and graphics on bee types, ecological roles, and pollinator-friendly practices, while the other (n=26) used the AR Bee Explorer a pp, which contained the same content but added interactive 3D bee models triggered by flower recognition through the iPad camera. The AR features allowed students to touch, rotate and enlarge four types of 3D bees, hear buzzing sounds, and overlay virtual bees onto real plants. Both groups then photographed flowers or plants they thought bees might visit, though the AR group’s photos could include augmented bees superimposed on their chosen flowers. We did not treat these two bee-encounters as control and experimental groups in the conventional sense, but as distinct material –semiotic assemblages through which students, bees, technologies, and knowledge intra-acted. Following these activities, students responded to two open-ended questions: (a) their emotions toward bees (i.e., What emotions do you have about bees now that you have finished this activity?) a nd (b) something they still wanted to know (i.e., What is one thing you would like to know about bees?). These responses are the primary material of this study and read as students’ situated expressions of affective attunement and curiosity-in-the-making. We approached the research material through a diffractive reading (Barad, 2007; Mazzei, 2014), resisting coding or categorization as a process of reduction. Instead, we read the students’ responses through theoretical lenses from posthuman and common worlds scholarship (Atkinson, 2015; Nxumalo, 2018; Weldemariam, 2017), attending to how concepts and expressions came into relation with each other. This involved tracing resonances and divergences between the students’ factual, ethical, and affective expressions, while remaining attuned to how these were enacted differently across the iPad and AR encounters. Rather than treating responses as discrete data points to be measured, we approached them as material-discursive traces of relational encounters, that is, moments where children, bees, and technologies intra-acted to generate possibilities for cognition and affect. Patterns such as repeated references to “queen” or emergent terms like “protect” were not analyzed for frequency alone but taken up as diffractive intensities, signaling shifts in how students were orienting toward bees and toward their own responsibilities in more-than-human worlds. Through this analytic stance, the results; From Learning About  to Learning With  and Embodied Encounters towards Affect and Response-Abilities should be understood not as themes imposed upon the material but as provocations that emerged through the entanglement of theory, data, and researcher, generating new ways of thinking-with children’s responses.

From learning about to learning with Patterns in the students’ responses suggest that the iPad and AR encounters resulted in different possibilities for relating with bees. In the iPad-based reading activity  (Figure 1), responses focused on factual words such as “queen,” “hive,” and “how many.” For example, student asked, “I would like to know how many bees?” or “How does the queen bee lay eggs? Also, how does the beehive look like?” This orientation exemplifies what Atkinson

(2015) and Nxumalo (2018) describe as “learning about”: a mode in which  bees are positioned as objects of

inquiry, external to the learner, to be known through taxonomies and metrics. The AR encounter (Figure 2) also generated factual responses but more often opened new lines of inquiry that intertwined  curiosity with ethics and ecology. Terms such as “help,” “protect,” and “extinct” appeared frequently in AR students’ reflections but were almost absent among those who read the iPad booklet. Students posed more abstract and ethically entangled questions, often imagining bees as sentient beings with social lives: “Do bees have names for each other?” “Are they aware of the impact they have on the ecosystem?” Others posed action-oriented questions, such as “What can I do to help their population?” or “I feel a mixture of emotions… happy because there are lots of bees… sad because in some places, lots of bees may be dying… reassured knowing 1140 that there are actions taken to protect bees.” These reflections signal a shift  from knowledge acquisition toward responsibility and participation in shared futures, where children not only learn  about bees but also begin to imagine what it means to live with and care for them.

The students who took part in the AR activities more frequently described bees in relation to protection and positive affect, with terms such as “protect” and “happy” appearing in their statements. One student reflected: “ I feel grateful that we live on a planet filled with bees, and I hope that we help bees by planting more flowers and not use pesticides,” while another said, “Bees are part of our everyday life, so if we don't have bees then the whole ecosystem will die. So we need bees and we all need bees. I have a mixed feeling! Happy and interested.”  These 1141 expressions illustrate what Weldemariam (2020) describes as response-abilities: affective openings in which students oriented toward care, attunement, and responsibility with more-than-human life. The iPad reading engagement included more neutral or cautious orientations, with  students describing bees in hesitant terms.  For instance, one student wrote, “Bees are interesting and important to nature, but I still feel cautious around them.” while another noted, “Bees are very cool insects but sometime aggressive. ” Another student recognized bees’ ecological value while expressing fear: “I think they are very important because bee help plants to grow better and faster. Sometime, when bees get too close t o me, I will get scare.” These expressions highlight how affect in the iPad reading engagement tended to settle at interest or risk, rather than cultivating relational care.

In this sense, the AR bee assemblage did not simply convey content differently; it reconfigured how the students could think– feel–inquire with bees. The movement from factual curiosity to ethical and relational questioning illustrates an emergent pedagogy of becoming-with, where students’ imaginations were mobilized toward ecological entanglement, vulnerability, and multispecies flourishing. The differences between the iPad-based reading and AR engagements can be understood not simply as contrasts between instructional modalities, but as enactments of distinct pedagogical and material–semiotic assemblages. In the iPad-based reading condition, students’ questions gravitated toward factual knowledge, such as, “How are queen bees produced?” and “How many flowers does a bee pollinate in one day?” These queries positioned bees as objects of epistemic extraction, where knowledge is gathered about them but rarely with them. This mode of inquiry reflects what Atkinson (2015) refers to as a “learning about” orientation, where nonhuman life is positioned at a distance, as an entity to be known, catalogued, or mastered. By contrast, the AR Bee Explorer appeared not merely to present additional information but constituted an intra-active site where students, technologies, bees, and flowers co-composed possibilities for thought. The questions emerging from this assemblage, including “What can I do to help the bee population?” and “Are they going extinct?” signal a shift toward what Haraway (2016) terms becoming-with: practices of curiosity and concern that recognize interdependency and shared vulnerability. In this sense, the AR encounter cultivated not learning outcomes in the conventional sense, but affective attunements and ethical openings, moments where students were called into response-ability (Barad, 2007) with more-than-human others. The emergent orientations in our study resonate with Nxumalo’s (2018) notion of worlding pedagogies, where children’s encounters with dying bees in preschool settings became openings to embodied care, attunement, and situated ethical response. Like those moments of children building shelters or offering sugar water to sluggish bees, the AR encounters in our study invited students into practices of noticing, questioning, and caring-with. We suggest that the immersive and embodied qualities of AR, including its capacity to render bees as more-thantextual presences, helped catalyze these relational openings, foregrounding children’s affective attunements and ethical imaginaries. These responses can be read as traces of relational becomings, where children, bees, and digital-material worlds entangle to generate new inquiries, new sensibilities, and new possibilities for living well together.

In this study, we have traced how students’ engagements with bees, mediated through iPad-based reading and augmented reality exploration, enacted different possibilities for inquiry, affect, and responsibility. While the iPad encounters focused on factual curiosity and positioned bees as objects of knowledge, the AR encounters opened relational and ethical orientations that invited children to imagine how they might live and act with bees in shared, precarious worlds. These moments illustrate not definitive  outcomes, but diffractive traces of how technologies, children, and bees come together to generate distinct pedagogical openings. AR experience invited students to become-with and feel response-ability towards bees, leading to noticing, questioning, and caring-with. The significance of these findings lies not in privileging one encounter over the other, but in recognizing how different assemblages are. Digital texts, AR apps, children’s bodies, bee imaginaries can call forth divergent modes of learning. Such multiplicity underscores the importance of approaching pedagogy as a matter of cultivating spaces where becoming-with more-than-human others is possible, where response-abilities may be activated, and where ecological imagination can flourish. In attending to the students’ responses, questions and affective expressions, this study contributes to broader conversations in education that seek to move beyond human centric frameworks of learning “about” the natural world.
