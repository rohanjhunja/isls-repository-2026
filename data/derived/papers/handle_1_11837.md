# Meaningful Automated Feedback? Balancing Tensions in Participatory Learning Analytics Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Simulators have become central in professional training, in this case maritime education, where they support the development and assessment of skills and competencies required of competent seafarers. This study aims to inform the design of a learning analytics dashboard utilizing multimodal learning analytics to deliver automated, adaptive feedback to maritime students. Feedback is critical in simulator-based training, yet the characteristics of meaningful automated feedback in professional training remain underexplored. Drawing on a participatory design workshop with maritime students, the study examines tensions arising from the conceptualization of automated feedback. Using cultural historical activity theory, we frame tensions as opportunities for innovation, highlighting the dynamic interplay between students, instructors, and technology. In doing so, we offer insights into designing meaningful automated feedback for simulation training, and the need to balance students’ dual roles as learners and future professionals, reconciling collaborative learning with personalized feedback, and prioritizing educational goals over technological innovation.

## Introduction

In professional educational settings, simulators have become an essential part of training to become a professional (Lehtinen, 2023). In maritime education, previous studies have shown how simulators are frequ ently used both for training and assessment of professional competence, and that simulator-based training holds a strong position within the industry (Wiig et al., 2023). Previous studies have also shown t he role and importance of instructors with professional experience to provide feedback to students during and after training, confirming the importance of feedback in the learning  process (Abid et al., 2024). Despite this, what constitutes meaningful feedback in simulation-based training remains an open question. According to Hattie and Timperley (2007, p. 81), feedback is defined as “[…] information provided by an agent […] regarding  aspects of one’s performance or understanding”. They specify that while it is a powerful tool that greatly influences learning, it cando so in a positive or negative way, depending on how it is co nstructed and presented. Initial efforts to develop automated and adaptive feedback systems for desktop simulator s reveal the complexity of designing educational to ols that create engaging and effective learning experiences in maritime education and training (Gyldensten et al., 2023). Their findings, based on interviews with students i n maritime education, stress that such systems must  a lign feedback with students' prior knowledge to provide appropriate scaffolding during training. The overall aim of this study is to inform the desi gn of a learning analytics dashboard (LAD) that uses multimodal learning analytics (MMLA) to provide automated and  adaptive feedback to students engaged in simulation-based maritime training. Drawing on a video recorded participatory design wo rkshop with maritime students, we explore the design tensions that arise when students engage with the conceptual  idea of automated feedback and their creative process of sketching to visualiz e design ide as (Sarmiento & Wise, 2022). For the design of the LAD, it is important to find a balance between what should be and what is feasible or realistic within the context (Tatar, 2007). While users may express preference for certain features or functionality, effective design of educational technology must focus on what best facilitates and supports learning, rather than primarily on what users express as desirable. In focus for this study is exploring tensions between three aspects central to this specific design context: (1) students as students vs students as future professi onals, (2) collaborative learning vs personalized feedback (3) focus on learning and learners vs technical innovation and development. In exploring these tensions, we draw upon Engeström’s (1987) work on cultural historical activity theory (CHAT) and expansive learning, recognizing the role resolv ing tensions plays in driving innovation and development. As we discuss the study, we focus  primarily on tertiary tensions, i.e. the kinds of tensions and contradictions that emerge when a new educational technology enters into practice. If the new technology reflects an advanced or idealized way of doing things, it might clash with the existing or historically de veloped activity system (Engeström, 1987). In this study, tertiary tensions emerge on issues related to how feedback is delivered following simulation training currently, and as imagined after the LAD is implemented in maritime education. This theoretical lens allows us to highlight how students’ engagement with these tensions during the d esign workshop, reflecting broader systemic challenges and opportunities withi n maritime training  as well as the dynamic, collective, and innovative nature of learn ing in such a professional context. The participatory nature of the study underscores the dynamic and collective pr ocess of co-design with users, where students’ perspectives, professional goals, and creative contributions serv e as valuable resources for envisioning and iterati ng the LAD (Tessier, 2022).

Prior research Feedback practices in maritime education Simulation-based training offers maritime students a work-relevant setting for experiential learning of the professional skills and competencies required of professional seafarers (Wiig et al., 2023). This training combines with theory-based classroom lectures and cadetships at sea to f orm an educational program intended to graduate students as competent mariners, capable of operatin g successfully as part of a bridge team onboard a s hip. As students work as teams on bridges designed to closely replicate those found on board real ships, instructors observe student interaction with the various instruments on  the bridge, communication within the team, and stu dent performance of carefully designed simulated scenarios (Harrington, 2024). Sellberg and Sharma (2024) highlight the importance of the active role played by experie nced instructors in simulation-based training. Their study emphasizes real-time assessment by instructors, who provide feedbac k to endorse or correct student behavior as appropriate. These feedback practices draw on a variety of social and material resources within a semi-automated simulator environment, where students interact with  both analog artefacts and digital instruments duri ng collaborative activities. Additional ly, feedback in the full-mission simulator and after the simulation, in the socalled debriefing, often resembles demonstrations of professional conduct and reasoning, showing students how to think, act, and perform in accordance with the s tandards of a professional seafarer  (Sellberg et al., 2021; Sellberg & Sharma, 2024). Given the multifaceted and multimodal nature of feedback in professional lea rning environments, the automation of feedback in collabo rative learning in this setting requires a range of d ifferent sensors and technologies to capture feedback practi ces on the simulated bridge, suggesting MMLA as an appropriate approach to automate feedback in this context. Multimodal learning analytics and the automation of feedback MMLA draws upon tools such as body sensors  and eye-tracking devices to measure posture, gestures, facial expressions and emotional or affective states to gain insight into the learning process (Spikol et al., 2017). Where collaborative learning is in focus, such as in the context of this study, the se insights are intended to shed light on those aspects of communication and interaction within team training that cannot be captured through system logs alone (Schneider et al., 2021). This approach aligns with the broader goals of learning analytics (LA), which seeks to measure, collect, analyze, and report data about  learners and their contexts to optimize educationa l practices and environments (Ifenthaler & Yau, 2020). While LA encompasses a wide range of methods and appl ications (López-Pernas et al., 2024), MMLA distinguishes itself by integrating multimod al datasets to increase our understanding of how learning unfolds in computer supported collaborative learning environments. However, before this data can be used to improve learning – the ultimate purpose of LA – it is necessary to understand who will use the data produced, and how and why it will be used. That is, LA design is not simply about what data can be obtained, and which methods can be used in obtaining it, but in how users interact with the learning environment, and learn from these interactions (cf. Buckingham Shum et al., 2019). Figure 1 Feedback to Students during a Navigation Scenario on a Simulated Bridge When the intention is to use a LAD to provide automated feedback, as in our context, care must be taken to ensure that the feedback presented to students is conduciv e to learning  (Tepgec et al., 2024). Rather than simply presenting feedback to students, effective feedback involves a process that students must actively engage and participate in (Boud & Molloy, 2013). Just as effective feedback requires feedback literacy on the part of both the students receiving the feedback  (Carless & Boud, 2018)  and the teachers providing it (Boud & Dawson, 2023), effective automated feedback must be designed in a way that promotes agency and action from learners (Buckingham Shum et al., 2023).

## Method: Participatory design and expansive learning

Empirical data for this study was collected during a two-hour in-person participatory co-design workshop (Sarmiento & Wise, 2022) held at a Swedish universi ty. The workshop was facilitated by three researchers, and attended by six maritime students regularly engaging in simulator-based training and subsequently applying their learning to cadetships onboard real vessels. Although the sample size was small, it was diverse in terms of demographics. Of the six participants, there were four females and two males, with an average age of 30 (range: 24-52). The group included different cultura l, educational, and professional  backgrounds. Three of the six participants disclosed or indicated some form of neurodivergence during the workshop, although this information was not deliberately sought.

The workshop comprised two parts – firstly, students were asked to consider the feedba ck they receive during simulation training, what they find meaningf ul, and how they think feedback could be better del ivered. Secondly, students were introduced to the concept of MMLA and the potential use of eye-tracking software, body sensors, and speech recognition during simulation (Sellberg & Sharma, 2024). Emphasis was placed on using data from these sources and the simulator itself to provide automated feedback. Fol lowing these discussions, participants were asked to sketch their ideal dashboard presenting this data and feedback. Each participant verbally explained the components of their sketch, and their  reasoning behind decisions made while sketching. The workshop was video-and audio-recorded using a dedicated portable video camera, w ith each participant and researcher wearing an individual microphone. Audio data from all sources was transcribed using OpenAI Whisper before being manually checked for accuracy by t wo researchers. Video data was used only when necessary for clarification. The resulting transcripts were analyzed manually by  two members of the research team, both individually and collaboratively. Four researchers engaged in discussing and finetuni ng the identified themes during the analysis process, with all five research ers later collaborating in a workshop where points of interest were discussed and narrowed down to key findings, p resented here as tensions on three levels (see Table 1). The research team also engaged in analysis of sketches produced b y each of the participants, to further explore thei r expectations of the proposed system. To protect the  confidentiality of participants, speakers are referred to as P1

– P6 where quotes are used to illustrate points made during the workshop.

Our study draws on Engestr öm’s (1987) theory of tensions as an analytic approach to exami ne the dynamics within the design workshop. By identifying  and analyzing the contradictions inherent in the workshop activities, we aim to understand how these tensions drive innovation and shape the development of new practices. Engeström views tensions as contradictions that drive movemen t and change within activity systems. These contradictions are not merely problems to be solved; they represent fundamental conflicts or tensions intrinsic to the structure and historical development of activities.

## Results: Exploring tensions

In this results section, we use anonymized quotes from student participants to explore the expectations expressed during the workshop in contrast with current approaches to providing feedback to maritime studen ts during and after simulation, and how th ese compare to what is known about the factors contributing to effective and meaningful feedback (cf. Ramani et al., 2019). Bearing in mind the context in which the proposed LAD is intended for use, three of the practice points for meaningful feedbac k posited by  Ramani et al.  (2019) were deemed particularly applicable: (1) feedback is only effective where it enables self-assessment and prompts learner growth, (2) top-down feedback is not meaningful, rather feedback should involve a two-way reflective conversation, and (3) acceptance of feedback relies upon credibility (Ramani et al., 2019, p. 1342). Each of these three points are reflected in the current model of feedback used in maritime simulator training, and must be at least maintained, if not improved, by any automatio n of feedback. As we outline the three tensions identified during analysis of the workshop transcripts and stu dent sketches  (see Table 1), we detail how the wants and expectations expressed by the participants must be balanced with  pedagogical concerns during the LAD design process.

Table 1 Tensions in the Design of Effective Automated Feedback Students as students, focusing on the here and now Students as future professionals, focusing on the then and there Engaging in collaborative reflection Student wish for personalized feedback Focus on learning and learners Focus on technical innovation and development Tension 1: Students as students vs students as future professionals The first tension arising from our analysis of the workshop data was that between the two roles enacted by students during maritime simulator training  – that of students, and that of future professionals.  As students, they tend to seek a ‘perfect’ solution or an ‘ideal’ way to complete a simulated scen ario, focusing on achieving strong performance in the educational activity here and now. As future professionals, they recognize that they are being trained to become competent seafarers, and that this involves being socialized in the norms and behaviors expected on board a ship.  During simulations, students take on the roles of a bridge team, communicating and acting as they would while working within the team, rather than as a group of students in a simulated environment. Speaking of the immersive nature of simulation training, students express how “I become the captain […] I’m not afraid to give orders […] and it will be more my boat” (P2).  In recounting how they “become” the captain, this student demonstrates their positioning as a future professi onal, practicing taking  on a professional role. However, as discussion unfolded, it became evident that the need to perform well as a student also exists. As discussion moved to the use of MMLA devices to inform the LAD, students were keen on ‘hard data’ and the perceived objectivity of simulator logs, video and audio recordings of simulations, and eye-tracking heat maps and gaze patterns. Claiming this data would be beneficial for their learning, students expressed a belief that this ‘objective’ data would be easier to accept tha n more subjective, discussion-based feedback from instructors. In other words, “this is like hard numbers” resulting in “feedback that you can’t feel that bad about because that is just the reality”  (P2). Later in this discussion around presenting data on the LAD, it became evident that providing such data may pull students from their ro le as future seafarers, back to being a student foc used on rote learning of how to perform a specific simulated task ‘perfectly’. Mentioning a student known to be a high achiever, referred to as a “ golden egg student ”, P4 says “where is she looking at? Even if she’s really mad, where is she looking? Because obviously she’s doing it right ”, as an explanation for wanting access to eye-tracking data from strong students.

This tendency to want a perfect example to replicat e, thus learning how to perform a specific task wel l rather than understanding why an experienced seafar er responds to an event in a certain way, is in dir ect conflict with the ethos of maritime education and training (cf. Sellberg et al. 2021). The simulated scenarios on which the students are trained are carefully designed by inst ructors to mimic professional situations encountered at sea, while also aligning with specific educational objec tives (Harrington, 2024). The scenarios require students to combine the skills and knowledge acquired during th eir education to date, not only to complete the sce nario correctly, but also to further embed professional skills such as communication and teamwork. While it is understandable that, as students, they want example s of how they should complete a task, there is a risk that providing such automated feedback may hinder their development as future professionals, a s they follow what they see as objectively correct, rather than engaging in  reflexive practice as intended. The tasks presente d to students during simulation are not intended for the m to learn how to handle a specific situation, but rather to develop into reflective practitioners so that they can handle future situations, both simulated and at sea. That is, the simulation is designed to contribute to develop ing the future professional, but students tend to f ocus on their immediate performance in the present moment.

Tension 2: Collaborative learning vs personalized feedback In this setting, where students train through collaborative simulation tasks, much of the  feedback is received verbally from instructors in the post-simulation debriefing. Feedback comprises both general feedback applicable to the group as whole, and specific feedback on one (or more) bridge team’s handling of the task. Currently, postvs simulation debriefing is structured in a way where instructors present segments of video and audio rec ordings from one bridge, highlighting instances for student s to reflect upon and learn from  (Sellberg et al. 2021). As the collaborative nature of simulation means that the a ctions of one bridge team can impact on how the oth ers experience the scenario, these actions are deemed r elevant to all students. The specific individual performance feedback is therefore used as a strategy to give mo re general collective feedback (cf. Lindwall et al., 2022). Instructors deliver the feedback in this way in order to instruct the students in how to develop their required skills, providing formative feedback intended to help students progress. However, while students appear to recogni ze the value of this group feedback, some also state  that it is only helpful if it applies to them personally – “[…] usually gives […] a general sort of feedback th at applies to everyone in the group, but that might mean not actu ally learning anything yourself because it doesn’t apply to your specific problems ” (P1). According to the participants, general feed back should be followed by specific individual feedback to ensure that relevant students are aware that the feedback being shared applies to them, and not just the student(s) whose snippets are used to prompt discussion. In saying this, the students rec ognize that, while they may know that they did something wrong, they may not be able to learn from it. To optimize learning, students do require collaborative reflection, with instructors, peers, or automated feedback,  on the simulated situation, helping them to engage in critical reflection and see what could have been done differently. This reflects Hattie and Timperley’s (2007) assertion that studen ts have a tendency towards bias and selection when considering feedback, which may hinder the possibil ity of accepting that general feedback is dire cted at, and useful to, them.

In order to be effective, feedback should be direct ed to “ the effort, self-regulation, engagement, or processes related to the task and its performance ” (Hattie & Timperley, 2007, p. 96). Feedback provide d by instructors during debriefing is tailored to the learning objective underlying the scenario being simulated, focusing on examples of common errors made by students, as well as examples of particularly good performance (Sellberg et al., 2021). This feedback takes the form of a di alogue, rather than unidirectional, top-down feedback from the instructor to the students. While this is reflectiv e of good pedagogical practice, students do not alw ays recognize this, perhaps understandably. During the workshop, students mentioned the need for instructors to “actually give details” (P4) regarding their performance, both to solidify knowledge of what they do well, and to highlight where to focus for development. Students tended to see th is specific feedback as an essential feature of the  proposed LAD, reporting it as lacking in current debriefing practices. Despite the reflective conversations held during postsimulation debriefing, students repeatedly restated a desire for detailed and constructive feedback. This indicates that these students may not differentiate between formative and summative assessment, and do not necessarily recognize instruction as being a form of feedback. From their perspective, feedback relates to formal assessment, involving a grade and/or individual guidance on specific elements of performance. However, that is not to say that the students do not recogni ze the value in the debriefing practices. Students report being comfortable with their mistakes being discussed collectively. As P4 says, “[…] a lot of the things they can give us as a constructive criticism doesn't really matter if they give us individually or in group because everyone else will […] already see what it is so the only thing that's different is th ey will know who did it”, indicating an understanding of the value of learning from others’ errors. P2 likens the slight embarrassment associated with receiving feedback in this way to “having something between your teeth”.

When receiving collective feedback, group size, gro up relationship, and individual characteristics influence the students’ perception of feedback. The  smaller group size of approximately ten students, which is used in full bridge simulation training, provi des a more intimate and informal feeling, which mak es “handling feedback and discussing it much easier as opposed t o the desktop simulator simulations, which we are u sually like 20 [students] or something” (P1). Students also linked smaller group sizes to more feedback, due to a higher chance of being put in the “hot seat” and receiving  individual feedback. This perception supports the idea that students tend to consider only individual, specific feedback as being feedback. Within the smaller student groups, relationships with peers and instructors foster a relaxed and informal environment to receive feedback. As students stay i n the same groups over the years, they develop “relationship[s] which allows you to laugh about th e things and discuss it in a more friendly, more in formal manner” (P1). One student exemplifies the friendly informal environment by saying: “So like, last week, me and [student name] had a simulator together and one of the ships did completely wrong and the teacher were like this is what not to do and all of us laughed and even th ey laughed and like yeah ‘we should have thought ab out this but we did not think about that in the moment ” (P5). The friendly relationship fosters an open, re flective environment where individual mistakes can be collectively described, analyzed, and reflected upon. Tension 3: Learning and learners vs technical innovation and development The third tension which we will outline here is tha t between learning and technology, or the tendency within learning analytics design to focus on what can be done with available technology and data, rather than what would be most relevant to measure (Ifenthaler et al., 2021). This particular tension exists between the students, who want to be presented with ‘objective’ data, the instruct ors, whose teaching practices aim t o train students to become competent seafarers, and technology designers, who want to have a LAD design (cf. Sellberg & Sharma, 2024). This is illustrated well by the students’ desire for eye-tracking data, for example. Students believe that eyetracking data from strong students would help them to learn – “even if she’s really mad […] obviously she’s doing it right” (P4). There are several issues with this statement, primarily the conflict between being a student who wants to get good grades right now, thus seeking re assurance that their action s are correct, and being a future professional training to act appropriately in a complex, high-risk industry. Even if we assume that the eye-tracking data was indeed collected during a perfect performance, how does this relate to learning? It may be interesting to view this data, but we hav e no way of making sense of it without a relevant baseline or criteria to measure against. That is, the data may be ‘hard’, but its status as a relevant indicator of competent performance is weak. The students participating in the workshop acknowledge this during their discussion on having the strong student’s data presented on the LAD, as they highlight the contrast between external appearances and internal experiences. One student is particularly critical of measures of  stress, asking how stress is measured, and what is meant by stress  in this context. Recounting a simulator session wh ere the instructor showed video footage of a strong student and remarked on how calm and in control the student was, P2 said “no, she’s furious. I can see that in her ”, going on to explain how this had been confirmed by the strong student themselves in a later conversation. P2 continued “the thing is, with her driving and her performance, you can’t tell. She’s doing everything perfectly […] she’s the golden rule on how to be a good seaman”. Perhaps more appropriate is the use of data to comp are and track one’s own performance over time. When asked how they thought that MMLA data could be used to support their learning, students expressed interest in comparing their own data from various years of s tudy to see how their behavior had changed over time, and depending on the type of vessel. For example, students were interested in viewing a range of eye-tracking recordings from different periods of time during th eir studies, to see how their use of  the various instruments on the bridge changed as they gained experience. Stude nts’ wishes in this regard ranged from having a sel ection of eye-tracking or movement data from previous simulations so they could perform a simple comparison at a glance, to having very detailed, in-depth recordings of simulator sessions, where navig ation, video, sound, and MMLA data were integrated in order to provide context on exactly what was happening when the student took a specific action. Students demonstrated an awareness of the importance of using relevant data, pointing out that comparing first-year data to fourth-year data would not be useful due to the inevitable gap. During the workshop, students also acknowledged som e limitations with the data they were requesting, such as compliance with GDPR, and the potential for  overload should too much data be provided. P6 questioned the usefulness of voice recordings for bridge commu nication “since there’s a lot of bullshit talking”. This comment prompted a discussion amongst all students present, where P2 expressed concern about communication being reduced when students know they’re being reco rded, although this was not of great concern to all students given that simulation is already recorded. Again, perception plays a role here, as students differentiate between a simulator recording only seen by students and instr uctors, and a recording of the same content that is  presented on an LAD.

## Discussion: Balancing tensions to drive innovation

In the results section of this paper, we have outli ned the students’ perspectives on feedback, what it  is, and what it should include. These findings will be used to i nform the next stages of the LAD development proces s, where we must balance the uncovered tensions between different expectations from the LAD. Additionally, the findings related to tensions appear to us to merit standalone discussion. User perspectives are an important element of designing useful and usable technology, but this does not mean trying to create a LAD that incorporates everything that students say they want from it. The same is also true when considering teacher perspectives on what the LAD should include – both viewpoints are valuable, but neither is a balanced view. So, how do the student perspectives outlined in this paper inform the design of the LAD?

Firstly, note that in keeping with Engeström’s work, we do not see tensions as inherently negative, bu t rather as opportunities for innovation and developm ent through highlighting tensions between current and future educational practices (Karanasios et al., 2017). Negotiating such tensions involves n ot only developing technological designs but also reimagining how future educational practices can be structured to align wi th the goals of simulation-based training (Muukkonen & Kajamaa, 2024). While we have focused our discussion mostly on tertiary tensions relating to feedback in simulation training, we acknowledge the existence and potential impact of other forms of tension in this context. Although  in-depth exploration is not possible within the limita tions of this paper, quaternary tensions relating to the impact of an LAD and automated feedback on the overall educational program beyond simulation training exists. We view these tensions, on all levels, as being an essential component of the design process, and having potential to incr ease the impact of the outcomes from a participator y design process. In addition to considering the perspective  of each user group, we must also consider the hist orical and structural elements, such as power imbalance between teacher and student, the culture present within the maritime industry, and policy and regulatory requirements of  the educational program. As the pr oject progresses, each of these will be given due consideration, informing the iterative process of designing the LAD. The workshop revealed some interesting points around what students consider to be feedback. Although learning in an environment where reflection is key, there is a tendency amongst students not to consider reflective discussions during debriefing to be feedback. Although Ramani et al. (2019) emphasize that meaningful feedback must involve dialogue, students do not appear to recognize this as being feedback. Students seek concrete answers from instructors on what is correct and what is les s so, expressing a wish for specific instructions o n what to do better. Combined with discussions around wanting a “golden egg student” whose actions and behaviors could be copied for perfect performance, this raises concern s around the risk of a LAD hindering rather than en hancing student learning. When designing the feedback element of the LAD, discouraging learning by rote and replication of ‘perfect’ performance is of paramount importance. As this exact scenario is unlikely to arise when students are at sea, the aim of th eir education is to equip them with a range of know ledge, skills, and competencies that they can call upon when required.

Balancing this tension seems likely to lead to the uncovering  of others, for example, the intention of instructors while preparing and delivering feedback  vs the message received by students. In other word s, the feedback literacy of both teachers and students mus t be considered during the design process, with act ive steps taken to increase it. For example, students at the workshop expressed great interest i n this “golden egg student” who personified good performance, however, this may  stem from how these examples are presented in class. In both face-to-face and automated feedback, care must be taken to ensure that the context of the feedback is clear, e.g., examples being intended as an illustration of one way of correctly approaching a task, rather than a blueprint. The intentions behind, and conversations around, th e use of examples such as this are beyond the scope  of this study, but serve as a reminder of the complexity of effective feedback. As we consider the potential use of MMLA devices in gathering data for the LAD, students’ tendency to focus on current performance rather than future pro fessional practice becomes evident. Students perceive simulator log data, eye-tracking gaze patterns and heat maps, and movement data from body sensors as being objective, whereas feedback from instructors is subjective and seen as less useful. This again raises concerns about the potential negative impact of providing students with ‘hard data’ which they are perhaps unlikely to be able to interpret effectively. The strength of current mari time simulator training lies in the professional experie nce and expertise of the instructors, who candraw upon their professional intuition when monitoring and assessing student performance. Traditionally, training for a career a t sea took the format of an ap prenticeship on board a ship, and the current formal university-based education has been designed to recreate this,  allowing students to learn both explicitly and tacitly from experts. Thus, providin g automated feedback which presents data for inexpe rienced students to interpret risks hampering their ability to train as competent seafarers. To mitigate this risk, we recommend that the design process for an LAD intended to provide automated feedback give consideration to solutions prompting and facilitating students’ reflection. Introducing such a technology to simulation training offers an opportunity to enhance current practice not only through ensuring that the technology encompasses the strengths of skilled  instructors, but also through using the design pro cess as a means for combining student and instructor perspectives in a solution to common issues, such as feedback literacy. In this way, the LAD has the potential to change current behavior for th e better. As we continue to progress this study, a participatory approach to design will be m aintained, with next steps including usability test ing of paper prototypes with students, and presentation of the r esulting prototypes to the software developers. As a result of the findings which we have discussed in this paper, we also propose the following additions to the design process, for the purpose of providing meaningful automated feedback via the LAD; (1) data storytelling, and (2) reflective prompts.

Data storytelling allows presenting data in a narra tive format, such as in a timeline-based scenario (Martinez-Maldonado et al., 2020)  or a written summary report based on generative art ificial intelligence (Fernandez Nieto et al., 2022). Adding such elements to the LAD helps students in terpret their data more accurately, avoiding misinterpretation.  Reflection prompts can then be added to further pro mote students’ reflection. For instance, Wise et al. (2013) added a shared reflection journal that included reflective questions for students and teachers in their LAD design to reflec t on their data interpretations. By adding such ref lection prompts, students are actively encouraged to apply the data to their learning activities, beyond simpl y understanding whether they are doing something right or wrong.
