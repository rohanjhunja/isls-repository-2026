# Empowering Undergraduate Computer Science Majors Through AI Literacy Research

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The rise of artificial intelligence (AI) in post-secondary education has prompted educational researchers to develop definitions of undergraduate AI literacy. However, undergraduate AI literacy in practice is unclear and overly theoretical. This multiple case study analysis centers the perspectives of five undergraduate students as they learned about and defined AI literacy during an intensive 8-week research course. Creating and enacting their own unique AI literacy research projects, the undergraduate students interrogated existing AI literacy literature, analyzed undergraduate AI literacy survey data, and conducted focus groups with undergraduate students to interrogate the relationship between AI and undergraduate education. Through the research process, the undergraduates constantly negotiated their understanding of AI literacy and developed research-based conclusions that merged theory with practice. We describe the implications of these findings on undergraduate computer science education, highlighting the role of social science research in computing education.

## Introduction

The rapid rise of AI is challenging traditional per spectives of post-secondary education, changing how undergraduate students learn and work (Kamalov et a l., 2023). The nascent and overwhelming presence of  AI in post-secondary education has led to a plethora of definitions of AI literacy, with most emphasizing student ability to critically evaluate AI technologies, communicate  and collaborate effectively with AI, and use AI as  an online tool in both personal and professional/academic set tings (Long & Magerko, 2020). Although important fo r understanding how students use and apply AI tools i n academic settings, AI literacy research tends to be highly theoretical and experimental in nature, focusing mo re on assessments of AI knowledge and skills rather  than the everyday lived experiences of students. More resear ch is needed to bridge AI literacy research and pra ctice, especially when undergraduate students actively use  free generative AI (genAI) systems like ChatGPT to  learn and complete coursework (Mohan, 2023). Computer science students in particular are likely to engage with these tools due to strong alignment with their coursework and career interests (Petrescu et al., 2022). One way to connect research with practice is to seek out bottom-up perspectives on AI literacy by talking directly with undergraduate students about whether existing definitions of AI literacy align with their experiences using AI. This multiple case study explores how undergraduate students majoring in computer science think about and use AI, specifically when enacting personal research projects related to AI literacy. We investigate the unique perspectives these undergraduates develop through s imultaneously enriching their understanding of a to pic and conducting research on that topic, aiming to answer  the following research question:  How does engaging in AI literacy research from a social science perspective influence how undergraduate computer science students define AI literacy relative to their own experiences?

## Literature review

AI has become a hot topic in education for a myriad  of reasons, either being heralded as a benefit or harm to student learning (Kamalov et al., 2023; Su et al., 2023). In some contexts, AI system s like ChatGPT and Google Gemini serve as tutors for students struggling to learn specific educational content (Extance, 2023). In other cases, students have been observed to rely too heavily on AI for knowledge processing (Bailey, 2023; Zhai et al., 2021). Therefore, AI can support or diminish student learning and development depending on how it is used. Rather than completely removing AI tools from the learning envi ronment, researchers argue that educators could foc us on developing students’ AI literacies (Extance, 2023).  To do so, educators and students must understand w hat AI literacy looks like in practice.

Definitions of undergraduate AI literacy Due to the quick rise of AI in education, concrete definitions of AI literacy are still developing (Southworth et al., 2023). These definitions lean more toward theoretic al constructs based on existing AI literature and/o r quasiexperimental data (Carolus et al., 2023; Long & Magerko, 2020). Long and Mager ko’s (2020) AI literacy definition is a widely used definition; they define AI literacy as a set of competencies that generally include being able to define AI, understand what AI cando, under stand the underlying mechanisms of how AI operates (i.e., machine learning processes, inputs and outputs, etc.), identify the ethical aspects of AI and how its design increases or diminishes existing biases, understand how people use and perceive AI, distinguish between how AI is used in the media versus in real life, and understand who has access to AI. Some researchers and practitioners are designing in terdisciplinary AI literacy curricula (Southworth e t al., 2023) and AI literacy assessments (Carolus et al., 2023; Ng et al., 202 4). However, these curricula and tools vary in how they define and measure AI literacy (Carolus et al., 2023). They also heavily rely on theoretical, topdown constructs of AI literacy. Although existing d efinitions of AI literacy are important and necessa ry, they do not always consider student agency. At the undergraduate level, students begin to identi fy study practices that work for them (Cottrell, 2024) and integrate AI int o these practices. These experiences allow them to develop valuable experiential knowledge (Kritt & Budwig, 20 22) that many studies may overlook, introducing pot ential disparities between theory and practice. By including undergraduate perspectives, theoretical definitions of AI literacy can become more practical to the populations that they define (Černý, 2023).

## Leveraging social science perspectives for AI literacy development

Unfortunately, computer science majors often confro nt a disconnect between the content that they learn  and its impact on society (Kallia & Cutts, 2021). As today’s current AI users and potential AI developers (Petrescu et al., 2022), it is critical that computer science students have multiple opportunities to develop their AI literacies beyond the technical. To bridge the technical and social aspects of the computer science major, educators could introduce undergraduates to social science theories and metho dologies, thereby intentionally positioning student s to understand computing topics relative to the lived experiences of individuals and communities (Lorey et al., 2022). To understand how under graduate computer science research opportunities th at integrate social science theories and methodologies impact undergraduate AI literacy development, our study follows five undergraduate computer science majors as they develop AI literacy research questions and conduct mixed-methods social science research projects. We explore how the students interpret AI literacy definitions relative to their peers’ experiences.

## Theoretical framework

Our study use d transformative learning theory (Kitchenham, 2008; Mezirow, 1978) to describe the complex, multifaceted, and critical reflective process that the students undergo when understanding AI literacy through the implementation of social science research practices. There are four distinct elements of transformativ e learning:

1) elaborating existing frames of reference, 2) lea rning new frames of reference, 3) transforming habi ts of mind,

and 4) transforming points of view. Our study applies Mezirow’s transformative learning theory by first trying to understand the undergraduates’ prior knowledge of AI literacy (element 1), the process they undergo to integrate new ideas or AI literacy theories into their existing frames of reference, their application of their modified frames of references to develop and conduct research studies to answer a question about AI literacy, and the conclusions they draw from their research experience to define AI literacy.

## Methods

This multiple case study analysis (Stake, 2013) exp lores how an eight-week intensive summer research course shapes undergraduate perceptions of AI and their peers’ AI use. The course took place in Summer 2024 at a large public university in the Mid-Atlantic region. Students applied to participate in the summer internship and indicated their placement preference by choosing between social science, technology, and natural science research options. This paper examines a technology-focused stream that teaches students how to clean a nd analyze data in R, develop social science research questions related to computing topics, and implement their research projects. Course structure The central goal of the course was to prepare under graduate students to use social science theories and methodologies to answer AI literacy research questions. The instructor focused on AI literacy because of the rapid growth of AI in society, particularly  in higher education (Rosenbaum, 2024). Course conte nt focused on AI literacy theories, social science research practice s, data analytics in R, and research report writing (Table 1). Throughout the summer, students were required to wo rk in the instructor’s lab space for 10-15 hours per week. At the end of the course, students presented their research to the broader university community. ## Data analysis

Following Stake’s (2013) definition of a case study, we c ategorically aggregated the data by identifying and grouping themes by course week and activity. Each r esearch team member looked at quotes collected from  each activity and performed in vivo coding. Since the data sources ar e strictly student quotes and ideas pulled from course artifacts and reflections, in vivo coding wa s determined to be the most appropriate qualitative  coding approach (Saldaña, 2015). Additionally, we use theory triangulation  to structure the across-case comparisons by leaning on the four elements of transformative lear ning theory (Mezirow, 1978) to describe the progres sion of each case’s (and each student’s) definitions of AI literacy throughout the research course experience. As the summer reflections asked students to reflect on their growth and development throughout the research experience, they are threaded throughout each case to help explain the transition of the student’s understanding of AI literacy.

## Discussion

The goal of this research study was to use transfor mational learning theory (Mezirow, 1978) to examine  how undergraduate computer science majors’ AI literacy  definitions transformed while conducting social science research projects. Each student began the summer wi th varying perceptions of AI and subsequently had m ultiple initial understandings of AI literacy. A common across all of the students’ work was learning how to balance their growing understanding of AI literacy with social sc ience research practices and their personal experiences. Through group discussions facilitated by the professor and exploratory analyses of the survey data, each stude nt blended their theoretical definitions of AI literacy with their experiences; Gerald initially thought about AI from a technical perspective but then leveraged his tech nical understanding to develop a research study int errogating the relationship between his peers’ use of AI and p erceived knowledge of AI.  Therefore, Gerald expanded his perception of AI literacy beyond his own personal e xperiences and understanding to include the perspec tives of his peers. Driven by critiques of Ng and colleagues’ (2024) AI  literacy survey, Gerald structured his analyses to determine if his peers know what AI is and if their  knowledge was connected to their use of AI. We observed Gerald’s transformation as moving from a technical understanding of AI to a socio-technical understanding of AI, specifically the use and effectiveness of quantitative survey measurements to articulate undergraduate student AI literacies.

Beginning the summer with more experiential positionings of AI, James and Carolyn merged their initial perceptions of AI with more complex forms of AI literacy, (i.e, ethic al uses of AI and the relationship between technical and perceived understanding of AI). The r esearch experience added to their prior knowledge of AI by exposing them to AI literacy theories and encouragi ng them tonegotiate between undergraduate AI use a nd underlying layers of AI literacy theory. Mike and J ames experienced similar transformations through th eir collaboration with one another. They brought multiple initial perspectives to AI literacy, and through collaboration and research project planning, narrowed their defin itions to ethical uses of AI. From a transformati onal learning theory lens, each student develop ed new points of view and holistic definitions of AI l iteracy throughout the research process that they then aligned  with their experiences and prior knowledge of AI.  Multiple AI literacy frameworks argue that an important component of AI literacy is to help students transition from passive to active AI users (Long & Magerko, 2020; Tzirides et al., 20 24). In this study,  the undergraduates merged their prior experiences and understandings of undergraduate AI use to develop research projects and conclusions th at highlight that students are already using AI tools. Their research conclusions and pers pectives provide new perspectives for AI literacy researchers to conside r, specifically developing explicit definitions of undergraduate passive and active uses of AI. For example, Carolyn’s analyses created space for undergraduate students to show that they are already using AI. She then proceeded to modify and develop research questions to acknowledge her peers’ AI use. Her work adds to existing literature that stresse s the importance of co-collaborating with undergraduates when constructing AI literacy frameworks and measureme nt tools (Černý, 2023). A main limitation of this work is the sample. Since  our work was done at a public university with a majority White, Asian, and male computer science student body, our research does not consider diverse racial, ethnic, and gender perspectives. Additionally, sinc e this is an intensive, summer internship experienc e with limited enrollment, the sample size was naturally s mall. We recommend that similar research actively recruit multiple diverse participant groups to ensure all voices are represented, especially since AI impacts everyone.

## Conclusion

This study highlights the potential role that social science research can play in developing undergraduate computer science majors’ understanding of AI literacy as a t heoretical and practical framework. The undergraduates’ transformed definitions of AI literacy emphasize that the use of social science theories and methodologies guided them to merge technical knowledge of AI, real-life undergraduate uses of AI tools, and AI literac y theory to develop complex and humanity-centered definitions of AI literacy. The findings suggest that social science research can be leveraged to help undergraduate computer science students learn about computing topics (in this case, AI literacy) from a more social lens. Additional research is needed to understand how social science research can help students transform their perceptions of their major’s relationship to society by integrating social science research practices into other computing education spaces.
