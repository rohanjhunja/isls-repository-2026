# First Encounters with Primary Source Projects in Undergraduate Mathematics

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates undergraduate students’ initial experiences in a mathematics course taught entirely with Primary Source Projects (PSPs). Using learning experience network analysis (LENA), we analyzed written reflections from the first two PSPs to map students’ early struggles and successes. Analysis revealed five distinct clusters. Students valued connections to prior knowledge and clear, step-by-step instructions which made the material feel accessible. They reported significant challenges interpreting unfamiliar historical notation and arguments, pointing to an immediate hurdle in this new learning environment. Collaboration with peers emerged as a valuable tool for overcoming these difficulties. Students also responded well when historical context was provided to situate the ideas. These findings highlight specific scaffolds needed to support students’ first encounters with historical mathematics, pointing to the importance of structured activities to smooth the transition into PSP-based learning.

Engaging students with mathematical ideas remains a persistent challenge in undergraduate education. One response has been the use of original historical texts, an approach that saw significant recent development through the TRansforming Instruction in Undergraduate Mathematics via Primary Historical Sources  (TRIUMPHS) project (National Science Foundation, 2015). This multi-institutional, eight-year collaboration focused on creating, testing, and distributing Primary Source Projects (PSPs) to be integrated into undergraduate mathematics courses. Each PSP is d esigned as a complete instructional unit, typically including selections from historical sources, commentary from the authors, tasks for students, and guidance for instructors. The TRIUMPHS project ultimately produced approximately 100 PSPs, all of which a re publicly available through the project’s website under a Creative Commons license (Barnett et al., 2014). The design of PSPs places students directly into the historical development of mathematical thought. Rather than presenting results in their finalized form, PSPs challenge students to work through mathematical problems as they were originally encountered. This requires interpreting unusual notation, reasoning within evolving conceptual frameworks, and adapting to strategies that differ from standard textbook methods (Jankvist, 2014). In this way, mathematics is presented not as a fixed collection of results, but as a creative and evolving discipline shaped by problem-solving within specific cultural and historical contexts. Early tasks often feel effortful, yet this effort can be productive when problems are sequenced to surface strategies and invite explanation (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2018). This approach reflects the learning sciences’ emphasis on contextualized learning where mathematics is presented as a human activity connected to broader societal developments (Hoadley, 2018; Massa-Esteve, 2025).

The learning sciences grew from a need to study learning in authentic, sometimes messy, contexts beyond the laboratory (Hoadley, 2018). This study is situated in that tradition. Instead of only asking whether an intervention “works,” learning scientists examine how students think and feel as they make sense of mathematics with new kinds of tools and activities (Clark et al., 2022). Understanding learners’ initial encounters with new types of learning activities is a key component of design-based research which seeks to refine innovations based on empirical evidence from real-world settings (Puntambekar, 2018). This study contributes to that work by analyzing students’ first reflections in a course taught entirely with PSPs. This project marks the beginning stage of a design-based research study (Barab, 2022) that explores how students engage with historical, problem-centered approaches to mathematics. To capture learning processes from the student’s point of view, we applied learning experience network analysis (LENA; Donaldson et al., 2024). LENA merges qualitative coding with techniques from social network analysis, enabling the mapping of themes and the visualization of relationships among ideas expressed in student reflections.

The study took place during the Fall 2024 semester in a History of Mathematics course at a large R1 university in the southeastern United States. The class enrolled 18 students; 17 were undergraduates and one was 1472 a graduate student. They came from different majors including mathematics, computer science, and engineering. The course traced the historical development of mathematical ideas, beginning with early number theory (Pythagorean triples, the Euclidean algorithm) and moving through early algebra, trigonometry from ancient Greece to the Renaissance, and foundational work in calculus during the 17th –19th centuries. Instruction was delivered entirely through nine PSPs, of which students completed seven in full and  two in partial form. For this analysis, we focused on written reflections associated with PSP 1 (Generating Pythagorean Triples) and PSP 2 (Greatest Common Divisor – Algorithm and Proof). The reflection assignments asked students to describe their learning experiences, challenges, and strategies as they engaged with the historical materials and problem sets. Altogether, 32 reflections were collected and coded using a codebook created collaboratively by the research team. The final version of the codebook contained 49 codes, which were applied across 373 text segments. Coding was conducted independently by two researchers, with weekly calibration meetings involving a third researcher to resolve discrepancies and confirm shared interpretations, ensuring inter-rater reliability. Once coding was complete, we constructed a correlation matrix of co-occurring codes across all student reflections. This dataset was then represented visually as a network map. Within the map, node size reflected betweenness centrality, highlighting the codes that acted as bridges across clusters of student ideas. To further investigate structural patterns, we used Girvan –Newman cluster analysis (Girvan & Newman, 2002) to detect cohesive subgroups of codes. This approach made it possible to identify recurring themes in how students made sense of their learning experiences in PSPs 1 and 2.

The LENA process produced a network map (Figure 1), identifying correlations at the p < 0.05 level. Analysis with the Girvan–Newman approach revealed five well-defined clusters (Q = 0.586), each illustrating a particular combination of student obstacles, achievements, and theoretical insights tied to the learning experience. In the yellow downward triangles cluster, students emphasized the importance of connecting new material to what they already knew. They described learning as easier, more enjoyable, and more useful when they could activate prior knowledge and follow a step-by-step structure. Visual representations also played a central role, helping students make sense of abstract concepts. The only significant struggle was when students encountered specific concepts, which appeared to be a localized challenge rather than a  broader obstacle. These experiences align with cognitive constructivist theory, particularly in how activating prior knowledge provides a foundation for new learning. A possible design move is to introduce brief visual pre-activities before each PSP, framing the historical problem with modern analogies and structured steps. For example, prior to engaging with the mutual subtraction algorithm in the “Greatest Common Divisor” PSP, students could work through a simple module that frames the concept as findin g the largest “measuring stick” that divides two lengths. Such activities would build directly on their strength in using prior knowledge, visuals, and step-by-step reasoning. In the purple diamonds cluster, students grappled with interpreting historical arguments, specialized terminology, and reconciling modern and historical notation. At times, unclear instructions within the historical excerpts or student tasks compounded these struggles. Yet these challenges also opened pathways for growth. Students valued the chance to compare historical and modern approaches, develop new perspectives, and explore multiple ways of reasoning. They highlighted perspective-taking and valuing diverse viewpoints as central to their learning. These reflections resonate with transformative learning theory and cognitive constructivism, both of which emphasize rethinking assumptions and engaging with multiple perspectives. A possible design move is to restructure PSP activities around explicit comparative analysis. For example, students could use a graphic organizer to place concepts from ancient Chinese mathematics, Euclid’s proofs, and modern methods side-byside. This makes comparison the primary task, transforming difficult terminology and arguments into data points for analysis rather than barriers to comprehension.

In the lime green hourglasses cluster, students’ struggles stemmed from limited background knowledge, which made higher-order thinking, idea generation, and recall more difficult. However, historical contextualization and exposure to alternative approaches helped them overcome these challenges. These strategies fostered an appreciation of mathematical development over time and ultimately led to improved understanding. Their reflections align with cognitive constructivist theory, particularly the importance of building context to support conceptual learning. A possible design move is to restructure each PSP so that the historical context section consistently appears at the start. A possible design move is to embed worked examples directly into PSPs, pairing modern and historical versions of the same concept. In the pink circles cluster, students’ experiences were shaped primarily by collaborative learning. Their main difficulty was unfamiliar notation and symbols, but peer discussion and problem-solving provided the support necessary to overcome this barrier. Reflections highlighted the role of scaffolding by instructors and peers, collaborative knowledge construction, and learning within a zone of proximal development. These outcomes align closely with social constructivist theory, which emphasizes knowledge-building through interaction and support. A possible design move is to formalize collaboration by turning the most challenging passages of PSPs into structured “jigsaw” activities. For example, different group members could each become responsible for interpreting a portion of Euclid’s proof or Liu Hui’s explanation, later teaching it to their peers. This would place collaborative knowledge construction at the center of the experience, directly leveraging the strengths students reported.

In the teal squares cluster, students described increasing difficulty with tasks but also identified reflection, hands-on work, and collaboration as essential tools for overcoming these challenges. Their reflections emphasized schema building, metacognition, and the development of critical thinking skills. Students noted that hands-on experiences, combined with opportunities for reflection, allowed them to build their own conceptual frameworks even as the material became more complex. These experiences alig n with cognitive constructivist theory, which emphasizes knowledge construction through active engagement and reflection. A possible design move is to integrate a structured “proof deconstruction” activity that prompts students to break down complex arguments into assumptions, claims, and logical steps. A final reflective question could ask them to pinpoint where confusion arose. This scaffold transforms increasing difficulty into a manageable process of deep engagement, reinforcing their strength in reflective, hands-on learning. The analysis of learner experiences engaging with novel learning activities highlights several themes that characterize students’ earliest encounters with PSP-based learning. Students thrived when they could connect new material to prior knowledge, often through visual representations and step-by-step reasoning (yellow downward triangles), which made the tasks feel accessible and rewarding. At the same time, difficulties with historical terminology, notation, and arguments (purple diamonds) were already evident, pointing to challenges that would persist later in the semester. Students without strong background knowledge reported difficulty generating ideas, yet responded positively when historical context and alternative approaches were emphasized (lime gree n hourglasses). Clear instructions and worked examples also played a critical role in helping students consolidate understanding and apply concepts (Salmon Upward Triangles), while collaboration, though not yet a coping strategy, emerged as a valuable tool for overcoming struggles with notation and building community (pink circles, teal squares).

## Discussion & Implications

These findings suggest several design considerations for PSP implementation at the beginning of a course. Structured pre-activities that activate prior knowledge and provide step-by-step scaffolding can smooth students’ entry into historical tasks. Consistent historical contextualization should be embedded to address gaps in background knowledge and situate mathematical ideas in their cultural and practical settings. Worked examples and clear instructions remain essential, especially when paired with modern-historical comparisons that explicitly highlight similarities and differences in methods. Finally, collaborative learning should be intentionally designed from the start —through group activities, jigsaw structures, or peer teaching—so that students not only benefit from immediate support but also establish collaborative habits they can rely on as the material grows more abstract.

These findings point to the deeply contextualized nature of learning. Nevertheless, examining the beginning of the semester reveals how students transition into PSP-based learning, with early successes hinging on clarity, context, and prior knowledge, and early struggles centering on historical engagement. By identifying these themes, this work highlights how scaffolding, contextualization, and structured collaboration can support students in navigating their first encounters with historical mathematics, se tting a foundation for deeper engagement as the course progresses.
