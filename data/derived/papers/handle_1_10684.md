# Theorizing Mathematics Teacher Noticing for Equity Through Collaborative Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research on equity in teacher noticing indicates the multi-dimensionality of teacher noticing and the sociopolitical awareness teachers employ to disrupt longstanding practices that persistently marginalize students from culturally, racially, and linguistically diverse communities. We draw on participatory design research to emphasize the value of theorizing with teachers, as methodologies that center practitioner expertise provide insight into the unique character of noticing and how it unfolds in local contexts. Informed by this perspective, we collaborated with mathematics teachers to jointly develop a model of noticing for equity rooted in their perspectives and experience. In doing so, we shed light on how noticing for equity is enacted in practice, bridging between theories of noticing and firsthand experiences of noticing and decision making that teachers engage in during instruction.

## Introduction

Research on how teachers attend to and support  equity indicates the multidimensionality in teacher  noticing, which includes teachers’ awareness and insights abo ut students’ past s and futures that shape students’ ongoing participation in the mathematics classroom (van Es et al., 2022). This perspective highlights the soci opolitical awareness teachers bring to the classroom and how that awareness can serve to disrupt longstanding practices that persistently marginalize students from culturally, racially, and linguistically diverse communities (H and, 2012; Louie et al., 2021). Informed by this perspective, we drew on participatory action research and participatory design research to co-develop a model of noticing for equity rooted in pr actitioners’ perspectives and experience (Gutierrez & Penuel, 2014). We  collaborated with a group of middle and high school  teachers to capture and characterize their noticing and the instructional p ractices they employ to advance equity. In this pap er, we ask: how do practitioners theorize teacher noticing for equity? What are the core dimensions of equitable noticing, and how do those dimensions interact and support each o ther? In doing so, we hope to shed light on how tea chers conceptualize noticing for equity emerging in practice, bridging between theories of noticing and specific instances of noticing and decision making that teachers engage in during instruction. An emerging line of research points to the sociopolitical nature of teacher noticing. This research shows that teachers’ noticing includes developing awareness of prevailing narratives of competence, which candispose teachers to interpret students’ experiences from de ficit perspectives (Hand, 2012; Louie, 2018). Other  research, however, demonstrates that teachers’ noticing cand isrupt deficit narratives of learners by attending to and building on the intellectual, relational, and linguistic features of classroom interactions that can position students with agency to support them developing positive mat hematics identities (Kang, 2021, Louie et al., 2021; Rubin & van Es, 2023). Research also documents how teache rs’ own positionality shapes how they make sense  of classroom interactions (Kalinec-Craig, 2017). This research suggests that teachers’  awareness of their own personal and educational experiences, the sociopoli tical history of different groups of students, and narratives of schooling more broadly can serve to disrupt persistent inequities in mathematics classrooms. Recognizing that teaching is embedded in personal, sociopolitical, and historical contexts, we drew on participatory design methodology to examine how tea chers understand their own noticing. Advocates for participatory design research note that engaging in  co-design can produce more nuanced and authentic conceptions of situated activity. For instance, Ede lson (2002) argues that design positions participan ts to “elaborate upon their initial hypotheses and princi ples, refining, adding, and discarding —gradually knitting together a coherent theory” (p. 106). This process of developing theories through design surfaces knowledge and ideas that might otherwise remain tacit or unexamined. Bang & Vossoughi (2016) emphasize the potential for codesign to develop what they call “axiological innovations”, which entail purposefully articulating and examining what co-designers value and how their values are enacted th rough practices and structures. The experience of theorizing and interrogating values through design in turn supports “significant reorganization of sys tems of activity” (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016, p. 566). In oth er words, engaging teachers in design can both reve al and transform how their understanding of teaching practice. In this work, participants concluded t heir professional development experience by collaborating to design a  new iteration of the professional development prog ram. To create materials that embodied their insight and un derstanding, participants narrated what they had le arned, mapped relationships between concepts, and represen ted concepts in ways that would be accessible and compelling for someone unfamiliar with their work. In doing so, they defined their own conceptions of noticing for equity and the values and principles that they aspire to in their teaching.

## Methods

This study is situated in one site of the CoATTEND research collaborative. At this site, 4 secondary math teachers and 1 community educator partnered with 3 university researchers in joint analysis and discussion of noticing for equity. PD activities inclu ded a summer institute that engaged with noticing a s a sociopolitical dimension of mathematics teaching (e.g. Gutierrez, 2018; Nasir &  Hand, 2008). This was followed by a series of clas sroom observations and video-stimulated interviews, video club meetings, and a s ubsequent summer institute focused on co-design. Participants advocated for designing PD for  other teachers; to that end, they led the process of applying, problematizing, and relating key concepts to develop an account of noticing for equity that was authentic to their experiences of teaching. Through this desi gn work, the participants re-presented the concepts they had elaborated throughout the PD, which afforded insigh ts into how they conceptualized key constructs of e quitable teaching and situated them in their practice as salient dimensions of their noticing. Data for this study consists of video recordings of  co-design meetings and reflective interviews with participants. The design meetings comprised approxi mately 10 hours of video, and each interview was approximately 1 hour long. We followed an iterative grounded theory approach (Corbin & Strauss, 2014), starting by reviewing the PD design that teachers produced. As participants were designing for others to experi ence the professional learning they had just completed, the design enumerated topics they felt w ere essential to address. This set of topics served as a priori codes that we applied in tracing back through the design meetings to understand how they came to be included (e.g. Coles, 2019). Sp ecifically, we looked for moments where they descri bed experiences in the PD program that made a lasting impress ion and thereby contributed to their current understanding of equitable mathematics noticing; th ese explanations shed light on how they conceptuali ze those dimensions (Feldman & Almquist, 2012). We then iden tified and refined sub-codes and cross-cutting themes (Locke et al., 2022) and reviewed participants’ ref lective interviews to elaborate our interpretations. From this analysis, we developed a graphic representation to represent the dimensions they discussed (Langley, 1 999). We then returned to the video data from the original p rofessional development to revisit the events they highlighted, both to triangulate statements about their learning  and to refine our emerging model with more detail (Engle et al., 2007). This phase involved watching videos of key e vents participants referred to, writing analytic me mos, and comparing the original events to participants’ retrospective accounts. Finally, we revised the gra phic representation to account for details that emerged from this phase of analysis.

## Findings

First, we present a model that represents the dimensions participants identified as essential in noticing for equity. This model indicates the integrated nature of teach ers’ noticing, which attends to a range of objects both within and beyond the classroom  context (see Figure 1). Second, we highlight how te achers account for the multidimensional character of their noticing. Throu gh the activities of the professional development, these teachers came to appreciate how intuitive and embodied responses reflect their multidimensional awareness. Figure 1 A Collaboratively Developed Model of Noticing for Equity The multiple objects of teachers’ attention and sensema king fall into three broad categories: student dimensions, teacher dimension, and contextual dimen sions. Noticing for equity involves attending to al l three categories and interpreting what one observes in  relation to each (e.g., what does this indicate abo ut students? How is this situated in context? How does my identi ty color my interpretations?). These teachers were attentive to both their own and students’ personal and social histories and the histories of the contexts in which they interact. For example, they drew on their own experiences as mathematics learners, situated inside of a system of schooling that designates students as “high” and “low”, to no tice their own instructional practices and their s tudents’ experiences. This model also includes the “x factor”, a term that participants adopted to describe how each teacher is sensitized to particular aspects that reflect their personal commitments (Hand, 2012). For instance, one teacher’s noticing centered on notions of classroom managemen t and how he sought to disrupt prevailing behavior management practices to reposition students to contribute meaningfully in his classroom. Another teacher attuned to how she organized the physical layout of her cla ssroom and the tools students used throughout the l esson to support interactions that were attentive and responsive to students’ needs. For example, she developed a two-sided note taking tool that scaffolded students’ learning, but also structured her noticing as she circulate d through the classroom. These local adaptations enabled teachers  to notice students’ histories and experiences and create classroom environments that helped them support students in more nuanced ways. The activities of the PD program supported particip ants’ awareness of these multiple dimensions by unpacking their noticing to identify the distinct e lements that they had attended to simultaneously. P articipants emphasized the power of articulating the dimensions  of their intuitive noticing, expressing that in th e moment, they were not consciously aware of the depth of the ir interpretations. One participant commented that “I didn't realize I'm multitasking and thinking about kids on so many levels all the time. Which is why I think the noticing took on so many more layers than what I thought it was.” Participants’ attending and interpreting were  often guided by embodied cues like excitement, joy, tension, or anxiety. They maintained that exploring these intuitive and embodied responses helped them understand how m ultiple dimensions present as a coherent phenomenon  in the moment of instruction.   Additionally, participants expressed that the braid ing and layering of dimensions in their noticing is inextricably  integrated into how they feel and intuit classroom interactions. This emphasis on intuition and multisensory cues is consistent with scholarship positing that the production of knowled ge and understanding are not solely cognitive acts, but are carried in the body (e.g., Anzaldúa, 2015). By recognizing that their intuitions were grounded in a deep understand ing of teaching, they understood these moments as c arrying with them their axiological commitments to disrupt inequities in the classroom. The teachers expressed that attending to multiple dimensions is itself an axiological commitment to notice student identities more expansively. They argued that the professional learning model sh ould provoke teachers to illuminate and narrate how  their noticing candisrupt inequities and carry out their philosophy of practice.

## Discussion

This study contributes detail and nuance to an emer ging line of research characterizing teachers’ noti cing for equitable math teaching. Some widely cited models, for instance, describe noticing as cycles that prog ress sequentially through attending, interpreting, and deciding to respond (e.g., Jacobs et al., 2010; Louie et al., 2021). Our model suggests that practices of noticing are s imultaneous and nonlinear. These teachers emphasize d that their noticing operates on several levels at once, continuously layering their attention, interpretation, and responses on the basis of multiple dimensions. Here, we exami ne the particular dimensions that teachers attend t o and how the integration of these dimensions supports more e xpansive interpretations and reflects the complexit y of equitable teaching and learning. Adopting this model of noticing as simultaneous and interwoven has implications for how participatory design can support teacher le arning. In particular, we note the importance of po sitioning teachers to un pack the dimensions of noticing and examine them se parately, as well as the importance of re-integrating them in practice. As we posit that the interweaving of dimensions is itself a central char acteristic of noticing for equity, the PD we co-designed does not call for separating dimensions in the moment of  instruction. Rather, examining dimensions separately in the PD s essions enables teachers to see their philosophy of  practice reflected in what they notice. In short, the process of separating out the dimensions of noticing supports axiological innovation (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016).

Future inquiry could concern how the collaborative and discursive nature of this PD supported participants’ articulating values and developing aw areness of their noticing practices. How, for insta nce, does including teachers, researchers, and community edu cators in the co-design process support more expansive reflection and theorizing on all members’ parts? Ho w does the social arrangement of the co-design meetings position teachers to generate novel insights that s upport axiological innovation and transform ative change in classroom practice? In the same vein, this study pr ompts questions about how teachers’ learning in thi s format differs from or complements other PD approaches that position teachers to collaborate in making meaning of their practice, such as lesson study and video clubs. Ans wers to these questions will contribute to research  on the relationship between teacher noticing and classroom  instruction, as well as push the field to develop theories of teacher learning for equity and social justice aims.
