# Leveraging Large-Language Models to Understand Self-Regulated Learning Processes in STEM Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The study employed Large-Language Models (LLMs) to analyze students’ sequential patterns in self-regulated learning (SRL). A pre-trained LLM was fine-tuned on 106 high students’ computer trace data to learn the relationship between students’ SRL sequence and their final learning outcome. The LLM was validated against a linear baseline to demonstrate that LLM can model the SRL sequence. Furthermore, an integrated gradient method was employed to compute the sequence attribution to reveal how different sub-sequences contribute to the final learning outcome. The study found that the “evaluation-analysis” sequence had the highest attribution to a positive learning outcome. This finding demonstrated the importance of self-reflection in SRL. The proposed LLM-based analysis is possible to help educators design real-time interventions for different students to enhance their learning process.

## Objectives and significance

As Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) education becomes increasingly integrated with engineering design activities, the  imperative to understand students’ learning processes in these complex, crossdiscipline projects becomes more critical (Chiu et al., 2013). In particular, self-regulated learning (SRL) depicts students’ learning processes by revealing how  students monitor and  control their  learning, using a variety of cognitive and behavioral strategies (Zimmerman,  2002). A fine-grained analysis of students’ strategies in observing, modeling, modifying, analyzing, and evaluating a project (Fan & Yu, 2017) will illustrate how students self-regulated their learning in STEM.

In computer-based learning environments, students’ learning processes become  accessible and transparent. Event logs of students’ interaction s during the learning  process have  been utilized to discover, monitor, and improve the education process. Educational process  mining (EPM), as a sub-field of educational data mining (EDM), focuses  on extracting  knowledge from event logs, including process mining, intention mining, sequence pattern  mining, and graph mining (Bogarín et al., 2018). There is a  large body of research in applying process mining (PM)  techniques to study and improve SRL (Bannert et al., 2014; Cerezo et al., 2020). For example, a fuzzy miner can only reveal the types of sequences that exist in the process and how often these sequences occur. However, these PM modes cannot identify the relationship between students’ sequential patterns and their final learning outcome. Understanding this relationship can help us under stand what types of student behaviors contribute to the desired learning outcome, and inevitably influence the regulatory processes and strategies learners employ (Poitras & Lajoie, 2013). This will allow educators to tailor instructional designs and interventions to the specific needs of different learning tasks and domains. The recent development of Large-Language Models (LLMs), such as Transformer-based language models (Devlin et al., 2018), can model students’ sequential behavior data in the SRL. Thus, we used an LLMbased model (Sanh et al., 2019) to uncover the deep relationship between students’ SRL sequential patterns and their learning performance. The results of this study offered both practical and methodological contributions  to the field of SRL. First, this study verified that LLM-based sequence modeling is an effective method for exploring the deep relationship between students’ sequential behavior data and their learning performance. This approach may be applied to other context s of SRL. Secondly, we utilized a model interpretation method, namely, the integrated gradient method (Sundararajan et al., 2017), to identify critical SRL sequences in predicting students’ learning performance. These critical SRL sequences provide implications for teaching and learning practices.

## Method

The participants were 106 ninth-grade students, and they used a simulated environment called Energy3D to design a solar house to optimize net annual energy gain. Students’ actions in Energy3D were recorded with timestamps such a s [(13:30:10, add solar panel), … (1 3:31:21, remove maple), …], and t heir final net annual  energy performances were also recorded. Details can be found in authors (2020). As displayed in Figure 1, we processed the data following  three major steps. In the first step, we pre-processed the SRL raw sequence data and corresponding energy performance of the learning  project to create the training and evaluation dataset. In t he second step, we  fine-tuned an LLM to learn the relationship between students’ SRL sequence and final energy performance. In the final step, we utilized the integrated gradient method to compute the sequence attributions to the learning outcomes.

Figure 1 The Modeling Procedure.

Data pre-processing The raw data underwent three pre-processing steps before transform ed into the training data used for  the LLM fine-tuning. First, "camera" events were removed from the raw event sequence because it does not represent students’ learning experiences. Second, we mapped the raw logging actions  to the SRL actions using a coding schema: observation, construction, modification, analysis, and evaluation (Authors, 2020), and removed consecutive repeated SRL actions while preserving the first occurrence. Third, the training objective was defined. As mentioned by the authors (2020), students’ energy performance was  measured using the net annual energy, where a negative value indicates an energy-plus house. Therefore, the objective of the model is to predict whether the learning outcome is an energy-plus house or not, where we labeled the energy-plus house as the positive label (i.e., 1), otherwise the negative label (i.e., 0). Overall, there are 106 valid examples. The average length of  the SRL sequence is 123, with a median of 108, a minimum of 7, and a maximum of 336. Among the 106 examples, there are 77 positive labels and 29 negative labels. The data preprocessing step is illustrated in Figure 1. LLM fine-tuning and evaluation After data pre-processing, we randomly separate d the dataset into a training set and an evaluation set. Only the training set was used for model fine-tuning, and the evaluation set was used to measure how well the model can generalize to the unseen data. The BERT tokenizer (Devlin et al., 2018) was used to tokenize the SRL sequence, and the lightweight distilled BERT model (Sanh et al., 2019) was used as the pre-trained LLM. Before the finetuning, a simple hyper-parameter search (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012) was used to find the best learning rate by varying the learning rate from 10-7 to 0.001. After selecting the optimal learning rate to be 10-4, the pre-trained LLM was fine-tuned on the SRL training set. The evaluation loss plateaued around 23 epochs while the training loss kept decreasing, indicating over-fitting after 23 epochs (as shown in the embedded graph in Figure 1). Computation of sub-sequence attribution In order to understand how different action sequences contribute to  the final learning outcome, we computed the sub-sequence attribution of positive learning outcome s using the fined-tuned LLM. The integrated  gradient method (Sundararajan et al., 2017)  was used to find the attribution of each action in the sequence to the model outcome. Figure 1 shows two examples, one for a SRL sequence with a positive learning outcome (i.e., designed house has net positive energy), and the other with  a negative learning outcome (i.e. designed house has net negative energy). Based on the action-level attributions, we computed the normalized attributions for each bigram (two consecutive actions) and trigram (three consec utive actions) sub-sequence. The normalized attributions are the summation of all attributions of a subsequence, divided by the number of occurrences in this subsequence. Baseline model In addition to the  LLM approach, a simple logistic regression model was also used to set  a baseline for model performance. In this logistic regression, the input feature vectors were a set of frequencies of each unigram, bigram, and trigram sub-sequence. Frequency was defined as the number of occurrences of a bigram (or trigram) in a sequence divided by the  length of the sequence. For example, if a bigram subsequence “evaluation-analysis” occurred 10 times in a student’s sequence, and this  sequence has a total of 100 actions, then its frequency is 0.1. The binary label followed the same definition as mentioned above.

## Results

Model performance The fine-tuned LLM achieved  90.9% accuracy,  94.12% precision, 94.12% recall, and 94.12% F1 scor es. In comparison, the logistic regression model achieved 77.27% accuracy, 77.27% precision, 100% recall, and 87.18% F1 scores. It shows that a simple linear model cannot captur e the deep relationship between the SRL sequence and the learning outcome, therefore  it always predicts the positive label because it has a larger likelihood (recall 73% of label is positive, 27% is negative), which leads to 100% recall with low precision. In comparison, the finetuned LLM can learn this deep relationship, as evidenced by the good precision and recall achieved at the same time. From this result, we conclude that this fine-tuned LLM is reasonably good to be used to study the attributions of different sub-sequences to the learning outcomes.

Sequence attribution Based on the attributions computed by the integrated gradient method (see Table 1), the top bigram subsequences and trigram subsequences attributing to the positive label mostly consisted of analysis and evaluation actions. On the other hand, the top bigram subsequences and tri gram subsequences attributed to the negative label mostly consisted of construction and modification actions. According to the existing models of SRL in  the STEM field (Authors, 2020), analysis and evaluation actions occur when students derive functional infor mation and access how good the design is. Whereas construction and modification actions occur when students make design changes. According to the cyclical view of SRL (Zimmerman, 2002), analysis and evaluation actions help students learn from the prior effort and affect subsequent learning. This initial result from sequence attribution analysis indicates that among other SRL actions, analysis and evaluation are more important to influence the final learning outcome. ## Discussion, conclusion, and future work

In this short paper, we propose a new LLM-based analysis method to understand students’ SRL sequences. As an initial step in this research direction, we demonst rated LLMs can be used as powerful models to learn the deep relationship between students’ sequential behavior data and their learning outcome s; and the integrated gradient method can be used to understand  the SRL sequence attribution to students’ learning outcomes. The top two bigram and trigram sequences are heavily indexed in evaluation and analysis, and the third most important bigram and trigram sequences are observation-evaluation and evaluation-analysis-observation. Based on this finding, educators should design the SRL learning experience to encourage students to self-reflect more often. For example, by incorporating the LLM-based interventions, right after the forethought phase (i.e., observation), the Energy3D environment can prompt a review session to ask students to revi ew what they have learnt. In addition, if the software detects the students haven’t done any eval uation and analysis for a while, it can prompt a gu idance to suggest students to run some performance analysis on their designs. The prompts can help stu dents discover the issues in their design, help them to improve their understanding, and make the subsequential design more efficient. In a future finer-grain analysis, we will correlate the SRL sequence attribution with different student populations to gain deeper insights. In addition, we will study SRL sequence attribution in the context of students’ cyclical learning phases to formulate actionable learning theories assisted by LLM-based analysis. Finally, we can leverage learning theories to design and conduct controlled treatment experiments to understand how to design the right intervention at the right moment to help stud ents improve their SRL learning experience.  This datadriven LLM-based analysis approach can be transferred to other  research contexts, especially those involving students’ sequential learning behaviors.
