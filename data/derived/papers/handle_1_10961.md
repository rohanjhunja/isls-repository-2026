# Capturing Epistemic Engagement on Social Media

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Responding to calls for increasing learning sciences’ efforts to understand how people reason with online information, we present a methodological tool for qualitatively capturing epistemic engagement on social media. Drawing on the AIR framework and prior efforts to conceptualize epistemic engagement on social media, we developed a codebook to capture Aims, Ideals, and Reliable epistemic process. This contribution discusses this effort, and how it can be used to understand apt epistemic engagement on social media.

## Introduction and background

Online misinformation holds the potential to erode trust in established institutions, as recently evidenced by vaccine hesitancy during the COVID-19 pandemic; i t has also been recognized and discussed as a  pressing educational challenge (Barzilai & Chinn, 2020). Examining how people reason with knowledge claims encountered online can illuminate the role that education can play in such information-turbulent contexts (Chinn et al., 2020); this may guide learning scientists in how to design environments that bolster resilience towards misinformation (Barzilai & Chinn, 2020).  While empirical research has focused on understanding how students reason with information retrieved through search engines (e.g., Coiro et al., 2015) and websites (e.g., Kiili et al., 2022), social media contexts are still underexplored, even though information encountered incidentally on social media may also lead to epistemic engagement  and informal learning (Greene et al., 2021). We ground our understanding of epistemic engagement in the AIR framework (Chinn et al., 2014), which comprise s three components: Epistemic Aims, the goals set during the encounter; Epistemic Ideals, the criteria relied on to judge if aims are achieved; and Reliable Epistemic Processes, the strategies deployed in order to reach epistemically sound judgements. We ask: How can we qualitatively characterize epistemic engagement on social media? We posit that it is worth exploring the nature of e pistemic engagement on social media because such spaces differ from search engines and websites. On social media, the information environment is determined by algorithms relying on user profiling; a lgorithmic curation presents information in a context that fosters the perception that reliable information can be accesse d through one’s trusted social connections (Lee et al., 2023). However, can one’s actions on a social media post indicate how they epistemically engage with information? Driven by this question, we turned to quantitative and qualitative research examining epistemic engagement in other online information contexts to understand how to code for Aims, Ideals and Reliable epistemic  processes. Our review of available coding schemes indicated that existing work has mostly focused on examining epistemic ideals and processes; studies examining aims were scarce. Examining non-epistemic aims or ideals  — whose focus is unrelated to knowledge generation  — alongside epistemic ones was also underexplored. Therefore, we elaborate on how we developed a qualitative methodo logy for capturing epistemic engagement on social m edia, initially informed by work in other online information contexts, and expanded inductively. The set of coding schemes described below were used for coding and analyzing data collected via a think aloud and semi-structured interviews, accompanied by behavioral data captured via screen recording. The study was conducted with young adults (ages 18-29 years old) who used social media as part of their daily routine.

## Coding for the AIR framework in a social media context

We developed a set of coding schemes to understand the nature of epistemic engagement in  the context of a qualitative study with a simulated, interactive, Twitter-like, social media post. To develop the coding schemes across the three components of the AIR framework, we initially drew from existing empirical research, but given the novel information context and the exploratory nature of our study, we also coded for “other” to capture aims, ideals and processes that may be unique to this context. Two of the authors independently coded 30% of the data.  Intercoder reli ability assessment was satisfactory (k=0.846), and disagreements were disc ussed and resolved.  Episodes coded for “other” were supplemented by discussions among all three authors. We elaborate on our approach for each component below, and illustrate the application of this approach by reporting on aims, ideals and processes not previously discussed in the literature.

Coding Scheme 1: Aims We coded for epistemic and non-epistemic aims, since individuals may engage with a social media post with goals other than knowledge acquisition; it may be possible that both types of aims may facilitate epistemic engagement (Chinn et al., 2014). Beyond normative epistemic aims  (e.g., explanatory understanding), we also found that participants reported global aims, e.g., using social media to stay informed, or local aims relevant to the post, e.g., the aim of information gathering referring to the goal of acquiring information without assessing whether it is sufficiently warranted. Aiming to satisfy one’s curiosity in relation to aspects of the social media post was also mentioned. Non-epistemic aims such as avoiding effort and enjoyment were mentioned, as well as influencing others, which may motivate endorsements of the post so that others could view it on their timeline. Coding scheme 2: Ideals Similar to aims, we coded for epistemic and non-epistemic ideals that individuals might rely on while evaluating a social media post’s trustworthiness. Ideals were distinguished in relation to their focus, which included (1) sources, (2) the claim, and (3) evidence. Participants mentioned range of viewpoints as an epistemic ideal, which referred to the presence of multiple perspectives on a parti cular topic; this was not used as corroboration, but rather as an ideal in and of itself. We also coded for non-epistemic ideals that emerged from our data; these included usefulness of the information in the post, social influence characteristics such as number of followers, and the real-life consequences of information. We also found that consistency could be coded as epistemic or nonepistemic depending on the context; as an epistemic  ideal, it concerned alignment with prior knowledge, beliefs and values, while as a non-epistemic ideal it concerned alignment with one’s own practices and values. Coding scheme 3: Reliable epistemic processes for assessing a social media post’s trustworthiness This coding scheme adopted a levels-based taxonomy to describe how individuals engaged with the post to assess trustworthiness. In a social media context, the lowest level of engagement (Level 1) manifests via scrolling without pausing, while browsing (Level 2) indicates more selective attention. Pausing on a particular social media post may signify reading (Level 3), indicated by the time spent on the post. This may be followed by checking post content  (Level 4), indicated by clicks within the social media  post (e.g., opening a URL), which may be followed by a general evaluation of the post  (Level 5), indicated by “lik ing”, sharing, or no action (coded only when inaction is justified). Evaluation may also be specific to the source, claim or evidence. Seeking to purposefully support or refute the claims in the post may lead individuals to seek additional information by checking external sources (Level 6), indicating greater epistemic engagement.

## Implications of this work

This work responds to calls for empirically examinin g how individuals engage with social media posts in personalized information environments (Greene et al., 2021), by making a methodological contribution a nd extending research focused on epistemic cognition to social media, a novel information context. We recognize that epistemic engagement with a simulated post may vary from authentic social media contexts, but the developed coding schemes provide a valuable starting point fo r understanding how individuals epistemically engage with a social media post. We hope that other learning scientists can adopt and extend our research on social media, to inform existing and future educational efforts focused on fostering epistemic cognition.
