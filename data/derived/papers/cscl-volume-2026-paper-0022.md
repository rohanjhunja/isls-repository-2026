# Close Encounters: Teacher Proximity and Student-Reported Emotions While Using ST Math

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Students’ academic emotions are critical to learning (Pekrun et al., 2002; Tan et al., 2021) and are linked to teacher actions (Meyer & Turner, 2007), some of which require close proximity. Prior studies emphasize the importance of proximity in shaping cl assroom environments, student behavior, and math anxiety (Shores et al., 1993; Quinnell, 2022). Yet, research on proximity is limited, especially in technology-mediated classrooms where teachers may not necessarily be in close proximity to students. This study examined the moment-to-moment, bidirectional relationship between student-reported emotions and teacher proximity during ST Math sessions. Using multilevel vector autoregression on experience sampling data from 92 elementary students, we found that frustration was associated with closer subsequent proximity, but it is the opposite for boredom. Conversely, closer proximity predicted lower subsequent boredom. These findings suggest that teacher proximity may play an important role in managing students’ emotions in technology-mediated settings.

Students’ academic emotions play an important role in learning, as they are linked to motivation, self-efficacy, achievements, and self-regulation (see Schutz & Lanehart, 2002; Tan et al., 2021). These emotions are associated with teachers’ actions in the classroom (Lei et al, 2018). Moreover, teacher-student proximity, the physical distance between teachers and students in the classroom, is also linked to student participation, engagement, and performance (Dong et al., 2021; Marshall & Losonczy-Marshall, 2010). Teachers' proximity to students can shape how the students perceive their teacher's presence. For example, being nearby may convey care and togetherness (Plante & Asselin, 2014). Moreover, when teachers are physically closer to students, students tend to participate more actively in discussions and feel greater engagement with course content (Meeks et al., 2013). Given these links, it is important to understand what drives proximity and how proximity may lead to outcomes. The relationship between a te acher's physical closeness and student engagement or performance may be complex. There may be variation between teachers and students in how teacher proximity is associated with emotions; for example, a teacher standing nearby may elicit anxiety in some students, which can be negatively associated with their participation or achievement (Brumariu et al., 2023; Pekrun et al., 2017). Examining students' emotional responses may provide a more complete understanding of how physical distance in the classroom is linked to learning. Furthermore, the association may also operate in reverse. For instance, certain students' emotions, such as signs of frustration or disengagement, may trigger teachers to move closer to those students to provide support (Guther et al., 1995). Investigating bidirectional links between proximity and emotions will shed light on the dynamics of this important teacher-student classroom interaction. Within the current study, we investigate these associations in the context of a learning technology. Student use of learning technologies in the classroom presents a unique wrinkle to understanding student/teacher interactions, as students often tend to work independently on technology with minimal teacher guidance (e.g., Weng et al., 2020). However, teachers continue to play an important role in supporting and facilitating student engagement with these technologies by providing assistance when needed (Negoescu & Boştină -Bratu, 2016). Teachers can support students’ emotional experiences with technology by checking in, encouraging them, and providing scaffolding to students with negative emotions, such as confusion and boredom. Understanding how teachers’ movements within classrooms relate to students' emotions while they interact with technology can provide insight into the teacher-technology-student ecology and allow for the development of practical recommendations for teachers in supporting their students' emotions, engagement, and performance. In this paper, we aimed to answer the following research question:

1. How is teacher-student physical proximity associated with subsequent students' reported emotions

while using ST Math?

2. How are student-reported emotions associated with subsequent teacher-student physical proximity

## Theoretical framework

Within the current study, we explore these associations through the lens of the cognitive-motivational model of academic emotions (Pekrun et al., 2000), sociocultural theory (Vygotsky, 1978), and proxemic theory (Hall, 1968). These theories provide a comprehensive framework for understanding how to examine the complex interplay between emotions, social interactions, and physical proximity in the classroom environment. These perspectives together allow us to conceptualize student emotions as dynamically shaped by both internal appraisals and external classroom interactions, including teachers’ physical proximity to students. Academic emotions Academic emotions, as conceptualized by Pekrun et al. (2002), refer to the specific emotions students feel during academic activities. These emotions can include both positive feelings (e.g., enjoyment, interest) and negative feelings (e.g., anxiety, boredom, and frustration), all of which can impact students' motivation, engagement, and learning outcomes (Pekrun et al., 2002). Positive emotions can improve motivation, promote flexible learning strategies and self-regulation, allowing more cognitive resources for engaging with a task (Pekrun et al., 2002). On the other hand, negative emotions are believed to decrease motivation and cause students to rely on more restrictive learning approaches, like basic rehearsal and algorithmic procedures (Pekrun et al., 2002). Moreover, negative emotions can reduce the cognitive resources and self-regulation available, because when students experience these negative emotions, they may have less mental energy to focus on learning tasks, less motivation to engage with the m aterial, and may struggle with effectively regulating their own learning process (Pekrun et al., 2002).

Moreover, emotions can also be distinguished by their level of physiological activation: positive activating (e.g., enjoyment, excitement), positive deactivating (e.g., relief, contentment), negative activating (e.g., frustration, anxiety), and negative deactivating (e.g., boredom, hopelessness; Pekrun, 2006; Pekrun, 2024). Activating emotions are characterized by heightened arousal that mobilizes cognitive and behavioral resources toward the task at hand (Pekrun, 2006).

Deactivating emotions, by contrast, are characterized by low arousal that tends to reduce engagement and effort (Pekrun, 2006). Therefore, activating emotions may be more visible and therefore more detectable compared to deactivating emotions, such as boredom, which are more passive. Positive activating emotions are associated with increased motivation, flexible and deep learning strategies, and sustained cognitive engagement (Pekrun et al., 2002). Positive deactivating emotions (e.g., relief, contentment) are linked to lower urgency to engage further, and thus have weaker or more mixed associations with subsequent learning effort (Pekrun, 2024). Negative activating emotions (e.g., frustration, anxiety) are characterized by heightened arousal and continued e ffortful engagement with the source of difficulty; although they can motivate persistence in some circumstances, they typically reduce available cognitive resources and promote more rigid, surface-level learning approaches (Pekrun et al., 2002). Negative deactivating emotions (e.g., boredom, hopelessness), on the other hand, are characterized by low arousal, passivity, and withdrawal from the task altogether, and consistently show the most detrimental effects on motivation, self-regulation, and achievement (Pekrun, 2024). Sociocultural theory Although academic emotions are often conceptualized at the individual level, sociocultural theory provides a lens for understanding how these emotions are shaped through social interactions in the classroom. Sociocultural theory, as developed by Vygotsky (1978), emphasizes the importance of social interactions and cultural contexts in shaping individuals’ cognitive development and learning. The theory suggests that learning is inherently a social and cultural process. From a sociocultural perspective, emotions can be viewed as both individual and social phenomena, without separating them into different realms or aspects (Sánchez, 2014). In other words, the sociocultural perspective implies examining emotions as socially situated rather than purely internal s tates. This viewpoint suggests that the interactions between teachers and students in the classroom play an important role in shaping students' emotional experiences. An ecological dynamic systems synthesis of classroom emotion research by Savina and Fulton (2024) reinforces this position by identifying three immediate contexts in which emotions are generated and shaped in the classroom. The first is the emotionally expressive environment, in which teachers and students continuously elicit and respond to each other's emotional expressions, including through nonverbal cues, such as physical proximity, facial expressions, and body orientation, which create conditions for emotional contagion and affective mimicry. The second is the learning and instructional context, in which the nature of tasks, students' learning behaviors, and teachers' instructional actions intersect to produce emotions; for instance, unclear instruction or tasks that exceed students' perceived control can give rise to frustration and boredom, whereas supportive instructional behaviors promote positive emotions. The third is the relational context, in which the 120 quality of teacher-student relationships shapes students’ emotional baseline.  Students in warm, close relationships with their teachers experience more positive emotions and less anxiety and boredom, but conflictual or distant relationships are associated  with more negative emotional experiences. These three contexts can be active simultaneously during a classroom session where students’ emotional experiences are shaped by the expressiveness of the classroom environment, the instructional demands of the tasks, and the quality of their moment-to-moment interactions with their teacher, including how physically close that teacher is to them. Proxemic theory Within these social interactions, teacher-student physical proximity serves as an important component of classroom communication. Proxemic theory, as proposed by Hall (1968), examines the spatial relationships and distances between individuals in social in teractions. According to the theory, the physical distance between individuals influences their interpersonal dynamics (Hall, 1968; Miller, 1988). For example, in a classroom setting, proximity between a teacher and students is linked to the quality of interactions, perceptions of support, and students’ emotional experiences (Miller, 1988). Proximity is likely to have a role in students’ feelings in class (Hall, 1968; Miller, 1988), attitudes toward academic tasks (Dong et al., 2021; Marshall & Losonczy-Marshall, 2010), and classroom participation (Meeks et al., 2013). Shapiro et al. (2024) proposed that teachers’ movement through the classroom space reflects and enacts their responsiveness to students’ needs, ideas, and emotional states. Moreover, research on teacher nonverbal immediacy, a construct that operationalizes physical and psychological closeness between teachers and students, has found that physical proximity and body orientation are associated with students’ situational emotional engagement (De Loof et al., 2025) and that teacher immediacy behaviors more broadly predict student academic outcomes (Özdaş, 2022). The framework of different zones of proximity was developed by Hall (1968), which examines how people perceive and use physical and social space across different cultures, including “intimate,” “personal,” “social,” and “public” zones. Miller (1988) viewed proximity as a non-verbal cue that can communicate teacher attitudes and influence student performance and feelings. Kale (2008) adopted Hall’s proximity zones as a coding scheme and found that classroom interactions were more likely to occur at certain p roximity zones. Most other prior work examines physical proximity based on classroom seating arrangements instead (e.g., front row, back row) to measure its association with students’ performance in college-level courses with little instructor movement (e.g., Marshall & Losonczy-Marshall, 2010; Meeks et al., 2013). In the context of K-12 classrooms, more teacher movements may be expected to occur. Therefore, we adopted Hall's (1968) framework of proximity zones. In our study, we examined the bidirectional relationship between physical proximity and student-reported emotions while students use the math game, ST Math.

## Literature review

Academic emotions in the classroom and in technology environments Prior research has indicated that students’ academic emotions are linked to learning processes and outcomes, including motivation, engagement, and achievement (Pekrun et al., 2012; Tan et al., 2021). These emotions vary across individuals and contexts, shaped by factors such as students’ prior experiences, self-concept, and perceptions of task difficulty and value (Goetz et al., 2010). Emotions are also domain-specific. For example, students may experience different emotional patterns in mathematics compared to language-based subjects due to differences in perceived difficulty, value, and self-efficacy (Goetz et al., 2011). Classroom environments may further shape emotional experiences. Instructional practices, peer dynamics, and classroom climate can influence whether students experience more activating emotions (e.g., excitement) or deactivating emotions (e.g., boredom; Goetz et al., 2011; Pekrun, 2006). In technology-mediated learning environments, emotional experiences may differ from those in traditional classrooms. Although some studies suggest that digital learning environments can promote positive emotions (Loderer et al., 2020), students may still experience negative emotions, such as boredom or confusion, particularly when tasks are misaligned with t heir skill levels (Acee et al., 2010). These this growing body of research, less is known about how teachers’ in-the-moment behaviors, such as physical proximity, relate to students’ emotional experiences during technology use. Understanding these dynamics is critical for clarifying how teacher presence continues to shape learning in increasingly technology-mediated classrooms.

121 Teacher-student interactions Teacher-student interactions may be an important part of students’ school experiences, as they serve a variety of purposes in the classroom, including instructional, organizational, and emotional support (Pianta & Hamre, 2009). For example, teachers can offer instructional support by giving clear feedback to students, creating opportunities for conceptual thinking, and modeling new vocabulary. Studies have shown that students who receive more support from teachers have more positive academic emotions (e.g.,  enjoyment and interest) and fewer negative emotions (e.g., anxiety and boredom) (e.g., Ahmed et al., 2010). Moreover, instructional behaviors, the ways instructional support is carried out, were also associated with students' emotions. These behaviors can  directly shape students’ perceptions of control and value, which have been shown to be linked to emotional experiences (Bieg et al., 2013; Pekrun, 2006). For instance, providing clear explanations and becoming more engaging when teaching can help promote positive control and value appraisals, respectively (Bieg et al, 2013). Prior work confirmed that both of these instructional behaviors are related to students’ positive emotions in different subjects, such as mathematics and physics (e.g., Goetz et al., 2 013). Conversely, students are more likely to experience negative emotions when teachers provide unclear explanations or communication (Mazer, 2014). Teacher-student proximity can play a role in this. Being in closer proximity to students may allow teacher s to implement more effective instructional practices as they can be more audible and become attuned to gaps in student understanding, which can promote students' positive emotions.

Alternatively, teachers’ actions can also be influenced by students’ emotions, as teachers respond to the emotional needs of students in their classrooms (Jennings & Greenberg, 2009). Teachers may provide emotional support to students by being attentive, understanding, caring, and mindful of students' needs and interests (Pianta & Hamre, 2009). Emotional support has been shown to be beneficial in math classes, with students showing higher emotional engagement, academic effort, and social engagement when the y feel the emotional support and care from their teachers (Sakiz et al., 2012). The term “Emotional Scaffolding” has been coined to describe such support (Meyer & Turner, 2007). Stemming from the scaffolding component of Vygotsky’s zone of proximal development (Vygotsky, 1978), emotional scaffolding is defined as teachers fostering students’ positive emotional experiences to benefit learning and growth (Meyer & Turner, 2007). The Meyer and Turner (2007) emotional scaffolding framework proposed that students  need to receive temporary support from teachers’ scaffolding of their emotions when they are in urgent need of that assistance. Therefore, teachers may approach students exhibiting frustration, anxiety, or low confidence to provide emotional scaffolding. The physical proximity between teachers and students may be one observable manifestation of this emotional support. During class, teachers navigate closeness to or distance from individual students. For example, teachers may intentionally approach students they believe are struggling or feeling frustrated to provide them with targeted assistance. Similarly, students exhibiting signs of disengagement may cause teachers to move closer to engage them (Etscheidt et al., 1984; Guther et al., 1995; Weaver et al.,  2020). However, as the teachers move closer to some students, teachers will be at a farther physical distance from some other students. This may inadvertently limit emotional support for other students. Our work examined in-the-moment teacher-student interactions through the lens of physical proximity. This perspective highlights the need to observe and analyze the physical distance between teachers and students during real-time interactions. Mapping cross-time changes in proximity to student emotions can expand the area of optimal teacher responsiveness. ## Context

The current study is embedded in a larger project studying the mathematics learning software, ST Math, which is used by over one million students across the United States. The project is funded both by the authors’ university and by the National Science Foundation. Project researchers collected classroom data as students interacted with 122 the mathematics learning platform, ST Math, as implemented during the 2022-2023 school year in a school district in the mid-Atlantic region of the United States. ST Math is an educational game-like online supplemental curriculum that students use on their computers or tablets throughout the school year, typically for an average of 90 minutes per week. ST Math use has been shown to be positively associated with mathematics scores and student self-beliefs (Rutherford et al., 2019).

## Sample and participants

The participants included 92 students from grades four and five across two public elementary schools within the classes of 10 teachers, each teaching one class, and two additional co-teachers; the co-taught class was treated separately for analysis. Eight teachers taught at School A and four teachers at School B. The school district comprises 7,826 students enrolled in public schools, 52.1% of whom are boys. The district serves a relatively diverse student body; 92% of students identify as a single race, and 8% identify as multi-racial. Of the single-race responses, 33% identify as White, 43.5% as Black, 3.5% as Asian, and 11.7% as other races (National Center for Education Statistics, 2023).

The study adhered to ethical guidelines for research involving human subjects; an IRB was secured from the authors’ university. All participants voluntarily participated in the study and provided informed consent. For students, parental or guardian consent was also obtained. Participating students typically used ST Math either in group rotation or individually at their desks as a whole class. The group rotation was based on predetermined schedules given by the teacher, typically shifting every 15 minutes. When students were not on an ST Math rotation, they would work with the teacher or an assistant on other math-related content. Teachers would sometimes walk around the class giving students individual attention; some teachers asked that students with questions approach their desks for help, forming a line when other students were already being helped. In other cases, teacher assistants helped with student questions related to ST Math while the teachers either conducted other work or assisted other students. In both group rotation and whole class approaches, students used ST Math individually.

Each observation, conducted by teams of three undergraduate and/or graduate research assistants, captured teacher proximity and actions and lasted 18 –51 minutes. The observation involved creating classroom layouts (or using pre-created layouts), tracing teacher movement with timestamps, and synchroniz ing to map locations. These actions were recorded in real-time as they occurred by researchers on paper maps of the classroom. Senior researchers trained each team through on-site supervision for at least three sessions. Hands-on training continued until teams could reliably conduct observations independently. Students were given experience-sampling method (ESM; Csikszentmihalyi & Larson, 2014)  surveys that consisted of tomato-like emojis called “tomojis,” which visually represented the emotion listed on a tomato's face. To capture emotional variation, experience sampling measures were administered at a fixed interval and at the beginning of each observation. Prior to data collection, students were trained on how to use the emotion form. Students were asked to fill out one set of emotion entries as soon as they received the survey, and up to eight additional sets based on how much time was observed in the lesson. The additional sets were timed every five minutes by researchers, at which time students were asked to choose as many emotions as they were feeling at the time (a maximum of seven) from the following: bored, confused, frustrated, nervous, excited, happy, interested in the form of Tomojis (see Rutherford et al., 2021, Figure 1). This allowed continuous tracking of academic emotions over time and in response to classroom events (see Hektner et al., 2007). Figure 1 Tomoji Form 123 Note. ## Data analysis

Teacher-student proximity data were coded using Hall’s (1968) framework relevant to each student with consent who was observed (see Table 1). Two researchers independently coded the proximity data to reflect the appropriate category at each observation tim estamp. Only teacher actions within two minutes before/after ESM surveys were matched to surveys in order to capture the proximity closest in time to emotions (see Figure 2). After coding two to three teachers, researchers conducted consensus meetings to r eview codes, resolve any discrepancies, and agree on final consensus codes. In addition to the final consensus codes, the original codes from each coder were retained to calculate interrater reliability (IRR) using Cohen’s kappa, with a value of k = 0.804 indicating a substantial level of agreement between coders (McHugh, 2012). The dataset consisted of 2,338 momentary observations from 92 students. At the within-person level (Level 1), the outcome variables included teachers' physical proximity to students, treated as a continuous variable, and seven binary-coded emotional states: bored, confused, excited, frustrated, happy, interested, and nervous. We used multilevel vector autoregression modeling (Bringmann et al., 2013) to examine temporal dynamics among student emotions and teacher proximity. Multilevel vector autoregression was selected because it allows modeling of within-person temporal dynamics and bidirectional relationships while accounting for the nested structure of repeated observations within students. The model examined how variables predict themselves and each other over time while accounting for the nested structure of repeated observations within students (Bringmann et al., 2013). The model included two levels. At the within-person level (Level 1), we specified three types of temporal relationships for each student's moment-to-moment fluctuations: (a) autoregressive effects, where each variable predicted itself one moment later (e.g., bored at time t predicting bored at time t + 1), capturing temporal stability or “carry-over”; (b) cross-lagged effects where one variable at time t  predicted a different variable at time t  + 1 (e.g., proximity at time t predicting frustrated at time t +1), testing temporal precedence among emotions; and (c) contemporaneous residual correlations among all variables at the same time point, accounting for shared variance not explained by lagged predictors. We specified bidirectional cross-lagged paths between all emotions and teacher-student proximity to examine whether emotions drew teachers closer and whether proximity influenced subsequent emotions. We also included theory-driven cross-lagged paths based on 124 prior research (D'Mello & Graesser, 2012), specifically testing: confusion predicting frustration, frustration predicting boredom and nervousness, and bidirectional confusion-boredom relationships. However, for this paper, we only reported the findings on the reciprocal relationship between proximity and reported emotions. At the between-person level (Level 2), we estimated random intercepts for all variables, which allows individuals to differ in their average levels of each emotion and typical teacher proximity. For binary emotion variables, random intercepts were specified as thresholds in the probit model, representing individual differences in the propensity to endorse each emotion. We assessed the model convergence using the potential scale reduction (PSR) statistic, which compares variability between chains to variability within chains. PSR values close to 1.0 indicate that chains have converged to the same distribution. Following standard recommendations, we considered PSR < 1.10 as indicating adequate convergence (Gelman et al., 2013). We present results organized by research question (RQ). All models converged successfully (highest PSR = 1.096 at iteration 10,000).

Our study investigated the dynamic, bidirectional relationship between teacher proximity and student emotions during technology-based math lessons. The findings reveal an interplay where teachers were responsive to certain emotional cues (i.e., frustration, excitement, and confusion). Our results align with research on teachers’ emotional support (Pianta & Hamre, 2009), which emphasizes the ability of effective educators to respond to student needs. We found that teachers moved closer to frustrated students, potentially demonstrating “emotional scaffolding,” which may be important for supporting students in learning (Meyer & Turner, 2007). However, this proximity response did not extend to boredom. Teachers seemed to be farther from bored students, which may be concerning, as boredom has negative relationships with outcomes, such as academic performance and self-regulated learning. (e.g., Tze et al. 2013). This pattern may also align with the Control-Value Theory of achievement emotions (Pekrun, 2006), which distinguishes between activating and deactivating emotions. Activating emotions (e.g., excitement) often involves visible effort and engagement, which perhaps makes it easier for teachers to detect and interpret them as cues for instructional or emotional support (D’Mello & Graesser, 2012). In contrast, boredom is a deactivating emotion typically characterized by passivity and disengagement, which may not elicit immediate teacher response.

Interestingly, we found that closer teacher presence was associated with less subsequent student boredom. This may align with studies on the link between physical proximity and student engagement and participation and can non-verbally communicate a sense of care and togetherness (Plante & Asselin, 2014). These facilitates individualized learning, the teacher’s role as a facilitator providing support may be critical. Even though this work highlights the importance of proximity, its limitations include a reliance on self-reported emotions, which may manifest differently than observed emotions. Additionally, proximity was operationalized as physical distance and does not  capture the underlying meaning of teacher proximity (e.g., instructional support, or behavioral control). It is likely that the effect of proximity depends on teachers’ actions and instructional approaches. More support from teachers, for example, can promote positive emotions and lower the likelihood of students’ negative emotions (Ahmed et al., 2010). In our study, we observed teachers’ actions as well, but there were not sufficient data points to be meaningfully analyzed and fit into our models within the current study, which should be examined in future research. This study contributes to understanding the association between teacher proximity and student emotions during technology-mediated mathematics learning. Using moment-to-moment data and multilevel vector autoregression, we found that teachers moved closer when students reported being frustrated, confused, or nervous, but teachers tended to be farther away when students were bored, even though closer proximity predicted lower subsequent boredom. These results highlight the importance of teacher movement as both a behavioral and emotional scaffold that supports engagement in individualized digital learning contexts. Future research should further explore how teachers can strategically balance physical closeness across students and how other interactions can sust ain emotional engagement in technology-rich classrooms.
