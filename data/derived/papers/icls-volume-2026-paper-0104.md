# From Exposure to Encounter: Methodological Reflections Concerning Sociotechnical Politics of Visibility for Black Learners

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This conceptual paper introduces exposure as a sociotechnical and racializing process that mediates how Black learners are (mis)represented in educational video analysis. We argue that visibility is not a neutral act but a historically and politically cons tructed phenomenon shaped by technologies normed to whiteness—video methodologies, when uncritically applied, risk reproducing deficit narratives and objectifying Black knowledge-making. In response, we propose encounter as a methodological provocation—an ethical, relational stance toward video analysis that turns inquiry inward to understand how Black learners' strategies for navigating visibility. Through reflections on design contexts involving Black girls' digital photography and Black women YouTube content creators, we examine how exposure shapes our methodological approaches to understanding Black knowledge-making. Ultimately, our provocation for video-analytic approaches, which are accountable and relational, and that affirm Black learners' ways of knowing, is a methodological contribution to ISLS 2026's theme "Partnering with Purpose."

That the field of representation remains a place of struggle is most evident when we critically examine contemporary representations of Blackness and black people. (hooks, 1992, p. 3) The political, economic, and ideological function of representations of Blackness within a white-supremacist, capitalist, patriarchal society is a problem Black feminist scholar bell hooks (1992) succinctly names in Black Looks: Race and Representation. While hooks (1992) discusses media portrayals of Black individuals and communities, her insights illuminate the broader ways in which implicit deficit narratives often undergird representational ecologies. We see a connection between how educational research is produced and contributes to representational ecologies of Black lives and Black knowledge-making through the enactment of video analytic activity that researchers use as data and have become commonplace within the learning sciences (e.g., De Leima et al., 2023). Across varied video-methodological traditions, there is a shared understanding that while video offers many affordances for close analyses of learning, video analysis is laden with the theoretical work of rendering the video phenomena legible (e.g., Hall, 2012; Ochs, 1979; Tobin, 2019). However, less discussed in the theory and practice of video-analytic methodologies are the politics of visual representation and the history of anti-Blackness (a broad antagonistic relationship between Blackness and the possibility of humanity) that are endemic in society (Bell, 1992/2018) and in educational research (Dumas & Ross, 2016). As we point to anti-Black processes of representations which refuse Black humanity, we suggest attunement to Black learners' processes of navigating these forces as self-definition –an "existential practice, self-definition is a process; a deliberate warring of deconstruction and reconstruction, vis-à-vis, translating needs into demands" (Okello, 2018, p. 255).

Before beginning this conceptual piece, we use this introduction to locate our positionality within this work. We are Black women, mothers, daughters, and academics, which orients us in this work. Group knowledge of Black women often points to the importance of multifaceted legacies of struggle (Collins, 2020). This group offering, which dares to move beyond dominating methodologies toward collective liberation, emerged from the place of struggle. We write to think through what it means to make space for Bl ack learners and Black learning while engaging in the very tools that render us both hyper and invisible. I (Nicollette) remain ambivalent to the privileging of video analytic tradition that elevates researchers above the researched in search of 'truth'. N aming this discomfort as my encounter with anti-Blackness, reasoning through it, and envisioning an otherwise, outside of processes of domination has been life-giving. Thinking collaboratively, I (Natalie) write to think through ways in which Black expressive cultures are modalities of Black peoples' reconstructions of what it means to be human in a world that denies our humanity. I consider Black expressive cultures to have immense contributions to my work as an educational researcher committed to connecti ng with and contributing to the vitality and history of 928 Black knowledge-making. We submit with work as a part of the practice of Black Feminist self-definition, that Black people might be more fully seen and known through our writing. As the learning sciences took on the mantle of engaging more explicitly in the politics of learning (e.g., Political of Learning Collective, 2017), some video analytic traditions were elaborated to emphasize their utility in understanding how learning, identity, and power are co-constituted in interaction, locating the micro-scale as a politically viable site of intervention (Philip & Gupta, 2020). Thus, some threads of video-analytic methods in the learning sciences have established concepts and frameworks  for engaging with the politics inherent in analyzing learning through viewing video records. Even so, few of these analytic traditions engage the particular trouble of viewing Blackness explicitly – the historically uneven viewing relationship that often locates Black peoples and communities as objects for others' consumption, pleasure, and violation (Hartman, 1997; hooks, 1992). Our concern is theoretical and empirical; it is not as if educational research is missing empirical examples of the ways that Black learners navigate issues of representation and visibility within the classroom; for instance, Wright et al. (2018) examined Black children's strategies for negotiating practices of scientific argumentation, and the dangers of being attributed as causing "commotion" by their white teacher. In this research project, the researchers noticed that Black elementary students in this classroom were not performing collective reflection decision-making – a practice associated with the professional conduct of engineers that researchers aimed to cultivate within elementary students to support their engagement with disciplinary practices. These students' conceptions of what it meant to be "doers of engineering" in these STEM learning opportunities were "also informed by previous experiences at [their school] that privileged compliance and docility" (p. 296). Importantly, the authors reframed students' participation as "strategies for managing risks" to "interrupt the deficit perspectives that are often associated with students within urban schools" (p. 286), where these students were not meeting expectations for this engineering practice. The inclusion of interviews in which students offered insights into their approaches and video recordings of classroom interactions provided evidence to shift attention to how students were responding to the risks and challenges of engaging in collaborative de cision-making. Black learners' actions and non-actions are at risk for misinterpretations, as when Black learners' silence is misrecognized as non-engagement in recorded classroom settings. When this approach is taken, relying solely on the record does not provide insight into the conditions that produce silence. However, research on Black girls' silence in educational settings (Brown, 2013; Mizell & McCoy, 2022; Nxumalo, 2021) points to how Black girls' non-verbal performance is a strategy to navigate racial-gender dynamics in learning settings. While a review of the record may reveal no substantive verbal or gestural contributions, research emphasizes these moments as rich sites of meaning, shaped by racialized forms of viewing. Relying primarily on participants' (in)actions on the video or audio recording as evidence of learning is a weak empirical tool for analysts, particularly when making assertions about Black learners' knowledge and the strategies they deploy to navigate racialized and gendered politics of visibility and presence in settings.

As advances in video technologies have facilitated the ubiquity of video-analytic methods, communities have developed different orientations toward video recording. Specifically, the commercial prevalence of videorecording technology within consumer devices like phones and laptops has also been aligned with a growing presence of corporate and state surveillance (Browne, 2015). Differentially impacted by this intensification of surveillance and monitoring, Black communities face multiple vulnerabilities for being overpoliced, as the use of biased-algorithmic technologies virtually misidentifies Black people. The contradictory reality of being both hypervisible in cases of policing and monitoring (Benjamin, 2019; Harry, 2014) intersects with the technological capacities of the video-making and video-sharing ecologies. Importantly, Black learners are navigating these realities in the classroom, a process that is further complicated when they choose to participate in video-recorded educational research (Vossoughi & Escudé, 2016).

Our critical examination of these empirical examples across educational research exemplifies hooks’

(1992) assertion that representation remains a place of struggle, Black learners negotiate the process of being seen

in learning settings, and that the pres ence of videorecording technology complicates this process. This paper develops a lens to understand exposure as the interweaving of historical injustices, technical functionality, and agentic responses to how Black learners are made visible or invisible in educational video analysis. Exposure is not merely the act of being seen; it is a historically and politically constructed condition shaped by technologies, institutions, and ideologies that mediate visibility. We propose that video methodologies must interrogate the politics of visibility and the processes of representation that shape what is seen, how it is interpreted, and whose knowledge is recognized. To develop this argument, we first conceptualize exposure as a sociotechnical process of domination. We then point to Black feminist self-definition as a useful conceptualization to underscore how Black people navigate racialized processes of exposure. 929

## Conceptual framework: Exposure as a site of contestation, encounter as a

practice of relational In this section, we weave together literature in science and technology studies and Black studies —an interdisciplinary field that understands history, politics, and cultures of Africandiasporic communities —to problematize Black learners' relationship to the video camera. Rather than treating the experience of video recording as neutral, commonplace, or having negligible effects, our goal is to contextualize video recording technologies within educational research as part of the same sociocultural context as other technologies. This locates Black individuals' participation in educational research within longer genealogies of resistance. Exposure as a sociotechnical mediated process of domination From Merriam-Webster (2025), exposure is defined as a noun referring to: The fact or condition of being exposed, such as: the condition of being subject to some effect or influence. the condition of being unprotected … the condition of being at risk … the condition of being presented to view or made known. The act or an instance of exposing, such as: the treatment of sensitized material (such as film) to controlled amounts of radiant energy. Merriam-Webster's (2025) definition of exposure provides a generative starting point for thinking through an interwoven set of processes and conditions. Different disciplines offer conceptual resources for engaging with exposure. Where a physical scientist can treat exposure as a form of energy transformation to understand changes in physical systems, a visual artist might view the aesthetic experience of exposing cyanotype to light as a form of cooperation between materials and radiant energy. Exposure is manufactured, in the sense that controlling the amount of radiant energy incident on a material sensitized to it is happening. While exposure in film and photography refers to the technical calibration of light, in educational research, exposure is a contested site where Black learners are rendered visible. Thus, we can see that the condition of being presented or made known is not a one-sided, compulsory imposition, but a process by which people navigate processes of visibility. This distinction is importa nt to emphasize, as it is informed by experiences, strategies, and choices within a given context.

We caution against using exposure as an overly deterministic, top-down theory of hegemony; instead, describing exposure as a process of domination is meant to draw our attention to the sociopolitical strategies and active practices deployed by Black people to navigate exposure. This resists the mechanisms of damage-centered analyses of Black lives and communities. Instead, it emphasizes how Black people's ways of knowing, being, and learning are always in response to but not wholly defined by racism. Insight into the strategies Black communities use to navigate anti-Black racism enables analysts to draw connections between Black individuals' practices and the longstanding genealogies of struggle. In this way, exposure both points to the historical quality of domination and to the histories of resistance and struggle in response to relations of domination. Visibility is not neutral; it is historically and politically constructed To understand exposure as a process of domination, we must interrogate the mechanisms through which visibility is produced and regulated. In Race after Technology, Benjamin (2019) provocatively names race a technology— a human-made tool that reliably organizes the social world, stratified by notions of humandifference. Through this framing, no technology can evade how anti-Black racism orders social reality. The analytical processes Benjamin (2019) uses allow readers to resist the ahistorical rendering of technologies as new, novel, and thus removed from historical inequities, for example, the strategy to examine seemingly disparate technological tools (i.e., automatic soap dispensers and facial recognition exam-proctoring software) together to see inequitable systems, not just isolated technological tools. These analytical moves locate the mechanisms of technological tools within legal codes and processes of power. Benjamin's (2019) conceptualization of race as a technology informs our conceptualization of exposure.

When automatic soap dispensers and facial recognition exam-proctoring software, for example, are understood within the same conceptual frames, the sociotechnical tools that mediate visibility can be understood as part of a material infrastructure in which whiteness is profitably normative. Benjamin's (2019) examples of visual technologies – from contemporary technologies such as digital facial recognition to Kodak's "Shirley Cards" or film standardization cards in use in the mid twentieth century, which were biased towards lighter skin tones – are calibrated to whiteness, down to their computational, physical, and mechanical operations. As a result, 930 how Blackness is made less visible, or hyper-visible, is mediated by sociotechnical systems fundamentally normed to whiteness, where whiteness is coded as the default. Narratives about what gets recognized as beautiful, healthy, or normal are fundamentally encoded into technical processes (e.g., in algorithmically determined beauty contests) with and through racist, sexist, and ableist logics (Benjamin, 2019). In education, facial recognition exam-proctoring software was instituted in response to the novel coronavirus-19, with poor outcomes for nonwhite learners (Logan, 2020). In fact, research has identified how these students are more likely to be labeled as cheating because of the issue of training data normed to white individuals. This connection signals how processes of exposure function within social, political, and economic systems that shape opportunities for Black people and communities. When facial recognition technology is dispersed to all facets of daily life, from personal devices, exam proctoring, criminal detection algorithms, or citizenship determinations, the adverse consequences span Black individuals' concerns of privacy, surveillance, racial profiling, eugenics, and citizenship (Browne, 2015; Noble, 2018). We argue that when exposure is a phenomenon organized by domination, learning analysts attune to the processes that organize exposure. This understanding of exposure accounts for how historical legacies of dispossession shape the relations through which video production and analysis occur. This is contrary to the work that aims to understand the orderliness of social activity through empirical engagement with the record. Exposure, as a sociotechnical process, is the mechanism through which visibility is regulated. Technologies like facial recognition software do not simply 'see' —they also expose subjects to processes of surveillance and misrecognition, particularly when calibrated to whiteness. When we locate these technologies in the same analytic frame as the educational researchers’ recording technologies, exposure can explain how Black learners experience processes of hypervisibility, invisibility, and misrecognition. Analysts’ methodological choices are implicated within processes of domination; when video data is treated as a primary empirical source, it risks reinforcing white normative frames of visibility, misinterpretations of silence or non-verbal cues, and ignoring historical and relational dimensions of Black knowledge-making. Video analytic methodologies that do not consider the strategies Black individuals use tonegotiate exposure risk objectify Black knowledge-making practices. Here, objectification refers to video analytic practices that take on detached forms of observation — looking at Black knowledge and Black people — as if we were static objects of inquiry. When we, as researchers and analysts, take the video camera as a means of constructing a site of contestation, this opens  different ways of relating to our participants and of encountering Black knowledge-making.

Ethical relation to Black knowledge-making: Black aesthetics In the preceding sections, we argued that exposure —understood as a racializing, sociotechnical process — structures how Black learners are made visible or invisible in educational research. Here, we turn to Black expressive cultures to propose an alternative  framework for engaging ethically with Black knowledge-making. Rather than treating Black aesthetics as peripheral or decorative, we position them as intellectual traditions that offer critical insight into the politics of visibility and the ethics of encounter. Black expressive production as intellectual genealogies of thought and action Black feminist thought (Collins, 2016; 2022) refers to a collective genealogy of Black women's and Black nonbinary, agender, and gender diverse peoples' thought and action opposing the myriad, intersecting forms of domination on our lives and our communities. Collins (1990/2022) discusses at length how stereotypes of Black women function as controlling images. These political, economic, and ideological representations serve racist, sexist societies and are externally imposed on Black women specifically and Black communities in general. Within Black feminist thought, attention to Black processes of self-definition attunes us to how Black people inquire into forms of self-valuation and move to author themselves outside of imposed representational grammar. In t his way, Black expressive traditions —art, film, music, photography—are not merely cultural artifacts but epistemic sites that theorize and contest dominant visual processes. These traditions have long grappled with the politics of representation, offering strategies for resisting objectification and asserting self-definition. As bell hooks (1992) writes, “representation remains a place of struggle,” and Black expressive works are central to this struggle, shaping how Blackness is seen, interpreted, and responded to. While traditional video-analytic methods offer tools for understanding interactional order, they must be expanded to account for the affective, historical, and political dimensions of Black learners' visibility. To substantiate this argument, we can turn toward contemporary ethnographic work in anthropology, a field of shared origins for video analytic practices in the learning sciences. Steven P. Black's (2017) analysis of experiences conducting fieldwork with a Zulu gospel choir composed of people living with HIV in South Africa was a meditation on the communicative affordances of different recording devices. Black's (2017) work highlights how 931 decisions about the choice to capture voices, faces, and bodies interact with the participants' understandings of anonymity that differ from definitions established by institutional review boards, in which disclosure interacts with trauma long after the in quiry. Transduction (in sound anthropology) refers to the transformation of sensory input into data, but also implies ethical choices about what and how to record. Black (2017) points to work within sound anthropology that locates transduction as a necessarily creative endeavor (e.g., Feld & Brenneis, 2004) – where "creative" is in reference to a freedom that comes from limiting the epistemic authority of audio-and videorecords as "objective" data. These decisions are not merely technical but deeply relat ional, shaping how researchers come to know and be accountable to their participants. Black's (2017) work underscores that the selection and practice of researchers' transduction involve relational practices. This is discontinuous with videoanalytic traditions, which ground claims in the study of naturally unfolding interaction through forms of disinterested viewing. This is not meant to comment on the validity of those approaches, but to signify the different epistemic and axiological orientations to knowledge production. Much of the established traditions of videoanalytic inquiry in the fields of learning sciences (De Leima et al., 2023) and CSCL (e.g., Uttamchandani & Lester, 2021) take up these epistemic orientations to different degrees, raising a necessary question about their suitability for this form of inquiry.

Building on this, we turn to Black expressive cultures to explore how Black visual artists are reframing the viewing relationship. A longstanding history of Black art and film studies that deeply engage with ontoepistemic questions of what it means to come to know Blackness. Black studies, particularly Black arts, emphasize the generative potential of examining Black visibility as a site of struggle. While Black art and Black film are "fictional," these expressive modalities offer much for understanding Black ways of knowing and being in navigating visuality. Drawing on such intellectual genealogies to unpack how Black learners understand and negotiate visibility in learning settings would be a generative way to affirm and cultivate Black ways of knowing on our own terms.

These artists reject the idea of the camera as a neutral tool. Instead, they explore how visual media can foster ethical relationships with Blackness. For example, Black feminist art scholar Tina Campt's (2021) book A Black Gaze examines how Black artists produce visual art meant to structure different forms of viewing relationships with Blackness. Rather than "seeing" Blackness, Campt argues that these artists aim to produce art that disrupts dominant forms of looking towards "looking with, through, and alongside" Blackness (2021, p. 8)— looking alongside treats the viewing relationship as an encounter. Encounters reject the way that viewing Blackness as an unmediated reality happens within a system that normalizes whiteness. Campt's provocation is generative for examining contemporary Black visual art, such as RaMell Ross'

(2024) film Nickel Boys, a film following the friendship between two Black boys imprisoned within a reformatory

school taking place in the early twentieth century, based on Colson Whitehead's (2019) novel of the same name dramatizing the horrors of the real Arthur G. Dozier School for Boys operating from 1900 to 2011 in Florida. Shot from a first-person point of view, Ross's directorial choices resist the audience's easy engagement with the protagonists, strategically employing disorienting camera perspectives to frustrate the normative viewing relationship. The director's choices around the viewer's perspective become especially significant during scenes in the film involving moments of physical abuse of these children, to which Ross refuses visual exposure, instead using time jumps, still images, and discordant sounds. These choices disrupt the normativity of a gaze that objectifies Blackness; what Campt (202 1) calls "looking at" Blackness in disembodied, unaccountable, consumptive practices. Instead, Ross's (2024) aesthetic selections require viewers to engage with experiencing these scenes that involve looking with and through Blackness, involving active engagements of feeling and sensemaking, which has the effect of feeling alongside the precarities that make these Black boys vulnerable to violence, where violence is not momentary or episodic, but embodied, life-long, and generational. Ross's refusal to visually depict violence invites viewers into a relational mode of witnessing —one that parallels the ethical stance we advocate in educational video analysis. This example illustrates how ethical decisions about recording are inseparable from the politics of exposure.

How can video analysis recognize, affirm, and sustain Black knowledge-making that resists and defends against oppression? We argue that considering the racialized politics of exposure as a sociotechnical and sociopolitical process requires us to locate the viewing relationship as the object of study, not the recorded media, and to focus on the politics of how, when sociotechnically mediated, coming to know Blackness can never be neutral. This move locates Black knowledge-making within emergent and longstanding collective strategies of Black communities. We also emphasize that this knowledge is historical, rooted in a tradition of Black people's self-definition, and collective in nature, even if individually articulated, the expressive enunciation of strategi es. 932 Reflections on design contexts In this section, we discuss the ways we have engaged with this conceptualization of exposure and encounter to create opportunities to ethically come into relation with Black learners' forms of knowledge. In the previous section, we explored how Black expressive cultures offer generative tools for inquiry into how Black learners navigate exposure as a sociopolitical process. The previous section highlights two contexts by which these concepts have offered us valuable methodological insights into practices of  encounter, constructing ways of looking alongside Black learners and Black knowledge-making. First, we describe the settings of our inquiry and some methodological insights we have developed from them. In this conceptual paper, we develop the concept of exposure to interrogate how video recording in educational research participates in sociotechnical processes of racialization and domination. Exposure, as we have argued, is not merely the act of being seen—it is a historically and politically constructed condition shaped by technologies, institutions, and ideologies that mediate visibility. When visibility is normed to whiteness, video methodologies risk reproducing deficit narratives and objectifying Black knowledge-making. To counter this, we propose encounter as a methodological provocation. Encounter is not simply an alternative to visibility—it is a relational stance that reorients the researcher's gaze from detached observation to ethical engagement. Where visibility may  render Black learners legible within dominant frames, encounter demands a mode of inquiry that affirms Black learners' self-definition, expressive agency, and collective creativity. This shift foregrounds the politics of the viewing relationship itself as  the object of study. Our goal through video analysis cannot merely be visibility; instead, such methods challenge the structure of power that determines what can and cannot be seen (Hammonds, 1994). Encounter, however, emphasizes the need to consider video-analytic methodologies as ways for analysts to enter into ethical relations with Black communities, lives, and knowledge-making.

There are several implications for video-analytic methodology. First, researchers must critically examine how their analytic practices —such as clip selection, transcription, and interpretation —are shaped by sociotechnical regimes of exposure. Treating vide o as self-evident data risks reinforcing white normative frames and misrecognizing Black learners' silence, gestures, or aesthetic choices. Instead, encounters invite methodological shifts, such as co-selecting video artifacts with participants, including participant-produced media, and reflexive annotation practices that foreground positionality and relational accountability. Second, encounter aligns with Black feminist epistemologies that center self-definition as a practice of resistance and world-making. As Okello (2018, 2024) and Collins (1990/2022) emphasize, Black knowledge-making cannot be understood through frameworks that objectify or extract. Researchers must relinquish control over meaning-making and co-construct knowledge in ways that honor Black expressive traditions. This requires a reimagining of video analysis —not as a tool for capturing behavior, but as a site for ethical relation, historical accountability, and collective liberation. Such work would complement research exploring Black learners' creativity as integral to learning settings (Brown, 2013; Griffin, 2022; Mims et al., 2022) and as ways of knowing tied to political genealogies of thought and action (Rae, 2025). While not always drawing on video technologies, generative work exploring Black girls' future-making, writing, and new literacies engages with questions about coming into ethical relation to Blac k girls' knowledge-making (e.g., Turner & Griffin, 2020). Video analytic methodologies can benefit from treating the video record itself as a modality for Black girls' intellectual, cultural, and creative development.

Finally, for the learning sciences, these insights offer a pathway toward justice-oriented research design. By locating exposure as a site of contestation and encounter as a practice of ethical engagement, scholars can better attune to the lived experiences of Black learners and the historical conditions that shape their visibility. This reorientation informs how we design learning environments, analyze interaction, and represent knowledge-making. It also challenges the field to move beyond inclusion toward transformation—toward methodologies that sustain Black life, creativity, and community.

The “so what” of this work is that it helps us understand how educational research with Black learners is entangled with the broader context of endemic anti-Blackness. By foregrounding encounter, we offer a methodological stance that affirms Black learners ’ ways of knowing and contributes to more just and ethical futures for Black learning and thriving.
