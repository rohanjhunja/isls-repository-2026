# Montgomery’s Lessons for Desettling Science Learning: Understanding Black Feminist Knowledge-Making in Science

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this conceptual paper, I suggest cultivating Black feminist knowledge-making within science education can address the alienation and marginalization experienced by Black women. I begin by identifying features of Black feminist knowledge-making involved with developing critical consciousness among Black women, explaining how Western science perpetuates white masculine norms. To analyze an example of Black feminist knowledge-making in science, I focus on storytelling as a modality of Black women’s oppositional knowledge-making. I highlight how Beronda Montgomery's (2021) storytelling in Lessons from Plants both contributes to and draws on an intellectual genealogy of Black feminist thought in interdisciplinary ways. I conclude with theoretical and methodological implications for designing science educational environments that foster Black feminist knowledge-making, aiming to create more meaningful and liberatory science learning experiences. This work underscores the need to reimagine science education to support diverse ways of knowing and being, ultimately challenging the dominance of Western scientific paradigms.

## Introduction

Momentum within science education and learning scie nces emphasizes expanding the discipline to include alternative ways of knowing and being in the world to support more meaningful learning for learners of nondominant backgrounds (e.g., Warren e t al., 2020). Black women, an oppressed group in the context of  white supremacy, continue to experience alienation, isola tion, stereotypes, and dismissal in science learnin g settings (e.g., McGee, 2016; McGee & Bentley, 2017), often manifesting in a pressure to assimilate to the norms of Western science  (Nordling, 2018). Black feminist knowledge-making is an intellectual genealogy of Black women's oppositional thought and action, referring to knowledge developed by subordinated groups to de fend against oppression by cultivating self-definition and actions that foster sel f-determination (Collins, 2022). Black feminist knowledge-making in science learning settings can be meaningf ul for Black women because it supports the development of critical consciousness to resist  their oppression in society broadly and in science  learning settings, specifically.

This conceptual paper begins by naming features of Black feminist knowledge-making and using settled expectations to highlight the mechanism by which Bl ack feminist thought is suppressed in Western Scien ce. I unpack the epistemological principles that cons titute Black feminist knowledge-making to illuminate incompatibilities with knowledge-making in western science. Considering Black women’ s storytelling as a modality for Black feminist knowledge-making, I unpack how compositional elements, such as metaphors, signal oppositional ways of knowing and being within the i ntellectual genealogy of Black feminist thought. To ward these ends, I analyze an excerpt from the book by biologist Dr. Beronda Montgomery, Lessons from Plants (2021) as storytelling of Black feminist knowledge-making in science. From this analysis, I propose wa ys that Mongomery’s work can be used to desettle, or reimagine more liberatory science learning, and discuss theoretical and methodological implications for the continued study and design of environments t o cultivate Black feminist knowledge-making in science  learning settings. As a Black fe minist scholar, scientist, and science educator, my use of personal possessive pronouns such as I, we, and our throughout this piece signals how I know my self as a Black woman, sister, and mother (Evans-Winters, 2019).

Features of Black feminist knowledge-making Black feminist thought refers to the collective wis dom generated by Black women's oppositional thought  and actions in the context of white supremacy (Collins, 2022). It is not a singular thread of canonized ideas, but rather heterogeneous, dialogic knowledge production among Black women in and beyond the United States, orient ed towards consciousness development and resisting opp ression (Collins, 2022). The enslavement and domina tion of Black communities involved the  lethal suppression and surveillance of Black women' s speech, ideas, and actions (Woods, 2017) requiring Black women's oppositional knowledge-making to continually evade capture (McKittrick, 2021).

Black feminist epistemological principles While varied in modality, Black women's oppositiona l knowledge-making shares common principles about how knowledge and action serve ending oppression and fostering group empowerment. For this paper, I highlight four broad epistemological principles  (Collins, 2000) of Black women's oppositional knowledge-making: 1) lived experience as a criterion for meaning, 2) dialogica lity as the basis for assessing knowledge claims, 3) an ethic of caring, and 4) an ethic of responsibility. I then h ighlight how these epistemological principles serve the goals of ending oppression and fostering group empowerment  with a particular focus on the modality of storytel ling. Specifically, I consider how the material and compositional aspects of storytelling—folktales, quilting, the Blues, gospels, fiction, poetry, dance, literary criticism, and community organizing —serve as relational technologies which facilitate dialogic knowledge-building among Black women (Collins, 2022). Lived experience as a criterion for meaning.

Black feminist thought is rooted in the everyday id eas and practices Black women develop to resist the ir oppression in a white supremacist society (Collins,  2022). As is understood within sociocultural theor ies of learning (Lave & Wenger, 1991), Black women's meaning-making is rooted in site-specific activities. Thus, meanings of Black women's oppositional knowledge and action index the experiential domain of their oppression. As researchers, we cannot fully account for the myriad ways Black wome n develop political consciousness, just as we cannot account for all the ways their oppress ion manifests. In short, Black women's concrete, li ved experiences are the basis by which meaning is developed  about our lives, and these meanings reflect a diale ctic between our oppression and our activism.

Separating the meanings Black women develop about r esisting their oppression from their experiences reinforces their marginalization, such as when narr atives celebrate Black women who remain in science amidst the durable findings of their marginalization i n these spaces. In this case, the "Strong Black Wom an" trope is a controlling image that explains Black women's resil ience, strength, and service for others as intrinsi c characteristics rather thandeveloped strategies in the face of endemic racism and sexism (Collins, 2022). This controlling image perpetuates Black women's ideological, political, and economic exploitation by concealing their struggles, vulnerability, and humanity. Recognizing  how Black women's lived experiences ground the mea nings they develop contextualizes a granularity of oppression unaccounted for by top-down theories of hegemony. Dialogicality as the basis for assessing knowledge claims. Because Black women's oppositional knowledge-making and actions are rooted in meanings developed  in their everyday lives, collective dialogue helps understand commonalities and contradictions between each other ’s experiences. Collins emphasizes dialogicality as core to developing coll ective consciousness, writing of the transition from individual to collective thought: "[s]uch consciousness may stimulate [a Black woman] to embark on a path of personal freedom, even if it exists pr imarily in her own mind. If she is lucky enough to meet others who are undergoing similar journeys, she and they can change the world around them" (Collins, 2000, p. x). Black women coming together —through physical co-presence or varied citational practices — to make sense of their lived experiences forms the basis of their oppositional knowledge-making. Thus, assessing knowledge claims, the process of coming to know, is a relational activity grounded in accountability to others. An ethic of responsibility.

Banks-Wallace (2000) describes the Black feminist ethic of responsibility as how Black women develop insights about themselves relevant to ensuring credibility a nd commitment to Black women and Black communities. Within Black feminist knowledge-making, professional authority does not confer cred ibility. Instead, one's commitments and reflexive insights allow readers and participants to see knowing as a collaborative pr ocess of knowledge construction. In research, an ethic of responsibility means approaching knowledge-making "away from the traditional metaphor of research as recipe to f ix some problem to a metaphor that centers reciproc ity and relationship between the researcher or teacher and those who, in that moment, are engaged in the research or teaching with us" (Dillard, 2016, p. 407). In this context, Black feminist knowledge-making leverages the academic medium in service of teaching and sharing with each other. An ethic of care.

Towards group empowerment, Black women caring for one another evidences knowledge, action, and feeling s that support the evidentiary basis of knowledge cla ims. As Collins elaborates: "emotion indicates that  a speaker believes in the validity of an argument" (2000, p. 263). Within Black feminist epistemologies, observi ng how statements are expressed affectively is part of int erpreting the knowledge claim —distinct from conventional scientific communication, which positions emotions as inferior (Čufar, 2023). Empathizing or showing concern for others' well-being when working with Black women is a politicized investment in our wellness. Features of Western scientific knowledge-making The construct of settled expectations of science (Bang et al., 2012; Bang & Marin, 2015; Medin & Ban g, 2014) emphasizes how Western Science —normative conduct of disciplinary science in learni ng and professional settings—centers white masculine ways of knowing and thinkin g. Recognizing and cultivating Black feminist knowledge-making in science requires desettling —reimagining ways of teaching and learning in scienc e that refuse mechanisms that recursively construct white male-knowing subjects as the only knowers and the world as an object of knowledge.

In conceptualizing settled expectations of science education, Bang et al. (201 2) drew on Cheryl Harris'

(1995) foundational analysis of whiteness as property —how racial hierarchies are maintained within domina nt

society through the enactment of property rights —to understand how science education reproduces science as a property of white society. In particular, Bang et a l., (2012) point to how the doctrine of  Manifest Destiny legitimized exploitation, theft, genocide, and enslavement by constructing culture (meaning: white men as owners of property), through its oppositional relation wit h nature (meaning: ownable property). White propert ied ownership serves both as the grounds for legitimacy  for white settlers and as the rationale for displa cing Indigenous African and American peoples (Bang et al., 2012). For this paper, I focus on how Western scientific knowing manufactures dominance through the binary contradistinction of a devalued other. The nature-culture divide is one example of Cartesiandualisms: binary hierarchies  which organize meaning in Western knowledge systems (and thus, society’s commonsense, e.g., Pfeifer, 2009). Often attributed to Descartes, Cartesiandualisms can be understood within the wor k of other Enlightenment-era Western philosophers to craft a hereditary and racialized notion of “mind” (Harfouch, 2018). While a further historical and philosophical treatment of the racial project of We stern science is outside the scope of this paper (e.g., CastroGómez, 2021; Palmié, 2002), histories of Atlantic modernity highlight how mind-body, emotion-reason, subjectobject, and nature-culture function as hierarchical binaries to emplace white men colonists as legitimately belonging and displace (violently expropriate) indigenous African and American peoples. Cartesiandualisms in Western science recursively center on a white masculine knower, displacing Black women's legibility as knowers, a pattern recognized by Black feminist physicists and astronomers (Akyea, 2024; Prescod-Weinstein, 2020). Black feminist knowledge-making is displaced in science, not fitting into th e binary hierarchical categories which recursively emplace w hite masculine ways of knowing. Consequently, attem pts to reduce Black feminist epistemological principles into categories leads to devaluation (e.g., dismissed through the emotion-reason divide) or illegibility (e.g., non-categorical: non-abstractable, non-mathematizable, non-inscriptional knowledge products). While Black feminist knowledge-making can enable Black women to draw on and contribute to an intellectual genealogy of thought and resistance against their oppression, enactments of Black feminist knowledge-making cannot be conceptualized within Western scientific frameworks.

## Methodology

Taking up a Black feminist ethic of responsibility (Dillard, 2016), I enter into dialogue with other Black women’s ideas with the aim of  understanding, supporting, and cultivating Black fe minist knowledge-making in science learning settings. Specifically, I consider how metaphorical aspects of Black women' s knowledge-making in science signal oppositional ways of knowing and bei ng within the intellectual genealogy of Black femin ist thought. Toward these ends, I analyze an excerpt fr om the book by biologist Dr. Beronda Mont gomery, Lessons from Plants (2021), a work that understands the adaptive behaviors of p lants as lessons for plant and human thriving.

Beronda Montgomery, a Black woman professor of biology, vice president of academic affairs, and dean of Grinnell College, enacts a way of being in acade mia that centers mentorship in academic and profess ional settings. Her biology research unpacks photosynthet ic organisms' adaptive behavior, including plants a nd cyanobacteria, is just one aspect of her multifacet ed work. Montgomery's administrative work aims to i ncrease the retention of graduate students and junior faculty from nondominant groups within STEM through mentorship and leadership training. Within Lessons, Montgomery integrates what she has learned from her  experiences in biology research, academic mentorship, and life to discuss the lively world of plants. I chose Lessons because Black feminist thought is never explicitly mentioned in Montgomery’s writing. However, several Black feminist scholars have described Lessons as a Black feminist text (Schuler Books, 2021), suggesting that content  and compositional (e.g., metaphorical) elements are resonant with an intellectual genealog y of Black women’s oppositional knowledge production.

Sharing, listening, and telling stories hold intact  Black feminist epistemological principles of concr ete experience as criterion for meaning, dialogicality in the assessment of knowledge claims, and ethics o f responsibility and care. Thus, Black women’s s torytelling is a relational potentiality, materiall y and ideationally encoding ways of knowing rooted in an intellectual genealogy of Black women’s oppositional knowledge-making. My analysis of Montgomery’s Lessons is within this practice of sharing stories: Montgom ery’s narrative signals ideas, feelings, ways of knowing, evoked, and other  Black women's oppositional knowledge-making to me. In particular, I focus on the preface of Montgomery's Lessons for how the preface introduces “lessons” and enfleshes "lessons" with meaning. Because the preface structu res the organization and meaning for the rest of th e work, understanding the stylistic dimensions of the preface highlights Montgomery’s storytelling within the entire work. I argue that Montgomery’s storytelling in Lessons signals Black feminist knowledge-making. However, to argue that Lessons evinces Black feminist knowledge-making,  I must call on McKittrick’s  (2021) work to underscore the fictive work of theory. “Signalling”  as a methodology to render legible Black feminist epistemological principles within Black women’s sto rytelling calls into question the stability of "empirical evidence." In Black practices of storytelling (McKittrick, 202 1), telling and sharing storie s signals —cues up within listeners and readers — feelings, ideas, memories, and ways of knowing. Sig nals from stories make connections without having to explain or overly des cribe those connections. This is important in the r ealm of understanding Black lives and Black living because our normative knowledge-making practices, rooted in Western science, demand a burden of proof which has  the effect of knowing Blackness only through narra tives of harm, deficits, and indexing death (McKittrick, 2021). Thus, telling and sharing stories are interdependent and interdisciplinary acts of Black living: rigorous, c o-present work of listening, feeling, and being which  are in response to but not wholly defined by oppression.

Montgomery’s Lessons as Black feminist storytelling Montgomery, in the preface of her 2021 book Lessons from Plants, develops the metaphor of “lessons” in ways which signal to me Black feminist knowledge-making in science. Montgomery's preface begins with  reflecting on her early experiences with plants, rooted in lea rning from her mother's gardening. Montgomery contr asts features of idyllic tropes of a bucolic (white) childhood (Nxumalo & ross, 2019) with her upbringing, where her mother's gardens became her sanctuary: "... I did n ot grow up on a farm or near a forest, the classic kind of upbringing that might explain my later enchantment with botany" (p. ix). Instead, she positions her mother's plant caretaking as her foundation, describing her mother's talents in detail: “My mom's gardens were-the gem of the neighborhood, a green oasis, a frien dly abode for plants in a large city. She was able to cultivate s uch incredible flowers and healthy greenery-because she was closely engaged with her beloved pl ants. She read and responded to their cues— this-wilting plant needed more water, that one with the yellowing leaves required fertilizer, and the plant bending toward-the light from the nearest window must be rotated so it could re-orient itself. Mom's plant tending was part of her daily routine, an ingrained part of my childhood. She wat ched her charges closely in a way that can only be described as "listening" to them. When she noticed what they nee ded and supplied it, they responded by growing, and growing well. I can't-say I fully understood the shared communication be tween my mom and her plants, but I noticed the beneficial outcome.” (Montgomery, 2021, pp. ix-x) Starting the preface with a personal anecdote of he r mother's gardening practices roots the lessons Mo ntgomery learns from plants to how her mother learns from pl ants. Specifically, when 'listening' to plants serv es as the modality for learning, a form of botanical relation  surfaces, which disrupts human-centered, unidirectional knowledge-making privileged within settled nature-culture relations (Bang et al., 2012). Further, 'listening' is beyond a literary flourish: 'listening' as a regist er of perception concretizes the materiality of non human plant animacies.

Montgomery's use of 'listening' signals a dialogical perspective within the Black feminist epistemological principle where dialogicality is the basis of const ructing knowledge claims. Dialogicality in the prod uction of knowledge undermines unidirectional k nowledge-making. Rather thandissolving into relativism, dia logicality locates the construction of knowledge claims in co-presence and relation. This process stands in opposition to the normative location of Western science knowledge-making where knowledge  claims are constructed through an objectivity whose credibility derives from presiding atop a geopolitical position of nowhere (Castro-Gómez, 2021; Mignolo & Walsh, 2018). Instead dialogicality, when serving as the basis for knowledge-making, grounds claims within relational frameworks of accountability.

Importantly, knowledge claims are grounded in accou ntability and foreground how one's socially situated identities are constructed through responsibility to one anothe r (Lorde, 1998). Responsibility to one another enacts a politics of knowledge-making based on the process of ethically encounteri ng one another rather than reifying Western frameworks of knowledge credibility premised on territorially staking claims to "legitimate" grounds by which to know. Black feminist, anticolonial pers pectives on what it means to produce knowledge as enacting practices of responsibility for one anothe r signal the Black feminist epistemological princip le of responsibility.

Continuing in Montgomery's writing, Montgomery's mo ther's listening sparks an "enchantment" with plants, which drives her journey through life as a researcher, educator, and mother. In Montgomery's p rose, enchantment develops alongside her narrative of her  personal and career journey. Montgomery returns to this enchantment as guiding her path toward biology research in academia: “My enchantment with plants ultimately led me to pu rsue a career as a professor, with opportunities to continue doing research and teaching about these fascinating organisms. In both the classroom and the laboratory, I learned how imp ortant mentoring and l eading were to my pursuit of success. Having not formally learned abo ut either of these in my academic career, I began to seek out resources and opportunities to im prove my skills as a mentor and a leader in science, as well as the means to share insights with others in my community who are also eager to learn these skills. My primary aim in this process has been to fully inhabit my space, my life, and opportunities  as I honor my goals and my own humanity, even as I ensure I have the skills to support and value the humanity of those with whom I engage" (Montgomery, 2021, p. xiii). Montgomery's compositional choices construct enchantment as a maturation and coordination of ways of feeling, knowing, and acting. Enchantment is developed as a set of value-led feelings, actions, and knowing, developed from her mother's practices and informing her profe ssional work. Black lesbian poet, mother, and autho r Audre Lorde forwarded many treatises on this interconnect ion, naming how "[o]ur feelings are our most genuin e paths to knowledge … we must key into those feelings and begin to extrapolate from them, examine them for new ways of understanding our experience … Our visions begin  with our desires" (Lorde & Tate, 2024, p. 141). Reading Lorde's interventions on the power and connectednes s between knowledge, emotions, action (Lorde, 1998) through Mongtomery's sense of enchantment signals t he Black feminist epistemological principle of an e thic of care, whereby Montgomery's aim to honor and others' humanity are value-led ways of knowing, feeling, and being as an researcher, educator, and administrator.

Crucially, when contextualizing Montgomery's approa ch to Black women's experiences of marginalization, alienation, and exclusion in STEM learning settings (Charleston et al., 2014; McGee, 2016; McGee & Bentley, 2017), Montgomery's work to foster safe spaces for nondo minant groups in STEM indexes her experiences of oppression without overly describing them. This is crucial because Black women's oppositional knowledge-making through Black feminist thought maintains a d ialectic between oppression and activism. Celebrating Montgomery's activism without acknowled ging the harm she has had to navigate forward narra tives that conceal Black women's oppression via the "Strong Black Woman" stereotype. This one-sided narrative works to maintain Black women's exploitation by ignoring their experiences of harm in a white supremacist so ciety. Montgomery's approach to building counterspaces in STEM is rooted in the epistemological principle of an ethic of care (e.g., Montgomery et al., 2014)  and evidence of her navigating experiences of oppression. Montgomery constructs the metaphor of "lessons" from plants as an activity of collective empowerment rather than only of individual resilience. This eco logical perspective is most evidenced when Montgome ry summarizes the "lessons" she learns from plants: I have learned a great-deal about how to “be” in this world from my studi es of plants. With this book I offer you a similar journey: to see how  plants’ individual and collective strategies and behaviors result in adaptable and productive li ving, and how we can learn from them” (Montgomery, 2021, p. xiv) Montgomery's framing of "lessons" as both individua l and collective empowerment reflects how Black wom en's oppositional knowledge-making is both a process of individuals coming to c onsciousness and efforts towards collective empowerment (Collins, 2022). Not to be m istaken for an individualistic approach towards sel fimprovement, Collins  (2022) narrates how Black women's individual experiences  of oppression gain meaning towards collective efforts on behalf of the group. Importantly the coalitional domain of Black feminis t thought, rooted in the acknowledgment that interlocking syst ems of oppression that harm Black women harm everyone, highlights how Black feminism is for everybody  (Gumbs, 2020) in the sense that dismantling these systems that harm Black women would also mean the end of oppression for everyone (Combahee River Collective, 1995). Lastly, how Montgomery uses personal anecdotes sign als the Black feminist epistemological principle where concrete experience is the criterion for mean ing. Weaving together personal anecdotes, scientifi c explanations, and reflections of mentoring all inform Lessons, where these narrative devices co-constitute what it means to know and be in science. This is crucial be cause the concrete experience valued within Western  science is often constrained to visual perception or, less often, other sensory experiences that underwrite oc ular-centric empiricism. People's feelings,  memories, and lived experiences are often positione d as "subjectivity" — unnecessary metadata that interferes with objectivity in knowledge-making. Montgomery does not reproduce the false binary of objectivity-subjectivity by privileging her lived experie nces over scientific explanations. Instead, scientific explanations, personal anecdotes, and re flections on mentoring are integrated, forwarding t he aims of Lessons.

Montgomery’s lessons for desettling science learning Montgomery's development of the metaphor of "lesson s" signals Black feminist knowledge-making in science, revealing opportunities to desettle science (Bang et al., 2013) — reimagine ways of teaching and learning science that are capacious enough for learne rs to draw on and contribute to an intellectual gen ealogy of Black women's oppositional knowledge-making.

First, through the use of personal narratives,  Mon tgomery's "lessons"  forward a Black feminist perspective on the practice of knowledge-making. Highlighting how Black women's oppositional  knowledge-making is governed by a dialogicality in making knowledge claims, concrete experience as a criterion for meaning, and ethics of care and responsibility shift what it  means to know in science, as accountable to how we  encounter and relate to each other. This disrupts the white-masculine objectivity, whose claims for credibility are detached from all geopolitical locations—instead, forwarding a socially situated and relational ethics of knowledge-making grounded in how we are accountable to our relationships. Desettling science with this in mind would reshape how learning goals relate (if at all) to the learning of canonical science content. Instead, emphasis on interdisciplinarity in how we come to know shifts how we understand rigor, systematicity, and objectivity as operating within dialogic knowledge production.

Montgomery's metaphorical construction of "lessons"  refuses a white-masculine knowing subject hegemonically constructed as the knowing subject in  Western science. A poignant example is how Montgom ery features Black motherhood in Lessons, bookending Lessons with stories of Black motherhood by concluding with a personal anecdote about planting a tree for her s on. Aligned with the anecdote of her own mother's liste ning in the preface, Montgomery's use of circular structure positions the intimacies of Black motherhood as foundational to her life. This framing is significant in the con text of Western science, as Black  women are pathologized both as knowers and as mothers within the dominant socie ty (Collins, 2022). In Montgomery's narrative, Black motherhood is not recuperated from its devalued pos ition within the dominant social hierarchy by adjac ency to white-masculine norms of knowing (e.g., abstraction, math ematics, dispassionate logic). Instead, Montgomer y positions the intimacies of Black motherhood as pro viding a capacity for knowing, feeling, and acting,  engaging Black feminist epistemological principles that emph asize the connectedness between thought and action.  Thus, Montgomery's development of "lessons" as capacities towards value-led action reorients learning from individual enrichment to collective empowerment.

## Discussion

Montgomery’s Lessons also function as an apt lesson for researchers aiming to understand, cultivate, and amplify alternative ways of knowing and being in science. Methodologies that valorize the product of knowledge-making by attuning to outcomes rather than its process wil l conceal the alternative modalities of knowing and  being that are currently happening in science learning settings. Black feminist knowledge-making in science is by no means new. However, for the fields of Learning Sciences and Science Education to recognize Black feminist knowledgemaking, these fields will need to seriously contend  with how Western Science limits learners' access to meaningful, affirming science learning (e.g., Warren et al., 2020) just as much as contending with how Western Science constrains the empirical study of learning in science. For instance, how do the Learning Sciences conceptu alize where Black learners' alternative approaches to knowing and being in science happen? Where does Black women ’s storytelling fit within the evidentiary demands of Western scientific empiricism? From my analysis, I argue that the answer is considering the material and metaphorical dimensions of  epistemological, ontological, and axiological domai ns of Black expressive, creative, and cultural production in learning setti ngs (e.g., Mims et al., 2022). For example, the dominant view that Black expressive texts only offer metaphorical, ideational, and stylistic elements, reifies a hierarchical Western ontology that constructs the metaphorical i n contradistinction to the material, physical world. Specifically, white social subjectivity is premised  on the "rightful" owning of physical spaces to jus tify the dispossession of "irrational" Black and Indigenous peoples, who are "irrational" for not "cultivating"  and "developing" land in "rightful" ways. These alterna tive cosmologies are displaced as outside of the material, physical world, which underwrites their recursive devaluation. The above analysis demonstrates how underlying spatial assumptions about the "seeability" of "physical space" underwrite spatial processes that dislocate nonwhite peoples from physical space and material ground as a way to justify their dispossession. Again, the view that Black expressive texts are “simply” metaphorical-having no spatial or physical referents-reifies the racist, colonial codes where space is n eutral and simply a container for [white] subjectivity (McKittrick, 200 6; 2021). As Smith and Katz (1993) conclude their discussion on the materiality of metaphors of space: “Newton, Descartes and Kant were the philosophical progenitors of spatial modernism, as much as Columbus, Napoleon and the Duchess of Sutherland  were its practitioners … the taken-for grantedness of the absolute space they established, is only beginning to be challenged. That this space is quite literally the space of capitalist pa triarchy and racist imperialism should hasten critique and reconstruction” (p. 78).

I invoke Smith and Katz's (1993) remarks to emphasi ze how the spatial modernism advanced through Weste rn science and philosophy was predicated on a racial-economic arrangement with developing nation states which benefited European people at the expense of indigen ous African and American people's cosmologies and l ives. While addressing the inadequacies of science educat ion reform, Mutgei (2011) asserts the “worldview th at supports and reinforces Western modern science is the same worldview that supports and reinforces colonialism” (p. 305). Separating  the production of modern scientific knowledge from the racial-economic conditions which made such knowledge-production possible is a mythology which cannot mat erially contribute to the political efficacy of Black and Indigenous peoples’ ongoing struggles for self-determination. Taking seriously Philip and Gupta's (2021) argument that theories of learning are theories of society, I end by modifying McKittrick's (2006) assertion: Bla ck learning matters are spatial matters. For two ex amples, Moll et al., (1992) and Gutiérrez (2008) demonstrate how understanding nondominant groups' knowledge-making as socio-spatial assertions has been a generative framework to emphasize the power and politics of disciplinary knowing. I aim to highlight the need for such treatment in s cience education, where our understanding of the world is based on these unexamined spatial narrativ es. The commonsensical view that the world is entir ely seeable, knowable, and physical shapes disciplinar y conceptions on the evidentiary basis of such a wo rld: a reliance on "concrete" facts. Instead, this perspec tive must be critically examined, considering how u ntroubled spatial commonsense reifies settled expectations of  Western science. Towards these ends, I suggest making familiar and commonsensical seem strange. For instance, why are Black feminist knowledge-making and science oxymoronic (Perkins et al., 2024) when Black feminist knowledge-making has made significant contributions to contemporary conversations on the intersection of social life and technology (e.g., Benjamin, 2019; Buolamwini, 2023; Noble, 2018; Prescod-Weinstein, 2021)?

Lastly, liberatory approaches for Black students' S TEM learning (Miles & Roby, 2022; Morton et al.,

2022) often articulate aims to cultivate affirming, joyful, transformational learning and disrupt inequalities — not

assimilating Black ways of knowing into the scientific Western scientific canon. Efforts in science education and learning sciences to "expand" and "broaden" what co unts as Western science must trouble the material a nd the political domains of those spatial metaphors and how these ends correlate with Black scholars' conceptualizations of meaningful learning (e.g., Ladson-Billings, 2009; Muhammad, 2021). More specifically, I question how including Black women's oppositional thought within  the ontology of Western science —  whose credibility is constructed through the placement of Black women on  the devalued rung of hierarchical binaries (e.g., manwoman, Black-white, subject-object, nature-culture)— serves Black liberatory conceptions of learning. Where Mutegi (2011) warns,  “[t]o position African American students to  accept Western Modern Science (or Western knowledge generally) as part of their own intellect ual culture, is to indoctrinate them with the very ideas that support their colonization” (p. 305), Montgomery ’s Lessons offer alternative intellectual genealogies.  This analysis sets forth tools to more explicitly unders tand and cultivate Black women's oppositional knowl edgemaking in the context of science learning to produc e more livable arrangements for Black women specifi cally, Black people broadly, and our world.
