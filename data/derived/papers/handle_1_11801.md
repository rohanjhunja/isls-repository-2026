# Utopian Futures, Ends of the World, and Other Stories We Tell Ourselves About the Roles of Race in the Learning Sciences

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper I make plain the need for the Learning Sciences to think more closely with Black Studies due to the ways race and anti-Black racism shape learning. In thinking about utopias, I contribute the theoretical perspective of Plantation Prophecy to illustrate how, utopia can require the subjugation of group(s) of people. In conversation with interviews of Black computing practitioners, I further explicate how this is linked with computing and computing learning through critical discourse analysis.

## Introduction

Formalized education has always been a world-building project. In the United States (US), the worlds envisioned by education, however, began with targeted visions of who utopia is for and who is deserving of educat ion (Anderson, 1988). The purpose of education was (and in many ways continues to be) a project of making “good” citizens that uphold particular order and values of  nation-states (Anderson, 1988). This world-making project involved ends of the worlds through  the killing and removal of Indigenous peoples to cl aim their lands and the forced labor and negative social caste of the enslaved African peoples and in dentured Indian peoples (Givens & Ison, 2022). This world-making project also required a unifying and emblematic representative of the aims of education. In the case of the United States, which is the domain for this research, the representative has been those racialized as white which has included more ethnic groups over time (Givens & Ison, 2022). The ways racialization operates in the United States has been a model for other countries to take up and use for the further subjugation of peoples across the world (Wilderson III, 2018). Understanding history is critical as we work to examine ideas such as utopia, agency,  and future thinking – because, for some, one group having domination of all people is utopia. Intervening in utopian conversation, learning scientists have drawn on the work of scholars of Black Studies, Black Psychology, Ant hropology, and Education in order to open conversat ions on culture and ethnicity (Lee, 2002; Nasir & Hand, 2006). While also introducing how understandings of culture and race matter for our learning, including centering r elationality and students’ histories  (Medin & Bang, 2014; Warren et al., 2020)  and their repertoires of practice (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003; Nasir & Hand, 2006). Sociocultural work has opened up  further exploration of critical theorist perspectiv es on learning. For example, more scholars are working to address how racializat ion shapes learning for Black youth (Booker et al.,  2014; McKinney De Royston et al., 2017). In prior work, I contributed to intensive discussion of anti-Blackness in the Learning Sciences (Jones & melo, 2021; Jones et al., 2023). Histories of ongoing race based harm are what brought me to deeper Black Study and why, I model how the learning sciences is in need of a greater understanding of how racialization, race, and racism shape learning and education in local and global contexts. Understanding antiBlackness matters not only for our studies of learning, but the analyses we conduct, methodologies we integ rate, and futures we work towards. I present an analysis grounded in the context of computing practice and learning in the US where I ask, “What might be the role of computing in rela tionship to Black Life?” In conversation with the interviews of 20 Black computing practitioners, I offer insights and reflections to this question.

## Introduction to Blackness and learning in the United States

I draw on the racial category black to refer to how racialization is a tool of white supremacy that attempts to place an “other” as subordinate. In tandem, I draw on the social life of those who consider themselves to be Black, one of care, love, pain, joy, mourning, and human agenc y. Black people may share cultural norms influenced  by geographies, class, gender, sexuality, (dis)ability, and other intersectional forms of identity. Members of different ethnic groups who are a pa rt of the Africandiaspora are non-monolithic, and their specificities are worth payin g attention to (Lee, 2002). Black as a shared category is contested and fille d with tensions, with Black being a shifting signifier for people depending on socio-political contexts (Dumas, 2014). Due to how race is socially reproduced, race and racism manifest in learning environments (Dumas, 2014). Anderson describes how public schooling in the Unit ed States was modeled after freedmen’s schools and shaped by the perceived danger from southern wh ites who worked to maintain a planter class (Anderson, 1988). Different than Freedman’s schools, Normal schools  and industrial education worked towards institutionalizing Black folks as a laboring class that subscribed to moral standards aligned with white supremacist values (Anderson, 1988). Within legacy Black institutions, white funders worked to dictate what kind of teaching was possible through philanthropic domination (Ande rson, 1988). However, Givens describes aspects of Black interiority and self-determination that led to the creation of fugitive learning spaces where Black educators shared lessons with youth on how to navigate an “anti-black school ecology ” (Givens, 2021). Educational scholars offer a basis from which learn ing scientists candraw insights regarding writing about anti-Blackness in Education. Dumas & ross note the absen ce of culturally relevant and sustaining pedagogies, maladministration of school discipline policies, and true stories of desegregation as reve aling how anti-Black racism shows up in schools (Dumas, 2014; Dumas & ross, 2016). Dumas invites us to meditate on “la petite misère” of Black youth in schools, where com pounding anti-Blackness leads to suffering within education (Dumas, 2014). That is not to say that the experien ce is only suffering but to deny the harm experienc ed would be an attempt at sanitizing history (Dumas, 2014). With literacy tied to self-possession and alleged with revolt and insurrection, literacy was a marker between the free and the enslaved (Givens & Ison, 2022). Attempts by all folks to create pathways towards literacy were met with jail time, schools being burnt down, physical beatings, and social ostracization. In addition to historical  accounts, education and learning sciences scholars  work to empirically theorize about Black Life in education.  For example the importance of educators invested in youths’ political education and well-being (McKinney De Royston et al., 2017). Race and learning in computing education Computing education researchers and scholars have worked to bring conversations of race to the field. One of the struggles of looking for racial analyses is identifying those that go beyond statistics and demographic information toward understanding how race and racism mediate le arning experiences. Margolis and contributors, soci al scientists, write about how racism  and segregation, as witnessed in swimming, are also  shaping c omputing education such that Black and Latinx youth are kept  on the periphery (Margolis, 2017). One of the challenges within the framing of this book and much of computing and race-related work is the gaze with which researchers approach the field.  For example, label ing educators perpetuating racist beliefs about African  American intelligence and proclivity towards computing as be ing “well-meaning” and “unaware”  (Margolis, 2017). Framings of (white) innocence operate to hide the patterned anti-Blackness perpetuated by teachers, administrators, and funders within educational spaces. However, early work opened the conversation for a wider variety of educators and scholars to respond to questions of race and computing. Rankin & Thomas noted how Black women experienced f eelings of isolation, often being the only member of that particular identity group in the classroom (Rankin & Thomas, 2020). In addition to binary gender analyses, there is room to grow in terms of the lim ited scholarship that addresses experiences of Blac k gender non-conforming individuals in computing (Klassen et al., 2024). Towards the end of the 2010s, there was a shift in a subset of the literature to draw connections b etween the experiences Black students have with rac ialization and experiences in STEM education (McGee & Bentley, 2017; Vakil & Ayers, 2019; Shaw e t al., 2023). These authors bridge computing education and the learning  sciences with discussions of how cultural practice s can change computing education. These articles engage with Black Studies and Afrofuturist literature to explore race in learning sciences and computing. Afrofuturism considers experiences of the Black Ame ricandiaspora in speculation and future thinking, sees blackness as integral, and is a methodological toolkit for Afro-diasporic people (Harrington et al., 2022). However the learning sciences literatures’  analysis of anti-Blackness as a mediating relation to race and racism is limited, which is a critical aspect of analysis I put forth. Plantation prophecy In this paper, I introduce the theoretical perspective of Plantation Prophecy  (Jones, 2024). Plantation Prophecy describes the ways investments in ideologies of whi teness at structural levels work to continue to rei fy, uphold, and inspire structures of slavery and plantations in the United States and globally and work to diminish Black Life in the future. While framing Plantation Prophecy, I build on the work of scholars who have brought into question how whiteness works to define who is “human” (Wynter, 2003), the embeddedness of anti-Black racism in society (Bell, 1992), the afterlives of slavery in present time (Hartman, 1997), how the plantation changed global formations (Sharpe, 2016), as an education model (Marquez, 2024),  and how violence & control are used to maintain the plantation (Browne, 2015). Alongside Afrofuturism, Plantation Prophecy offers a lens towards cultural critique.

The definition of humanity predicated on whiteness has shaped knowledge structures, social scripts, and laws that enshrine race as a technology of human-making. To gain access to the racial category white, European ethnic groups were asked to buy into the subjugation of others predicated on physiognomy, such as skin tone and facial features (Givens & Ison, 2022). As ethnic, cultural, and rac ial groups work to define their distance from Blackness, they take up participation in anti-Black racism and enforce it through global patterns of domination to move closer to whiteness (Bell, 1992; Sharpe, 2016;  Wilderson III, 2018). Through the lens of Plantati on Prophecy, I explore how maintaining structures of p lantations is enacted through avenues such as indiv idual and collective imaginations, policing, and capital. I draw on the framing of prophecy as a vision that tells the future and becomes embedded in actions and how people work  to define truth. If our future is grounded this wa y, maintaining the plantation will continue to be impor tant socially as well as economically. For example currently the plantation’s form  has shifted towards the prison s, primarily populated by Black people in the U.S., where many imprisoned laborers are not paid. As plantations were profitable for a white slave owner-class, so too prisons are profitable for governmental and private owners, technology companies, and maintaining anti-Blackness. For those engaging in liberatory practice – I argue that we must use all that is available to us to navigate our past, current, and future timelines. A map is m ore powerful when paired with a point of reference (the cardinality of the solar system, the com pass, the tree that helps us remember our way). I b elieve in the Black radical imagination, and striving for futures that sustain Black Life, but I do so with my map and the  oak tree. This is aligned with the broader work of Black Educ ation studies, which delineates how anti-Blackness is an essential analytic for all social phenomena and tha t the field of Black Education studies does not req uire total alignment with Afropessimist worldview s (ross & Givens, 2023). Understanding that theorizat ion around anti-Blackness predates afropessimism provides an opport unity to acknowledge projects of hope (ross & Given s, 2023). To be clear, at this time,  my analysis is more about how collective white supr emacist imagination and systematic practice of anti-Black racism provide structure to maintaining the plantation rather than individuals.

## Participants and context

This study was conducted virtually over Zoom with  racially Black and African-American adults in Computing who were working or learning in the US. Participants could be of any ethnic background (including Senegambian, Nigerian, Caribbean-American), nationality or stage in their career so long as they were in a relevant computing field such as software engineering, computer science, or research. I aimed to have participants of various genders, ages, and ethnic backgrounds so that I might get cl oser to the varied experiences of Black Life.  I offered participants the opportunity  to self-describe their gender, which included, man, woman a s well as gender non-conforming, identifying as non-binary, and/or as a Black woman. They ranged in age from 21 to 59.5 and lived in every region of the country. Participants were currently in school, doing internships and Research Experiences for Undergraduates  (REUs), being entrepreneurs,  team members and leads, VPs,  consultants, and CEOs. Participants discussed how their experiences of gen der, sexuality, and neurodivergence affected how th ey navigated computing learning and work environments. Lastly, I use the terminology Black, but I note that two of the participants named that they do not necessarily  align with this racial category due to the ways it  is used to 1) dehumanize people of Africand escent and 2) decenter “family values.” In these st atements, I heard them describing the ways the category black is negatively racialized (Britton, 1999). Data and analysis Critical discourse analysis What we talk about and how we talk about topics inform our relationships. On one side of the spectrum one might analyze how language is used to control consciousness and on another how language is used to liberate. Engaging in critical discourse analysis (CDA) provides an opportunity to make plain patterns of “structural relationships of dominance, discrimination, power and control when t hese are manifested in language” (Wodak, 2011, p. 5 3). Wodak’s definition emphasizes that CDA focuses primarily on dominating opaque power, however, there are also transparent structural relationships (Blommaert & Bulcaen, 2000). As I was interested in analyzing how participants discourse around race in computing hig hlighted opaque and transparent power relations, CD A was an ideal method. I engaged in CDA by  iteratively 1) determining the topic at hand, 2) gather ing sources on the topic (interview data, documents, photos, etc.), 3)  engaging in open coding of the data to determine themes and discourse patterns, and 4) discuss ing how these themes and patterns are in conversation w ith structural relationships of power, hegemony, and control. One of the primary limitations of CDA is its brief descriptions of history and context (Blommaert & Bulcaen, 2000). Within CDA, I am also wary of how the method might enclose thinking about what people are up to as only being resistance. I pay great attention to contextualizing the present and past moments and the willed future moments described within the interviews as also being beyond resistance. Process: Interviews, data attunement, qualitative coding Over up to 90 minute  zoom conversations participants completed the demographics and consent form,  and we entered the interview space, where I asked them questions about their background in computing.  In between questions, I would sometimes share parts of my expe rience in computing as well. Interview structures c an often reify power dynamics of interviewer and participant, someone who captures information and someone who gives information. In telling parts of my story, I found that it also allowed participan ts to ask me questions. Questions about what I wanted to do after graduating, what I hoped to do with the study, and why I think the topic mattered. Collins (1990) asserts that “in the Afrocentric call-and-response tradition … power dynamics are fluid” (p. 236237). In opening up space for me to respond to part icipants’ answers, they also took up the role of th e caller, asking questions of their own.

After the interviews were conducted on Zoom, I was able to download both audio and generated transcript files. I then initiated data attunement, which I describe as the ways a researcher familiarizes themselves with the data and works to authentically document w hat participants said. Zoom does not provide suffic iently accurate transcripts. For example, the auto-transcript failed to register famous African Americ an figures such as Ida B. Wells, and W.E.B. DuBois, as well as events and acronyms like Kwanzaa and the NAACP. As I sat with the data to explore answers to the research questio n, “How do Black computing practitioners describe t he relationships between computing and Black Life?,”  through CDA, I reflected on both the what (topic) and how (mechanics) of their descriptions. This  process led to themes that situated the data historically, socially, and textually: Textual Adornments, Technical Expertise, Afrofuturism, and Plantation Prophecy. Textual adornments The code group Textual Adornments focuses on discou rse-as-text, drawing on the work of Zora Neale Hurston. Hurston’s work as an ethnographer of diasporic Afri cans, particularly in the US South and the Caribbea n (Haiti and Jamaica), has led to history preservation and theory. Republished in 1997, Hurston’s Characteristics of Negro Expression (CNE) provides a purview on what is now known as African American Vernacular English as well as cultural practices that shape communication and art  (Hurston, 1997). She describes how diasporic Africans in America brought language practices to their use of what might currently be referred to as Mainstream A merican English (MAE). I found Hurston’s work  illustrative in articulating diasporic language practices among the participants. Hurston describes the regionality of CNE in terms of dialect, which affirms the cultural  specificity that can come with geography. However, with migrati ons across history, CNE made its way north, and wit h the rise of social media, much of it has made it into MAE. As such, some members of the Africandiaspora outside of the US and Caribbean have adopted these forms of ex pression. Within Textual Adornment, I use parent co des Drama, Will to Adorn, and Quiet to refer to Hurston and Quashie’s work (Hurston, 1997; Quashie, 2009). Theory is applied at the level of code generation as each of the initial child-codes are themes that come up in Hurston’s essay. Additions to the naming come from sitting with the participant’s data, such as the code “I see it, I like it, I use it,” which describes the use of “dictionary words” such as impugned, lambaste, and afoul. Technical expertise The Technical Expertise code group describes how Black computing professionals talked in interviews about their understanding of computing learning spaces, includi ng school, work, and extracurricular spaces. Throug hout the interviews, I witnessed participants constructing b oth school and work as learning environments where they learned more about coding and hardware and how classmates, teachers, colleagues, and business structures work to maintain racialized hierarchies. Within Technical Expertise, I focused on the places participants mentioned as learning spaces and the kinds of skills they named as essential to their work. The nature of community and tensions of being in the computing space were present amongs t these conversations. Learning spaces mentioned included work, k-12, college, and club / extracurricular  learning environments. Within learning experiences,  I coded for how they described learning experiences and describ ed mediating factors in those experiences (age, neurodiversity, gender, sexuality, race, and class). I also documented what they saw as important aspects of their computing work (teamwork, communication, coding), t he ways they spoke about community (like me, POC, white men), and tensions (representation, isolation, values). Afrofuturism Within the Afrofuturism code group, I began with th emes from the Afrofuturism literature and expanded from there around how participants were describing Black Life. Particularly relevant was the work of Y’tasha Womack, Ingrid LaFleur, Christina Harrington, and André Brock (LaFleur, 2011; Womack, 2013; Brock, 2020; Harrington et al., 2022). Prominent codes describe present, historical, and future-oriented methods of thinking about place, time, and the purpose of computing. As an example, I began the process of building Afrofuturism codes began by drawing on literature such as Womack, including rei magining identity and Afrocentricity, and then movi ng up a level; I saw these as aspects of self-determination. I then fleshed self-determination out further, thinking about what was mentioned in interviews, including aspects  of non-Western beliefs, cultural practices, ancestral traditions, indigeneity, gender, and (dis)ability. Of focus to the method of CDA is intertextuality wh ich I most describe within the groups of “Know where you are" and “Know  where you’re going.” In these code  groups I refer to how participants engaged various types of fiction liter ature and  historical knowledge to explain their experiences as a computing practitioner. I created the code group Getting My Black Life  (GMBL) to describe how and where participants found community, joy, physical and psychic wellness, and quiet. Plantation prophecy The code group Plantation Prophecy describes the way practitioners name anti-Blackness, violence, and control and how slavery is reproduced currently and built into the infrastructure to continue to be replicated. To reiterate, Plantation Prophecy is a way of thinking about how transatlantic slavery in the United States was not just a historical period,  rather how it has become a motif that is willed into the future through policy, law, violence, and racism. Participants wrestled with how at times it  feels like they are making anti-Blackness up, how they endure and brush off the racism, and the qualms they have about the kinds of industries and technologies they  are being invited to participate in. Plantation Prophecy works as a compliment to Afrofuturism as we work to  understand both desires for Black Life and desires for Black death as not only emotive but put into action. The codes are built off of my theorization of varying aspects that go into maintaining Plantation Prophecy as well as participant interviews. Parent code “who is human 0” refers to ways whiteness is centered as the model for the human and is further specified with its chi ld codes knowledge structures, social scripts, and  law. Parent code “Race-ism” refers to the way race is employed in varying ways including as technology, as biology, and as conveniently ‘over.’ Parent code “anti-Blackness” includes child codes that describe different methods of operationalization of anti-Blackness such as origin stories, am I making it up, colorism, denials (of contributions), and mammyism.  I offered the parent code “Violence & Control” and child code s such as medicalization, criminalization, limitation of the imagination, and  legislation. These child codes were broken down fu rther; for example, criminalization involves the use of surveillance as a tool at both physical movement and thought levels. The last category, “dreaming slavery now and forever,” talks about the ways that desire for the plantation is caught up in actions such as the creation and maintenance of prisons, engaging in labor abuses, and creating race-class divisions to ensure a lack of wealth among Black pe ople (“stay broke”). While not all of the codes within plantation prophecy came up in the interview, they are helpful for understanding relevant discourses.

## Findings

Across the findings I do not segment how the code groups Textual Adornments, Technical Expertise, Afrofuturism, and Plantation Prophecy  are present, as often their themes overlap with one another. For example, in the same breath that a participant talked about technical expertise they describe d how their intelligence was doubted because of anti-Blackness (Technical Expertise, Plantation Prophecy). Black life in computing The route towards computing practice differed for participants, including the beginning of their journey, learning spaces. Some found themselves enamored by the hardw are aspects, and others were more interested in sof tware and programming. However, many saw the space as a part of their calling, to be a part of a community and “elite” class. Some began computing as a part of their education  at the K-12 level. Anansi, a computing educator, was homeschooled by his parents. They emersed him in African-American history as well as programming, so he felt like “I've been pretty into it from the get-go.” Prince Markie D, a senior systems engineer, de scribed being into computing via video games alongside his experience as one of many siblings born to a single mother without computing expertise. One of his older brothers eventually bought him a Commodore 64, and he was able to learn programming at school and on forums from “weirdo but benevolent” older white men. Participants accounts  were consistent with computing literature that  details how historically and presently computing teachers were more often white  (Margolis, 2017) and teachers of color or familial teachers an exception. Pursuing collegiate computing learning environments was influenced by participants' relationships with mentors in computing, whether family or teache rs. Part of how folks got into computing was through socialization (discourse) that computing was a decent field for them to pursue, whether that was via interest or financial incentives.  Those who began learning computing at the collegiate level were often motivated by media such as sci-fi and narrativ es around financial mobility. However, after being a part of the computing space participants are not sure that the lure of financia l mobility is enough to make someone feel well as a  computing student. For example, Maria, a computing researcher, said, “What drew me to computer science as a whole? Just the economic potential.”  However, they did not have a great experience in undergrad, and described that “I would like go to my teachers for support, and that wasn't  really like I guess, handled wel l.” Participants learned to look to Black peers for advice on mentorship, work, friendship, tutoring, and socio-emotional support. At Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HB CUs), students found community to be less of a challenge, but noted that the ease of perception of technical competence experienced was not the same when they went to internship and working spaces. President Ba rbie, a computing researcher, and HBCU alum noted, “I'm very aware where, like, the boundaries of other peo ple's lack of exposure to Blackness really shape th eir notions about me, or why I'm here.” Within these examples, I see evidence of how negative  racialization shapes social practice in these computing environments. The conne ctions between race and power were present in how p eople talked to their classmates, chose group members, decided on the curriculum, and decided who was equal at work. When participants entered the workforce or pursued further higher education, similar patterns in the positioning of race occurred. Race was often talked  around, and kept secretive by faculty, managers, a nd CEOs; even when it felt obvious that anti-Blackness was affecting participants’ daily lives and  computing experiences. For example, during a moment where race was a highlight of discussion in the US, summer of 2020, also considered a time of racial reckoning, companies fe igned interest. Mango Smith, a sof tware engineer, reflected, “Even in the midst of 2020, when it's like, okay, a ll ears are open, everyone can… ‘You can say what i t is now we're listening.’ … It, it still wasn't you know, received ….” On a larger scale, 2020 brought to the precipice of many Black workers’ minds as they experienced negative racialization on a regular basis – what is my expectation of a corporation? As Prince Markie D and Poppy both  said, “Don’t play yourself.” Putting hope in these institutions does not make sense, given an understa nding of how race shapes social order in the US (Bell, 1992; Sharpe, 2016). Not only were participants expected to be experts on race, but they were also expected to know how and when it was appropriate or requested for racial expertise to be summoned. Plantation prophecy as haunting computing With two exceptions, participants explicitly articu lated anti-Blackness as having shaped their experiences in computing learning environments where they named active disregard and harm. They shared how experiences of anti-Blackness shift ed across time while in computing but was still present. For some, the language of anti-Blackness itself was new, but as it was described, they articulated having experienced anti-Blackness socially, psychically, and sensorially. As Darius put it, “I see it, I hear it, I feel it, right?” In the space of the interviews participants offered insights as to how plantation formations of the “human” are replicated in computing learning environments by asking participants to continue to not discuss racialized harm, caste, surveillance, or violence.

## Lacking discussions of race in computing spaces

For participants, learning about Blackness in the c ontext of computing was often relegated to clubs and research with “social justice” oriented professors who were also in the computing education space. In most class learning environments, computing was constructed as neutral, which participants also equated as holding white values. In searching for information on curriculum that talked  about race or Blackness, 16/20 participants said t hey never encountered anything like that. The first reaction for participants was often “no,” where some asked for a second, “let me think,” and their answer remained, “still n o.” Once in a while, a participant would reflect on  their ethics class as a space, but that was rare. Earth, Wind & Fire pushed their undergraduate advi sor to support them in making a student group for Black and Brown students in computing at their school because such a program didn’t exist. Similarly, for Poppy, he never encountered race in his undergraduate engineering program, and his school’s multicultural office recommended he travel 30 minutes by car to another campus to join their school’s NSBE. Those who participated in REUs  opted into environments where faculty and graduate students laid the groundwork for them to explore race in computing. T opics in these experiences ranged, including algori thmic bias, predictive policing, and justice (Noble, 2018; Benjamin, 2019). In the broader context of these experiences, participants talked about how ethical understandings in computing classes were limited. For example, ethics being that computer scientists should “check their biases ” or “not be racist.” Computing practitioners who attended HBCUs and/or took classes in Black and Africana Studies often named their humanities coursework and ge neral environment, flavoring their perspectives on comput ing and race. Anansi was home-schooled by his educator parents and h is father was a computer scientist. His parents instilled African  American cultural knowledge alongside his computing education, but after he lef t that environment, he “would say, most of the time, people kept things pretty separate.” However just because race was rarely an explicit point of conversation did not mean participants didn’t link race to their learning experiences in computing. Computing and violence When asked about experiences of anti-Blackness in computing, some participants quickly t old stories. Others wondered if covert attacks like microaggressions or patterned exclusio n from group assignments even counted. One of the ways anti-Blackness operates is through making one feel like they are imagining it or overthinking different aspects of interactions. Thinking about e xperiences of anti-Blackness for long periods of time can also lead to deterioration in mental and physical health. However, many participants not ed they needed to be able to recognize that it was happening so that they knew how to navigate that moment. Across the examples are the ways that racialized violence is normalized within computing discourse and how discourse-as-social-practice works to support the control of workers’ and students’ ideas. For example, based on conversations around Darius, he  began questioning whether he could be successful at his computing work despite his high position in the company. As a high-level researcher, Darius said: “So there is respect that comes based on your perfo rmance, but on an additional caveat that is hate. So, you can't win, right? … you had the white power stuff, or you know. Yeah, white male ego or....” In response to white male ego (white supremacy), Da rius often found himself relying on other Black peers in his network to decide how he s hould respond. He detailed how the frequency of ant iBlackness meant that it would be challenging or potentially detrime ntal to one’s career to report every single interaction. As a part of upper management, he found that people were more calculated in what they said, so antiBlackness might not be overt but it was still present. An exa mple of a subtle interaction came from Earth, Wind, & Fire, who said, “There were other people who I've  been in class with for 3 years who would act like they had never known me before when we were in the  same classes for, like 3 years, asking me what my n ame is.” When Arie was asked about experiences of anti-Blackness, she said, “Absolutely in every step of t he way? Probably in every company, that I work with. Every position tha t I face or have been a part of I've I've received notions of anti-Blackness in different way.” After her experiences in Big Tech, Arie experienced  homelessness for nearly a year, which raises the average amounts of encounters with the police and opens one up for additional violence.

## Discussion and implications

Doing it fly Participants found joy in the topic and practice of  solving challenging problems which is a prominent activity within computing. For some computing interest  started through being challenged in video games and  wanting to understand the underlying mechanics. Others found inspiration from sci-fi media, particularly those that featured Black technologists including comics, shows, and movies which utilize Afrofuturist aesthetics. Prominently, The Matrix (1999) and Black Panther (2018) were named f or sharing positive imagery of Black folks in compu ting. Part of the appeals to these media is the depiction  of ease and swag with which the Black characters a pproached technology. Prince Markie D shared that ease mirrored in the workplace can be a cause of tension, “Whenever we do stuff, we gonna do fly. When you do it fly, it makes it look like it came to you easy.” Despite the time, research, and study Black computing practitioners put into their craft, they can be passed up for opportunities as their effort may not be  obviously visible. In order to not be identified di minishingly as “fly,” or put differently, negativel y racially marked, participants found themselves code-switching to tone down humor, confidence, and direc tness. When Black practitioners learn and experience their  cultural practices as being seen negatively, it ca n lead to community disengagement and lapses in self-worth which is a problem for persistence in computing. Means of discourse that are common within code-switching and were identified within the code group Textual Adornment were racially marked and associated with a lack of profe ssionalism. Those who don’t code-switch experience additional challenges with promotion and the reception of their leadership. Participants wrestled with the nature of representation and what it might  provide access to. Ideally, the increased represen tation of Black computing practitioners would increase wealth in the Black population and shift conversations in computing spaces towards ethics, justice, anti-racism, and products that are relevant to varying n eeds within Black communities. However, race is not a proxy for moral compass or ethical training. Other factors such as isolation, class, white supremacy, gender and respectability politics, capacity, and need for economic stability can mediate decision-making for advocacy. This means despite having the Technical Expertise to be in the room, compounding discourses shape how practitioners are empowered to advocate for themselves and others. Haunting and plantation prophecy Plantation Prophecy describes efforts to limit the imagination such that the futures imagined always r eplicate slavery and the plantation. In their interview, President Barbie described the January 1957 "Mechanix Illustrated" ad for a fantasy product, a robot, which would provide white people (as depicte d) with the opportunity to “own slaves again.” Named in several interviews was the computing syste m of Master and Slave, where one part of a computer (the Master) controls all sub parts (the S laves). President Barbie went on to say, “So I don' t know. I'm just like, what parts of computing are anti-Black? I'm like, all of it.” These examples begin t o highlight how computing is prefigured on anti-Blackness and technological imaginaries of the plan tation and slavery. It seems as if around any corner in computing learning space s, one might encounter these phenomena, like a ghos t over one’s shoulder, haunting the culture of technological development. Within the architecture of the workplace, while Bla ck practitioners are skilled at computing, the discourse around their role at the company is often  pejorative. Participants referred to be ing called a “DEI hire” as if their technical skills didn’t matter, and only their Black bodies did for the numbers and optics of the company. When hired into upper management these Black computing practitioners learn they will be asked to take on antiBlack values of the company while also being lauded as a person with ethics.  Plantation Prophecy needs peop le of all backgrounds to buy in for it to be successfu l, and particularly for all people to buy into anti-Blackness (Wilderson III, 2018). It requires sacrifices to maintain the structure of the plantation, where there are those who are rewarded for upholding the Master’s values. Fur ther, it asks the white poor and people of color to  buy into anti-Blackness such that they can also be promoted and reinforce order (Wilderson III, 2018). Without discussions of race and anti-Blackness in computing learning environments we will continue to see these patterns repeated.

## Implications

Within this paper, I described insights offered by Black computing practitioners on the relationships and tensions between Black Life and computing. However, the full expertise I offered was not gained in the computing  or learning sciences classroom; it was developed in humanities spaces su ch as Black Studies. Participants who had taken classes in Black, Africana, and African Ameri can Studies used specified language to describe the ir experiences in computing and futures for computing. Beyond a generic understanding of ethics, the racial analyses offered in the humanities  helps shift attention around the reproduction of harm thr ough technologies. Ruha Benjamin and Safiyah Noble have both written books that are entry points for understanding connections between computing and racialized harm (Noble, 2018; Benjamin, 2019). However, these, amongst others, have not entered into core computing curriculum. Racial analyses are often relegated as not necessary or on the periphery of “real” computing work. While programming and hardware skills are described as “technical skills,” I would like to work towards an understanding of racial analysis as another skill that computing students and practitioners might have. All people, and particularly educators and researchers  must become cognizant of how anti-Blackness works to uphold the plantation and cause suffering for current, past, and future generations of people. As everyone is asked to buy into Plantation Prophecy, everyone must also consider how they will respond, refuse, a nd resist. This requires unsettling the canon of computing, th e ideals of innovation, and the use of technology t o further subjugation. In the classroom, this necessitate s engagement with histor ical and present texts in relation to computing and critiquing ed-tech profits above student learning. We must understand – whose nightmares and whose dreams are represented in utopiandepictions of learning.
