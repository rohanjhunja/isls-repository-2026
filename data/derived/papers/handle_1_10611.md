# The Role of Causal Inferences in Everyday Reasoning: Study on Oncology Patients

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Science education increasingly aims to prepare all learners for civic participation in a quantified digital‐ and science-infused world. To inform how educational experiences and curricula should be designed, the current study explored cancer patients as a locus for scientific practices. The patients were asked to reason about medical information related to the chemotherapy treatment they received and to fill out self-reports on their self-efficacy and engagement in decisions related to their own disease and its treatment. Our findings revealed that the most common scientific practice involved was causal inferences, which in turn had a significant impact on patients’ self-efficacy and engagement in their own disease management. These findings suggest that direct evaluation of medical information using causal reasoning is important for everyday science literacy. Importantly, causal reasoning is associated with everyday functioning and behavior, which underlines the importance of further research on causal reasoning structures.

## Introduction

One of the main goals of k-12 science education is to provide students with opportunities to develop the knowledge and skills they need to live in a scientifically and technologically enhanced society (Baram-Tsabari, 2022; Feinstein et al., 2013; Lee & Dubovi, 2020). To meet this goal and to inform educational experiences and curricula design, many recent studies have focused on scientific practices that are being appl ied by laypersons to navigate daily issues. However, those studies yielded mixed findings regarding the extent to which people  are inclined to draw on scientific practices in their everyday lives and what practices are involved. On the one hand, recent discussions concerning the “post‐truth” era show that  people are disinclined to draw on  scientific practices, portraying the public as being more readily swayed by partisanship, sensation, and emotion than by scientific evidence (Valladares, 2022). On the other, some research indicates that individuals are more likely to engag e in scientific and data-driven reasoning when the problem at hand is signif icant and holds personal value for their functional and personal everyday lives  (Lee & Dubovi, 2020; Tabak & Dubovi, 2023). These mixed findings imply the need for further studies that are grounded in s cientific practices pertinent to the everyday needs  and interests of laypersons.

With this aim in mind, in the current study we perceive laypersons as “competent outsiders“ with respect to science when science has some bearing on their needs and interests (Feinstein, 2011).  Particularly, we examine scientific practices with regard to the meaningful and functional context of cancer patients during their chemotherapy treatment. Such patients  often experience various adverse effects  due to the chemotherapy treatment; therefore, they are required to rapidly learn how to monitor, manage, and prioritize side effects  to prevent their aggravation. In addition  to daily decisions related to disease management, they also often face harmful disinformation (Lazard et al., 2023). This poses a unique opportunity for a better theoretical understanding of how “forced choice”  daily-life circumstances and bodily “episodic feelings” can trigger, sustain, and support scientific practices to discern inaccurate information (Nemirovsky, 2011).

## Research questions

What practices (scientific and others) do cancer patients who undergo chemotherapy treatment use when reasoning about inaccurate non-scientific claims about the treatment? How do scientific practices impact patients’ behavior in terms of self-efficacy and engagement in disease self-management?

## Participants and instruments

Patients  (n=22)  diagnosed with the most common GI cancer diseases (i.e., gastric, liver, c olorectal, esophagus, and pancreas) (Arnold et al., 2020)  and receiving chemotherapy were recruited for this study.  Participants were asked to reason with three open-ended questions that represent common inaccurate non-scientific claims about cancer treatment  (Lazard et al., 2023):  (1) Antiemetic treatment prohibits the efficacy of chemotherapy; (2) Medical marijuana makes you feel better; if it does, one can stop chemotherapy treatment; (3) Immunological or biological treatment is always better and preferabl e to regular chemotherapy. This procedure was done  at the beginning of their chemotherapy protocol and after the fourth round of chemotherapy (approximately two months later). The responses were coded for patients' grounds and justifications. The autho rs independently coded 20% of the responses and found that an inter‐rater reliability test of this random selection of responses was substantial, Cohen's kappa = 0.75 (McHugh, 2012). All disagreements between the authors were discussed and resolved. In addition, to assess patients’ disease and its self-management, we used two questionnaires. T he Decision Self-Efficacy Scale  (O’connor, 1995)  assesses patients’ self-confidence in their ability to make decisions. It includes 11 items on a 5-point scale, ranked from 0 (not at all confident) to 4 (very confident). The reliability of the previously translated Hebrew version was α=.93 (Zisman-Ilani et al., 2017). The Shared Decision Making Questionnaire (Kriston et al., 2010)  evaluates patients’ involvement in shared decision-making during their encounters with a clinician. It has 6 items, with a  Hebrew version Cronbach ’s α of.94. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, Helsinki Committee approval no. 0177-22-TLV.

As presented in Figure 1, the most common practice was causal reasoning (50% of explanations), which is the only scientific practice of the three identified. Analysis revealed that patients distinguished between two primary causal links. The first associates chemotherapy efficacy with its success in combating cancer. The s econd connects chemotherapy efficacy with the sensa tions or side effects experienced during treatment.  This differentiation guided patients in discerning inaccurate information regarding emetic treatment (i.e., “ If you receive chemotherapy and don’t experience nausea, the chemo still works… those are two different things; nausea is a side effect and not part of the treatment”) and pain treatment using medical marijuana (i.e., “Marijuana makes nausea easier, but it does not fight cancer cells  as chemotherapy does”). Additionally, patients presented causal links such as: “Treatment type depends on the genetic structure of the tumor”, “Treatment success can be jugged by tests and not by sensations”, and “Chemotherapy affects healthy cells, which causes side effects”.  An additional type of explanation,  as presented in Figure 1,  was grounded in trust in professional sources. Specifically, 36% of the explanations suggested that “One should consult with his physician” or “ One should seek a second opinion”. Finally, 14% of the explanations were based on personal experience (i.e., “It also happened to me”, “This treatment helps me”, or ”In my experience, you should…”). Interestingly, patients' explanations remained stable across two months of chemotherapy. Namely, there was no significant change in patients ’ practices between pre-and post-intervention (Figure 1) regarding the tendency to use causal inferences (t=0.865, p=0.397), trust in healthcare providers (t=.109, p=0.915), and personal experience (t=-1.073, p=0.296).

## Discussion

Science education seeks to equip students with the ability to critically engage with scientific and data-driven issues they will encounter in their personal civic lives. Designing curricula to better  prepare learners for such civic participation requires an understanding of function al everyday practices. To address this, t he current study explores cancer patients as a locus for functional practices utilized for reasoning about issues related to their treatment over a period of two months. This study’s findings expand existing studies anchored in daily experiences that have  already demonstrated the importance of trust in science  for discerning inaccurate information  and reaching decisions (Lee & Dubovi, 2020; Tabak & Dubovi, 2023), toward the importance of causal inferences. Particularly, in this study, we asked patients to reason on three common inaccurate non-scientific claims regarding chemotherapy treatment, which in turn revealed three strategies that guide laypersons ’ reasoning: causal inferences, trust in professionals, and previous personal experience. Causal inferences were the most predominant strategy and the only scientific one.

The importance of causal reasoning is supported by previous studies that point to causal reasoning as a key player in human learning and problem‐solving  (Ahn & Kalish, 2000; Haskel-Ittah & George-Levi, 2023; Koslowski, 1996). The current results underline that the ability to  negotiate uncertainties  within medical information using causal links relates to scientific literacy. In add ition to causal inferences as the most common strategy, the next common was trust in health care providers. These findings suggest that laypersons, though lacking medical expertise, assess the information predominantly via “first‐hand” evaluations and less via “second‐ hand” evaluations (Scharrer et al., 2017). Although this study presents an extreme case in which laypersons are engaged in a personal life or death matter, taking such an extreme case may reflect on how laypersons engage with scientific and medical information more broadl y. To this end, we might suggest that preparing stu dents for dealing with information uncertainties requires  more than information sourcing  or using heuristics to identify inaccurate information, but the ability to analyze the information and to reason with the evidence might be crucial (Duncan et al., 2018).

Importantly, our case study also demonstrates that the scientific practice of causal reasoning had an impact not only on patients’ ability to discern inaccurate information, but also on their daily life functioning. We show that patients’ tendency to use causal inferences is associated with their self-efficacy and active engagement in their own disease self-management. Patients’ engagement with disease management has been shown to improve patient well-being and is associated with increased survival (Basch et al., 2017). We might cautiously assume that causal reasoning as a scientific practice carries importance not merely for navigation in a digital science and datainfused world, but also for patients’ self-care and health.
