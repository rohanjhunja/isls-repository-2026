# Team-Based Dynamics of Diagnosis and Intervention in a VR Simulation: Towards a Reasoning Process Model

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Accounting neglect of intervention reasoning in CSCL research, we propose a new process model for team-based diagnostic and intervention reasoning in acute care, focusing on interactions among diagnostic activities (DAs), intervention activities (IAs), and collaborative activities (CAs) such as joint information processing, coordination, and communication. Using epistemic network analysis, we analyzed data from a VR-based cardiac arrest simulation to validate this model by comparing expert-and novice-led teams. As expected, expert-led teams demonstrated faster, more cohesive transitions between DAs and IAs, with a streamlined, linear CA pattern, while novice-led teams exhibited slower, fragmented transitions with cyclical CA patterns. These findings support the model’s potential to capture expertise-driven coordination and efficiency in high-stakes settings. Future research may expand this model across diverse team compositions and problem contexts. By refining understanding of acute care team dynamics, this model paves the way for instructional strategies enhancing coordination and performance in collaborative problem solving.

Team-based diagnostic and intervention reasoning: A process model In professional practice, such as in healthcare, people from different professional backgrounds often work together in teams (e.g., acute care teams), engaging in diagnostic and intervention reasoning, to solve complex problems. Acute care teams, typically composed of leaders and team members, engage in a range of time-sensitive diagnostic activities (DAs; e.g., generating and evaluating evidence; Fischer et al., 2014) and intervention activities (IAs; e.g., implementing and monitoring interventions; Richters  et al., 2024), e.g., cardiac arrest resuscitation. E ffective teams rely on rapid DA-IA cycles, supported by  collaborative activities (CAs)  such as joint information processing, closed-loop communication, coordination, and monitoring (e.g., Liu et al., 2016; Meier et al., 2007; Salas et al., 2008; Witti et al., 2023). While more recent research has begun to examine the interplay between CAs and DAs (e.g., Radkowitsch et al., 202 2), prior work has  largely overlooked IAs (Cook et al., 2019; Monajemi, 2011; Richters et al., 2024) and how team interactions shape diagnostic and intervention decisions through shared knowledge and collaborative problem solving. To address the research gaps, we developed a novel process model that integrates previous research (Figure 1).

Figure 1 Process Model for Collaborative Diagnostic and Intervention Reasoning The model visualizes the dynamics of DAs, IAs, and CAs through concentric circles —an approach not systematically represented in previous frameworks. The outer circle represents the primary DAs and IAs. The mixed arrangement of arrows represents the dynamic relationship between DAs and IAs, which occurs without a predefined order and allows for repetition and omission of activities. This cen ters on CAs, such as joint information sharing (i.e., sharing, eliciting, evaluating  information), with the rotating design illustrating  how different CAs become more or less important depending on the current diagnostic or intervention  phase. For instance, coordination may intensify during intervention implementation, while information sharing peaks during diagnostic hypothesis generation. This interdepende nt system highlights how effective problem  solving emerges not only from DAs or IAs, but also from their coordinated execution through purposeful  collaboration. In this paper, we focus on expert-vs. novice-led teams as a validation test to evaluate the model’s ability to differentiate activity patterns and to capture expertise-driven coordination in collaborative problem solving. We predict that expert-led teams will be characterized by their ability to quickly identify current diagnostic and intervention needs, move efficiently from DAs to IAs through targeted i nformation sharing, and engage in intervention-critical CAs such as task assignment and check-back statements (i.e., the receiver notifies the sender t hat the task has been completed). In contrast, novice-led teams are expected to show  greater variability in their activity patterns, to move more slowly from DAs to IAs, and to be less able to focus on the most relevant CAs. This study We use data from a multi-user virtual reality (VR)-based cardiac arrest simulation training (Popov et al., 2 023) and examine differences in the sequences of DAs, IAs, and CAs in expert-and novice-led teams. We hypothesize that (H1) our  process model will adequately represent  DAs, IAs, and CA s and differentiate between DA s, IAs, and CAs across expertise levels, and (H2) expert-and novice-led teams will exhibit differences in activity patterns, with expert-led teams following certain patterns more consistently and efficiently than novice-led teams.

## VR simulation and participants

This study uses a VR simulation (Popov et al., 2023) that requires a team of four individuals. The VR simulation places team members in an in-hospital rapid response scenario where they must work together to collaboratively provide life-saving care to a virtual patient in cardiac arrest.  VR provides a realistic yet controlled environment for capturing time-sensitive team interactions. To effectively complete the ~15-minute simulation, the team must designate individual roles and responsibilities, such as team lead er, chest compressions, airway management, electrical shock management, and medication administration (Figure 2). Figure 2 Multi-User Virtual Reality (VR)-Based Cardiac Arrest Simulation Participants must communicate dynamically, exchangin g information under time pressure and shifting workloads. Participants were placed in the same room, each wearing VR headsets (i.e., f acial cues unavailable) and standing in a semicircle to allow for verbal coordination. A convenience sample is currently being recruited as part of a mandatory monthly code team training a t a large US academic medical center with extensive experience in cardiac arrest simulation. Participan ts include Advanced Cardiovascular L ife Support (ACLS) instructors with clinical leadership experience (pa ramedics, nurses, emergency and family physicians; experts) and emergency or family medicine residents with pri or ACLS training but little to no experience leadin g such teams (novices). For this paper, we analyzed data f rom N = 40 participants, grouped into five expert-led teams (one expert, three novices) and five novice-led teams (four novices). Data coding and analysis Team interactions were automatically recorded and transcribed. Using a coding scheme based on our model (Table 1), we coded the CAs in the interactions in the se transcripts and further coded whether the interactions were diagnosis-or intervention-related (DA or IA) to test H1. Further, to test H2, we calculated the total time the teams spent in the simulation and counted the distributio n of DAs and IAs over this time. Finally, we analyzed the patterns of CAs across both types of teams and compared them using epistemic network analysis (Shaffer, 2016). ## Results

H1: Based on the model, the coding scheme adequately represents, and differentiates between DAs, IAs, and CAs across expertise levels. H2: E xpert-led teams spent an average of 10.5 minutes (SD = 1.12) completing the simulation, whereas novice-led teams spent an average of 15.1 minutes (SD = 3.29). In addition, expert-led teams performed an average of 72.8 IAs (SD = 6.27) and 29.6 DAs (SD = 6.27). In contrast, novice-led teams used an average of 57.2 IAs (SD = 12.46) and 45.8 DAs (SD = 9.52). To compare expert-led and novice-led teams, we created a difference network (Figure 3, b)  by subtracting the average connection strengths of exp ert-led team actions from those of novice-led team actions. Strong cooccurrences  appeared between sharing information, expressing uncertainty, and questioning in the novice-led teams—these connections do not have a strong presence in the expert-led teams. The difference between the expert-led (M = 0.14, SD = 0.18) and novice-led networks (M = -0.14, SD = 0.18) was statistically significant (t(7,99)= -2.53, p =.040, Cohen’s d =1.60). In expert-led networks, information sharing, task allocation, and check-backs were central CAs, with fewer links to others (e.g., eliciting information or stating hypotheses), forming a clear, linear CA pattern.  Novice-led networks showed a less consistent, cyclical pattern with more frequent links between CAs (e.g., evaluat ing shared information, expressing uncertainty, and eliciting information).

## Discussion

Our analysis of ten teams provides initial evidence to validate the proposed model. DAs (Fischer et al., 2014), IAs (Cook et al., 2019; Richters et al., 2024), and CAs (e.g., Liu et al., 2016; Meier et al., 2007; Salas et al.,  2008; Witti et al., 2023) derived from the process model were adequately represented across expertise levels. Interactions in the VR simulation  revealed distinct activity patterns between expert-led and novice-led teams. Expert-led teams were more efficient, following a linear, intervention-focused CA pattern reflecting advanced diagnostic skills (Charlin et al., 2007).  Using established schemas (Boshuizen & Schmidt, 199 2), experts fluidly integrate diagnos tic and intervention  processes. They use forward reasoning, moving  from initial cues (e.g., cardiac rhythm recognition)  through diagnosis to interventions (e.g., cardiover sion, intubation). Their CAs are targeted and efficient, involving relevant information sharing, specific confirmation, and minimal coordination overhead (transactional processes by  Janssen & Kirschner, 2020). This efficiency likely stems from pattern recognition (Charlin et al., 2007) and anticipates likely intervention needs, enabling a coherent thread between diagnostic insights and intervention decisions. Novices, by contrast, exhibit fragmented patterns. Lacking established internal collaboration scripts (Fischer et al., 2013), as indicated by frequent expressions of uncertainty, they rely on backward hypothetico-deductive reasoning and often struggle to connect diagnostic findings to timely interventions (Boshuizen & Schmidt, 1992). Their CAs are more numerous but less focused, involving broad information sharing and less targeted information elicitation, resulting in disjointed DA-IA progressions. While prior work has linked CAs and DAs (e.g., Radk owitsch et al., 2022), IAs remain largely underexplored. Expert-novice differences in reasoning are well documented but are often examined without considering CAs or IAs. Our model addresses this ga p by integrating DAs, IAs, and CAs as interdependen t components of collaborative reasoning. This study represents a  first validation step, focusing on differences in activity patterns. Thus, our contribution lies in three aspects: the introduction of IAs as a distinct analytic construct, the modeling of reasoning as a dynamic process linking DAs, IAs, and CAs, and the application of this model in a high-fidelity VR simulation, which enables fine-grained analysis of collaborative reasoning in a realistic and immersive clinical context.

When interpreting the results of this study, it is important to consider that the VR simulation may have influenced communication patterns. The absence of facial cues, limited co-presence, and potentially lower stress levels compared to real life may have led to increased or altered communication, especially among novices— e.g., more frequent use of CAs such as role assignm ent or check-back statements. However, the simulation was carefully designed by experts (Popov et al., 2023) to resemble real-world scenarios, without prompts or feedback, providing an authentic representation of acute care team interactions. Future steps will involve coding additional transcr ipts, examining the relationships between DAs, IAs, and their interaction with CAs, and testing the applicability of our VR approach to other acute care teams. It is important to note the differences between acute care teams and stable teams in terms of patterns of DAs, IAs, and CAs. Acute care teams operate with high urgency, simul taneous interdependent tasks, limited patient input, and changing roles, in contrast to  chronic care teams, where stable membership, sequential interdependence, active patient participation, and low er urgency allow for  more deliberate decision  making across encounters. F uture research could examine how longer diagnostic and intervention cycles  (e.g., cancer treatment) affect patterns of DAs, IAs, and CAs. Different team compositions (e.g., diverse vs. uniform; interdisciplinary vs. interprofessional) may also influence how teams coordinate DAs, IAs, and CAs. Similarly, the domain of the complex problem  (e.g., educational challenges faced by  K-12 teachers) may shape re asoning processes. Investigating these factors will help refine the model and advance our understanding of collaborative reasoning in complex domains, contributing to instructional strategies that support both diagnostic and intervention decision making.
