# Augmentation, Enhancement, or Replacement? Classifying Generative AI Use in Workforce Development Practice

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how frontline workforce development practitioners use GenAI. A survey of 181 practitioners from seven organizations revealed use cases concentrated in learning and instruction (40%), communication and design (39%), and research and analysis (36%). A typology classifying use cases as augmentation (32%), enhancement (26%), replacement (23%), or unspecified (18%) shows that a majority reflect augmentation or enhancement. These results can inform practical guidelines that optimize human-AI collaboration while minimizing replacement of critical thinking.

OpenAI's ChatGPT ushered in a new era of technological advancement when it was introduced in November

2022. Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) and its expanding ecosystem of tools have begun reshaping how

people work, learn, and live, offering benefits such as increased productivity and access to information (Noy & Zhang, 2023). At the same time, concerns have emerged around cognitive offloading, dependency, and harm to learning (Bastani et al., 2024; Cheung, 2025; Kosmyna et al., 2025). While a growing body of research examines GenAI in formal K–12 and higher education settings (Cao & Dede, 2023; Yan et al., 2023), far less attention has been paid to frontline workforce development practitioners —those in community colleges, public workforce agencies, apprenticeship programs, and skills bootcamps—despite their central role in preparing individuals for a changing labor market (Ogunleye et al., 2024).

Understanding practitioner use of GenAI in these contexts is critical because workforce development serves diverse populations —including career changers, jobseekers, and individuals from underrepresented communities—whose instructors and coaches often impl ement AI-enabled practice with minimal guidance. This paper advances a practice-grounded account of GenAI use in workforce development by asking: (1) how frontline practitioners currently use GenAI across tasks; (2) to what extent those use cases represent  augmentation, enhancement, or replacement; and (3) what implications these patterns have for GenAI integration. Drawing on a mixed-methods survey of 181 practitioners across seven organizations, we develop a functional typology and discuss implications for learning design, equity, and policy. We developed an anonymous online survey administered via Qualtrics consisting of ten items: two screening questions, one multiple-choice item on use frequency, and seven open-ended questions informed by existing literature on GenAI adoption (Yan et al., 2023; Ogunleye et al., 2024). The full instrument is available upon request.

Through the researchers' professional network, seven workforce development organizations were invited to distribute the survey to frontline practitioners. These organizations represented a range of contexts: five nonprofits spanning professional certification, career services, clean technology training, labor union apprenticeships, and IT skills development; one regional workforce agency; and one metropolitan workforce development board. They were geographically distributed across the United States and served diverse populations including jobseekers, career changers, apprentices, young adults, and military-connected individuals. Participation was voluntary, and the study received IRB approval prior to data collection. The survey yielded 181 responses, of which 44 were excluded for ineligibility, leaving 137 qualified respondents. A thematic analysis was conducted following Naeem et al. (2023): two coder-researchers developed a codebook inductively after reviewing a sample of responses, iterating until reliability was validated. We then extended the analysis abductively (Tavory & Timmermans, 2014), posing a theory-driven question: What kind of work is GenAI doing in these contexts —augmenting, enhancing, or replacing practitioner tasks? This cycle yielded the typology in Table 1. The key distinction between augmentation and enhancement is whether the practitioner could complete the task independently: augmentation involves AI supporting tasks already within the practitioner's capability (e.g., drafting a document faster), whereas enhancement involves AI enabling tasks that exceed the practitioner's existing skill set (e.g., correcting grammar for a non-native English speaker). Frequencies 755 were analyzed at the response level so that multiple mentions of a single code by one respondent counted as a single instance.

Learning and Instruction encompassed lesson planning, personalized materials, and assessment design. Communication and Design included refining written communications and creating presentations, website content, and images. Research and Analysis i nvolved information synthesis, simplification of complex topics, and data summaries. Applying the typology, most use cases reflected augmentation (32%), followed by enhancement (26%) and replacement (23%); 18% were unclassified. Overall, 58% of uses fell into augmentation or enhancement. When examined by category, Communication and Design showed the highest rate of replacement (24.5%), while Learning and Instruction leaned toward enhancement (53.5%). At the respondent level, 61.3% reported at least one augmentative use, 45.3% at least one enhancement, and 41.6% at least one replacement —suggesting that most practitioners engage GenAI fluidly across modes rather than being locked into a single pattern. Across use cases, most GenAI activity centers on extending or refining existing tasks rather than replacing them, with 58% of uses falling into augmentation or enhancement. Replacement appears mainly in lower-stakes communication and design work—formatting  slides, editing text, generating visuals—while the deeper cognitive work of instructional design, assessment, and personalization remains with practitioners. One reason this pattern may emerge is that workforce development tasks vary in the degree of professional judgment they require. Formatting and polishing are procedural and well-suited to delegation, whereas designing a lesson for a specific learner population or evaluating assessment validity demands contextual knowledge that practitioners are reluctant—or unable —to hand off. This suggests that the distribution is not arbitrary but reflects an implicit triage: practitioners delegate where the cost of error is low and retain control where pedagogical or relational stakes are high.

The findings rely on a convenience sample and self-reported data, limiting generalizability, and the study does not assess downstream learning or workforce outcomes. Future research should use in-situ observations and outcome measures to determine whether and how GenAI use translates into measurable learning and workforce gains.
