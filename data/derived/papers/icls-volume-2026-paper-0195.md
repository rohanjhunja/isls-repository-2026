# Exploring Emotional Language and Knowledge Building Practices in Middle School Mathematics Classrooms

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how emotional language interacts with knowledge building (KB) practices in a Taiwanese middle school mathematics classroom. Thirty seventh-grade students participated in a three-phase lesson design that integrated mathematical concepts (coordinate plane and linear equations) with emotional reflection. Data were collected from students’ reflections on a digital platform and analyzed through open and axial coding, Russell’s circumplex model of affect, and Epistemic Network Analysis (EN A). Results indicate that positive low-arousal emotions (e.g., calm, content) were the most prevalent, fostering a stable atmosphere for sustained KB discourse. “Improvable Ideas” emerged as the most central KB principle, frequently co-occurring with students’ emotional expressions. ENA revealed three dominant pathways linking emotions and KB principles: (1) positive low arousal with dialogue and collective responsibility, (2) negative high arousal with refinement and authoritative sources, and (3) positive high arousal with diversity and agency. Findings highlight the role of emotional language as a driver of knowledge advancement.

In recent years, the learning sciences have emphasized that education should not only support students’ acquisition of disciplinary knowledge but also their active engagement in knowledge creation and refinement. Knowledge Building (KB) theory highlights the importance of advancing community knowledge through improvable ideas and collective responsibility (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). While KB has been applied across diverse domains, the role of emotional language in sustaining and extending knowledge advancement remains underexplored. Emotions shape learners’ motivation, collaboration, and conceptual transformation (Immordino-Yang & Damasio, 2007), yet mathematics classrooms are often treated as rational and de-emotionalized spaces. Prior work suggests that creating room for emotional expression can reduce math anxiety and enhance self-efficacy (Pekrun, 2017; Orgel, 2022), but systematic investigations of how emotions interact with KB processes in mathematics remain scarce.

This study addresses this gap by examining how middle school students express emotional language in a KB-oriented mathematics classroom and how such expressions support or constrain knowledge advancement. Drawing on Russell’s (1980) circumplex model of affect and KB principles, we focus on the intersection of emotions and collaborative knowledge practices. Specifically, this paper explores: (1) what kinds of KB practices students demonstrate in a mathematics KB classroom, (2) how students express emotional language during KB-oriented learning, and (3) how these emotions support or limit the enactment of KB principles. ## Methodology & Study Context

Participants were 30 seventh-grade students from a public middle school in northern Taiwan. They were at a developmental stage where mathematics learning transitions from concrete operations to abstract reasoning, making emotional expression and peer interaction particularly salient. All participants and their parents provided informed consent, and all data were anonymized for research purposes. Lesson design A three-phase mathematics lesson was designed to integrate KB principles with emotional expression. The unit centered on the coordinate plane and linear equations, embedding opportunities for students to represent emotions mathematically and reflect collaboratively. Activities included mapping emotions onto the coordinate plane, 1457 creating “emotion constellations,” and developing strategies for constructive emotional dialogue. The design aimed to blend disciplinary learning with emotional awareness, fostering conditions for KB practices such as improvable ideas and community responsibility (figure 1). These emotions fostered a stable and sustained environment for KB discourse, enabling students to maintain reflective engagement rather than abrupt shifts of attention. For example, one student noted that plotting emotions on the coordinate plane “felt steady, like putting pieces of my thoughts in order.” Such expressions illustrate how PL emotions provided a foundation for collaborative idea refinement, supporting the continuity of community discourse. This finding resonates with research emphasizing the importance of emotional regulation in sustaining complex cognitive engagement (Pekrun, 2017).

Improvable Ideas as the central KB principle Among the KB principles, “Improvable Ideas” emerged as the most central, co-occurring with diverse emotional categories. Students often framed their emotional reflections in ways that invited further elaboration, such as: “I felt anxious at first, but then I realized we could combine ideas to make the solution clearer.” This integration of emotional language with knowledge refinement highlights that emotional awareness does not detract from disciplinary rigor but instead supports deeper inquiry. The prominence of Improvable Ideas aligns with KB’s theoretical stance that ideas should be treated as improvable rather than final (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). Importantly, this study extends prior KB research by showing how students’ affective states can become resources for sustaining iterative knowledge advancement.

Epistemic Network Analysis: three pathways linking emotions and KB practices ENA results revealed three dominant pathways illustrating how emotions structured KB practices (figure 2). First, PL emotions frequently co-occurred with Knowledge-Building Discourse and Community Knowledge, indicating 1459 that emotional stability underpinned collaborative responsibility. Second, negative high-arousal (NH) emotions, such as frustration, were connected to Improvable Ideas and Constructive Use of Authoritative Sources. These moments often occurred when students encountered challenging problems, suggesting that heightened tension prompted critical engagement with external resources. Third, positive high-arousal (PH) emotions, although less frequent, were strongly associated with Idea Diversity and Epistemic Agency. For instance, excitement or curiosity often preceded novel contributions, signaling that bursts of enthusiasm can catalyze creative shifts in discourse. Together, these pathways demonstrate that emotions are not peripheral but actively mediate how students engage with KB principles in mathematics learning. Figure 2 Epistemic Network Structure of Emotion-Knowledge Building Principles Relationships The findings underscore the productive role of emotions in mathematics KB classrooms, countering the common assumption that mathematics should remain detached from affective expression. Rather than undermining rational inquiry, emotions structured opportunities for collaboration, idea refinement, and creative contributions. Specifically, PL emotions created an enduring background for reflective discourse, while NH and PH emotions acted as catalytic moments for transformation. By situating emotional expression within KB principles, this study provides empirical evidence that affective dynamics are integral to the advancement of collective knowledge. This has implications for the design of mathematics instruction: teachers may intentionally provide spaces for students to articulate and reflect upon their emotions as part of disciplinary discourse. Doing so not only normalizes affective expression in mathematics but also positions emotions as resources for sustained inquiry and innovation. This study examined how emotional language interacted with knowledge building (KB) practices in a middle school mathematics classroom. By combining Russell’s circumplex model of affect with KB principles and applying Epistemic Network Analysis, we identified how different types of emotions served as resources for sustaining or transforming collaborative inquiry. Positive low-arousal emotions provided a stable background for reflective discourse, while negative and positive high-arousal emotions acted as cat alysts for moments of refinement and creativity. These findings suggest that emotions are not incidental to learning but are deeply interwoven with how students engage in advancing community knowledge. Theoretically, this research contributes to the learning sciences by extending KB frameworks to include affective dimensions. It demonstrates that emotions can be systematically analyzed and linked to core KB principles, advancing our understanding of how socio-emotional processes shape collective cognitive work. For mathematics education, the study challenges the persistent view of mathematics as a de-emotionalized domain. Instead, it shows that acknowledging and leveraging emotional language can enhance collaborative problem solving and knowledge advancement. Practically, the results imply that teachers should intentionally design opportunities for emotional reflection, positioning affective expression as a legitimate and valuable part of disciplinary disc ourse in mathematics classrooms.
