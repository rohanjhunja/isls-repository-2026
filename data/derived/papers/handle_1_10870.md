# Identity, Power, and Imagination at a Children's Museum

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We share insights on co-design to support critical engagement with museum content towards identity development. We explore how a co-designed visitor activity can 1) support critical engagement with exhibit content; and 2) support youth to reflect on their identities and power to enact change in their communities. Data are generated in a participatory co-design process with museum leadership. We engage three themes that drove exhibit creation: identity, imagination, and power.

## Introduction

This participatory design research is a collaborati on between a Midwestern U.S. children’s museum and a collective of Learning Sciences researchers. The mu seum’s mission is “to create extraordinary learning experiences across the arts, sciences, and humaniti es that have the power to transform the lives of ch ildren and families.” Our focal exhibit shares the stories of four children who inspired or called for transforma tion (Anne Frank, Ruby Bridges, Malala Yousafzai, Ryan White). Museum partners expressed concerns that due to the large amount of written informational content in the exhi bit, scaffolding deeper engagement with that conten t was a challenge. We all saw the potential for the exhibit visitors to learn about authoring themselves as agents of change in their communities, alongside exhibit-featured children. In other words, we saw its poten tial as a site for rich identity development, or the ongoing process of lea rning and positioning within-and-by community in power-mediated ways (Holland et al., 1998), a process involving imagination work daily (Kondo, 2018). We explore how a co-designed visitor activity can 1) support critical e ngagement with exhibit content, and 2) support yout h to reflect on their identities and power to enact change in their communities. We are interested in how exhibits can support child ren to critically reflect on their communities and identities as justice-aligned learning spaces. Museums have been position ed as learning environments where “visitors… get to decide what to see and do” (Falk & Dierking, 2018, p. 87). Children’s museums, in pa rticular, follow Piaget’s tradition of taking seriously how young learners seek to navigate the world around them (Mayfield, 2005). This highlights designed spaces’ potential i mpacts on youths’ sense of their place in the world  (including their ability to wie ld power). Supporting children’s sense of power and  its enactment through control over decisions/actions is thus central to high-quality museum learning design (Falk & Dierking, 2018). Three-pronged theoretical approach We use a participatory design research approach (Gu tiérrez et al., 2016; Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) that invites a transparent partnership of thought in all steps of the research process. We entered this project with a three-pronged theoretical lens: power, i magination, and identity. This three-pronged lens came from shared conversations on themes the museum sought to explore in their origin al design of the exhibit, which university partners  also identified as helpful for scaffolding children’s critical conversation and reflection. To explore this lens, we orient to the figured worlds of the focal exhibit children  through which their unique navigations and enactme nts of identities are positioned (Holland et al., 1998). C hildren’s navigations of figured worlds i n their daily lives illustrate their capacity to reframe their positions and identities within their lived experiences. We explore identity development as always interwoven with power and ima gination, in that the figured worlds of young learn ers’ developmental contexts are socially constructed and always under continued, collective negotiation. Imagination centers this action in terms of desired positions, thereby facilitating novel forms of power-shifting participation and identities (Nasir, 2002). Through imagination, figured identities arise and are reproduced. Adults can support this by focusing on youth voice and ownership, (Gui térrez, 2012) and on how youth can claim power towa rds enacting community change (Tivaringe & Kirshner, 2021). Youth imagination presents an expanding envisioning of possibilities that contend with existing histories of power and injustice towards emergent forms of co llaborative development (Kondo, 2018). Through imagination, children can explore possible futures and co-create new possibilities (Yuen et al., 2013; Carabelli & Lyon, 2016). As such, we seek to position youths’ a gentic worldmaking through their articulation and u se of resources and visions of value through their naviga tion of the constructed resources of a museum exhib it displaying youth engaging in social, cultural, and political action and change. Design We take a participatory design research (PDR) approach to prioritize doing research with and by, rather than on, people and communities (Gutiérrez et al., 2016). Gr ounded in several traditions, including design research, research-practice partnerships, and social design experiment s, PDR focuses on collaboration and redefining traditional researcher-researched dichotomies (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016)  and a commitment to center communities that will be affected by research. PDR requires foregrounding relationality and contending  with issues of power (Gutiérrez et al., 2016). We aim to continually assess power and relationships in the process, and the kinds of learning and engagement we foreground in our designs and for whom. This alsonecessitates continually interrogating how our own identities sh ow up in this work. We engage this effort as a shar ed project (e.g., asking each other for feedback, constructing  a conceptual framework together w ith all partners, openly questioning assumptions about exhibit engagement together, etc.). Our design focuses on supporting visitors to reflec t on their power and identities in their current wo rlds and to imagine how they might use their power to ch ange these worlds or to take on new identities. Vis itors will begin by engaging with the figured worlds of the ex hibit-featured children. They will then reflect on their own identities and power and consider how they might re imagine their communities. This design is aimed tow ards imaginative expression of identities in power-visible storytelling with multimodal materials and structures.

## Implications

This study could contribute to understanding of chi ldren’s museums as a setting for engaging youth in identity development as agents of change in their communitie s. This process holds implications for both the des ign of museum activities that are criticall y oriented towards reflections on power and justice  in children’s daily lived experiences, and for understanding how identity, power, and imagination intersect.
