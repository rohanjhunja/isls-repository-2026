# Demographic Characteristics Predict Latent Patterns of Resource Use in Introductory Biology Courses

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Introductory STEM courses play a critical role in shaping undergraduate students’ success, yet resource use behaviors and their relationship with academic outcomes in these courses remain underexplored. This study examined latent patterns of resource use among 1,884 students in introductory biology courses at seven research-intensive universities using Latent Class Analysis. We identified four distinct resource use groups characterized by variations in the frequency of social and material resource engagement. Demographic factors, including race, gender, and college generation status, significantly predicted latent group membership. Contrary to prior assumptions, minoritized students, such as Black students, women, and first-generation students, reported frequent engagement with multiple resources, including instructors. These findings highlight the complexity of resource use behaviors and challenge deficit-oriented narratives about minoritized students. Future research will link these patterns to academic outcomes to better understand persistent disparities and inform inclusive strategies to promote equitable success in introductory STEM courses.

## Introduction

Introductory courses in science, technology, engine ering, and mathematics (STEM) are critical ‘gateway s’ for undergraduate students; students’ experiences in these courses disproportionately impact their outcomes in terms of STEM persistence (Snyder et al., 2016), degree completion (Hatfield et al., 2022), and s cience identity (Starr et al., 2020). It has been well documented that int roductory courses produce inequitable student outco mes, often across the axes of race and ethnicity, gender, and college gene ration status (Whitcomb et al., 2021). While previous studies have sought to explain and address  these disparities, few have explored the central r ole that resource use may play in shaping student outcomes.

Today’s students have access to a diverse array of academic resources aimed at helping them succeed in their introductory STEM classes. We define a resource as any person, place, or object that helps students achieve a goal; this includes textbooks, teaching assistant s, peer support, and online platforms, among others. Effective resource use has been shown to improve students’ ac ademic outcomes, making it an important leverage po int to increase equity in introductory biology courses (St ites et al., 2021; Ramirez et al., 2021). However, substantial variation exists in students’ actual use of resourc es, both in terms of which resources students utili ze and the frequency with which they use them (Weatherton & Schussler, 2022; Stites et al., 2019). While resource use is a ubiquitous factor of the academic environment, i t remains unclear what factors may drive variation in resource use and how variation may impact academic outcomes in introductory courses. Variation in resource use may be explained by Expec tancy-Value Theory for Help Sources (EVTHS; Makara & Karabenick, 2013), which suggests that resource use is proximally influenced by students' expectations and values associated with each resource and distal ly shaped by demographic characteristics, such as c ultural background and gender identity. Indeed, previous wo rk has found that first-generation college students are less likely than their peers to interact with faculty me mbers and other campus support s ystems, due to differences in knowledge of resource availability, perceived judgment, and perception of time cost, highlighting the connections between demographic factors, perceptions, and resou rce use outcomes (Soria & Stebleton, 2012; Engle & Tinto, 2008). Beyond college generation status, other demo graphic factors have been shown to significantly im pact resource use outcomes in graduate students, like race and gender identity (Weatherton & Schussler, 2022). However, the extent to which these factors influenc e resource use within undergraduate introductory ST EM courses remains unclear.

Given the importance of equitable success in introd uctory STEM courses and the potential for resource use to impact academic outcomes, understanding both  the drivers and outcomes of resource use variation  is critical. As such, our research aimed to answer two questions: 1) What resource use patterns exist in introductory biology courses? and 2) How do these patterns vary a cross demographic characteristics? We took a person-oriented methodological approach, using latent clas s analysis (LCA) to explore latent  patterns of resource use among introductory biology students. This study contributes to our understanding of how students from different demographic backgrounds engage with academic resources, highlighting the ways in which resource use patterns vary acro ss groups. By identifying these differences, our st udy aims to provide empirical evidence to inform pedagogical strategies that can improve the accessi bility and effectiveness of resources, particularly  for students who may face unique barriers to engagement, ultimat ely supporting more equitable outcomes in these cri tical gateway courses and advancing efforts to diversify STEM fields.

## Methods

To address our research questions, we conducted a s urvey of students’ resource use in 23 Introductory Biology classes at seven large, research-intensive public universities in the United States. Data collection is ongoing, with a current sample size of 1,884. This study was approved by the University of New Hampshire institutional review board (#IRB-FY2025-13).

Survey design The survey consisted of 15 questions across three s ections: consent information, resource use, and dem ographic information. The list of resources used in the survey was adapted from previously published survey questions and refined with feedback from the i nstructors of the surveyed courses during the summe r of 2024. Cognitive interview were conducted during the fall of 2024 to ensure that the questions were clear, relevant, and accurately interpreted by students in the intended participant  population. The final list of resources included seven social resources: course instructor, undergraduate course assistants, discussion section instructor, lab section instructor, tutoring, classmates, friends; and seven  material resources: course textbook, lecture materials, optional revi ew materials, online homework systems, self-created study materials, electronic resources not provided by the course instructor, and artificial intelligence chatbots. T he survey asked students to select ho w frequently, on average, they had interacted with the social resources listed and how  frequently they used the material resources listed. Frequency of use was measured on a 4-point Likert scale, where 1 indicated “Never”, 2 indicated “Once or twice a month”, 3 indicated “Once a week” and 4 indicated  “Multiple times a week”. Participants were also pr ovided an ‘N/A’ option if a resource did not exist at their institution.  The survey collected the following demographic information: gender identity, race and ethnicity, year in school, college generation status, and whether participants took an advanced placement biology course in high school. Following the conclus ion of the course, researchers will collect final grade data for all consenting participants.

## Data analysis

We conducted an LCA in MPlus version 8 (Muthen & Muthen, 2017) using the participants’ frequency of use of each of the 14 resources as indicator variables. Broadly, LCA aims to identify unobserved (i.e., latent) subgroups in a data set (herein referred to as ‘latent groups’) based on patterns in observed data (Masyn, 2013). As opposed to variable-oriented analyses (e.g., cluster analysis, regressions), LCA and other person-oriented analyses do not assume a homogeneous sample and are therefore useful in exploring population heterogeneity. Following Masyn’s (2013) guidelines for best practi ces in model enumeration and selection, we evaluated one through six latent class solutions, a nd selected the final model based on multiple fit i ndices and considerations of parsimony (Van Lissa et al., 2024; Masyn, 2013). After identifying the best-fitting unconditional model, we added the following demographic variables as covariates to examine diff erences in resource use frequency across the derived latent groups: gender, race, year in program, college generation status, and whether or not participants took an advanced Biology course in high school (herein, ‘AP Bio’). Categorical variables were dummy coded with the following groups as reference levels: men, white students, continuing-generation students, no AP Bio, respectively. Covariates were examined u sing the manual Bolck, Croons, and Hagenaars method, where the latent group variable is regressed onto the covariates (Bolck, Croons, & Hagenaars, 2004).

## Results

As data collection is currently ongoing, we present  preliminary results below, based on a current samp le size of 1,884. We expect to have final results prepared by January 2025. Our participants were majority white (53.3%), women (73.7%), and in their first year of college (60.4%). 17.9% of our participants reported being fi rstgeneration college students, and 32.9% reported hav ing taken an advanced placement Biology class in hi gh school.

Fit indices indicated that a four-group solution was the best fit for the data. Figure 1 shows the probability of resource use frequency across the four latent gr oups: “Overall Frequent Users ” (OF), “ Frequent Near-Peer and Material” (FNP), “Moderate Social, Low Material ” (MS-LM), and “ Low Social, Moderate Material ” (LSMM). The OF group (31.5% of sample, n=594) includes stud ents who report ed using all resources at a high frequency. FNP (36.9%, n=696) includes students who reported frequent use of material resources and near-peer resources, but low use of ‘authoritative’ social resources. MS-LM (12.1%, n=228) includes students who reported a lower probability of using material resources, bu t moderate probabilities of using both authoritativ e and nearpeer resources. Finally, LS-MM (19.4%, n=366) reported the lowest average use o f social resources, including both course instructors and peers, but reported a moderate use of material resources. To examine the associations between demographic factors and latent group membership, we added gender identity, race, and college generation status as primary covariates in our model, with year in program and AP Bio as control variables. We used the ‘OF’ group as our reference in the BCH multinomial logistic regression analysis (Figure 2). Gender: Compared to men, women were less likely to belong to the MS-LM or LSMM groups relative to the OF group (Figure 2), suggesting that women in our sample utilized resources at an overall higher frequency compared to men. Race: There was no significant difference in the odds of being in a particular group between Asian, Middle Eastern, or Indigenous students and white students (i.e., our reference category).

However, Black students had significantly higher odds than white students of being in the OF group compared to any other group (Figure 2), suggesting that Black students in our sample utilized resources at an overall higher frequency compared to white students.

Similarly, Hispanic students had higher odds of being in the OF group relative to the IS-MM group (Figure 2), suggesting differences between Hispanic and white students on the basis of social resource use.  College generation status:  Compared to continuing-generation college students, first-generation college students had higher odds of being in the OF group relative to the MS-LM group, suggesting differences based on the use of material resources (Figure 2).

## Discussion and Implications

Grounded in Expectancy-Value Theory for Help Sources (EVTHS, Makara & Kara benick, 2013), our study sought to address key gaps in the literature related to student resource utilization in introductory biology courses. We found four distinct patterns of reso urce use in our dataset; these latent groups were distinguished by their differential frequency of use of social and material resources. Furthermore, we found that race, gender, and college generation status emerged as significant predictors  of latent group membership, with Black, Hispanic, first-generation students and women all showing higher od ds of being frequent resource users (i.e., higher o dds of being in the OF group, compared to other  groups). Overall, our results reveal insights into how under graduate students use resources in their introductory biolog y courses, and how resource use varies across demog raphic groups. By exploring this heterogeneity, this research cont ributes to a deeper understanding of how resource u se shapes student learning experiences and outcomes, a nd can provide actionable insights for instructors,  helping them tailor support strategies to better address th e diverse needs of their students and foster more e quitable learning environments.

The four latent groups of resource use in our data set (OF, FNP, IS-MM, MS-IM) differed based on variation in students’ reported use of key resource  types, like material resources, near-peer social resource, and authoritative social resources. For example, students in the IS-MM group were far less likely to report interacting with their instructors compared to students in the MS-IM group. This contrast suggests differences in perceptions between the groups, like differences in students’ perceptions of social cost or expectations of value derived from interaction with an instructor (Makara & Karabenick, 2013; Weatherton et al., 2023; Soria & Stebleton, 2012). By identifying distinct patterns in students’ resource  use, our work reveals the varied ways students lev erage resources to support their learning, underscoring t he complexity of resource engagement in STEM educat ion. Understanding these behaviors can inform broader eff orts to create inclusive educational practices that  meet students where they are. For example, to better ser ve students in the IS-MM or FNP groups, instructors could consider recording office hours, allowing students who are less likely to engage directly with instruc tors to still benefit from the content discussed during these sessions.

Our findings build on prior research showing that r esource use varies across demographic groups  (e.g., Weatherton & Schussler, 2022), while also challenging assumptions that mi noritized students (e.g., Black students, women, first-generation students) underutilize certain resources. Instead, we found that these students engage with many resources at high frequencies, inc luding course instructors. Previous work has hypoth esized that minoritized students may be less likely to use  resources like course instructors and campus resources due to factors like stereotype threat or differences in cu ltural capital (Engle & Tinto, 2008; Payne et al., 2023). While the context between the previous studies  and current study  is largely the same (i.e., undergraduate students, introductory courses), previous studies often exami ne a single university’s context. Our findings may differ because we analyzed a much broader and more nationa lly representative sample, including student s from Hispanic-and Minority-Serving Institutions. It may be that these types of institutions  provide more supportive environments where minoritized students feel more c omfortable seeking help, but follow-up work is needed to better understand these patterns.

Overall, our results partially confirm our hypothesis (i.e., that differential resource use patterns contribute to disparities in academic outcomes); we found that  minoritized students exhibit distinct resource use  patterns compared to their majority peers (e.g., whit e, male, continuing-generation students). However, contrary to expectations, minoritized students appear to use more resources overall. This raises an important questio n: if minoritized students are utilizing more resources, why do disparities in academic outcomes persist? To fully evaluate this hypothesis, future work linking varia tion in resource use to academic outcomes is an ess ential next step. Furthermore, future work should consider other ways to measure resource use, like asking students how they use these resources, or creating a measure of resource use efficacy.

## Conclusion

This study has the potential  to contribute valuable insights to the literature o n student resource use in undergraduate STEM education and would be of interest to many attendees at ISLS. Before presentation at ISLS, we will have further explored the relationship betw een latent group membership and final grade, in ord er to understand how variation in resource use relates to  academic outcomes. As data collection concludes and additional analyses are completed, we anticipate that the results will provide a clearer picture of how variation in resource use may mediate the relationships between demographic factors and academic success, thereby providing empirical evidence that can improve equity in critical gateway courses like introductory biology.
