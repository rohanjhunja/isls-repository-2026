# Artifactual Knowing in Collaborative Modeling Practices

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We use artifactual knowing and positioning theory as a lens to examine the development of a small group model. In this exploratory case study, we analyzed how a group of middle school students positioned themselves, their peers, and the emerging modeling artifact during a collaborative modeling task. We find that as the model emerged, it created a space where youth challenged assumptions, negotiated content, and redefined their positions through the artifact. Study implications suggest that how modeling artifacts emerge significantly shapes how modeling practices support student learning.

Major issue addressed In science education research, significant strides have occurred in understanding how models serve as generative tools for science learning, such as in cognitive (e.g., Fuhrmann et al., 2024; Nersessian, 2002), disciplinary (e.g., Li et al., 2024), interdisciplinary (e.g., Cole et al., 2024), embodied (e.g., Wilensky & Reisman, 2006),  and linguistically responsive ways of knowing (Fackler & Harman, 2025). Little attention has been paid to  how the modeling artifact symbolizes intellectual, cultural, and social processes and carries meaning. An “artifactual knowing” perspective (Dijksterhuis, 2022, p. 385) offers an approach to explore how students engage with models and modeling to shape their science learning. Artifactual knowing views models as learner-made artifacts that embody learners’ experiences, which includ es how individuals positioning themselves and the arti fact during modeling experiences. Positioning occurs as learners negotiate social authority about the artifact, themselves, and others as the y co-develop the artifact  (McVee et al., 2021). Yet, there has been little exploration of how the artifact’s emergence has shaped the moral order (i.e., rights and obligations of speaking and actin g) of those involved in artifact creation. This, in turn, shapes student learning. The potential significance of this work is that this insight can address issues of expansive and equitable model ing practices for how collaborative models and modeling mediate and shape youth’s social roles and  positions during science learning. Focusing solely on traditional aspects of models may overlook how models and modeling practices done col laboratively shape, and perhaps alter, science learning (Rost & Knuuttila, 2022). This study investigates knowing through exploring how positioning interacts with modeling artifacts to in fluence youth participation in collaborative modeling experiences. We asked:

1. How did youth position themselves, their peers, and modeling artifacts?

2. What role(s) did modeling artifacts play in facilit ating or constraining youth participation in modeli ng

practices?

Theoretical approach Artifactual knowing Artifactual knowing refers to understanding that ar tifacts embody human thought and cultural context (Dipert, 1995). These artifacts symbolize intellectual, cult ural, and social processes and carry meaning. This perspective is particularly relevant in modeling practices, where models—specialized artifacts—play a crucial role in science learning. While tools and artifacts are often used interchangeably (Engeström, 1990; Wartofsky, 1979),  not all tools qualify as artifacts. A rock used to crack open a coconut is a tool, but not an artifact, because it is not human made (Markauskaite & Goodyear, 2017). In science education, models are traditionally viewed as cognitive tools to illustrate, explain, and predict phenomena (Schw arz et al., 2009). However, models are better under stood as emergent artifacts, developed intentionally by modelers. Emergent artifacts are “in the process of becoming,” and are created by individuals who “have the most control over what is happening” (McVee et al., 2021, p. 204). They carry intellectual, cultural, and social meanings, reflecting the modelers’ experiences, thoughts, and roles (Rost & Knuuttila, 2022). Artifactual knowing, therefore, plays a critical role in modeling, as it highlights how learning unfolds through the social interactions fostered by these artifacts. Our focus emphasizes how models, as artifacts, mediate social interactions and shape roles among m odelers (McVee et al., 2021). By examining the arti factual dimensions of models, we propose a perspective that  underscores their influence on the social dynamics of knowledge construction in a community of modelers through various positioning scenarios. Positioning theory We use positioning theory (Harré & van Langenhove, 1999) as a theoretical and analytical lens to expla in how artifacts mediate roles and authority within social  learning environments. As individuals interact wit h each other and with artifacts, they positio n themselves relative to the artifact and are also positioned by others in these interactions (McVee et al., 2021). Positioning theo ry, grounded in social constructionism, posits that  individuals follow “rules that shape episodes of social life” (van Langenhove & Harré, 1999, p. 4). Positioning theory operates through a triad of positions, storylines, and acts to create various positioning scenarios in social interactions (Harré & van Langenhove, 1999). Positions are the identities that individuals take on, assert, or are given by others within social contexts (Pattison et al., 2020). These posi tional identities come to play within the moment-by-moment interactions that occur within the classroom. They are not solely tied to the individual but are assig ned by and negotiated with community members. Storylines are d ynamic and emerge through the patterns of social interactions that arise from the positioning made available by acts. These acts are performed through both verbal communication (e.g., words  and phrases) and non-verbal communication (e.g., body placement  and gestures), contributing to the unfolding of social interaction s. Overall, positioning theory provides a fruitful lens for uncovering the complexities of participation in a collaborative modeling experience. Methodological approach We employed an exploratory case study approach (Yin, 2018) to analyze how a group of middle school stu dents positioned themselves, their peers, and the modeling artifact during a collaborative task within a model-based unit socioscientific issue  unit. The larger project explores how mechanistic, computational, and systems modeling supports youth reasoning about the scientific and societal dimensions of respiratory viral spread. The focus of this study is small group co-development of systems model that occurred during a  three-day summer science camp. Campers (n=21) were taught systems modeling through developing small group systems models about John Snow’s investigations of cholera in 1854. Next, in small groups of three to four, the campers created systems models for the scientific, societal, and economic factors about a respiratory disease outbreak scenario (Figure 1). ## Major findings

Due to space limitations, we provide a summary for each of the three located themes. Theme #1: The modeling artifact as a site of negotiation and contestation The system model became a space for students to engage with the complexities of representing knowledge in their model, which led to moments of conflict, debate, and critique. These negotiations revealed how student s positioned themselves and others as knowledgeable, or uncertain, often leading to accountive positioni ng where students’ authority or understanding was challenged. For example, Hayat indicates in her notebook that  they should add all the listed symptoms, positioning the teacher created artifact as the knowledge authority to be represented in the model. Masks and vaccine are the main ways to stop the symptoms,” positioning herself as knowledgeable to decide what to include in the system model (Figure 1). That’s (pointing to what Jane wrote) is not the cause” pushing back with an accountive positioning by challenging Jane’s stance. The group went quiet for 12 seconds. The system model artifact thus foste red a space where ideas could be proposed, conteste d, and refined. Through these exchanges, the youth continually redefined their positions within the gr oup, shaping their roles. It underscores how positioning around the artifact influenced the direction and outcomes of modeling practices. Theme #2: The modeling artifact as shared understanding The modeling artifact facilitated collaboration, pr oviding students with a shared platform to express their ideas, visualize relationships, and align their thinking through various positioning scenarios. For instance, when students attempted to incorporate  the concept of quarantine, the conversation highlig hted how their initial differing perspectives evolved into a shared understanding as  they negotiated the meaning and implications of qu arantine. Jane first asserted, “Quarantine means keeping distance, ” positioning herself as knowledgeable. Hayat quick ly countered this interpretation, responding, “No, qua rantine means staying at home” explicitly positioni ng herself as the one with the correct definitio n. Without a shared understanding of this concept, the y were unable to make cause and effect connections about quarantine in th e model. Jane next attempts to combine the two ideas stating “Going into quarantine, you are staying away from other people” suggesting that quarantine does involve staying at home but also connects with keeping distance. This attempt was critical because it signaled an effort to reconcile the two conflicting ideas about quarantine. Taylor then added: “Quarantining for long amounts of time also means that you’re not bringing in money,” implicitly posi tioning herself as broadening the discussion to inc lude economic implications. This shift helped move the g roup toward a more shared understanding, without  direct confrontation.  The concept was no longer only about physical distancing o r staying at home —it then included the practical and social consequences, such as the inability to work and financial strain. This conver sation highlights how their initial differing perspectives  evolve into a more shared understanding as they ne gotiate the meaning and implications of quarantine represented in their modeling artifact. Theme #3: The modeling artifact as a tentative space for uncertainty While the system model facilitated shared understanding as well as negotiation and contestation, it also created a space for less confident students to position themselves with the artifact in a tentative or disengaged way. In these cases, the artifact bec ame a source of confusion, rather than clarity. For  instance, when the teacher visited the group and asked them to explain what they included on their model, Sia suggested drawing two arrows be tween “the virus spreading through the air” and “mystery disease outbreak” to indicate a two-way relationship. The teacher asked,  “So why would you draw two arrows over there?” Sia quietly responded, “Because these are bacteria, right?” This was an explicit attempt to position herself as contributing to the group’s understanding, yet her response signaled uncertainty about the nature of the pathogen. By stating “right?” she sought validation from the teacher, indicating tentativeness in her unders tanding.  Jane looked at Sia and said quietly, “The virus,” in response to Sia’s question of “right?” J ane stated more loudly, “I think this arrow would go bo th ways [between the virus in the air and disease outbreak] because I think air is causing this but because of this [ai r] it goes back even more [disease outbreak].” J ane positioned herself as the most knowledgeable within  the group. Her correction was a performative positioning act, as i t was not directly challenged by the others in the moment, signaling that J ane claimed more authority over the content. Her follow-up (“I think this arrow would go both ways...”) continued this positioning. The long paus e and group silence following J ane’s explanation highlighted potential disengagement, where the model did not ne cessarily clarify their thinking but created space for confusion. Sia stated in her interview: “I kind of didn’t know what was going on [about virus spread]...” in which she reflexive positioned her understanding as less than others.

## Conclusion and Implications

Significant advancements have been made in understa nding how models function as generative tools for s cience learning (e.g., Pierson et al., 2021; Schwarz et al., 2009). Modeling artifacts are crucial for foster ing both conceptual understanding and social engagement in c ollaborative learning (Silvestri et al., 2021). How ever, modeling artifacts may also empower and constrain camper lea rning, reflecting the complexity of collaborative learning (McVee et al., 2021). This paper examines how the interplay between students, artifacts, and positioning shapes the learning dynamics in modeling practices.  Attending to how artifactual knowing may create mo re expansive modeling experiences can assist educators, and researchers  in fostering e xpansive modeling experiences.

In this study, the system model expanded ways of knowing within modeling practices by creating a space where youth challenged assumptions, negotiated content, and red efined their positions. Through artifactual knowing, students positioned themselves and the mod eling artifact as they negotiate what to represent and how to represent it. Their positions shifted based on how ideas were received, which influenced individual participation and agency in the collaborative modeling experience. The artifact facilitated shifts in positioning, a llowing students to assert or question roles, moving betwee n uncertainty and confidence. Disputes over model development, while limiting some students’ agency (such as Sia), created valuable opportunities for ne gotiation and deeper engagement in modeling, such as Taylor attempting to tentatively test their ideas. A key implication of this study is that centering a rtifactual knowing in modeling practices can reshape how students collaboratively use models for knowled ge construction. Collaborative modeling not only prompts students tonegotiate both knowing and being in sci ence learning but also influences their agency in group tasks, with some leading and others contributing tentatively. However, for this to occur, teachers m ay need supports in leveraging modeling artifacts to distribute agency equitably and provide opportunities for less confident students to test ideas and build confidence, enabling all students to participate meaningfully.
