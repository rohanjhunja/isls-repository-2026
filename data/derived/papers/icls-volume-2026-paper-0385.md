# Upper-Elementary Students’ Baseline Ideas of Computer Vision

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how 14 upper-elementary students conceptualize computer vision as an entry point into AI literacy. Students completed an open-ended drawing task to elicit baseline ideas. Analysis revealed three patterns: intuitive feature noticing, symbolic image matching, and early mechanistic reasoning. Mapping responses to a Computer Vision LP showed that students with prior AI exposure reached higher levels, while those without were at lower levels, underscoring the need to tailor instruction to students’ prior experiences.

Students regularly encounter Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems that “see” on their behalf, from face recognition to navigation. Yet few understand how these systems process what they perceive. Developing this awareness is key to AI literacy, as it helps learners move beyond the “black box,” think critically about system limits, and engage responsibly with technologies shaping daily life. Computer vision provides a concrete entry point by linking children’s everyday noticing of features like color and shape to computational processes (Touretzky et al., 2019). While research shows children can engage deeply with such complex ideas (Yim & Su, 2025), they also bring naïve or anthropomorphic notions about how machines see (Mertala & Fagerlund, 2023). Documenting these early conceptions is critical, as t hey form both resources and constraints for future learning. Learning progressions (LPs) provide a way to trace these entry points and inform instruction that meets learners where they are while supporting movement toward more mechanistic reasoning. In this paper, we focus on students’ baseline ideas about computer vision prior to formal instruction, attending to the varying levels of prior exposure to AI that they bring to the task. Specifically, we ask:  How do upper-elementary students’ initial conceptions of computer vision reveal entry points into a learning progression? To address this overarching question, we examine two subquestions: (1) How do students represent and justify computer vision processes in an open-ended drawing task?(2) How do these representations align with levels of a proposed Computer Vision Learning Progression, and how do they differ by students’ prior AI/STEM exposure?

Learning Progressions as a Framework LPs capture students’ initial ideas and trace how reasoning develops over time, outlining pathways from everyday ideas to more sophisticated understandings (Shouse et al., 2007). They allow educators to tailor instruction to learners’ starting points and identify next steps (Otterbreit-Leftwich et al., 2024). From a social constructivist perspective, LPs are not fixed benchmarks but tools for connecting student thinking to instructional design. Entry points are shaped by cultural experiences and prior exposure, and movement depends on opportunities to engage, reflect, and refine reasoning. In the case of computer vision, mapping how students explain machine perception provides a foundation for tracing shifts from intuitive, feature-based ideas to pixel-and mechanism-based conceptions. This study draws on a Computer Vision LP developed within a broader AI literacy framework for upper-elementary students (Chakraburty et al., 2025), which specifies five levels from recognizing images as pixels (Emerging) to designing rule-based feature detection (Expert). Students with varied prior AI/STEM experiences (none, STEM classes, math clubs) completed an open-ended drawing task illustrating how a computer would recognize a dog. Although drawing-based, the task also elicited verbal explanations and annotations, and all sessions were video recorded. Two researchers independently coded the multimodal data for thematic patterns (Braun & Clarke, 2006; RQ1) and for placement along the Computer Vision LP (RQ2), establishing inter-rater reliability on 20% of the data (κ =.82). Given the small sample size, differences in LP placement were summarized using frequency counts rather than inferential statistics.

Mechanistic reasoning (Level 3) Students gave early computational accounts, referring to pixels, RGB values, or edge detection. Drawings sometimes included grids, outlines, or terms like “RGB” or “outlines.” Together, these patterns reflect a developmental gradient in the LP —from object-based (Level 1) to comparison-based (Level 2) to early mechanistic reasoning (Level 3), marking distinct entry points into computer vision thinking. For RQ2, responses differed by prior exposure. Among students without AI/STEM experience (n = 4), three were at Level 1 and one at Pre-Level 1. Students with experience (n = 10) spanned Levels 1–3 (two at Level 1, six at Level 2, two at Level 3). Overall, prior exposure was associat ed with more advanced baseline reasoning, while others relied more on intuitive explanations. The findings point to three main implications. First, computer vision can serve as a concrete, age-appropriate entry point into AI literacy. Children already attend to colors, shapes, and outlines, which can be connected to pixels, RGB values, and edges to help them understand how machines process images.  Second, students’ initial ideas varied —from object-based descriptions to comparison-based analogies and early mechanistic reasoning. Surfacing these ideas early helps reveal both strengths and limitations in their thinking and creates opportunities to build forward.  Finally, the link between prior exposure and more advanced LP levels highlights the need for differentiated instruction. While some students reason in terms of pixels and computational steps, others rely on surface-level noticing.
