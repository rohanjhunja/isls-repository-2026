# Learning From and About Failure in a Hybrid Collaborative Game Environment

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Traditional classroom culture and social norms might stigmatize failure and hurt learners' self-esteem. Consequently, learners might miss learning opportunities by avoiding failure to cope with the negativity. Scholars argue the importance of creating learning environments that allow learners to reframe failure as learning opportunities through iteration. This paper re-examines an instructional failure session in a hybrid collaborative game environment to understand how failure and learning processes emerged. We adopt a micro-ecological approach to analyze video format data collected in an afterschool design club. The analysis shows that failure and learning might begin as individual actions but can ripple across the small group and the community to create learning opportunities, especially when the environment supports playfulness and improvisation. Findings suggest that allowing the learning design and enactment to interact can support expansive learning.

## Background

Failure can provide an opportunity to help learners realize that their current information, knowledge, and skill are not enough to solve the problem in the learning process (Vanlehn et al., 2003). This realization can motivate learners to deepen knowledge, understanding, and practice and b enefit more from instructional explanations. Schola rs in education argue that failure signals a time to prov ide support to approximate learning gains (Darabi e t al., 2018). Though productive failure as an instructional approach was first introduced in the STEM context, scholars now argue that this approach can also support creativity and collaborative problem solving (Manalo & Kapur, 2018). This approach has been extended to art education (Sawyer, 2018) and fosters creative collaboration (Hod et al., 2018). However, failure alone does not lead to learning if learners avoid it and the learning process is not supported (Jackson et al., 2021). One of the supports identified in the literature frequently was to adopt a failure positive mindset (Canon & Edmondson, 2005; Ryoo et al., 2015).

Video game and design contexts are gaining more attention as scholars argue that both can foster learners to frame failure as a process that houses learning opportunities. Failure in gaming marks critical learning opportunities for players. While failures occur within a game, players are given more opportunities to develop a core understanding of gameplay elements. With educational games, these opportunities are greater exemplified in increased collaborative discourse moments between players that provide a de eper understanding of gameplay elements and learnin g goals (Anderson et al., 2018). Unlike many students' opportunities in class, video games can provide students with a playful and "optimistic motivational style" of learning (Gr anic et al., 2014). Games, such as Minecraft, allow  students to explore creative design and choice-making with imme diate visualization that promotes authentic dialogu e between members (von Heland et al., 2015). Cultivating a failure positive mindset is also repeatedly discussed in the making, creating, and innovating activities (Martin, 2015).  A failure positive mindset is considered a belief that designers embody through practice and is encultured in their environment and activities (Brown, 2008). In these activities, designers are allowed to learn from failure through tinkering and iteration (Ryoo et al., 2015). Though game and design contexts can potentially support learners to frame failure with a positive atti tude, the learning process to make that shift in mindset is a complex phenomenon involving interaction between participants in multiple levels, learning objects, and teachers. Persistence to overcome and learn from failure is influenced not by individual characteristics such as grit or a growth  mindset (Ryoo & Kekelis, 2018) but by the sense of  playfulness, collaboration, and ownership of projects (Yan & Borge, 2019; Ryoo & Kekelis, 2018). As conversing with materials and teams members are the core of the collaborative design process, learning from failure also ascribes to this sociomaterial nature which calls for an ecological approach to account for its complexity (Borge & Mercier,  2019; Hakkarainen et al., 2013; Mehto, 2020). This framing calls for abandoning the fixation on the arbitrary distinction to identity "success" or "failure" and focusing on understanding the conditions that construct learners' learning (Varenne & McDermott, 1998). The teacher who orchestrates the learning environment should also be able to see beyond failure as disruptive and appreciate the learning opportunities it can provide.

## Theoretical Framework

ICLS2022 Proceedings 815 © ISLS Learning from failure in a collaborative learning e nvironment requires us to take a micro-ecological l earning perspective where learning is transformational (Borge & Mercier, 2019). Students are creating novel digital objects, and the approach to that creation is constantly cha nging. As stated by Sannino et al. (2016, p. 600), "The collective design effort is itself the core of an expansive learning process." This is even more so in a technology-rich collaborative design environment. In this type of learning system, individuals, groups, and the whole community are interrelated where students' sense-making and meaning-making are constantly influencing each other. Looking from an expansive learning lens, we see failure events as potential learning opportunities rather thandisruptive events that hinder learning. We apply the playfulness perspective to understand  the emerging learning opportunities and the interaction between video games and players. We follow the post-structuralist perspective and highlight the emerging nature of learning from failure in such an open virtual world  of Minecraft as in this study. Looking through the  lens of playfulness, we are in a better position to reframe learning from failure by fully conceptualizing the change in sensemaking around failure events (Ryoo & Kekelis, 2018). At the same time, as learning is unpredicted in such an environment, it gives rise to the need for both teachings and learning to be improvisational (Sawyer, 2000, 2004), where facilitators and student groups spontaneously respond to the happenings in a specific situation. Failure events could trigger negative feelings in both facilitators and students for facilitators who have designed the curriculum a nd instruction. These events hinder the achievement  of learning objectives. For students, these events often destroy or hamper their design processes. Being improvisational in such emotionally challenging situations often requires shifts in interpreting these events from negative to providing learning opportunities such as socio-emotional regulation, problem-solving, design practices, etc. Playfulness, in turn, plays a critical role in promoting such a shift in sense-making around failure events (Mortensen & Younas, in press). With a playful mindset, facilitators and student groups could feel the lightness of those high-stakes events. They could venture more into risk-taking and take the failures as learning opportunities to problem-solve jointly (Lemke, 2013). Synthesizing studies on game and design research around failure, we re-examine a curriculum failure event identified by the first author, who also served as the lead facilitator in the learning space. We aim to understand how failure and learning from failure emerged at the intersection of a collaborative game and design environment. Situating this work in the design context, we ask the following question to guide the study: How do failure and learning from failure events emerge in a collaborative hybrid game environment?

## Research context and population

The study took place in an afterschool design club where the curriculum targeted fostering productive failure in a collaborative design context with elementary studen ts. Design groups were asked to complete a butterfl y garden challenge using different tools such as paper and pencil, LEGO Blocks, and Minecraft to iterate in the club. This study included 16 students from 4 th to 6 th grades, 5 female and 11 male students. Four return ing students had been in the club for over two semesters. They were grouped as an expert group. We then grouped the remaining 12 students based on their ages, gender, and expertise.

The data reported in this study came from the penu ltimate in the club, which the first author planned as the last building session using Minecraft. ## Data collection and analysis

Video cameras were used to capture activities in all four groups, totaling approximately 72 hours of video recordings. Guided by Interaction Analysis (Jordan & Henderson,  1995), we first created content logs for all the r ecordings to describe group verbal and nonverbal interactions. This step was critical to systematically selecting data from the large corpus of video data. Triangulating the content log  with the first author's field notes, we selected t he last Minecraft building session for in-depth analysis. We identified the episodes of failure, defined as the errors/mistakes committed by players in the Minecraft game that negatively af fected design processes. We tracked the development  of these events over time. The initial analysis showed that when failure events occurred in the shared virtual space, they created community-level interruptions that involved multiple students from different groups. These interruptions affected their groups' design process. As such, rather than analyzing each group as a unit, we conducted a multilevel analysis, i.e., individual, group, and community, to present the ca scading effects of failure events and how, in this community, individual students shifted their perspectives on t hose events (Borge & Mercier, 2019). To organize th e multilevel analysis, we followed Ash's (2007) three-level approach – flow chart, significant events, and microgenetic. Using the concept of transecologcal event from the micro-ecological approach (Borge & Mercier, 2019), we identified and analyzed two events where problems at one level of interaction (individual, group or community) became  central across interactional levels. These two events showed how failure and learning in the collaborative game environment emerge and develop as part of an ecological system.

## Discussion

To understand how failure and learning from failure  emerged in a hybrid collaborative game environment, we synthesized three analysis approaches (Ash, 2007; A uthor, 2019; Jordan & Henderson, 1995;) to analyze two significant transecological events. The analysis sh ows that the hybrid collaborative game environment supports transecological interaction, allowing players to le arn game features from failure and take failure pla yfully. The findings also show how failure events, when supported by guided reflection, can support shared regulation processes. The findings show a ripple effect of learning when the configuration of the learning environment allow s transecological interaction. The interaction between physical club space and virtual Minecraft space allows failure to be experienced collectively, helps construct a shared understanding within the whole club and supports dialogues that aim to create collective solutions. One student or group's changed view on a failed event could ripple  through other groups and change the community's perspective. In our hybrid collaborative game environment, learners expansively transformed their sense-making around failure. Stud ents from other teams affected by the individual ac tion learned valuable game skills in both significant events. In  the teleport event, Teddy (Team 3) and Adben (Team  4) learned that they could use this feature to bring teammates back to their building sites which was the cause of the chaos in the first place. In the transition to the second event, we observed the shift from denying to claiming res ponsibilities and frustration to laughing. The playfulness behaviors such as laughing at breaking the server together emerged from the classroom in the second transecological event and f ostered a learning and playful community. Instead o f avoiding failure, players laughed and broke together to learn. The playful environment triggered the competitive sense to claim ownership of the failures. The second event started  with multiple players trying to build giant struct ures and ended with students learning how to use build mode to cre ate giant structures quickly and reduce the damage by using the "undo the last fill" feature. It also led to the student-led creation of shared community practices, rules, and values. Taking a micro-ecological approach to re-examine an instructional failure, we see how the technical is sues that disrupted the lesson plans could be transformed into joint learning activities for the community if the environment allowed playfulness and improvision to emerge and have the existing configuration to support learning. This finding implies the interactive relationship between design  and enaction of the learning environment. The less on plan set a structure that guided the general flow of the event s. Though, its grand level structure allowed moment-by-moment improvisation and playfulness. It allowed students and the facilitator to collaborate creatively to ad vance a disadvantaged situation with playful attitudes (Saw yer, 2017). At the same time, the intentionally des igned and cultivated guided reflection practice was there to support the whole club to learn from the failure. F ailure in the Minecraft world can still be high-stakes and with real consequences, which was evident by some students' reactions toward teleporting and server crashes, but the feel ings and meaning-making around those failure events  promote playfulness (Juul, 2009; Lemke, 2013), and psychological safety (Edmondson & Lei, 2014), which offer opportunities for facilitators to help students learn. For facili tators in this environment, we argue that we need t o incorporate a playful mindset to be more open to the emergence of problems and react accordingly. We argue that we should allow our teachers to treat every instance of failure as an opportunity to learn, let it play out, and then improvise on the emerging moment to support creative, collaborative learning (Sawyer, 2004).
