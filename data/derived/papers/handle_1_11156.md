# A Case Study of Activity Structures and Resources that Support Teacher Agency and Leadership in an RPP

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This case study reveals how one teacher’s agency and leadership in a Research Practice Partnership (RPP) impacted the design of a justice-centered science unit. We analyze RPP documents and data, from design meetings, workshop transcripts, observations, and informal co-design conversations, to reveal the ways RPP activity structures helped to support the teachers’ leadership. We report our findings in three illustrative episodes. Episode 1 reveals how the teachers’ longstanding involvement with the RPP empowered him to bring a new unit topic to the collaboration. Episode 2 documents the teachers’ leadership during workshops. Episode 3 reveals how conversations between the teacher and the learning scientist during unit implementation supported the refinement of the unit and reflection on teaching practice.

## Introduction

Research Practice Partnerships (RPPs) are long-term relationships among teachers, school leaders, designers, and learning scientists who jointly seek to improve tea ching practice, curricula, and pedagogy (Coburn et al., 2013). This work illustrates how a t eacher can navigate the power dynamics of an RPP to  exert agency (Penuel et al., 2023). To build equitable, effective instruction, R PPs need to attend to power dynamics that exist wit hin them. They especially need to navigate relationships between teachers and other partners to ensure that teachers’ voices are incorporated. We explore how activity structure s within an RPP supported Gregory (pseudonym), an 8 thgrade science teacher, to take the lead on the desi gn of a justice-centered science unit that conne cted NGSSaligned genetics content to the story of Henrietta Lacks. Gregory was involved in the creation, design, and implementation of this unit. He also introduced it to other teachers. He worked closely with the first  author to refine the unit over two iterative design cycles. I n understanding how the RPP supported and constrain ed his agency, we contribute to recent research illuminati ng challenges and opportunities within RPPs (Goldma n et al., 2022). We draw on Knowledge Integration and Justice-Centered Science Pedagogy to analyze how resources and activity structures uphold or disrupt typical power dynamics within the RPP. The following two research questions guide this work: ● How did RPP activity structures and resources suppo rt Gregory's agency and leadership in refining the curricular unit?

● How were typical power structures disrupted in the RPP?

## Background on RPPs

Many studies involving RPPs highlight the products of those partnerships (e.g., curriculum) and how th ose products support student learning. Fewer studies fo cus on the structures of RPPs themselves and how th ose structures impact teacher learning, agency, and pow er dynamics in RPPs (Penuel et al., 2023).  Teacher s are experts in the classroom context. They have vital k nowledge of pedagogy, their school sites, and stude nts. However, researchers may act as authorities in RPPs  and teachers may be positioned as  enactors of curriculum, rather than co-designers and co-researchers. Activity structures that position researchers as authoritative can imply that teachers’ knowledge is less valuable than researchers’, stifling teachers’ voices and agency in the partnership. Structures candisrupt these typical dynamics to po sition teachers and researchers as respecting each other's perspectives (Kyza & Agesilaou, 2022). Researchers are exploring ways to reframe RPPs to value teacher s’ voices. For example, Philip et al., (2022) propose a teacher solidarity methodology that brings together co-design and participatory design frameworks to support teac hers and school communities. This framework emphasi zes teacher expertise and considers the unique power dy namics navigated by teachers, such as the political terrain of schooling. Cheuk & Morales-Doyle (2022) focus on interactions in the “middle” of partnerships where participants negotiate individual priorities and th e resources and constraints of institutions involve d in partnerships. Both frameworks emphasize the importance of  empowering teachers (and students) through partnerships that disrupt typical power dynamics. Building on these frameworks we analyze the power dy namics and resulting agency in a co-design partnership between a learning scientist and a teacher. Research has highlighted specific RPP activity structures and resources that facilitate collaboration and increase teacher agency. For example, Matuk et al.,  (2016) highlight how certain artifacts, such as fu nctional prototypes or student work, help to el icit teachers’ ideas. The prototypes elicited teach ers’ ideas about what technology would be most useful in the classroom, as well as critical feedback on the prototypes themselves. The teachers’ ideas often illuminated considerations th at had not been previously discussed by the researc h team. Severance et al., (2016) use cultural-historical activity theory to organize co-design materials and analyze activity in an RPP. They found that a standards-aligned storyline tool was key in supporting collaboration and eliciting teachers’ ideas about the design of unit activities. Both studies involve tools that gave teachers agency tonegotiate redesigns that better fit their classroom context. The studies illustrate how RPP activities can affor d or constrain teachers’ ability to share their ideas as well as reinforce or disrupt power relationships.

## Theoretical framework

We merge Knowledge Integration (KI: Linn & Eylon, 2 011) and Justice-Centered Science Pedagogy (JCSP: Morales-Doyle, 2017) to support the RPP co-design of justice-centered science units. Both frameworks stress the importance of building on students’ ideas and empow ering students to make changes in their communities. KI provides a research-tested sequence of activities to support students i n building on their initial ideas to form an integrated understanding of complex science phenome na and social justice science issues (Bradford, 202 3). This sequence features: eliciting students' initial idea s; creating opportunities for students to discover new ideas; supporting students in distinguishing between their new and initial ideas; and enabling students to link their ideas to explain the science phenomenon or social justice  science issue. JCSP centers equitable science inst ruction around anti-racism and local social justice issues to motivate students to disrupt unjust power systems. JCSP extends KI in three ways. First, connecting science  content to relevant social justice science issues aligns with KI’s emphasis on connecting science learning to stu dents’ lives. Second, centering instruction on anti-racist and equitable science builds on KI’s design principles for reconfiguring instruction to integrate social justi ce science issues. Finally, positioning students as transforma tive intellectuals resonates with KI’s development of autonomous learners who use their expertise and age ncy to bring about change. Merging the  frameworks emphasizes the importance of viewing each student idea as an asset. These two frameworks align in their emphasis on con necting science content to students’ experiences and valuing the rich collection of ideas students b ring into the classroom. Together, KI and JCSP prov ide a framework for designing the unit at the center of t his study. They also offer a shared language that t he RPP uses to discuss their co-design work with one another. Workshops and other R PP activities explicitly call out KI and JCSP pedagogy, scaffolding an understanding of each framework and how they work together to support student learning. Participants use this shared language to discuss unit co-design, feedback, and student learning. Additionally, by embracing a justice-centered approach to student learning and curriculu m design, we center discussions of race and power within RPP activities, reflecting  the RPP goals of positioning all participants as learners and experts working towards justice together.

## Curricular context

In this work, the teacher, Gregory initiates the id ea of integrating the story of Henrietta Lacks into  an existing genetics unit previously developed by the RPP. Henrietta Lacks was a Black woman living in Baltimore, Maryland who went to Johns Hopkins Hosp ital, a public hospital that served African-American patients, for medical treatment. Doctors collected her cancer tissue without her consent and inadvertently discovered the first immortal cell line. Her cells would prove to be revolutionar y for medical research though her family would be u naware of this for many years. Henrietta Lacks’ story has bee n taught in biology and biotechnology classrooms at  the high school, college, and graduate levels. Gregory saw t he value of introducing this story in middle sc hool science to connect to students’ concerns about data privacy. The story also illustrates the relevance of learning how mutations in DNA disrupt cellular division leading to unregul ated cell growth and cancer. The final version of t he unit developed by the RPP, supported students to discove r how Henrietta’s privacy was violated and how her story raises issues of privacy and medical racism. Studen ts explored how medical data is accessed and what q uestions or issues a patient might consider when deciding to share their data and participate in medical research.

## Participants and positionality

The first author and Gregory worked together during  the design and refinement of the unit. Both value the collaborative nature of the RPP and believe that the RPP provides a space where teachers can share their expertise and knowledge of classrooms to impa ct learning sciences research. Gregory is a White t eacher who has worked with the RPP for twelve years since he joined his s chool site as an eighth-grade science teacher. During the reflective interview, Gregory stated that he valued the RPP because “It allows me to do action research in my own classroom that feels fun and easier than if I were doing my own thing.” Gregory initially suggested th at the RPP use the story of Henrietta Lacks to raise questions for students about the role that race and power play in science. He feels that discussions of social justice and race are “important to teach in all classrooms.” The first author is a white woman, a former high school science teacher, and a Ph.D. student in learning science. She taught  in a different district in the same metropolitan area as Gregory for four years. Her previous teaching experience allows her to bring the perspective of both a teacher and learning scientist to collaborative work with Gregory.

In this case study design, multiple data sources helped understand how Gregory took on a leadership role in the RPP and how activity structures supported his agency in the co-design process (Yin, 1998). We sequenced the data sources to create a timeline of  events documenting Gregory’s involvement in the des ign and refinement of the Henrietta Lacks unit (Fig 1). The first author identified episodes within the data corpus where Gregory was involved in the decision-making of the unit and coded each episode as primar ily KI-focused, JCSP-focused, or both. KI-focused episodes were further aligned with one or more of the KI processes (elicit, discover, distinguish, connect/reflect). JCSP episodes were further aligne d with one or more core aspects of JCSP: integratin g local social justice issues, creating anti-racist and equitable scie nce classrooms, and positioning students as transformative intellectuals. These coded episodes were brought to the second author for discussion, e pisodes where there was disagreement were discussed in more depth, and codes were revised if necessary. Once all codes were agreed upon the first and second authors ident ified three episodes (one JCSP, one KI, and one bot h) to illustrate how Gregory was involved in the co-design of the unit and how Gregory used the language  and perspectives aligned with each frame work to share his ideas and take on a leadership role. The authors then designed a short, 45-minute reflective teacher interview. This interview  asked Gregory general questions about his involvement in the RPP (“How would you describe  your role in the partnership? What are the highlig hts of your collaboration with the university researchers? ”) and presented the three episodes to gain insight  into his perspective of his role during the episodes (figure 1). ## Results and discussion

Gregory was involved in the design of the Henrietta Lacks unit from the beginning and continued his involvement throughout two iterative design cycles. Episode 1 (JCSP) highlights how Gregory’s long-standing relationship with the RPP empowered him to bring  a social justice science issue to the RPP. Gregory was the first teacher to use the unit. This positioned Gregory as an expert during Episode 2 (KI) where he shared his experience using the unit. During the workshop he discussed his ideas fo r improving the unit with other teachers who planne d to use the unit. Episode 3 (merging JCSP and KI) documents how Gregory and the first author worked together to refine an additional activity to scaffold a whole-class discussion about Henrietta Lack and how her story connects to the issues of injustice today. In this episode, Gregory led the creation of the activity. Conversations between Gregory and the first author provided spaces to reflect on pedagogy and refine the discussion activity between subsequent periods.

Episode 1: Proposing Henrietta lacks as a social justice science issue Gregory felt empowered to bring a new unit idea centered around a social justice science issue to the RPP. Unlike other RPPs (Severance et al., 2016) this RPP did no t have formal structures for teachers to suggest un it topics or storylines, though teachers were often asked about future topics in design meet ings. Gregory initiated the design of the Henrietta Lacks unit, likely due to his long-standing relationship with the RPP that supported h im to feel that his ideas held value. While working on a diffe rent anti-racist science unit, Gregory suggested that the sto ry of Henrietta Lacks could be an important case to di scuss in future curricula. When asked about his des ire to introduce these topics in a unit during the reflect ive teacher interview, Gregory said “I just feel like it was an opportunity to bring in a lens of social justice and racism and talk about it in the science classroom”  Here, Gregory emphasizes the importance of a social justice focus  in science which is in alignment with the goals of  JCSP. He then elaborated that this work needs to be done int entionally with clear, authentic connections betwee n science content and justice issues that are meaningful for students “It can be difficult to find natural ways to talk about this stuff without it feeling contrived. Students notice that especially students of color, and it feels fake.” To him, Henrietta Lack’s story felt like an authentic way to connect big issues of medical racism and privacy to the genetics content he was already teaching. His desire aligns with JCSP’s goal of bringing justice and science in  authentic, meaningful ways for students. Gregory’s comfort in expressing his thoughts and opinions to learning sc ientists was evident in the reflective interview. He stated that teachers' ideas often moved units forward and that he could “push back a little bit” when necessary. Episode 1 highlights the importance of creating longstanding partnerships with teachers to support teacher agency, which aligns with suggested best practices for RPPs found in the literature (Penuel et al., 2023).

Episode 2: Implementing the knowledge integration pedagogy Gregory was the only teacher among those working on the unit during the summer workshop who had taught the pilot. All other teachers were preparing to teach a n updated version of the unit during the following school year. This positioned Gregory as an expert. Gregory acted  as a leader during both the summer and winter work shops by sharing his experience implementing the unit with other teachers and providing continued feedback on the unit during unit customization discussions. Unit discuss ion groups brought  together teachers who were planning to use the same unit with learning scientists to examine the prototype in the Visualizer and discuss improvements to the unit. This activity structure created space for teachers to walk through the unit, discuss teaching strategies, and provide feedback on the unit. Many of Gregory’s insights aligned with the KI framework. For example, he shared how certain strengths of the unit engaged students and supported them in discovering new ideas about H enrietta and her family. Gregory highlighted a video in the unit that described the story of Henrietta and her family. He emphasized that he found this video to hook student s into the unit and that students “perked up here a  little more and asked more questions and clarifications”  as they heard more about how her family took action  to educate others about Henrietta. He encouraged other teacher s to spend time discussing the video with students to foster engagement and discovery of new ideas. Gregory’s pr evious experience with the un it positioned him as an important source of knowledge to his teaching colleagues in the workshop and the unit discussion groups provided space for Gregory to share his knowledge with his p eers. In this space, Gregory took the lead instead of the learning scientist, disrupting power structures that normally position the learning scientist as an expert. Interestingly, when asked about his work with other  teachers, Gregory emphasized his role as a learner alongside other teachers rather than a leader among st teachers. He emphasized that learning from other  teachers was especially important as a White man w hile working on justice-oriented science units because of his limited perspective due to his positionality. “I've really appreciated teachers at different schools and diffe rent demographics, and different identities of teachers… seeing their perspectives because I don’t have that experience. It's another lens to think about everything, anothe r lens to think about as a person who is a White ma le.” Though Episode 2 documents Gregory’s leadership in the wor kshop, he was clear in emphasizing the work he need ed to do. These kinds of learning opportunities were what  kept him coming back to workshops to collaborate w ith fellow teachers. His comments appear to demonstrate that, though Gregory did take a leadership role, he sees his work with other teachers as a collective learning opportunity where all teachers have knowledge to share. Gregory also continued to provide feedback and work  toward improving the unit with the first author and fellow teachers. Though the unit had not been updated with his changes yet, he was able to explain his initial feedback to the group and expand upon it using the pilot version of the unit as a reference. During th e winter workshop, Gregory continued to provide specific, ac tionable feedback to improve the unit, rooting his feedback in both student work and the Visualizer representation presented at the time of the workshop. This representation allowed Gregory to point out how the unit aligned with the KI framework. During the workshop, Gregory shared that his students saw Henrietta’s story as removed from their own experiences, something he noticed in both student w ork and student conversations. He suggested reframi ng the unit to elicit students’ ideas about their own experiences with medical professionals. This would set the stage for discussions of the sharing of  Henrietta Lacks cell s. For example, he suggested asking students about when they were asked before their health information was shar ed. This suggestion resulted in adding scenarios to  the unit about young people managing their health data with stakeholders like family members, their doctors, and the school nurse.  Gregory’s expertise about his students allowed him to think through how to make a compl ex story more relevant to their lives. By making the unit personally relevant for his students, Gregory  could elicit student ideas related to their experiences with doctors, gi ving them a reference point to distinguish between their experiences and Henrietta Lack’s story. His knowledge of KI allowed him to communicate his ideas in a way that aligned with the curriculum design.

Gregory’s actionable feedback on the draft version of the unit aligns with the benefits other research ers have uncovered when using resources, such as protot ypes, to elicit teachers’ ideas during RPP activiti es (Matuk et al., 2016). Gregory centers his feedback on the prototype, highlighting what was missing and connec ting his suggestions back to student work and to the KI fram ework. This allowed Gregory and other RPP participa nts to collaborate in designing a solution to the problem he uncovered in the pilot teaching of the unit. Gregory felt that giving feedback on the unit was his most important role within the RPP, stating that his perspective as a teacher was important because it was rooted in the classroom context. “The lens of the teacher is important for the researchers, the y (researchers) can go on the theoretical side too much. I have a practical perspective.” Gregory, due to his experience with t he RPP, was able to apply his practical perspective  and his understanding of KI to suggest important changes to  the unit. Gregory’s explanation of his role differ s from the typical power structure that positions teachers’ knowledge as less valuable than that of learning scientists. Gregory clearly articulates the importance of his teacher knowledge in his collaboration with learning scientists. Episode 3: Merging knowledge integration and Justice-Centered Science Pedagogy In the final episode, Gregory and the learning scie ntist collaborated to create a customized activity in the unit to support students in distinguishing and connecting t heir ideas (KI) about complex justice issues (JCSP). Conversation between class periods facilitated the collaborative refinement of the activity. A persistent challenge identified by Gregory and other teachers was studen ts’ perception of Henrietta’s story, and the injust ice she experienced, as being resolved. This prevented stud ents from connecting her story to justice issues experienced by communities of color today. During the pre-meeting before using the second version of the unit, Gregory emphasized the need for additional activities to ad dress this issue. He stated that he wanted to facil itate a class discussion to explicitly call out why Henrietta’s s tory is relevant for understanding justice today to  help students make connections. Gregory suggested using a Padlet to scaffold student learning, as it would elicit students' written ideas to teacher-generated questions, and allow students to discover new ideas from their peers (Figure 2). Figure 2 Padlet Activity Created by Gregory Gregory utilizes the Padlet to organize and respond  to student ideas, pushing their thinking and suppo rting a class discussion about the question in the third co lumn. The prompt on the first comes from the unit, and the second and third prompts were generated during the pre-meeting. Gregory, his teaching partner, and the first author discussed possible prompts before settling on taking one prompt from the unit and creating two additional prompts. Gregory asked students to complete the Padlet at th e end of class independently and used the comment function of the Padlet to provide feedback on students’ ideas. He used these comments to, push students’ thinking about ra cism beyond “bad apples,” push their conceptions of  the relevancy of Henrietta Lacks’ story beyond the medi cal system, and consider their assumptions about groups of people when making their posts.

Gregory then revisited the Padlet responses with students as an opener in the next class. During the f irst period, Gregory decided to project students’ writte n responses and go through them during the discussi on. However, after the period concluded, he app roached the first author about the activity, expres sing that he felt he did more talking during the discussion than student s and that it had been challenging to get them to r aise their hands to share ideas. Gregory and the first author discussed how to fac ilitate the discussion in a way that would increase student voice and provide students more ti me to think through the ideas their peers shared in  the Padlet. In the next two periods, instead of having students  simply read their peers’ responses before init iating a class discussion, Gregory prompted students to discuss whether they agreed with the responses with their table partners. This resulted in a more engaged class discussion, with multiple students sharing alternative perspectives. Students shared various ideas about how injustices experienced by Henr ietta are still experienced by Black people today, including issues of medical racism, unequal access to education, and police violence against communities of color. This episode highlights how even informal RPP struc tures, such as the conversations Gregory and the first author had between class periods, can serve as important spaces within RPPs for teachers to voice their ideas and collaborate with learning scientists  to tackle tough pedagogical questions, such as supp orting students to connect their ideas about racism and social justice. Though the structured pre-meeting served as an opportunity to co-create the Padlet activity, the discussions between  classroom observations helped to refine the activi ty and develop the pedagogical practices to include studen t voices and support students to wrestle with compl ex topics. These results underscore the value of capturing info rmal interactions to illustrate teacher agency when  studying RPPs.

When asked about collaborating with learning scient ists in the classroom, Gregory stated that his work in the classroom with learning scientists was one o f his favorite parts of the partnership. “I’d say m ost of the highlights come when the researcher is in the classroom when we get to see how things are unfolding.” He found this especially exciting when students interacted w ith the materials in unexpected ways or shared idea s that he and the learning scientist had not anticipated. Gre gory remarked, “planning something a certain way, a nd when the kids do it, I didn’t think it would happen like  this.” Then talking with the learning scientist ab out, “where do we go from here?” Gregory views collaborating with learning scientists as generative. He views this wo rk, including the messier, unexpected aspects of the wo rk in classrooms, as an exciting opportunity to thi nk about students’ ideas and work with learning scientists to refine the curriculum to better serve his students. This disrupts the distinction between the roles of observer-researcher and performer-teacher often found in RPP collaborations (Kavanaugh et al., 2022).

## Conclusion and significance

This case study highlights how Gregory, a science t eacher participating in an RPP focused on designing  justicecentered science curricula, positioned himself as a  leader during RPP activities. We document how RPP activity structures provide opportunities to support Gregory ’s agency in the co-design process. Episode 1 reveals how Gregory’s long-standing relationship with the RPP was empowered to  bring new social justice science issues to the collaborative. Episode 2 documents Gregory’s le adership role during workshops, utilizing his knowledge of his students, the unit, the Visualizer, and KI pedagogy to communicate his ideas. Unit discussion groups provided a space for him to share his ideas and feedback wit h both teachers and the first author, positioning h im as an important source of knowledge about the unit. Resources such as student work and unit prototypes supported him in sharing his ideas during the RPP, which aligns w ith previous research (Matuk et al., 2016) Despite his leadership, Gregory emphasized that the workshop was a space for collective learning, suggesting that he saw all teachers as equal contributors to knowledge. Finall y, Episode 3 highlights how Gregory and the first a uthor facilitated the merging of the KI and JCSP to refin e the unit. They strengthened the Padlet activity t o support students in making connections between Henrietta’s case and persistent issues of injustice today. Thei r collaboration disrupted the typical roles of researcher and teacher (Kavanaugh et al., 2022). Gregory’s reflections highlight his use of KI language. He sees himself as an important resource to incorporate the pedagogy behind the instruction. He provides a practical perspective ro oted in classrooms and students yet attuned to the mechanisms of KI and JCSP. It is clear from this analysis that Gregory feels his knowledge is valued by the learning scientists he works with. This case demonstrates how the RPP a ctivity structures support teacher agency, includin g willingness to collaborate on changes in real time.

Limitations: This is one case study. Teachers withi n this RPP likely had different experiences due to differences in previous experiences with the RPP, prior knowledge about anti-racist science teaching and KI, and other factors. Gregory’s story does not speak for all teachers involved in the partnership. More work is needed to understand the experiences of teachers involved in this RPP as a whole. These findings will help to identify areas of growth for the RPP. Additionally, this case docu ments a teacher’s involvement in a longstanding RPP  with existing structures that seek to amplify teachers' voices (Matuk et al., 2016). The RPP structures were not changed to support teacher agency for this study. Other RPPs have different structures that also support teacher agency and disrupt traditional RPP power structures. Many are making an effort to institute new structures to sup port this work. Continued research is needed to illuminate pr actices across RPPS in hopes that the field can com e to an understanding of best practices to support equitable collaboration among all RPP participants (Penuel et al., 2022)
