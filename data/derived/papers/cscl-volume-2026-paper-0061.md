# Exploring Unstructured Collaboration While Navigating Misinformation in Computer -Supported Collaborative Argumentation: Implications for Design

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The proliferation of socio-scientific misinformation is an inherently social challenge. While Computer-Supported Collaborative Argumentation (CSCA) environments offer a promising pedagogical approach for collective knowledge construction, their effectivene ss relies heavily on productive group dynamics. To avoid over-scripting future interventions, this formative Design-Based Research (DBR) study explores the baseline collaborative breakdowns that occur when students navigate misinformation without explicit social guidance. The study analyzed the audio transcripts of two middle school triads collaboratively mapping arguments about a controversial topic, utilizing established interaction analysis frameworks to evaluate peer talk and social modes of co-construction. Findings indicate that, within these groups, students defaulted to two distinct dysfunctional patterns: an epistemic conflict avoidance pattern, characterized by cumulative talk and quick consensus-building, and a social dominance pattern, characterized by disputational, competitive fact-checking. Structural scaffolds alone appeared insufficient to foster the critical exploratory talk required to evaluate deceptive claims, absent social interaction protocols. The study concludes by offering targeted design implications, suggesting that future CSCA environments might benefit from embedded collaboration scripts, such as structured "Devil's Advocate" and "Lead Fact-Checker" roles, to disrupt these natural tendencies and better support apt epistemic performance.

The proliferation of misinformation surrounding socio-scientific issues presents a societal challenge with the potential to undermine informed decision-making (McBrayer, 2020). In what has been characterized as a “post-truth” condition, educational responses have frequently relied on individualistic media literacy approaches, focusing on teaching students isolated fact-checking skills (Barzilai & Chinn, 2020). Yet such approaches may struggle to address the reality that misinformation rarely exists in a vacuum; rather, it thrives within social networks where echo chambers, socio-affective factors, and collective validation allow false claims to take root and persist (Ecker et al., 2022). Navigating this landscape may therefore require moving beyond individua l factchecking toward cultivating “apt epistemic performance”, the capacity to achieve epistemic aims through reliable processes across diverse contexts, an ability that involves learners participating in epistemic evaluations together with others (Chinn, Barzilai, & Duncan, 2020). Because the spread and acceptance of  misinformation are inherently social phenomena, educational interventions designed to counteract them could benefit from being socially embedded (Mercier & Sperber, 2017).

Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) offers a relevant paradigm for addressing this challenge by shifting instructional focus from individual cognition toward collective knowledge construction. Within this domain, Computer-Supported Collaborative Argumentation (CSCA) employs digital environments to foster evidence-based dialogue, encouraging learners to jointly articulate claims, justify them with data, and evaluate competing perspectives (Andriessen & Baker, 2013). Recent advances in digital epistemic scaffolds suggest that explicitly visualizing multiple documents and their sources through shared argument maps can help improve students’ abilities to critically evaluate and integrate conflicting information (Barzilai, Mor-Hagani, Zohar, Shlomi-Elooz, & Ben-Yishai, 2020). By rendering reasoning visible and encouraging participants to draw on a shared corpus of information, these environments hold potential for building resilience against false narratives.

Despite this theoretical alignment, simply placing students in a CSCA environment with a digital tool does not necessarily ensure productive argumentation. The educational quality of peer discourse can vary considerably based on underlying group dynamics. Wegerif and Mercer (1997) distinguish between three types of peer talk: “disputational talk,” characterized by competitive assertions and individualized decision-making; “cumulative talk,” where students build uncritically on each other’s ideas to maintain  social harmony; and “exploratory talk,” where ideas are critically yet constructively challenged and justified. Similarly, Weinberger 404 and Fischer (2006) observe that learners frequently default to “quick consensus building”, accepting contributions without critical modification, or “conflict-oriented consensus building”, replacing a partner’s perspective, rather than engaging in integration-oriented perspective-taking. When students are tasked with evaluating misinformation collaboratively, their existing cognitive biases, social hierarchies, and conversational habits can shape the trajectory of their learn ing (Ecker et al., 2022). Without structural support, students may struggle to achieve the exploratory talk conducive to robust misinformation literacy. To mitigate such discourse challenges, CSCL research has highlighted the importance of scaffolding and scripting (Kollar, Wecker, & Fischer, 2018). While macro-scripts can structure the overall sequence of classroom activities, specific micro-scripts and structured role assignments may help balance participation and guide learners toward critical, evidence-based peer feedback. However, a tension exists within the design of these environments: although scripting is often recommended to guide peer discourse, over-scripting risks stifling natural collaboration, limiting student agency, and constraining organic meaning-making (Kollar et al., 2018). Before designing complex CSCA interventions, therefore, it seems useful to establish a baseline understanding of what students naturally do when confronted with misinformation in a group setting. This study adopts a formative, Phase 1 Design-Based Research (DBR) approach to explore this space (Puntambekar, 2018). By intentionally removing micro-scripts, this initial cycle aims to observe middle school students’ natural collaborative tendencies when facing the cognitive and socio-affective demands of misinformation. Exploring such unstructured collaboration may help identify where and how future CSCA designs might provide targeted scaffolding. To that end, the primary empirical objective focuses on observable phenomena within student groups, asking: (1) What specific collaborative breakdowns occur when middle school students navigate socio-scientific misinformation in an unstructured CSCA environment? Subsequently, as a secondary theoretical synthesis  of these findings, the study asks: (2) What are the design implications of these observed breakdowns for structuring targeted digital scripts and roles in future CSCA environments? The following section describes the methodology employed to investigate these questions, after which the findings and their implications for design are presented.

To investigate the research questions, this study adopted a qualitative, formative Design-Based Research (DBR) introducing complex interventions, allowing researchers to gather foundational insights that can inform subsequent design iterations (Puntambekar, 2018). The study was therefore designed to observe how students naturally interact when asked to evaluate and map arguments regarding a polarizing socio-scientific issue: the efficacy of homeopathy. It was selected for its cultural familiarity to participants yet considerable surrounding digital misinformation and conflicting claims (Ernst, 2016). The pilot study included six secondary school students aged 11 to 15 from middle-class backgrounds in Mumbai, India, recruited through convenience sampling. To facilitate observation of small-group dynamics, participants were divided into two triads: Team 1 (Anika, Ananya, Mitali) and Team 2 (Shivam, Kriti, Anuj).

The pedagogical intervention took place during a single-session workshop. The procedure began with a brief assessment of students’ baseline beliefs, followed by a tutorial on basic argumentation principles and the digital mapping platform, Miro. Students then viewed an animated scenario depicting a two-person debate about homeopathy between characters Riya and Priya. They were provided with internet access and tasked with collaboratively constructing an argument map to reach a final group verdict on the iss ue. Students received a visual argument template loosely based on Toulmin's framework to structure claims and evidence (Toulmin, 1958), providing structural scaffolding. However, their social interactions and group roles were intentionally left unstructured. This absence of social scripting was implemented to help surface students’ baseline epistemic practices and natural collaborative tendencies when confronting misinformation. The researchers functioned as observers and facilitators, with the reflexive deci sion to minimize real-time intervention intended to allow authentic peer dynamics to emerge rather than eliciting teacher-pleasing performances. The primary data sources consisted of continuous audio recordings of the two triads during their collaborative argument-mapping phase, transcribed verbatim to capture nuances of peer discourse including overlaps, interruptions, and off-task commentary. To systematically analyze the transcripts, a deductive coding framework (Table 1) was synthesized from foundational literatures on Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning and misinformation. The interaction analysis applied three distinct theoretical lenses. First, the quality of peer talk was evaluated using Wegerif and Mercer’s (1997) dialogical framework, categorizing utterances into exploratory talk, disputational talk, and cumulative talk. Second, to understand how knowledge was negotiated, the analysis utilized Weinberger and Fischer’s (2006) social modes of co-construction, coding for interactions such as quick consensus building, conflict-oriented consensus building, externalization, and elicitation. Third, 405 students’ engagement with informational content was coded using a misinformation evaluation framework, tracking instances of source evaluation, fact-checking, and recognition of cognitive biases or barriers to belief revision (Barzilai, Mor-Hagani, Zohar, Shlomi-Elooz, & Ben-Yishai, 2020; Ecker et al., 2022). While both groups utilized the digital argument-mapping template, their underlying social dynamics appeared to influence their engagement with the task’s epistemic requirements in markedly different ways.

Team 1: Epistemic conflict avoidance and cumulative consensus Early in the task, an unequal distribution of cognitive labor emerged in Team 1. One student, Anika, explicitly requested collaboration, asking, “Mujhe kyun, tum dono bhi to bolo” (Why me, you both should also speak). When another member, Mitali, opted out by replying, “I don’t know,” Anika assumed the role of content director while Mitali acted primarily as a scribe. As the session progressed under time constraints, the team appeared to prioritize task completion over complex reasoning. When finalizing the ir verdict, they chose a stance based on vague impressions of the characters’ arguments, with Anika conceding, “Huh... yeah! She had better points!” During a moment when Anika attempted to articulate a complex thought involving the word “argumentative,” Mitali, focused only on typing, asked for the spelling. When Anika expressed frustration, Mitali dismissed the task’s value entirely, stating, “No one cares! Just go ahead!” Team 1’s discourse predominantly reflected cumulative talk (Wegerif & Mercer, 1997), characterized by uncritical acceptance of each other’s ideas in service of maintaining group harmony. Rather than interrogating the scientific plausibility of information,  the team engaged in what might be described as epistemic conflict avoidance. Their collaborative process relied heavily on quick consensus building (Weinberger & Fischer, 2006), accepting contributions without substantive modification primarily to move the task forward. The dismissal of reasoning in favor of finishing quickly suggested a reliance on what Weinberger and Fischer (2006) term nonepistemic activities, such as focusing on spelling or making offhand comments, to cope with the task’s cognitive demands. By bypassing critical evaluation, the team reached consensus without engaging in the source evaluation necessary for robust misinformation literacy (Ecker et al., 2022). Team 2: Social dominance and disputational fact-checking 406 In contrast, Team 2’s interactions were characterized by interpersonal friction and highly asymmetric participation. One member, Shivam, largely monopolized both the digital workspace and the conversation. During a debate over the cost of homeopathic remed ies, another student, Anuj, interrupted to argue that conventional medicines are “more cheaper.” In response, Shivam immediately performed a web search and read from his screen: “There is no significant difference between the two,” retorting, “It’s the same price!” The social hierarchy driving these interactions was made explicit later when Shivam dismissed his peer’s input, stating, “I am smarter than you... You only said yourself.” This dynamic appeared to marginalize the third member, Kriti, who remained mostly silent, and prompted Anuj to resort to off-task behavior. Team 2’s interactions aligned closely with disputational talk (Wegerif & Mercer, 1997), wherein students appeared to treat the collaborative activity as a competitive arena rather than a shared epistemic endeavor. When disagreements arose, the group defaul ted to conflict-oriented consensus building (Weinberger & Fischer, 2006), resolving issues primarily through social dominance rather than reasoned negotiation. This hierarchical dynamic fundamentally shaped the act of fact-checking. When Shivam conducted his web search, it served as a reactive measure to win an argument rather than as a tool for shared critical inquiry. The team did not engage in source evaluation to determine the credibility of the information (Barzilai et al., 2020), suggesting that the d isputational dynamic short-circuited the collaborative sensemaking process. Synthesis of observed patterns Addressing the first research question, the observations suggested a bifurcation in group dynamics that appeared to hinder critical inquiry when students navigated misinformation in an unstructured CSCA environment. In these two cases, students tended to default either to cumulative talk (Team 1), which led to epistemic conflict avoidance, or to disputational talk (Team 2), which resolved issues through social dominance. In both scenarios, the absence of structured interaction protocols a ppeared to constrain the emergence of exploratory talk (Wegerif & Mercer, 1997). Consequently, neither group meaningfully engaged in the deep source evaluation or reflective fact-checking required to navigate deceptive claims. These patterns, while observed in only two triads, offered preliminary insight into potential breakdown points that might inform design considerations, which the following section explores.

The findings suggest that unstructured CSCA environments may not inherently foster the critical discourse needed to evaluate socio-scientific misinformation. Students defaulted to either cumulative talk, epistemic conflict avoidance and quick consensus building (Team 1), or disputational talk, social dominance and conflict-oriented consensus building (Team 2). Neither group engaged in the exploratory talk or systematic source evaluation foundational to misinformation literacy (Wegerif & Mercer, 1997; Barzilai et al., 2020). These observations align with Weinberger and Fischer’s (2006) claim that learners rarely achieve integration-oriented consensus without explicit guidance, extending that insight to the context of misinformation, where cognitive and socio-affective demands are high (Ecker et al., 2022). The results suggest that structural scaffolds, in absence of  explicit social protocols, do not reliably regulate the group dynamics that shape engagement with deceptive claims. This points to a need for targ eted social scripting (Kollar, Wecker, & Fischer, 2018). To disrupt quick consensus building, future designs could incorporate a structured “Devil’s Advocate” role that formally assigns responsibility for challenging emerging consensus. To counter weaponiz ed fact‑checking and social dominance, a “Lead Fact‑Checker” role paired with turn‑taking prompts might transform source evaluation into a shared epistemic responsibility. These proposed scripts aim to balance participation and foster the exploratory talk required for apt epistemic performance (Chinn, Barzilai, & Duncan, 2020). Methodologically, the decision to minimize researcher intervention proved valuable for surfacing authentic peer dynamics; the need for embedded scripts might have remained obscured had facilitators constantly intervened. The insights, grounded in a specific socio‑scientific topic and a small sample of Indian middle‑school students, may hold transferability to other collaborative settings where polarizing topics and adolescent social hierarchies intersect, though the sample size (six students across two triads) and single‑session design limit claims about generalizability or long‑term change. In summary, unstructured collaboration appears to devolve into either uncritical harmony or adversarial dominance, both impeding critical evaluation. CSCA environments designed to counter misinformation may therefore benefit from embedding structured roles and interaction protocols that gently guide students toward exploratory talk and equitable critique.
