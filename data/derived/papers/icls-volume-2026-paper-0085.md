# A Mixed-Method Analysis of Facilitator Sensemaking around Social-Emotional Learning in a Refugee Summer Program

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study utilizes a mixed-methods approach to analyze facilitator sensemaking around social-emotional learning (SEL). Using Latent Dirichlet Allocation (topic modeling) and grounded theory coding, we analyze two corpora of SEL literature to identify key topics that comprise dominant and critical SEL frameworks. Our findings reveal that while dominant SEL frameworks emphasize institutional language, critical SEL frameworks better reflect the complex cultural negotiations that facilitators navigate in their practice. Qualitative analysis of facilitator interviews validates these computational findings, illustrating how facilitators draw on their own cultural backgrounds as underscored in critical approaches to SEL. This study supports the need for SEL frameworks that can accommodate multiple ways of understanding social and emotional development in culturally diverse contexts.

Social-emotional learning (SEL) offers frameworks for educators to foster emotional competence in students and attend to students’ wellbeing and positive development. However, less attention has been given to facilitator sensemaking around SEL and what counts as effective implementation. How SEL is designed  for and implemented in a program requires high quality training that pertains to the unique and varied cultural contexts of educators and students. For this paper, we draw on seven interviews from facili tators that were part of a summer program for refugee youth ages 5-19 years old. We start by sharing two key themes that emerge from qualitative materials designed to provide an improved, context-sensitive and culturally responsive approach to SEL which we refer to as the critical SEL framework, intended to better address and adapt to facilitator and program needs in contrast to dominant SEL frameworks that often follow rigid scripts. Finally, using topic modeling, we explore how we might bolster our analysis by first systematically comparing fa cilitator interviews,  materials used to develop a critical SEL framework, and dominant SEL literature and then assessing thematic alignment or patterns that may surface in the stage of qualitative coding and analytic memos. The aims of this study utilize a mixed-method approach to explore the alignment and gaps between SEL literature and facilitator experiences implementing and embodying culturally responsive SEL. After multiple cycles of grounded theory coding on interviews alongside analytic memos  to identify themes, we examine how facilitators understood SEL  and subsequently engaged with it in their practice. Through topic modeling, we analyze word clusters, emerging topics, and overlapping themes between the three datasets and identify how facilitator experiences aligned with or diverged from dominant SEL themes in literature. Additionally, we identify themes that are unique to facilitators that may not be emphasized in literature and provide reflections on how SEL frameworks might be refined to better support SEL practitioners and students. First, this  contributes insights into refinements for SEL training with facilitators that better meet the needs of practitioners and students from multiple diverse backgrounds, and second, provide further discussion around the usefulness of topic modeling for guiding and supporting qualitative data analysis.

Social emotional learning (SEL) is a predominantly school-based program that emerged in North America and was further established through the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL) formed in 1994. According to CASEL, schools can support students; short-and long-term academic success and positive social and behavioral adjustment by attending to instruction and learning approaches that help students acquire skills and attitudes in five domains: self-awareness, self-management, relationship skills, social awareness, and responsible decision making. A key tension within SEL efforts lies in its desire to translate across formal to informal settings using structured definitions and practices of SEL. Out-of-school settings often resist traditional structures and scripts and this often contributes to frustrations with implementing SEL and, in worse cases, reproduces harm within the relationship between educators and learners. SEL can also become a tool of White Supremacy, disproportionately polices (Kaler-Jones, 2020), and the spirit-murder (Williams, 1987; Love, 2019) 737 of Black, Brown, and Indigenous students. Critical SEL should address existing power relations in communities and schools as well as intersecting oppressions (Camangian & Cariaga, 2021). Hayashi et al. (2022) argue that SEL skills are inherently culturally responsive, must be situated in learners’ authentic experiences, and are inherently embodied. However, despite extensive work to study and implement SEL, or recent calls to attend to culture as an important factor in SEL implementation (Mesquita et al., 2026), less attention has been given to facilitator sensemaking around SEL and what counts as effective implementation. In our training materials and practices, we designed a model that drew on pedagogies of care in order to consider the nuances of multiple  cultural contexts for refugee, immigrant, and asylum-seeking youth identities (a majority of whom are racialized as Black and Brown), while building on culturally sustaining pedagogies (Ladson Billings, 1994; Paris & Alim, 2014). Researcher positionality The authors are women who hold immigrant backgrounds with transnational lived experiences and diasporic connections to East and West Asia.

## Research questions and hypothesis

We ask: 1) How do facilitator experiences and reflections reveal challenges or unique themes not addressed by or emphasized in existing SEL frameworks and literature? 2) How does existing SEL literature align with or diverge from facilitator interviews and experiences in a culturally responsive summer program? We hypothesize that some themes dominant in SEL frameworks like relationship building will overlap with facilitator interviews while environment and practitioner specific themes related to cultural identity will be more dominant in facilitator interviews.

The summer program took place within a collaboration between a refugee non-profit organization and the university-based research team. The study fulfilled Institutional Review Board (IRB) requirements and had established memorandums of understanding (MOU) with the non-profit that were revisited and renewed annually. Facilitators were informed of the study purpose and their rights as participants before they consented to being interviewed. We draw on seven interviews with facilitators in a summer program in 2022 who shared their reflections on SEL and culturally sustaining  pedagogy during training and the program overall. Facilitators were recruited through flyers circulated via university channels and the non-profit refugee organization job board. Three facilitators identified as men and three as women. Five of the seven facilitators identified as having an immigrant or refugee background and were multilingual speakers of either Arabic, French, Turkish, or Spanish alongside English. Two facilitators identified as Muslim and were active in their religious communities and faith practice. The interview protocol was semi-structured and guided by the following research question: How do we support facilitator training centered on pedagogies of care (“SEL”)?  Interview questions  were organized into several themes that addressed facilitator identities and background, their reflection on training (i.e. “What did you learn from training”, “How did training shape your understanding of pedagogies of care (“SEL”)?, “How did training shape your understanding of care throughout the program?”), their goals and reflections on learning and teaching, and the presence of pandemic realities or constraints due to returning in-person for the first time since widespread quarantining. It is important to note that our use of “pedagogies of care (SE L)” in the interview protocol was used interchangeably with “culturally sustaining pedagogy” during the interviews. Interviews were transcribed and coded using in v ivo codes and following a grounded theory approach (Charmaz, 2014; Miles, Huberman & Saldaña, 2018). Codes were refined through multiple cycles and organized into themes using analytic memos. Interviews were then analyzed using computational topic modeling to compare themes present in facilitator interviews and SEL literature to explore the differences between dominant SEL and critical SEL in addressing facilitator needs in the practical application of SEL. Themes represent clusters of words that frequently appear together in text. Through topic modeling, we identify these word groupings, which we interpret as distinct themes. Through our qualitative analysis, we develop themes by examining patterns that emerged from our codebook.

Three distinct text corpora were prepared for analysis: (1) dominant SEL literature, (2) critical SEL training materials, and (3) facilitator interviews. The dominant SEL corpus was assembled from materials (reports, academic and popular articles) taken from key foundations the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL), the U.S. Department of Education, and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). This corpus drew on materials examining SEL in out of school  spaces, for newcomers, and in classroom settings. The critical SEL corpus consisted of materials that were used in training and that drew 738 more specifically on culturally sustaining  pedagogies and pedagogies of care. This corpus reflects the research team’s design decisions and goals to more clearly bridge SEL training and culturally sustaining pedagogies. The documents were preprocessed using the Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK) library to prepare the data for modeling. We used tokenization to split texts into individual words, convert them to lowercase, and remove non alphabetic characters to clean the data. Then, we used lemmatization using NLTK’s WordNetLemmatizer to reduce the words to their base form. Additionally, we removed standard stopwords in addition to stopwords specific to our conversational text from the interviews (e.g. “um”, “yeah”, “like”). Finally, we detected and kept word pairs or bigrams that occur at least 5 times. This kept terms like “culturally relevant ” as one term.

Topic modeling Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic modeling identifies patterns of word co-occurrence across documents and clusters these patterns into meaningful topics. In LDA, each document is modeled as a mixture of topics, and each topic is represented as a prob ability distribution over words. This supports the discovery of latent thematic structures within large collections of texts (Blei. et al 2003). For this study, topic modeling serves three key analytical purposes. First, it allows for systematic comparison  across our three distinct datasets: facilitator interviews, papers from critical SEL materials, and papers from dominant SEL literature. After training the topic models on these corpora, we analyzed our interview transcripts by passing each transcript as a document through both models. This process generated probability scores showing how closely each interview aligned with the topics identified in both the dominant and critical SEL literature. By identifying overlapping themes between these sources, we can quantitatively assess the degree of alignment between facilitator experiences and different theoretical frameworks. Second, topic modeling provides an exploratory understanding of patterns in the data that might not be obvious through qualitative coding. This develops an intuition and generates hypotheses about how different SEL approaches align with facilitator needs. Third, the computational aspect of topic modeling provides an additional per spective to our qualitative analysis which can be helpful in reducing bias in thematic identification. Integrating topic modeling with qualitative without the loss of nuance.

Building topic model We use LDA from the Gensim library in Python to identify latent topics in each corpus. Additionally, we use NLTK for preprocessing text data, and NumPy for efficient numerical computations. To ensure that we achieve an optimal number of topics which don’t contain overlapping or redundancies, we grid search across different parameters: number of topics, alpha (document-topic density), eta (topic-word density), number of iterations, and number of passes. The most optimal model was selected based on three metrics: 1) topic coherence which measures the semantic consistency of the identified topics, 2) topic diversity which calculates the ratio of unique words to total words for all identified topics, and 3) topic overlap which computes the Jaccard similarity between topic words sets to avoid repeated topics. We weighted these three metrics as 40% coherence, 40% diversity, and 20% overlap. These weights were selected based on their relative importance in topic model evaluation literature. Coherence is widely considered to be a useful metric for ascertaining an optimal number of topics to represent a corpus (Syed & Spruit, 2017). We gave equal weight to topic diversity because we wanted to ensure our model captures distinct conceptual areas (Dieng et al., 2020). Mor e weight was given to topic diversity than similarity because we care more about capturing all unique topics than having no overlap or repetition. With the similarity of the texts in the corpora, we expect some level of overlap regardless. Sensemaking around critical SEL In their interviews, facilitators engaged in sensemaking around critical SEL that highlighted moments when the ir own culturally situated  values, beliefs, or  customs shaped their facilitation. Their reflections  surface two key themes that make practices around critical SEL more salient: 1) reflecting on shared experiences and 2) attending to family norms. Drawing on analytic memos, we examine how these themes offer considerations for educators and practitioners to design for and implement culturally sustaining, that is, critical SEL. Topic modeling provides insight into the relationship between SEL theory and practice, especially in contexts involving refugee youth. Dominant SEL topic model reveals a focus on institutional and structural aspects of SEL implementation, with topic 4 (focused on school, district, teacher, implementation) showing probabilities between 0.61-0.72 across all interviews. This emphasis on institutional frameworks misses key aspects of facilitator experiences that emerged in the qualitative findings. For instance, when Ali shared her experience connecting with an Afghan student through their shared refugee experience, or when Ammar carefully navigated cultural norms around physical contact with young Muslim students, these nuanced cultural negotiations aren't well-represented in the dominant SEL topics. While topic 0 includes important terms like 'refugee' and 'newcomer,' its low probability scores (0.16-0.20) and the isolation of these terms from cultural responsiveness or pedagogical practice suggest limitations in how the dominant SEL framework captures these experiences. This suggests that while the dominant SEL framework can identify institutional elements and acknowledge the presence of refugee students, it doesn't capture how these elements interact with cultural contexts (Ladson Billings, 1994; Bennouna et al., 2021; Hayashi et al., 2022).

The critical SEL topic model, with its balanced distribution across topics, better reflects the complexity of facilitator experiences revealed in the qualitative analysis. While the individual probability scores for topics 3, 5, and 7 (ranging from 0.19-0.37) might appear similar to topic 0's scores in the dominant model, their significance comes from how they work together, with overlapping terms that situate institutional elements within cultural contexts. For example, topic 3's focus on facilitation and cultural practice aligns with how facilitators actively drew on their cultural backgrounds in their work. This is particularly evident in moments when Eren described creating a safe space for students to ask questions about religious identity, or when Al i used her own refugee experience to build trust with students. The facilitators' careful attention to cultural context and shared experiences demonstrates why this topic emerged so strongly in the computational analysis. Topic 5's emphasis on community and care corresponds with the qualitative finding around attending to family norms. Mark's nuanced discussion of parental influence, acknowledging both its importance and potential limitations, exemplifies the kind of complex cultural negotiation that appears in this topic. Similarly, Ammar's careful consideration of parents' cultural expectations regarding physical contact illustrates why community and care emerged as a significant theme in both the qualitative and computati onal analyses. The themes community and care in topic 5 offer a useful frame for understanding how students’ familial contexts and relationships play a key part in their sense of community and care.

The consistent presence of topic 7, focused on cultural responsiveness and family, mirrors how facilitators regularly engaged with cultural identity and family norms in their work. When "school" and "student" appear in this topic, they're contextualized a longside terms like "culturally_responsive" and "family," reflecting how facilitators actually discuss these institutional elements in their interviews. This alignment is particularly visible in how facilitators like Ali navigated tensions between their ow n cultural backgrounds and program expectations, or how Eren engaged with students' religious identities while maintaining his own distinct perspective. The qualitative data shows that facilitators weren't simply implementing predetermined SEL strategies, but rather engaging in ongoing cultural negotiations that required deep understanding of both their own and their students' backgrounds.

## Limitations

Topic modeling’s "bag of words" assumption treats documents as unordered collections of words, disregarding context and semantic relationships that are crucial in understanding complex concepts (Blei et al., 2003; BoydGraber et al., 2017). Terms might appear in similar topic clusters despite having different contextual meanings in interviews versus theoretical literature. Additionally, topic models can overemphasize frequently occurring terms while missing important but infrequently mentioned themes such as those related to cultural responsiveness or specific pedagogical challenges. Text preprocessing decisions can also affect model results in unexpected ways (Boyd-Graber et al., 2014). To address these limitations, we use topic modeling as one component within a broader mixed-methods approach, validating and contextualizing its findings through qualitative analysis. 743 The balanced and overlapping distribution of topics in the critical SEL model across multiple cultural and community-focused themes reinforces what the qualitative data illustrates: critical SEL facilitation with refugee youth requires a framework that can accommodate complex cultural negotiations and multiple, culturally-situated ways of understanding social and emotional development (Camangian & Cariaga, 2021; Kaler-Jones, 2020; Simmons, 2019, Mesquita et al., 2026). Together, these three topics give a more complete picture of critical SEL in practice, demonstrating how the framework captures the interconnected nature of institutional, cultural, and pedagogical elements in facilitator experiences. The facilitators' sensemaking that surfaced through intervi ews provides concrete examples of how critical SEL better supports the needs of facilitators and students in culturally diverse contexts. Results from topic modeling reflected themes that surfaced in qualitative coding and analysis. This suggests two things: 1) topic modeling can be useful for directing qualitative analysis by highlighting themes and topics that are salient 2) in tandem with qualitative coding and analysis by researchers, topic modeling can help deepen analysis by pulling high-level themes and topics of interest.
