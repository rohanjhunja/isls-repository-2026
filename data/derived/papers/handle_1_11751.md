# Maker Mindset or Making What Matters: Journeys of Persistence Towards Identity-Driven Making

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The expectations within the maker mindset of persisting through challenges and risk taking assume that makers are comfortable with failing. However, for marginalized youth who have been traditionally told they do not belong in STEM communities, this is not the case. In response, we seek to reframe the maker mindset through a lens of computational action, suggesting that students are more likely to persist through failure when their making is driven by the identities and communities that are meaningful to them. To illustrate this, we apply a case study approach to two makers in a summer camp, to reveal how they persisted through the evolution of their projects that reflected their identities, consequently demonstrating and developing maker mindsets. We also explore practices in our maker camp that supported their identity driven maker mindsets, namely availability and aesthetics of materials, adequate mentor support, and curriculum scaffolds to integrate identity and computing.

## Introduction

Constructionist maker education encourages makers t o drive their own learning through the process of c reating an artifact, and unlike in formal settings, these l earners follow their own exploratory paths to under stand computing (Paper t, 1980). This process expects makers to persist thr ough challenges, take risks, and develop resilience, which are some of the major characteristics of the “maker mindset” (Regal la, 2016). However, this idea of persevering through challenges is steeped i n privilege and assumes that learners come from backgrounds where they feel supported through failure and are given the opportunity to persist through challe nges. For many students from marginalized communities, this is not  the case, especially in fields like computing that  are falsely believed to be meritocratic (Ryoo et al., 2015).

Scholars in maker education (Blikstein & Worsley, 2 016) suggest that one way to democratize STEM education through the maker movement is by encourag ing learners to make artifacts that resonate with t heir communities and cultures.  Along this line, computational action (Tissenbaum e t al., 2019) suggests that developing students’  computational identities and digitally empowering t hem can engage learners from marginalized communities in relevant computational learning. To understand how identity driven computa tional making can enable meaningful maker mindset developm ent, we follow the journey s of two makers – both identifying as teenage Black girls, recruited from a local afterschool  program – as they participate in the computational action driven Connected Spaces (C/S) summer maker camp. Both makers faced challenges due  to materials, technical difficulties, lack of support,  and other factors, which resulted in pivots in the ir final project ideas. We observed that both makers were more driven to persist throug h challenges when they were working towards a final project that was meaningful for the m and tied to their identities and communities. Thr ough their stories, we see how being supported to make identity driven artifacts can motivate learners to take risks and persist through difficulties. Furthermore, the makers appreciated the agency of being able to design their own projects, despite the challenges that accompanied the process.

## Background

Scholars have shown that “neutral” forms of partici pation do not exist, and that claiming that STEM fi elds like computing are “objectively merit based” disregards the unique experiences of those marginalized within  these fields (Thomas et al., 2018). Black girls, whose intersectional experiences are lost due to being grouped with that of all girls or all Black learners (Braswell & Rank in, 2023), frequently face hostile and unwelcoming computing environments, saturated with racist and sexist stereotypes, and report having little to no community or mentorship when engaging in computational learning (Braswell & Rankin, 2023; Erete et al., 2021). Further, most computing curriculum does not provide opportunities for them to explore their histories, representation, and belonging within computing (Davis, 2021), much less engage them in personally relevant learning (Thompson & Windschitl, 2005). Wade-Jaimes et al. (2021) showed that despite their inte rest in STEM, Black girls in their study did not identify with these fields, and expressed how other aspects of their identities were not cohesive with what they believed a STEM person was.

Scholars in maker education are developing approach es that combat these negative experiences and provide meaningful and empowering computational lea rning experiences to Black girls (King & Pringle, 2 019; Braswell & Rankin, 2023; Erete et al., 2021; Des Portes et al., 2022). Key characteristics of these a pproaches include creating supportive and safe communities of peers and mentors and providing personally relevant learning by engaging prior identities. Our maker camp drew o n findings from implementations  like Digital Youth Divas (Erete et al., 2021) to engage our makers in computing experiences that were personally relevant and empowering through Tissenbaum et al.’s (2019) computational action approach. Computational action seeks to combat traditional me thods of computing education that are typically appealing only to the stereotypical “tech-bro” demographic, by creating computing opportunities that are relevant to learners’ lives and impact their communities. Im plementing computational action driven learning ent ails supporting the development of learners’ computational identities, by allowing them to design their own computing solutions, rather than work towards  a predetermined “right answer.” It also involves di gitally empowering the learners by showing them that their learning has the potential to impact their lives and communities in meaningful ways. This can be a particularly powerful approach for learners wh o have typically not been provided with meaningful and equitable opportunities to engage in computing. Furthermore, computational action driven making all ows for rethinking some features of  “maker mindset” (Regalla, 2016), beyond just risk-taking and persisting through challenges and failur e. As pointed out by Ryoo et al. (2015), the maker mindset’s expectat ion of perseverance is built on the assumption that  learners feel comfortable making mistakes and that they know they will be supported through their learning, which is typically not the experience of students from under represented communities. Additionally, given that c omputing education has not traditionally catered towards Black girls’ identities and interests, asking them to persist through failure, towards something that they do not resonate with, can result in further feelings of not belonging. Ryoo et al., however, show that when marginalized learners are working towards something meaningful to them, they are more driven to stick through the challenges they en counter in the process. Similarly, Tissenbaum et al. (2021) reframe computational learning to serve multiple endpoints – such as creative expression and social justice – that resonate with people from diverse communities. This goes to show that learners can be excited towards computing and the challenges that come with it, when they are  working towards something that matters to them. Th rough this lens, we seek to extend the maker mindset beyond just persevering through challenges, to being driven to do so by the passion for the final project or the people it can serve. To this end, we closely examined the paths of two learners as they develop their maker projects in our computational action-focused summer camp, to answer the following questions: 1) How can a com putational action-focused maker camp support learners to persist in m aking through challenges? 2) What role do  materials, mentors, and curriculum play in supporting makers’ identity-driven project pivots?

## Study context

This study is embedded within the Connected Spaces summer 2023 maker camp which was conducted over two weeks (ten weekdays) at a predominantly White Midwestern university. Each day’s session was three hours long (including a snack break) and culminated with lunch at a university dining court. On Days 1 and 2 of the summer camp, to develop basi c skills and competence, the learners interacted with a variety of mini projects that introduced them to various tools and technology elements, like LEDs, motors, and sensors that they could interface with the Adaf ruit Circuit Playground Express  (the primary microcontroller for the camp). At the end of Day 2, with the g oal of understanding how technology can be developed to serve communities, the learners interacted with a local entrepreneur. To enact computational action, on Days 3, 4, and 5 learners created mind maps to represent their ide ntities and communities, and brainstormed how they can use technology within the context of their mind maps. Creating mind maps allowed learners to reflect on different aspects of their identities, and what they would li ke to use their computing for. By the end of Day 5 they started working on their final projects. On Day 6  after watching a video clip from Shark Tank (the TV series where entrepreneurs pitch their ideas to secure deals with investors), learners presented their own project ideas as “Shark Tank pitches” to their peers. This was done in orde r to develop learners’ confidence in their ideas and also give and receive feedback from their community of practi ce. Learners did not attend Day 7 (due to external circumstances). On Days 8, 9, and 10 they worked on their individual final projects, which th ey presented to community members and families during the showcase at the end of Day 10.

## Participants

The maker camp was attended by 17 participants, all  of whom identified as middle or high school Black girls. They were recruited from a local community organiza tion The Well Experience (TWE)  which works with Black/African American girls and families, with who m the project has had a multi-year relationship. Many participants had prior relationships with each other, such as sisters, best-friends, and cousins. They were supported by 3 mentors from the  TWE (Ms. J, Ms. Jz, and Ms. G) – with whom they also had prior relationships, and 3-5 facilitators from C/S (primarily Ms. A-first author, Mr. M-second author, and Mr. C). At the end of the two weeks, the first author condu cted computational identity (Bawankule & Tissenbaum, 2023) driven semi-structured, one-on-one interviews with six participants – chosen due to consistent attendance, varied types and  intensities of engagement, and varied prior experie nces with C/S. In the interviews (9-18 minutes), participants talked about their overall experience with the camp, their projects, and how they felt about their identities being reflected in their participation. The first author transcribed and loosely content logged the interviews, revealing interesting patterns of p ivots within final project ideas for a few of the l earners. In this paper, we use the word “pivot” to i ndicate any kind of change in the makers’ final project idea. After reviewing reflective field notes, we selected two of these si x participants, who displayed interesting changes i n their final project creation journey that we wanted to explore. Imani (14 years old) and Daisy (16 years old) had both worked with members of C/S (including the first author) in after-school maker activities in the year prior to the camp.

## Data collection and analysis

Data collected included the end of camp one-on-one semi-structured interviews, full room video and audio (from two video cameras in the room corners), table audio  (from audio recorders at each table of 3-4 participants), learner artifact photos (of their mind maps, of sheets they worked on to plan their projects, and of their projects), and reflective field notes from the end of each camp day from the first author, who was a primary camp facilitator. The first author identified the two participants ba sed on their final interviews and reflective field notes that indicated pivots in final project ideas. We ex tracted and collated room video and audio with resp ect to the two makers as they created their pr ojects. Using MAXQDA (tool for video analysis) we c ontent logged the collated videos to form narratives of each participant’s making journey using an insight-oriented approach (Scherr, 2009). Learners’ artifacts and final interviews were used to triangulate the findings, and we analyzed the narratives as qualitative case studies  (Creswell & Báez,  2020). Using these narratives, we identified elements in the makerspace to understand how they supported persistence towards making something that mattered to the learners. ## Discussion

Imani and Daisy’s stories show how in a computational action makerspace, learners persisted through challenges when working towards identity driven projects. Addi tionally, as seen in their interviews, when they completed these projects, they showed positive indicators of confidence, enjoyment, and satisfaction towards com puting. Imani said that having the agency to decide what to make made her feel respect ed in the space, and Daisy said that working on her own creations and ideas made coding and electronics seem easier. Imani’s first pivot away from her cheer pom poms was prompt ed by her disappointment with its aesthetics and led her to the lamp for her Grandma. Her second piv ot, encouraged by a mentor, brought her back to the pom poms. Her final pivot was an independent  decision due to aesthetic dissatisfaction, which led to her combining two ideas and creating the babysitting ro bot for her family. Daisy had been envisioning the shampoo dispenser idea for many months. Lack of confidence almost led to a pivot to a cup lamp, but a mentor encouraged her to continue with the dispenser. Despit e technical challenges she worked on the dispenser fo r multiple days. On the penultimate day of the camp, she and a facilitator decided that the dispenser might not be ready in ti me, leading to her first pivot to the cup lamp. On the final day, with a ray of hope from a facilitator, she pivoted back to the dispenser (her final pivot), finished cup lamp forgotten, and worked frantically to perfect the dispenser. Both makers showed more joy, and persistence in mak ing, and in the space when pivots in their project were driven by their identities (Imani’s pivots to lamp and babysitting robot, Daisy’s final pivot bac k to the dispenser) as opposed to when they were not (Imani’s second pivot to pom poms and Daisy’s pivot to cup lamp). In the section below, we look to answer our second research question by outlining some of the features within our computational action driven makerspace that supported (or did not support) makers’ identity-driven project pivots. Makerspace practices to support maker mindset towards identity driven projects Availability and aesthetic standards of materials and projects Materials in learning can be powerful “objects-to-think-with” (Keune & Peppler, 2019). The tech and craft materials in our makerspace were always openly acce ssible to the participants, and both Imani and Daisy took ample advantage of them. Both makers used the materials to support brainstorming ideas, with Imani finding the cardboard for the lamp, and Daisy finding the cup f or the cup lamp. Midway through the camp, the facilitators also brought in more materials that were specific t o the makers’ projects. When Daisy’s early concerns  about materials for the dispenser were assuaged by the fa cilitators (who said they would get a tube to dispe nse her shampoo), she started working towards the idea with more commitment. Imani emphasized the importance of the quality of the pom poms from the outset of her idea, and when these materials failed to meet her aesthetic standards, she pivoted towards working with cardboa rd that she could easily manipulate by cutting, gluing, and painting. In her final interview, she indicated that she gave up on the pom poms because the wires connecting the LED lights were too long and cumbersome, and she did not like what they looked like on the pom poms. However, she said she would like to make the pom poms in the future, indicating that although this idea was reflective o f her identity, the aesthetics were not. Although the  idea of “aesthetics” has often been seen as an aft erthought in computing, scholars in maker education have found the importance of creative self-expression through computing (Tissenbaum et al., 2021; Holbert et al., 2020). Imani’s story shows that creative expression and aesthetic appeal play a role in students’ persistence towards computing, and consequently their computational identities. Mentors that provide adequate support while also productively challenging learners and their ideas Given the student-driven nature of making, scholars have shown the im portance of providing adequate mentor support (Pinkard et al., 2017; Thomas  et al., 2018). Throughout the duration of the camp, Imani  and Daisy were aware of, and utilized the support that mentors and  facilitators provided, and indicated frustration w hen they felt a lack of support. On the last day, Daisy was supported by multiple mentors within a short span of time to ensure that she completed her project to perfection, allow ing her to rapidly debug and not waver in confidenc e despite the high stress environment. Imani worked collaboratively with mentors on the design of her pom poms and lamp and collaborated with facilitators when she needed tech nical support. Mentor and facilitator input was als o important to both makers, especially when they shared previously established relationships with them. For instance, when Ms. A (who Imani had worked with for  a year) asks Imani to consider going back to the p om poms, she takes it into serious consideration. Further, previous relationships allowed mentors to push the makers in adequate ways, as seen when Daisy is encouraged by Ms. Jz (who she is friends w ith) as opposed to by Mr. C (an intermittent facilitator in the  space). Daisy's reluctance to remote engagement cou ld also be due to a lack of established relationships with the online facilitat ors. It is important to note that although the makers we re influenced by the facilitators’ and mentors’ sugges tions (Imani attempting the pom poms again, Daisy sticking with the dispenser idea), they also made decisions in  direct contradiction to the mentors’ suggestions (I mani pivoting away from the pom poms and Daisy exclaiming that she was done with the dispenser). This is indicative of the importance of creating a student-driven learning environment where learners feel safe to express their ideas and make their own decisions, hence establishing agency and sense of belonging in the space. Scaffolds to connect computing and identities Scholars and practitioners (including the authors) often face the challenge of scaffolding students' i deation, such that it allows for relevant making (Blikstein & Worsley, 2016). In this summer camp, we first directed the makers to utilize mind maps (Authors, 2023) to reflect and represent their personalities, lives, and communities, followed by asking them to think about ways in which they could incorporate technology into or utilize technology towards what was important to them. This enabled them to approach technology through the lens of their lives, as opposed to trying to fit their lives into traditional and s tereotypical applications of technology. We see tha t each project idea that Imani was motivated and excited about, ca n be tied back to some element on her mind map: her pom poms were tied to her hobby of cheer; and her lamp was tied to her grandma, who she looked up to (it w as an early idea she and Ms. A had brainstormed); and she  created her babysitting robot to help mothers like  hers to care for children, where both her mom and siblings were on her mind map as people she cared about. Dai sy had created a detailed mind map, in which she ideated four applications of technology connected to things she was passionate about-one of which was a shampoo dispenser driven by her interest in hair. When she pivoted to the cup lamp due to technical challenges, we saw that she was less enthusiastic about it. We realize now that the cup lamp was not one of the other three projects, she had connected to her identity using her mind map (Figure 1(b)). Had the facilitators and Daisy thought to refer to her brainstorming, it might have inspired her to make something that would have been a better reflection of her identity than the cup lamp.

## Conclusion

With maker education’s increasing popularity, it is  important to remember that its origins lie in the heart of traditional hacker culture in Silicon Valley (Bliks tein & Worsley, 2016). The notions of maker mindset s, persistence, and risk taking originate from this re latively homogeneous, middle-class White male driven culture. With the proliferation  of maker education across various countries and div erse populations in the world, the definitions of maker culture and mindset and what i t means to develop a maker identity  (Bawankule & Tissenbaum, 2023) must be local and contextual for it to truly democr atize computing. Our study supports that contextualizing making within the pre-existing identities and communities of students can be a powerful technique to engage learners who have traditionally been on  the outskirts of computing, allowing them to define  the ways in which they want to become producers and not just consumers of technology. Despite all the challenges that Imani and Daisy faced, they were able to create projects that they identified with and  felt proud of. Both makers demonstrated various characteristics of maker mindset (Regalla, 2016) towards the end of the camp. Imani demonstrated a growth mindset, expressing that she “learnt that everything is not going to be how you want it to.” However, she expressed that she was proud of and happy with PomQueesha and would like to attempt making the pom poms in the fu ture. Daisy demonstrated significant resilience in the face of technical challenges, while working on her dispenser. Through both their case studies, we present an alternative approach to developing a maker mindset.  Although perseverance, grit, and learning from fai lure are important learnings within maker education, we suggest that developing these skills in the process of creating something that learners care about, is just as important. In fact, as seen through both Imani and Daisy’s stories, it is much easier for learners to stick through challenges when they get to choose their final projects, and that choice is driven by their identities and communities.

Although approaches like e-textiles (Searle et al., 2016)  within maker education have made significant progress to broaden entry points to computing for girls, they still offer a narrow view of their interests, accounting for only certain parts of their identities. The framing of computational action can enable Black girls to approach computing through more expansive avenues. Within our work, we used mind maps to scaffold thei r design thinking to meaningfully utilize computing within their live s. To enable them to create these computing designs, we utilized open-ended physical computing, given its extensive applications and interfaces. In our future work, we would like to expand our approaches to design thinking by incorporating methods like Afrofuturism (Holbert et al., 2020) and  involving learners’ community leaders in the process. We would also lik e to explore basic app development (Wolber et al., 2015) as an additional tool for our  makers to envision other forms of comput ing applications. Through these various avenues of computing, we seek to crea te opportunities for students from diverse backgrounds to bring in their lived experiences and expertise, to then understand how computing can be utilized within their individual contexts and identities.

## Limitations

Due to challenges like camera malfunctions, and loudspeaker music during the sessions, some of parts of the sessions were not accurately captured via the video and audio recordi ngs. We acknowledge that there may have been some incidents that our analysis might not account for. We attempted to make up for this lost data by listening to audio from other tables and full room audio, as well as by triangulating findings with field notes.
