# Democratic Interventions on Walls

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Graffiti and street art are unsanctioned urban artifacts outside art institutions. Nevertheless, graffiti and street art are seen as self-expression by their practitioners and these art forms have pedagogical values as they raise questions on political, social, and creative issues within the society. The myriad of voices that echo on these walls resembles a democratic space that accommodates plurality and differences. In this paper, we examine how six graffiti and street artists implicitly or explicitly practice public pedagogy as described in Biesta’s work. Our analysis shows that their urban interventions exhibited “activist”, “experimental” and “demonstrative” qualities of public pedagogy while also being engaged in active learning about their contexts and art practices.

## Introduction

Our study explored the dynamics of the modern graffiti movement and its global impact through the lens of public pedagogy and plurality. Originating in Philadelphia  in the mid-60s and evolving into diverse forms globally, graffiti has often served as a medium for marginalized individuals to express themselves (Stewart, 1989). Drawing on Biesta’s (2014) concepts, the study dealt with the interconnectedness of action, collective engagement, and the recognition of diverse perspectives within the arti sts’ portrayal of their work. The paper navigates the neolibe ral influences on urban spaces, the challenges posed by corporate dominance (Zieleniec, 2016), and gender disparities in street art. The analysis manifests narrative elements within street art, illustrating how artists interpret their city and their work within that environment for further narration associated with the physical location, and how the artists draw inspiration from their surroundings. S treet artists challenge the perceptions of public s paces a nd promote diversity, fostering public pedagogy and plurality in urban spaces through vibrant narrative expressions. Plurality in graffiti art refers to the diversity and complexity of graffiti as a form of expression, reflecting the hybridity and multiplicity of the artists and t heir communities. Different scholars have explored the concept of plurality concerning graffiti. Vanderveen and Ei jk (2016) argued that plurality entails recognizing  and respecting the diversity of perspectives, identities, and voices within society. They further suggested that plurality is a paradoxical aspect of graffiti, as it can be p erceived as both vandalism and art. Thus, plurality  can be a form of public pedagogy, social justice, and community empowerment, which can be seen as a way of challenging the dominant narratives and norms of society and creating alternative spaces for dialogue and representation. Our goal for this study was to comprehend how stree t artists experiment and interact with the public, each driven by diversified objectives and narrative s. We explored the varied voices, including women’s  and the narratives of these street artists to navig ate the nuanced process of expression, how this plu rality influences the formation of public perceptions, and how this influence reverberates back to the artists themselves. Street art, critical public pedagogy, and plurality Critical pedagogy involves a perspective that chall enges established power structures and seeks transf ormative education. The practices of graffiti and street art ists align with the transformative and empowering a spects of critical pedagogy, challenging au thority and expressing a reclamation of public spac e through creative and subversive means (Ho & Kim, 2022; Freire, 1970/2002). Street artists often challenge capitalist power structures by re-authoring spatial narratives, advocating for their inclusion in art curricula as a means of empowerment and transformation aligned with justice and activism (H o & Kim, 2022; Eldridge, 2013). Despite street art' s cultural and aesthetic contributions to urban spaces and nar ratives, the ongoing hostile portrayal of graff iti as destructive can be linked to a historical context of discrimina tion based on class, race, gender, and ethnicity (P ennycook, 2010). Scholars suggest that the actions of graffit i and street artists, as they defy the power and op pression they experience to transform public spaces, resonate with the principles of critical pedagogy (Freire, 1970/2002). In view of this, we turn to Biesta’s (2014) public pedagogy. The graffiti movement has far-reaching effects as it has inspired similar actions globally, and various kinds of street art emerged from its humble roots. This unfettered trajectory takes after w hat Biesta (2012, 2014) pointed to in Arendt’s work, that action requires others to act upon, in doing so, freedom can be achieved, and it must exist in collectivity. Biesta (2014) contended that the vibrancy of the public sphere and public p edagogy is inherently tied to the principle of plur ality, which involves recognizing and respecting diverse perspec tives, identities, and voices, encouraging democrat ic engagement. The acknowledgment that there is no universally accepted truth further underscores the significance of embracing this diversity. Public pedagogy for Biesta (2014) is to work in the interest of the public condition of plurality, creating a realm where engagement of discourse can exist openly, and it shifts away from sameness and homogeneity and embraces diversity. For Biesta (201 4), the ideal public pedagogy disassociates from th e traditional schooling of teaching and learning and is composed of multiple pedagogies (activist, experimental, and demonstrative). Some scholars emphasize the critica lity of arts, and use the term, critical public pedagogy (e.g., Zorrilla & Tisdell, 2016). Operating as experimenta l educational approaches, they generate numerous me thods and outcomes derived from their socio-cultural experiences, thereby manifesting the possibilities of approaching activities in varied ways (Biesta, 2014; Cooper & Sandlin, 2019). Definitions and terminologies Broadly speaking, graffiti is a letter-based expressive form found in public places, and m ay include illustrative elements like cartoon characters in its composition. Street art is an evolution of graffiti as a result of the rigorous crackdown on graffiti by the authorities. Street art tends to be image-centered. Stickers, stencil art, wheat paste, and tile art are examples of street art. From the stance of the law, graffiti and street art are both illegal as they are performed without permission (we are mindful of the  complexities of the definitions and the legality o f graffiti and street art; however, it is not the intention of  this paper to engage in a debate on those two area s). There are legal variations of graffiti and street art such as legal graffiti, murals, or other aesthetic forms that are carried out in public areas with the consent of property owners. Acknowledging these diverse practices, we refer t o those who practice illegal graffiti and street art, and legal graffiti and street art as “street artists” hereafter.

The study This study used phenomenological reflection on the experiences of street artists (van Manen, 2003), ex ploring how their experiences are narrated through their work and its impact on public pedagogy. The first author led the study by reaching out to artists active on social media platforms, specifically Instagram, from various parts of the world. For data collection, we used written and Zoo m interviews, social media conversations and posts,  and information from other websites. We first sent them  a set of questions through Google Form and communi cated through social media chat messages. We then asked f ollow-up questions using different response formats of artists’ preferences, such as email, Instagram messages, and audio chats. The analysis focused on the phenomenon of artists a sserting their presence and voice on the walls of their cities for the public to perceive. The artist s’ responses provided an opportunity for both the a rtists and the researchers to contemplate their personal narrative s, practices, and artifacts. We paid special attent ion to their presence as a means to diversify, alter, and destabilize the narratives of the visual urbanscape, typically dominated by establishments of authority (hegemonic values). This involved considering their use of space, how t hey reconfigure it, and how they appropriate the urban environment. Recognizing the limited representation of women’s v oices in street art and its research, we made continuous efforts to include their perspectives in  our work by reaching out to female artists around the world. We also gather perspectives of practitioners f rom countries with emerging graffiti scenes (Southe ast and North Asia, Eastern and Central Europe, North Africa, and Central and South America). Gaining access to non-English speaking graffiti and street art communities, however, proved to be a challenge due to our research team’s limited language capacities. We could only gather data from  artists around the world who were willing to conti nue our exchange in English. We engaged in online conversat ions with potential participants (10 female and 15 male artists) to develop trust and eventually invited them to  join our research. Of the four female and five mal e artists who agreed to participate, only two female and four  male artists fully participated in initial and fol low-up interviews. The female artists include Bruna from Brazil and Chinagirl from Austria. The male artists are Bamamo from Hungary, Drash from North Macedonia, Tukeone from the US, and EASY from Poland. While participants had the option to utilize pseudonyms and fictitious city names via informed consent to protect their identities, they agreed to use their artist names and their countries and cities. Although we gave them the option to conduct a voice  interview, most of them preferred to write their responses via email or online chats. We examined their written interviews and online chat recordings, looked for significant and reflective episodes, and analyzed t he artworks they sent or their posts on Instagram.  For data analysis, we conducted a detailed examination of th e interviews, carefully analyzing every word and li ne. For each sentence, we recorded our reflections on the a rtists’ patterns of thoug ht. These reflections were critically compared among us to identify distinct categories, which naturally evolved into themes. After examining the data, we found that the artists’ practices are explorator y and innovative, and have the potential to advocat e social changes. Hence, we adopted Biesta’s activist, exper imental and demonstrative pedagogies as a framework  to further the discussion of public pedagogy. In writi ng this article, the artists were provided a draft of how they were being depicted and they were allowed to contest it if they felt misrepresented.

## Findings:  Plurality and differences of graffiti narratives

Contemporary urban politics are largely defined by neoliberalist logic, driven by powerful corporation s emphasizing efficiency and consistency (Zieleniec, 2016). This homogenization is an adversary of democ racy (Giroux, 2006) that values plurality and differences. In neoliberalist logic, everything is commodified for profits, and graffiti and street art are no exception. Aucti on houses, museums and galleries are some of the cu ltural sites that profited from the commodification of the art f orm (Ross, Lennon & Kramer, 2020). Visual reconfigu ration of the city by street artists is at odds with neoli beralist vision to privatize public space for corpo rate needs. Historically, graffiti was framed as a crime associ ated with black and Latino youths, and these impove rished communities of color were scapegoated for the decline of the city (Dickinson, 2008). The neoliberalist cities strive to establish social and spatial control by policing, surveillance and enforcement of penalties. A part icipant from Brazil, Bruna, me ntioned how she seeks authorization to avoid engagi ng in illegal activities related to graffiti. Bruna said: “While graffiti becomes a contemporary art, graffiti is synonymous (in common sense) with vandalism. However, without proper authorization to  occupy spaces, whether public or private, both are considered a crime here in Brazil. As I work a lot with commercial art, I have authorization from comp anies and for public places I end up asking for it too” (Bruna, Written Interview, April 10, 2023). Corporate presence is prominent in the visual aspec ts of urban settings, contributing to visual clutte r through signs, advertisements, and billboards. This  control reveals the extent to which corporations s hape the urban visualscape (Christensen & Thor, 2017) without adequate consultation with the residents who live in these areas (Pennycook, 2010). Tukeone, a Denver-based graffiti artist questions its legitimacy: “Do es a billion-dollar company have the right to advertising space just be cause they paid for  it… well I’d say at this point it could be debated because we can easily take that space from you to use for ourselves and ironically I feel more  and more of society is agreeing with us” (Tukeone, Written i nterview, April 10, 2023). Biesta (2012) warned t hat as neoliberalism dictates our lives, private corporati ons become legitimate contributors in shaping our e veryday experience, thus threatening democracy and leading to the decline of the public sphere. Whether illega l or legal, graffiti and street art c elebrate a cornucopia of styles, forms, actions, me anings, personal experiences, and opinions. What binds them is their public character  and their accessibility to the people on the stree t. To remake public space, graffiti and street artists mobilize the necessary resources to create their art for public consumption. To understand how public pedagogy can be enacted in  the interest of publicness, Biesta (2014) identifi ed three aspects necessary to foster plurality and freedom: activist, experimental, and dem onstrative. Biesta’s (2014) interpretation of public pedagogy specifically addr esses the interplay between art and learning in pub lic spaces. Biesta’s (2014) framework uniquely highlights how s treet art can enact activism, experiment with new f orms of expression, and demonstrate alternative narratives, dir ectly correlating with our study’s focus on the educational impact of street art within urban environments.  Or ganizing the artists in thematic structure is not a n attempt to confine or limit them, but rather, to illustrate and rethink activist, experimental, and demonstrative pedagogies in a different light.

Activist pedagogies of female artists Drawing from Arendt, Biesta (2014) maintained that “to be able to act we therefore need others —others who respond to our initiatives and take up our beginnings. It also means that action is never possible without plurality” (p.19). This is the very nature of activist work and “we cannot act in isolation” (Biesta, 2014, p.19). Demonstrative pedagogies of sanctioned and unsanctioned street art Our findings demonstrate the transformative power o f street art in shaping public perceptions, with a particular focus on diversity and plurality. In their activist and experimental work, street artists embody notions of plurality, showcasing diversity and uniqueness. The artists in our study have demonstrated their capabilities by confronting challenges and expressing their identity, values, a nd skills. These actions align with the principles of demonstrative pedagogy, illustrating that “it is possible to do things differently” (Biesta, 2014, p.23). When addressing the challenges of women’s invisibil ity and underrepresentation in street art, Chinagir l Tile confronts these issues by creating opportunities for female street artists in the festival she initiated. Building support from arts and corporate partners not only strengthens their presence but al so validates their perspectives and talent. Bruna, in her effort to paint portraits  of women in the city, seeks permission to amplify her cause of empowering women’s voices and status. By gaining of ficial approval to feature empowering images of wom en, their lasting impact on the streets is assured, con tributing to the expansion, and normalization of wo men’s presence in the city. Both women are shaping alternative narratives of their urban landscapes. The graffiti and street artists in our study operat e in diverse circumstances—some in authorized spaces, while others navigate unsanctioned contexts. Operating within unsanctioned spaces exposes street artists to legal risks as they engage in demonstrative  pedagogy. Drash reflects on his experience, having been apprehended by law enforcement on more than 10 occasions. However,  perceptions of graffiti art have evolved over the years in Skopje, and it is now embraced by the public. For EASY, contemporary art is central to his graffiti. Continuously integrating and reinterpreting his vision has led t o unexpected results. He shares, “When I paint on t he streets or in a place where I do it illegally, people don’t bo ther me because they don’t associate what I do w ith vandalism” (EASY, Instagram chat, November 16, 2023). EASY int roduces a distinctive graffiti style to reclaim spa ce in a novel manner. To navigate vandalism laws, Bamamo opts for wheatpaste as his medium, as it can be misconstrued as party or commercial posters affixed to the streets. This strategy blends his art with other street artifacts, inserting his voice into public spaces. Tukeone, who engages in both sanctioned and unsanctioned street art form s, incorporates alternative exit routes into his plans when executing graffiti writing. The enforcement of rigorous state regulations to su ppress dissent erases narratives and a rich array o f artistic resources crucial for democratic societies. While illustrating the possibility of alternative  approaches in state-controlled and corporate-owned spaces, street artists may encounter strict policing and penalties. Conflicting forces may intervene, hindering their efforts to create democratic channels for dialogue. Biesta (2012) contended that to uphold plurality, “as soon as we erase plur ality, we deprive others of their actions and their freedom, and as a result, we deprive ourselves of our possibility to act, and hence our freedom” (p. 688).

## Discussion

This paper illustrates the intersection of street art and public pedagogy, drawing on the works of six street artists. Informed by Biesta’s public pedagogy framework, our  analysis reveals the activist, experimental, and demonstrative qualities embedded in their urban interventions. The artists challenge th e status quo and resist the homogenizing tendencies associated with neoliberalism, contributing to the democratic nature of public spaces. A significant observation we derived from the graffiti artist’ works and their explanations is the presence of narrative elements that convey stories within th e context. Regardless of whether the artwork carrie s deep, weighty themes or lightheartedness, or even the absence of profound meaning, it reveals how the artists interpret their living environment, which is reflected in the ir creations. When facing challenges such as gender  disparities and societal biases, the two female artists use their work to advocate for marginalized voices and contribute to the democratization of urban spaces. Chinagirl Tile men tions her role as the organizer of “Hands off the W all,” a street and graffiti festival for female artists, which aims to address the gender gap in the art world. Chinagirl Tile discusses the challenges faced by female artists, n oting that gender biases often limit their visibili ty, irrespective of their artistic quality. She contends that male a rtists receive more acknowledgment, and their work surfaces more prominently due to deeply embedded biases withi n the art industry. Pabón (2016) asserted that wome n frequently encounter biases and skepticism concerni ng their artistic capabilities within the male-dominated field of street art. This issue is further exemplified by our own study, which faced challenges in recruiting female street artists, indicating the scarcity of female voices.

Street art is a powerful form of expression that ca n enhance the learning experience of students and teachers in different contexts (Eldridge, 2013). Specifically, the symbiotic relationship between street art and the specific characteristics of its surrounding community can be a compelling area of learning. Artists, in intentionally choosing locations for site-specific exploration, foster dynamic interactions. This deliberate engagement encourages observations and reflective analyses of how the physical environment contributes to the lay ered meaning, and the context illuminates the depth of this relationship. Engaging artists with their surroundings fosters meaningful and authentic learning outcomes (Cooper & Sandlin, 2019). This engagement highlights the inherent connections between action, collective engagement, and the recognition o f diverse perspectives within artists’ works. Simultaneously, it embodies the synergy of p lace-based learning’s focus on the local context and community while emphasizing the body’s role in embodied learning.

## Conclusion

The paper provides a comprehensive exploration of the dynamics of the modern graffiti movement and its global impact through the lenses of public pedagogy and pl urality. We explored challenges posed by corporate dominance, and gender disparities, and empha sized the transformative power of street art. Using phenomenological reflection on the experiences of s treet artists, the study highlighted diverse narrat ives, challenges, and the potential of graffiti as a form  of critical engagement and public pedagogy. The fi ndings demonstrate the significance of plurality in urban spaces, inviting further exploration into the trans formative power of street art. While this paper has explored the perspectives of various street artists, it is evident that voices from people of color are underrepresented. Moving f orward, our research will expand on this group, aim ing to ensure a more inclusive and comprehensive examination of diverse perspectives.
