# Organizational Learning in Learning Sciences: A conceptual framework for future learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract

The main goal of this poster is to explore the idea of “Organizational Learning” in the context of structured learning environments. The ability to meet a requirement or standard over the duration of a professional workshop goes hand-in-hand with the structural systems put in place to keep track of the progress that is made. By having a clearly communicated system that can be developed as the needs of the instructor/classroom becomes apparent, the efficacy and success of instructors may be highlighted more thoroughly. Organizational learning in the learning sciences Organizational Learning is invested in three areas of learning: Knowledge creation, knowledge retention, and knowledge transfer (Argote, 1999).  Although distinct to the groups that  involve themselves in the process, organizational learning is a system that involves transferring knowledge amongst employees/individuals within an organization based on the active information gained from experiences overtime.  An example of this process happens when creating a learning agenda. The learning agenda is a set of questions that is created by a team before the launch of a project  which identifies the key strategies or learning goals throughout  the duration. Over the weeks, months, or years that the project may be facilitated, these key points are discussed and tracked using both explicit information (data from the learning groups) as well as implicit information (data gathered from the facilitators/data analysts). As the team learns what strategies are most effective towards the learning agenda,  the process of facilitating the project becomes easier and better received by the community. In this poster, I would like to argue that concepts of Organizational Learning can be applied to settings such as structured learning environments as a means of improving the efficacy of learning projects. Developing well-documented strategies to meet the specific needs of the learners within a community, as well as the instructors facilitating the projects, is a beneficial process to any learning environment. Shifting learning environments This type of research has been studied in both formal, and informal settings.  The shift of learning environments continues to expand as the world evolves and becomes more aware of the needs of its learners. Drastic events such as Covid-19 shifting the vast majority of business operations to an online-format, or even haulting them from physical locations all together have made communities aware of the importance of communication when face-toface operations may not be possible. These changes have caused many programs (i.e., school, training programs, etc.) to offer online-support when it would have otherwise been unavailable.  In the wake o f these shifts, understanding how to facilitate learning to a variety of new  environments (i.e., Zoom, Engagli, etc.) requires a system that flucuates with the needs of the community. Understanding how to create fluid and adaptable frameworks that work to incorporate the se shifting demands is a key area in this research. Unfortunately, the link between business strategy and knowledge creation is being widely ignored in organizational learning research (Yang et al., 2010).  In favor of the former, many organizations prioritize analysis of business strategy instead of linking the knowledge creation strategies that coincide with the outcomes. The combination of newly formed public knowledge with existing private knowledge can enhance new private knowledge creation in the future (Yang et al., 2010). Communicating feedback in a fearning sciences environment Effectively communicating the feedback between both the participants as well as the facilitators of a learning-centered project lies at the heart of how Organizational Learning can benefit the creation of learning environments. According to research, learning within an organization occurs at four various levels: Individual, group, organizational, and interorganizational (Argote, 2012). Creating a routine that allows for knowledge to persist even when individuals may no longer be a part of the process is beneficial for future learning. Before  the information is transferred on the organizational/ interorganizational level, the processes must be communicated and expressed effectively on the individual/group level of a project. Over the course of the project, the communication systems in place will grow according to the needs and experiences of the participants. Organizational Learning often occurs at four distinct levels. Individual, Group, Organizational, and Interorganizational. Understanding each level is important to the creation of  an organizations knowledge system, however this analysis looks to focus on creating a sustainable way that 2357 individua ls can communicate their experiences openely, creating an informed environment that grows with each iteration of the project. For organizational learning to occur, the individual must embed their knowledge in a database or repository. By communicating this knowledge in a supra-individual routine, the knowledge persists even if the member who acquire that knowledge left the organization (Argote, 2012, p. 20). This ensures that other members of the group as well as the organization itself can grow as the project continues to grow. Conceptual example Creating a Positive Youth Development program takes the combined effort of many members of the project team as well as the learners participating. While there is much discussion devoted to the project goals and yearly reports to be analyzed through the duration, many of the individual details involved with things such as data collection or survey creation goes overlooked. I propose that ideas from the organizational learning framework can help alleviate this problem while effectively capturing useful inform ation that may have otherwise been discarded or skipped within discussion. W hile there is an expansive literature about what school structures, programs, and processes are necessary for instructional change, we know less about how  these changes are undertaken or enacted by school leaders in their daily work… Knowing what leaders do s one thing, but without a rich understanding of how and why they do it, our understanding of leadership is incomplete. (Spillane, Halverson, Diamond, 2001). H aving a structured system of communication where individuals and groups can share their experiences and gain insight on the process of a project in realtime is an invaluable asset to any organization. Another great example of this process is found in the field of policymaking. Policy problems do not exist as social fact awating discovery. Rather, they are constructed as policymakers and constituents interpret a problematic portion of the social world… Ultimately, it proves important for motivating reshaping authority relations, coordinating action, and influencing teachers’ beliefs and practces (Coburn, 2006). Significance I believe that Learning Scientists must have the ability to adapt their knowledge in ways that keep up with the current learning environments they may encounter.  Organizational learning offers a framework that can help create a sustainable learning environment where information is detailed and shared amongst individuals and groups. This information can then be communicated between different parts of an organization, ultimately extending to useful knowledge that can be shared in a global environment. Balancing our learning environments to better serve the needs of the public can be achieved as  we create better knowledge systems that  highlight the needs of our instructors in the process.
