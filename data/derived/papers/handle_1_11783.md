# Freeze Framing: A Socio-Spatial Approach for Capturing Out-of-School Time Engagement

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Out-of-school time (OST) landscapes offer critical opportunities for youth to explore interests, build agency, and develop socio-spatial connections that traditional schooling often overlooks. However, inequities in mobility, resource distribution, and access to OST opportunities persist, limiting youth engagement in these spaces. This study introduces Freeze Framing, an iterative activity system that employs Freeze Frame, its eponymous geocapture app enabling youth to document and reflect on their OST experiences. Grounded in Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) and Expansive Learning, Freeze Framing is designed to reveal socio-spatial patterns of youth engagement and foster agency in navigating OST landscapes. Through spatial mapping, thematic coding, and activity system analysis, findings highlight the tensions and contradictions shaping youth participation, such as mobility constraints and social influence. By addressing these dynamics, Freeze Framing offers a promising model for how designing youth-driven activity systems can reveal socio-spatial inequities and inform the design of more equitable and accessible OST opportunities.

## Introduction

In the cinematic world, a freeze frame is “a single frame forming a motionless image from a film.” It is a directorial move used to emphasize a scene, creating a delibera te pause that invites the viewer to step out of the  flow of action—signaling them to focus on this moment of heightene d importance. Much like a director using a freeze frame to highlight a key moment, young people today  use social media to capture moments of their lives – highlights they deem worth sharing and remembering.  Before a phone is raised or a post is shared, a de cision is already at play: What moment is worth capturing, and why? It's not just the snapshot itself but the co ntext surrounding it that gives it depth and meaning.

Just as freeze frames capture a pivotal moment in a film, grades and transcripts reflect key milestones in formal learning but fail to account for the rich, e veryday learning experiences occurring beyond the c lassroom. Recognizing learning as mobile and embodied experiences that are not confined to traditional educational settings (Bang, 2020; Headrick Taylor, 2017), we turn to out-of-school time (OST) landscapes (Pinkard, in press), which can offer much more than a respite from the traditi onal school day. OST landscapes—spanning physical, digital, and social spaces such as community centers, parks,  and online commun ities—provide not just places of refuge but vital extensions of learning where youth can re claim agency often constrained in formal education settings, especially for youth in under-resourced schools (Dewey, 1938; Leander et al., 2010; Pinkard, 2019; Sheena Erete et al., 2020).

Despite the recognized potential of OST landscapes to support youth development, contradictions persist: What good is a healthy OST ecosystem if young peopl e are unaware, uninterested, or unable to access it s opportunities? For OST researchers, understanding p atterns of movement and engagement in these diverse environments requires innovative approaches that go beyond traditional methods. For communities, making sense of the OST landscape is key to creating equitable a nd accessible opportunities. For both o f these stakeholders, addressing these challenges calls for innovative, i nterconnected approaches that center youth agency a nd reveal the socio-spatial patterns shaping OST engagement. Socio-spatial patterns can be understood as the ways in which social phenomena and geographical space interact and influen ce each other. They encompass the distribution of social groups, activities, resources, and opportuni ties across different locations and how these distr ibutions are shaped by social, economic, political, and cultural factors (Barron, 2010; Burke et al., 2016; Leander et al., 2010; Miller, 2012).

While innovative approaches are essential for captu ring and revealing such socio-spatial patterns, it is equally important to recognize that not every OST space or moment holds equal weight in shaping young people’s growth. The decisions youth make —what to engage with, where to spend time, and why —are part of a larger story about how they navigate their environments and build connections to their communities. Yet, understanding how youth make such choices remains much of a myste ry—especially, and perhaps ir onically, to the adults shaping policies, infrastructure, and programming t hat can constrain or expand youth’s freedom of move ment within these spaces. Traditional methods often fall short of capturing the immediacy and personal significance of youth experiences, relying on tools like surveys th at miss the spontaneity of real-time interactions or external observations that overlook the subjective meaning o f a moment. This is where empowering youth to docum ent and reflect on their lived experiences offers a mor e authentic lens for understanding and addressing i nequities in OST access and engagement.

To amplify these hidden moments of engagement and u ncover their significance in the learning lives of young people, we introduce Freeze Fram ing: an iterative activity system designed to  invite youth to document and reflect on their OST landscapes in real time. T his system employs its eponymous geocapture app, Freeze Frame, as a mediating artifact that enables youth to freely capture and share, alongside their peers, the places and moments that matter most to them outside of school. In this paper, we examine the design and implementation of Freeze Framing to address the need for innovative, youth-driven approaches to understanding OST landscapes, guided by the research question: How can a youth-centered OST initiative like Freeze Framing be designed as an activity system to reveal socio-spatial patterns of youth engagement?  By uncovering these patterns through a youth-participatory data collection method, Freeze Fram ing provides a starting point for communities to explor e and address gaps in OST access and engagement while  encouraging youth to reflect on their roles within  these spaces. This approach offers a glimpse into how youth-driven methods might inform the design of more equitable OST systems and invites further iterations to deepen connections between learning, place, and community.

## Theoretical framework

Socio-technical tools and digital practices Today, people frequently use socio-technical tools like Instagram or TikTok to documen t and share their lives, whether it be for informative, entertainment, or social connection purposes. From posting photos accompanied by captions to narrating edited video montages of capt ured life moments, these digital, social-sharing practices can also foster subtle self-reflection in those who post (and those who spectate)– albeit reflections that are embedded in quick, everyday moments. It was precisely these sorts of  ingrained digital sharing practices of capturing, viewing, and narrating lived experiences that inspired the initial design of the Freeze Frame mobile app. However, to address the tacit gaps these societal p ractices leave in opportunities for reflection and growth, Freeze Frame could not be designed as just a mobile app. Rather, it needed to be reconceptualized to function within a holistic activity system —Freeze Framing —that incorporates tools, rules, and community structures to support youth in critically engaging with their OST landscapes. This expansive reframing  seeks to move past using socio-technical tools merely as aids for documentation bu t as mediating artifacts that are constantly being shaped by the broader ecosystem in  which they’re situated. Through this lens, the expanded act of Freeze Framing offers a potential structure that bridges youth mov ement and their utilization of community assets. Therefore, for the remainder of this paper,  Freeze Frame will refer to the geocapture tool, while Freeze Framing denotes the overarching activity system.

Cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT) and expansive learning Theoretical concepts like legitimate peripheral par ticipation and zone of proximal development have he lped us recognize learning as a process that does not just occur in one’s mind but is shaped by and thereby always situated within social contexts in whic h they are embedded (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Vygotsky, 1980). Extending such concepts, scholars like Vygotsky, Leontiev, and Lur ia developed and redefined activity theories to exa mine how human activity is mediated by tools and situated in  cultural, historical, and social contexts (Cong-Lem, 2022). Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) emerged as a broa der framework for analyzing activity systems, considering more than just the mediating artifacts but taking into account the entire ecosystem in which activities occur. In CHAT, activity systems  are made up of interconnected components –subject, object, rules, tools, community, and division of labor–that are constantly shaping each other through a process of mutual constitution (Cole & Engeström, 1993). Building on CHAT, Engeström introduced Expansive Learning, expanding the focus to include multi-voiced interactions and contradictions within and between activity systems. Most importantly, in this framework, the internal contradictions that arise can catalyze the transformation of individual components in the system (and thereby the system as a whole), creating opportunities for expansive learning to emerge. Freeze framing as an activity system With this theoretical grounding, we posit Freeze Framing to be examined and understood as “a collective, artifactmediated and object-oriented activity” (Engeström, 2001, p. 136) that o perates within an interconnected OST network. Analyzing Freeze Fram ing through the lens of Expansive Learning allows us to  examine the entire activity system as the primary unit of analysis. Fr eeze Fram ing is shaped by tensions and contradictions that emerge among its components: youth (subjects), Freeze Frame app (tool), the broader  OST community, and the rules and structures governing its use. These contr adictions—such as balancing youth agency with structured participation—offer opportunities for iterative refinement, enabl ing the act of Freeze Framing to evolve in ways that better support transformative, community-connected learning experiences. Freeze Fram ing’s emphasis on socio-spatial engagement highlights how contradictions wi thin OST landscapes, like limited mobility or inequitable access to resources, can shape youth ex periences. By leveraging CHAT and Expansive Learnin g, Freeze Fram ing aims to uncover these dynamics, fostering critical reflection and providing a framework for designing equitable OST opportunities.

## Methodology

To analyze the design and outcomes of Freeze Framing, this study adopts a dialectical positionality, emphasizing the dynamic interplay among the components of an activity system, where a change in one component inevitably triggers changes in others, reshaping the system as a whole. Rather than simply being reciprocal, the interactions occurring among the activity’s components generate tensions and challenges that must be reconciled fro m multiple angles. While the components of an activity system are relatively independent, they gain meaning when interpreted in tandem with one another against the backdrop of the entire activity system (Cole & Enge ström, 1993; Engeström, 2001). ## Participants

This study focuses on a subset of participants from the MCMF Summer Program, a six-week initiative based at a Black History Museum on the south side of a major U.S. city. Twenty participants, aged 16 –24, engaged with Freeze Frame during the program. The gr oup included nineteen participants from the south side of the city and one from the north side, with participants organize d into three smaller subgroups, each led by a colle ge-aged intern. The community also included program coordin ators overseeing the summer initiative and the researcher, who served as the app developer and facilitator for  this iteration. This culturally significant settin g and the participants' shared focus on community and civic e ngagement made the MCMF group an ideal context for exploring Freeze Frame as a socio-spatial intervention.

## Data collection process

The primary tool used in this study was the Freeze Frame mobile geospatial capture app, designed to en able participants to document their OST  engagement. Participants were tasked with capturing  at least three "frames" per week using the app. Each frame included (See Figure 2): Figure 2 Freeze Frame App Interface, Illustrating the Rules Shaping Participant Data Capture.

1. Name of place (user-written)

2. Current location (geo-coordinates, if location permissions allowed)

3. Tags for predefined questions that could have one or more responses:

a. What are you doing? (Lifestyle verbs: Helping, Soci alizing, Eating/Drinking/Chillin’, Traveling/Commuting, Learning, Exploring, Exercising, Fun) b. How did you get here? (Transportation modes: Train, Bus, Car, Bike/Scooter, Walk, Other) c. Who are you with? (Companions: Myself, Family, Friends, Co-Workers, Classmates, Other) d. Why did you come here? (Intent: I had to, I was inv ited, I wanted to, I’m Home, No Reason, Other)

4. Visual Content: A photo taken in real time or selected from the phone’s gallery.

5. Optional Captions: Providing additional context for the frame.

The activity was further supported by weekly in-person reflection sessions where participants shared and discussed their captures. Each group collaborativel y selected eight lifestyle verbs to guide their tag ging process, with “Learning” designated as a required tag by the  research team. These reflections, combined with ap p usage, aimed to reveal socio-spatial patterns and foster critical engagement with participants' OST landscapes. Additional data sources included field notes, surveys, and aud io recordings of the weekly reflection sessions, de signed to capture participants’ deeper narratives and insights. This multimodal approach ensured that data extended beyond the app to include rich, participant-driven reflections.

## Data analysis

The analysis employed a three-pronged approach: spatial mapping, thematic coding, and activity system analysis. Spatial mapping required q uantitative location data from the app, including t imestamps and geospatial coordinates, were analyzed to identify patterns of movement and engagement. This approach addressed questions such as: Where do participants spend their OST?  and What socio-spatial trends emerge in their engagement? Thematic coding required q ualitative data —including photo captions, survey responses, and fie ld notes from reflection sessions—were coded to explore themes related to OST activities, participant priorities, and interactions with community spaces. This layer added personal and communal meaning to the spatial data. Lastly, activity system analysis was guided by Engeström’s Expansive Learning framework, Freeze Framing was analyzed as an activity system. Components of the activity system —subject (participants), object (goals of OST engage ment), tools (Freeze Frame app), rules (data collection expectations), community (peers, mentors, and coordinators), and division of labor (roles and responsibilities) —were examined to identify tensions, contradictions,  and opportunities for learning. This approach provided insights into how Freeze Fram ing functions as a designed intervention to support expansive learning and foster socio-spatial connections. Use case In this iteration, Freeze Fram ing was designed as an exploratory tool to empower yout h to document, reflect on, and analyze their OST experiences. The geospatial a nd qualitative data collected through the app and accompanying interventions, such as reflective inte rviews and group discussi ons, created a multifaceted picture of participants’ socio-spatial engagement. These methods illuminated how F reeze Framing serves not only as a data collection tool but also as a catalyst for fos tering critical dialogue and community-centered engagement among youth. This integrated methodology positions Freeze Fram ing within an expansive learning framework, providing a basis for understanding how youth-driven data collection and reflection can reveal so cio-spatial patterns and support transformative learning in OST contexts. Findings from this study demonstrate the potential of such tools to navigate tensions and bridge gaps in youth engagement with their communities.

## Findings

The findings from the Freeze Fram ing activity are organized around the components of the  activity system: Subjects, Rules, Tools, Community, Division of Labo r, and Object. These elements reveal how Freeze Fra ming facilitated youth engagement, highlighted socio-spatial patterns, and exposed limitations in design  and implementation.

Subjects-Patterns of engagement and agency:  MCMF participants engaged with Freeze Framing in diverse ways that reflected their priorities, constraints, and agency in documenting their OST experiences. Participants mostly tagged leisure-oriented activities such as "exploring" and "chillin'," indicating their preference for engaging in recreational and curiosity-driven activities. Northern areas tended to offer more opportunities for these leisure experiences for “exploring,” “chillin ’,” and “fun,” as tagged by participants. Whereas m ore routine verbs like "learning" or "traveling/commuting" were  more commonly tagged in their familiar neighborhoo ds further south, suggesting a functional relationship with environments closer to home (See Figure 3).

The program’s design designated Tuesdays and Wednesdays as commun ity exploration days, where subgroups explored and documented their day via the Freeze Frame mobile app. These semi-structured exploration days subsequently led to the highest number of captures, demonstrating th e role of program scaffolding in driving engag ement. On the other hand, implicit norms emerged where participan ts were hesitant to document moments at home, citin g privacy concerns during open group discussions. Thi s limited their ability to capture a full picture o f their OST lives and contributed to gaps in the socio-spatial data. Tools-Strengths and limitations of the geospatial capture  mobile app:  The Freeze Frame mobile app was the primary tool for capturing socio-spatial data. While the app enabled participants to tag, caption, and map their OST experiences, effectively capturing moveme nt patterns and activity types, it also faced both technical and functional limitations. Reliance on internet con nectivity prevented offline captures, and participa nts experienced geolocation issues, leading to 13% of t he frames missing location data. Additionally, in-person conversations and survey feedback indicate d that the inability to post retroactively limited documentation flexibility. While participants engaged with the pr ovided tags, certain experiences, such as helping o r learning, were underutilized or tagged inconsistently, reveal ing potential gaps in the system’s use of predefined terms to capture nuanced reflections.

Community-Peer networks and socio-spatial contexts:  The Freeze Fram ing activity system was embedded in both social and geographic communities,  which influenced the participants’ experiences. Participants frequently tagged “Co-workers” and “Friends,” suggesting that peer networ ks played a central role in fostering engagement.  Embedding the activity within established social gr oups appeared to enhance participation. Participants captured more moments i n northern neighborhoods, often walking or using pu blic transit. In contrast, captures in southern neighborhoods relied heavily on car transportation, which was dependent on parents or other drivers. In-person conversations revealed that these patterns r eflected their lived perceptions of disparities in access to safe and appealing community spaces. Division of labor-Dynamics of autonomy:  The division of labor within Freeze Fram ing revealed a balance between participant autonomy and hierarchic al control. They could choose the places, activitie s, and moments they deemed significant. This is evident in  the diversity of captured moments, ranging from ro utine tasks like commuting to lei sure activities in unfamiliar spaces. Data shows th at some participants captured extensively, while others contributed minimally. For example, a small subset of participants accounted for nearly half (49.74%) of all captures, demonstrating that t he level of participation was uneven and tied to in dividual choices.

Object-Alignment with goals and emerging contradictions: The intended object of Freeze Framing was to reveal socio-spatial patterns of OST engagement and foster youth agency in exploring their local environments. The Freeze Frame mobile app and interconnected components like rules and division of labor successfully allowed the subjects to capture data that highlighted dispa rities in mobility, resource access, and activity p references, offering valuable insights into participants’ OST l andscapes. Still, while some participants captured meaningful moments and provided thoughtful reflections, others  documented routine activities without deeper engag ement. This variability iterates the need for continued design adjustments that take the entire activity system into account to encourage more consistent and reflective participation.

## Discussion

This discussion analyzes the preliminary findings f rom Freeze Framing by organizing them into three phases of its activity structure: (1) Freezing the Frame, (2)  Behind the Frame, and (3) Opening the Frame. Each phase highlights key processes, challenges, and implicati ons for understanding how Freeze Fram ing functions as an activity system to reveal socio-spatial patterns and foster youth engagement in their OST landscapes. By actively engaging with the contradictions identified in each  phase, Freeze Fram ing has the potential to foster expansive learning and transformative change. Addressing tech nical and structural limitations, embracing the multivoicedness of its participants, and iterativel y refining its design can help Freeze Fram ing empower youth, promote equity, and contribute to a more just and vibrant OST landscape. Freezing the frame The first phase, “Freezing the Frame,” focuses on capturing a specific moment in a participant's OST experience, aligning with the concept of microgenesis —the moment-to-moment unfolding of human experience (Cole & Engeström, 1993). By enabling participants to docum ent their immediate actions and interactions with t heir environment using the mobile app, this phase aims t o empower youth to notice and reflect on their OST landscapes. Participants used Freeze Frame to geocapture moments and add contextual details through predefined tags, including lifestyle verbs, transportation modes, companions, and reason for the visit. This structured process allowed for initial patterns of movement and engage ment to emerge, providing insights into the socio-spatial dynamics of their OST activities.

However, the phase also revealed a major contradiction in the act of Freeze Framing —the paradox of mobility (Stornaiuolo et al., 2017). This means that while the Freeze Frame app facilitated the fluid documentation of socio-spatial engagements, it simultaneously imposed cons traints that shaped and limited what was captured. Technical limitations and privacy concerns often  curtailed subjects’ willingness to capture sensitiv e or private spaces, such as their homes. Additionally, uneven participation patterns further  revealed this paradox—although Freeze Frame aimed to democratize data collection, a small subset of participants accounted for almost half of all captures, reflecting varying levels of interest and accessibility. These emerging tensions reveal a dual truth: the se mi-structured nature of Freeze Framing creates opportunities for revealing youth’s socio-spatial patterns while simultaneously restricting the a gency of participants to fully represent their lived experie nces. This highlights the importance of balancing autonomy and structure to ensure meaningful participation.  Recognizing these limitations, in-person reflections were incorporated to amplify underrepresented voices  and address such contradictions. Activities such as journaling, pair-based story mapping, and periodic surveys provided alternative ways for participants to share insights, navigating the tension between mobility and immobil ity inherent in the data collection process. These additions not only enriched the dataset but also offered a more inclusive approach to understanding youth engagement with their OST landscapes.

Behind the frame “Behind the Frame” explores the broader context sha ping the frozen moments, focusing on how systemic inequalities and community norms influence youth en gagement in their OST landscapes. This phase layers components such as captured tags, community practic es, and temporal habits to reveal the interplay of past experiences and structural barriers. Participants added meaning to their captures through captions, predefined tags, and group reflections. The collaborative process of defining lifestyle verbs illustrated how participants shaped the reflective process, but it also called attention to  the limitations of predefined categories in captur ing the fluidity of lived experiences.

Analysis revealed disparities in capture patterns b ased on transportation modes, perceptions of safety, and access to community spaces. For example, participants with limited mobility or safety concerns oft en documented fewer or less diverse locations, ref lecting underlying socio-spatial inequalities. Additionally, while the app aimed to reveal engagement patterns, its st ructure sometimes constrained authentic reflection,  as some participants found the predefined tags too rigid to  describe their nuanced experiences. This phase hig hlights the need to refine components like the Freeze Frame tool and rules of the Freeze Framing activity to better capture the depth and diversity of participants’ experience s. Moreover, it raises ethical considerations about  data collection and interpretation, particularly regardi ng privacy and surveillance. As the literature on t he paradox of mobility suggests, increased movement often coincid es with heightened regulation (Stornaiuolo et al., 2017). Again, future iterations must address these tension s to ensure the app supports equity and empowerment  without reinforcing existing power dynamics.

Opening the frame The final phase, “Opening the Frame,” focuses on fu ture possibilities and transformative potential. Th is phase aligns with expansive learning, which involves ques tioning existing practices, analyzing contradiction s, and envisioning new solutions, as well as the zone of proximal development, which represents the gap between current and potential capabilities. In-person reflection sessions provided opportunities for participants to share and discuss their captures, uncovering collective insights abou t communi ty assets and disparities in OST access. By integrating geospatial data with qualitative elements like captions and photos, Freeze Frame enabled participants to construct holistic narratives about their OST experiences. Group discussions often began by examining outliers  or unusual patterns in the data. For example, one participant tagged “Bus” as their mode of transit i n six frames but expressed a negative tone in only one of those instances–captioning that frame with “P ublic Transportation �� �� � ���.”  This prompted a deeper inquiry into the context of that capture during a group discussion. The participant explained that the frame represente d their first time taking a public bus because their parents could not pick them up from downtown. Unlike previous bus rides, which involved chartered buses provided by MCMF for field trips, this instance involved navigating an unfamiliar route alone from downtown to the south side in the evening. They expressed sa fety concerns and unfamiliarity with public transportation (bus), highlighting how external circumstances and context shaped their per ception of the experience.

Group reflections also revealed challenges such as uneven participation and time constraints. Additionally, tensions emerged between individual a nd collective interpretations of data as some parti cipants struggled to reconcile their personal experiences w ith broader community patterns. This phase highlight s the potential for Freeze Fram ing to transform individual captures into collective na rratives that foster community awareness and connection. For instance, data identi fying transportation barriers could be used to advo cate for improved public transit or ride-sharing initiatives. By leveraging these insights, Freeze Framing can contribute to broader efforts to address inequities in OST landsc apes and promote more equitable access to resources  and opportunities.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, Freeze Fram ing has potential to support the young people who participate to transform  routine movements and familiar spaces into purposeful condu its for exploration, equipping them to shape their own learning paths and forge meaningful community conne ctions. This activity system paves the way for more personalized and empowering learning experiences th at, as Dewey (1938) suggests, may live fruitfully i n one’s life, extending well beyond the confines of a traditional classroom. Still, for those unfamiliar with Freeze Framing, it can initially appear to be a way to promote “another app.” However, this study shows that socio technical tools like Freeze Frame are most effective when designed to be more than tools but designed to be activity systems that acknowledge the complexities and interconnectedness of our realities in and with the world around us. Accounting for things beyond the design of the tool itself, su ch as one’s community, division of labor, rules, ob ject, and the subject themselves, initial iterations of Freeze Fr aming have shown great potential for helping both youth a nd their communities make sense of their patterns of m ovement outside of school. Situating this activity system within the broader OST ecosystem, Freeze Fram ing takes a focused approach toward understanding youth development in relation to the communities they grow up in. Transformative impact relies on the reshaping that follows genuine engagement. So, in the context of OST, where research highlights the significant bene fits of connected and well-resourced ecosystems for youth development, the question remains: How can transfor mation occur if youth aren’t engaged in the first p lace? Centering and amplifying youth voices, this study addresses the need for learning scientists to design interventions that engage the full ecosystem they aim to impact. Achieving this requires designing, testing, and ite rating alongside participants while remaining embedded in the ongoing evolution of the activity system itself. Only through this iterative and collaborative process ca n Freeze Framing become a catalyst for real transformation — leveraging the socio-spatial patterns that emerge to redesign OST landscapes into spaces that are more connected, accessible, and desirable.
