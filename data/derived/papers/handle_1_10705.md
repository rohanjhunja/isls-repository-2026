# I’m a STEM Person, But do I Belong?

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While individuals may have a positive STEM identity, they may not feel they belong to a STEM community. STEM identity is a related but distinct concept from STEM sense of belonging. Yet, there is much to be learned about the similarities and differences between STEM identity and STEM sense of belonging and how they might separately or jointly affect students’ interest and persistence in STEM. This mixed-method case study explores the unique perspectives, reflections, and insights about STEM identity and feelings of belonging in STEM of STEM-inclined high school students who identify with communities historically marginalized in STEM. Drawing on survey and interview data, we share insights from these eight students and offer suggestions for future research directions.

## Introduction

Students’ participation and engagement in STEM activities are largely affected by their self-perception or identity (i.e., seeing themselves as a “STEM person”)  (Cian et al., 2022).  For youth that identify with communities historically marginalized and/or minoritized in STEM, seeing oneself as a STEM person can be challenging given

(a) the dominant “Discourse” (Gee, 2000) that STEM people are typical ly white, masculine, and nerdy (Cian et

al., 2022), and (b) the lack of visible representation in STEM fields (Singer et al., 2020). Despite these challenges, there are youth from communities minoritized in STE M that have a high level of affinity and interest in STEM and seek out opportunities to explore their interes ts and passions further. While many researchers have explored the role of identity in persistence in STEM, a related but distinct concept, belonging, may also be key to fostering a positive STEM identity and encouraging persistence in STEM activities. Feeling that one belongs in STEM is “the extent that a person feels that they fit into or are accepted by the STEM community ” (p. 207, Meadows & Robinson, 2023). Whi le individuals may have a high interest in STEM or a  strong STEM identity, they may or may not feel they belong to a STEM community.

We draw on Meadows and Robinson’s (2023) work that explored factors, including STEM-identity, that influence high ability adolescents’ STEM sense of belonging as it relates to their in-school academic experiences. Meadows and Robinson call for additional r esearch that explores the (a) nuanced relationship, similarities, and differences between  STEM identity and STEM sense of belonging, and (b) the relationship between STEM context (i.e. in-school vs out-of-school) and STEM identity to understand how student s see themselves within STEM in these different contexts. We take a first step to answer this call by exploring the unique perspectives, reflections, and insights about STEM identity and STEM sense of belonging of high school students who identify with communities historical ly marginalized in STEM  and were participants in an out-of-school STEM summer workshop, the Cellular Construction Workshop  (CCW). This mixed-method case study includes data from pre-workshop surveys and post-workshop semi-structured interviews. The following research questions guide d our study: (a) Do these students see themselves as a STEM person? and (b) Do these students feel they belong in the STEM community?

## Context and participants

This research study takes place in the context of a n integrated STEM hybrid teacher professional learn ing and student STEM out-of-school learning experience. The CCW is a two-week summer workshop that (a) provides an innovative introduction to cellular engineering, computational thinking (CT), and engineering design by utilizing robots as a model of cells  and (b) brings high school teachers and students together as co-learners. Participating students were 10th and 11 th graders attending a public or public charter school from a local school district and were individuals who identify with  historically marginalized  communities i n STEM. There were 15 student participants, 14 of wh om agreed to participate in the larger study, that documents and explores the influence of the CCW experience on students’ learning and STEM identity development. In this paper, we focus on data from the eight student participants who completed both the pre-workshop survey and post-workshop interviews (see Table 1 for their demographic profiles).

## Data collection and analysis

On the first day of the CCW, student participants completed the pre-workshop survey. We use d descriptive statistics to analyze the survey data, calculating mean scores for each participant for each category of survey items (e.g., Interest, Recognition, or Performance/Competence). Upon completion of the workshop, participants were emailed to request their participation in a 30–45-minute interview designed to learn more about their experiences during the CCW and their interest and connection to STEM. The inte rviews were conducted via Zoom, recorded and transcribed, and then imported into ATLAS.ti for analysis. We used s tructural coding (Saldana, 20 21) to categorize participants’ responses to interview questions, followed by in vi vo coding (Saldana, 20 21), which prioritizes and honors participants’ voices, to code the interview transcripts.

## Discussion

These findings provide valuable insight into the perspectives of STEM-inclined high school students who identify with communities historically marginalized in STEM. One limitation of this study is that students may h ave had different interpretations of the term s “STEM person” and “STEM professional”  when responding to the survey and interview questions. As Grimalt-Alvaro et al. (2022) suggest, STEM is an ambiguous concept and may need clarification for students, thus future research should explore this further. Future research should also explore the complex relationship between STEM identity and STEM belonging, as it is clear students may have one without the other. We agree with Meadows and Robinson (2023) that additional research is needed to help us understand the similarities and differences between STEM identity and feelings of belonging in STEM  and how they might separately and jointly affect students’ interest and persistence in STEM.
