# Understanding Success in Library Makerspaces for Assessment Development

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Makerspaces continue to grow in number and popularity and research on maker education has increased significantly, but most academic literature on maker education investigates the learning outcomes that are possible in these spaces (Mersand, 2021); fewer studies reflect an interest in understanding how we can recognize and assess learning outcomes for patrons (Lin et al., 2020). Current literature asserts that assessment in library makerspaces is a complex and thorny process because of the numerous learning goals and stakeholders that must be considered. This study confirms that success in these learning environments takes many forms. We introduce a Typology of Success that categorizes five types of success library staff value and want to assess. We also suggest an additional layer of complexity in assessment development may be due to the shifting nature of evidence of success that is dependent on the maker activity, the patron’s goals, and the patron’s disposition when it comes to facilitation.

Major issues addressed For some, it may seem that the Maker Movement is he ading toward the trough of disillusionment within i ts respective hype cycle (O’Leary, 2008), but educators across many countries are still committed to the promise of student-driven, hands-on learning through making (E riksson et al., 2018; Milara et al., 2020). Makersp aces continue to grow in number and popularity, and research on maker education has increased significantly, growing from six empirical studies in 2014, to 51 in 2018 (Mersand, 2021). Most academic literature on maker education investigates the learning outcomes that are possible in these spaces (Mersand, 2021), with particular focus on th e learning afforded by makerspace environments and the relationship between learners and making (Peppler e t al., 2016b, 2016a). Fewer studies reflect an inte rest in understanding how we can recognize and assess learn ing outcomes for patrons (Lin et al., 2020). This g ap in literature represents an imbalanced understanding of the educative value of making. Studies that identify what is knowable in maker education only accounts for half of the learning process; we must also explicate how  the knowable can be recognized, interpreted, and unders tood by stakeholders (e.g., educators, administrato rs, caregivers, learners).

Public libraries, in particular, are embracing make r education (Chang et al., 2019). The second most empirical studies on makerspaces come from the field of Library and Information Science (Mersand, 2021). This is perhaps unsurprising given that a common mission of libraries—to increase patron literacy—demands that they integrate emerging literacies (e.g., maker education, digital literacy) (Mann, 2018). However, only a small amount of the literature on assessment in maker education originates from libraries (Lin et al., 2020). This may be due to the fact that the majority of available assessments are designed for schools with relatively uniform standards and pedagogical expectations, whereas the heterogeneity  of library goals, and of making processes and outc omes (Teasdale, 2020, p. 1) fundamentally differ (Rogoff et al., 2016). For instance, library staff may be less interested in assessing individual learners through making and  would rather understand how their makerspace suppo rts family learning (Roque, 2016). Endeavoring to asses s family learning shifts the unit of analysis in as sessment from one learner to multiple learners and would lik ely demand different assessment tactics. To close t he gap in literature, scholars and maker educators across lib rary settings are beginning to place equal importan ce on studying maker-related learning outcomes and assessment of those outcomes. Due to unique practical and pedagogical demands, and the axiological commitments of library makerspaces, scholars intending to support assessment in these settings have taken to either developing bespoke assessments (Chang et al., 2020), or sharing assessment-building processes to help maker educators develop their own assessments (e.g., Cun et al., 2019). This stud y addresses the issue of limited scholarship on ass essment in library makerspaces and adds to literature about pr ocesses that help facilitators create assessments f or their makerspaces. We report findings from a qualitative research project in progress that investigated what library staff broadly consider evidence of success in their makerspaces and seek to assess. We introduce a Typology of Success, which encapsulates five major categories of success  that emerged from analysis, and we advance the ide a that ICLS2022 Proceedings 831 © ISLS success is, at times, a moving target in library makerspaces that requires flexible assessment tools with the capacity to capture evidence of success as it evolves.

## Potential significance of the work

To support facilitators of maker education in their  efforts to assess learning in their makerspaces, C un and colleagues (2019) generated a library makerspace assessment matrix to identify the “assessment needs of patrons and librarians” (p. 39). The purpose of the matrix is to clarify which types of assessment tools are appropriate for any given maker activity based on when and how a pa tron most benefits from assessment and what a libra rian wants to learn about the patron using an assessment. The matrix also provides a list of six categories of assessment “that could be implemented given typical librarians’ knowledge and resources” (p. 45). Though a promising tool that contributes to assessm ent of maker education both practically and conceptually, the matrix centers on a specific kind of learning. By focusing on skill development and mastery of technology, the matrix only documents learning that  is “acquisitional” (Sfard, 1998). This theory of l earning “implies that there is a clear end point to the process of learning” (Sfard, 1998, p. 6).” A library maker educator assessing for acquisitional learning might administer a pre-and post-survey under the assumption that  they can track the knowledge or skill a patron had before and after attending a program. In contrast to learning as acquisition, learning can be participatory. Learning as particip ation is “a process of becoming a member of a certa in community” (Sfard, 1998, p. 6), which is considered  an important outcome in literature on maker educat ion (Barajas-López & Bang, 2018; Blikstein & Worsley, 2 016; Einarsson, 2021). If one is interested in asse ssing learning as participation, one might provide exit surveys that ask patrons to describe how welcome they feel in a makerspace or observe moments of collaboration between patrons. Teasdale’s work (2020) incorporates different stakeholder perspectives “to foster in-depth understanding of evaluative criteria for the makerspace in its re al-world context, based on multiple sources of evidence drawn from multiple perspectives” (p. 4). She draws from observations of the makerspace, interviews with library staff and leaders to understand the goals of the library, and interviews with library patrons “to investigate their purposes and desired benefits related to makerspace particip ation” (p. 4). By triangulating these data sources,  Teasdale defines outcomes and visions of success that stakeholders want from maker education experiences for the purpose of developing evaluative criteria for assessments. Teasdale presents seven evaluative criteria that re flect conceptions of success held by library staff and makers: ¥ Access to technology ¥ Learning to use technology ¥ Fostering entrepreneurship ¥ Nurturing creativity ¥ Strengthening communities and families ¥ Realizing intrinsic benefits ¥ Saving money The seven criteria provide a broad picture of the k ind of success that is possible and desired by libr ary staff and makers, extending learning outcomes beyond those captured in the library makerspace assessment matrix. However, as Teasdale notes, each criterion will not  be relevant to all participants; a strong assessme nt would “identify the criteria that were relevant to each participant and apply those specific criteria to data collected from that individual” (p. 8). Gathering data from various stakeholders and soliciting information about their desires and intentions in a makerspace appears to be a promising approach to developing relevant criteria that can be used to assess maker activities. And yet, the criteria Teas dale offers do not clarify for maker educators the types of assessments that might be useful for capturing evidence the way Cun et al. do (2019). The findings from this study build off the contribu tions from Cun et al. and Teasdale by introducing a Typology of Success for maker education in librarie s. The Typology identifies five categories of succe ss that emerged from interviews with library staff at two l ibraries. Like Teasdale’s work (2020), this study s hares conceptions of success that can be used to inform which types of assessments employed or designed for a given maker activity or program. Like the work of Cun et al. (2019), the Typology is meant to direct library  maker educators toward a narrowed set of assessment possibilities to aid their decision making. While the Typology is still being refined, we introduce it as context for the broader finding we prese nt regarding the evolving nature of success. Both the Typology and our general finding contribute to burg eoning literature on assessment in makerspaces in library settings. The main significance of this work is to advance the idea that what appears to be noise in data about as sessment in library makerspaces (e.g., responses from library ICLS2022 Proceedings 832 © ISLS staff that contradict each other, or constant appeals to “it depends” when a staff member describes how they know a program is working) is in fact a feature of makin g and learning in library makerspaces that must be addressed more fully to authentically assess these unique environments without losing their richness. Theoretical and methodological approaches pursued Theory The study is grounded in the constructivist paradigm of epistemology, which asserts that “knowing” is a socially and culturally situated process that builds on prev ious meaning making and the activity of learners bo th individually and collectively (Efland, 2002). There  is nuance and debate within constructivism that is  worth exploring (Packer & Goicoechea, 2000), but it is beyond the scope of this paper. Most importantly, this work does not adhere to a positivist paradigm of epistemology, in which knowing, or truth is considered universal, complete, and discoverable. In concert with constructivism, o ur study was designed to understand how library sta ff experience assessment, how they practice it, and ho w they perceive both assessment and success in make r education.

More specifically, the research presented here draw s on evidence-centered assessment design (ECD) (Mislevy & Riconscente, 2005), a method for designing assessment using data associated with authentic practices in the learning setting in which assessment will ta ke place. ECD is multi-step process of designing as sessment that first asks what “complex of knowledge, skills,  or other attributes should be assessed” in a learn ing environment (Messick, 1994, p. 16, as cited in Misl evy & Riconscente, 2005). The five steps include: domain analysis, which includes substantial analysis of the conten t and concepts that will be assessed and identifyin g what evidence will indicate that the content or con cept has been understood by a learner; domain modeling, a process of adapting the information gathered from the domain analysis phase into a narrative argument that will become the foundation of the assessment; conceptual assessment framework, is the process of adapting the narrative argument into a blueprint of tasks or ite ms necessary to conduct the assessment; assessment implementation, which encompasses the development of assessment too ls (e.g., tasks, rubrics, and samples); assessment delivery, the process of enacting the assessment and reporting its findings to the appropriate audience (Mislevy & Riconscente, 2005). This paper presents findings on the domain analysis portion of ECD, the  only phase that has been completed thus far. Although the project was interrupted by COVID 19, we intend to follow the four subsequent steps and develop a suite of assessment tools for maker-based learning based on our domain analysis.

## Methodology

This study follows the methodological framework of a basic qualitative study (Merriam & Tisdell, 2016). “A central characteristic of all qualitative research is that individuals construct reality in interaction with their social worlds,” and the purpose of qualitative research is  to understand “the meaning a phenomenon has for th ose involved...to understand how people make sense of their lives and their experiences” (Merriam & Tisdell, 2016, p. 24). Our research questions ask: ¥ What learning goals do library staff and maker educators have for their maker-based learning programs? ¥ How do they define success in those programs?

¥ How do they recognize and describe evidence of success and the learning goals they identified? These research questions allowed us to make sense of what staff want to assess in their maker education programs without placing explicit emphasis on assessment. Focusing on assessment might have been more difficult for staff to articulate, especially if they did not already have robust assessment practices or assessment literacy. To elicit responses to those questions, library staff and maker educators were asked to explain how they know when their program is going well and what evidence they look for. Study sites Makerspaces in two libraries serving medium-sized c ities in the Midwest were included in this study. L earn to Make Lab (1) serves nine library branches in a single system, though it is physically housed in the centrally located downtown branch. Learn to Make Lab hosts artists-in-residence and drop-in sessions at their physical space, and shares maker-kits which include various materials t o the other branches, summer school sites, and outr each programs. Most of the materials at Learn to Make Lab are considered low-tech (e.g., craft materials, art supplies, recycled goods), but they also boast a media lab. The makerspace known as Making an Impact (1) is also located in the main, downtown branch of its library system. In contrast to Learn to Make Lab, it is one of 37 branches that ICLS2022 Proceedings 833 © ISLS have sites throughout suburban, somewhat rural, and inner-city regions alike. Due to limited residential housing surrounding this branch, it mostly serves people wh o commute to the library. Making an Impact has two full studio spaces in addition to other technology common in makerspaces, like 3D printers, laser cutters, robots, and interactive technologies, as well as more analog tools like a button maker. Sample Interviews for the full domain analysis were conducted with library staff and maker educators, and library patrons (e.g., learners and their caregivers). In this paper, however, we present findings based on analysis of the responses from library staff and maker educators only. Rather than target a large sample size and make cla ims about the average response to the research questions, staff were purposely selected to amass a maximum variation sampling of responses for each library site that would illustrate variety and range (Merriam & Tisdell, 2016). Library staff were selected to participate in interviews based on how directly tied their work and responsibilities were to their respective library makerspaces. Each staff member t hat participated was connected in some way to the m aker programs offered by their library, but the staff varied in terms of their exact roles within the library. The majority of staff who participated in the study were maker educators or librarians who also implemented maker education programs; staff supervisors and a manager of operations were also interviewed.

## Data collection

Seventeen library staff were interviewed through a semi-structured interviewing process (Rubin & Rubin, 2005). Interviews ranged from 25 to 50 minutes and were conducted in person by two members of the research team (one at each library site). The research team guided the  interviews so that conversation focused on learnin g and engagement in library maker programs, but participa nts were encouraged to draw from experiences with o ther programs as well, especially when discussing how le arner engagement can look different depending on th e programmatic focus. Specifically, they were asked, what is happening when things are going really well  in the Launchpad/Bubbler? How do/would you know? How could you know? If participants needed more scaffolding to answer the questions, the research team would probe  further by asking What does it look/sound/feel/smell like when things are going well? Even if hasn’t happened  yet, what would it look like?  They also encouraged participants to share about a specific time or program that was really successful and to describe what happened.

## Data analysis

After audio recordings of interviews were transcrib ed, the researcher who did not conduct the intervie ws underwent several cycles of inductive, emic coding analysis. Data was analyzed using (a) descriptive coding that highlights the topic of a response, (b) concept coding, which describes the content that is discussed in the response, and (c) subcoding, which assigns a secondary code to enrich the primary or parent code (Saldaña, 2016). The first iteration of coding was conducted using an analytic matrix that included verbatim quotes about success from the transcripts, analytic memos describing the topic and content of each quote from the researcher, and potential codes that encapsulated the main themes in the quote (Maxwell, 2013). The matrix was used to organize the data and trace the researcher’s interpretation. The initial phase of coding produced a long list of codes, whic h were then transferred to a code map (Saldaña, 2016), a list of codes and subcodes organized by theme. The researcher condensed and expanded the codes in the list while reviewing the analytic matrix. Following that, the researcher created a new analytic matrix, free of any analysis or notes, and coded the verbatim quotes again using the list of codes. This process afforded her an opportunity to test whether the same themes emerged. Once the second phase of coding was completed, the researcher updated the code list and began a process of coding the list for thematic patterns and organizing (and reorganizing) them into categories. Those codes were then shared and discussed with the library partner from each site, so they could c hallenge or confirm their accuracy and potential ut ility for developing assessment moving forward, a process called member checking (Merriam & Tisdell, 2016). With their input, redundant subcodes were eliminated, and pare nt codes were reorganized, revised, and condensed i nto central concepts that related to how library staff know when a program is working. Throughout data analysis, all three research members met bi-monthly to discuss th e data and engage in constant comparison of themes or “incidents” in the data as they emerged (Corbin & Strauss, 2008).

## Major findings

Typology of success Five themes emerged when library staff described how they know when a program works and what counts as success in library marker education. Success is encapsulated in the metrics they collect, the lasting impact a program has on patrons, the moments when patrons experience learning as participation, the opportunities to ICLS2022 Proceedings 834 © ISLS check off learning as acquisition, and the proficiency with which learning is facilitated by maker educators (facilitation of learning). Metrics refer to a concept of success as the accumulation and collection of quantitative data on magnitude of outreach and audience demographics, usually over time. When success is understood as lasting impact, it means a program influenced patrons in a way that was holistic (e.g., programs connected to school curriculum) or long-term (e.g., patrons are inspired to engage in similar programming outside of the library site). A program is considered successful if it demonstrates learning as participation, that is, learning as a “constant flux of doing” inseparable from the learning context and “a process of becoming a member of a certain community” (Sfard, 1998, p. 6). A program may also be considered successful if a maker educator observes learning as acquisition, in which a patron picks up a skill or learns something new (Sfard, 1998). Finally, success may be denoted by the facilitation of learning, including how “well” learning is facilitated during an activity or by the maker educator’s experience of meeting their own teaching and learning goals. These five types illustrate the varied ways in which success in library programming manifests according to library staff.

Again, this Typology is still being refined, but we include it here to provide context for an important idea that has emerged from the data regarding the complexity of assessment in library makerspaces. As it is now, the different success types suggest assessment is meani ngful at vastly different timescales, not only form atively (ongoing throughout the course of instruction) and summatively (occurring at the end of an activity, program, or series of programs) (Shute, 2009), but also in rela tion to whether success is assessed within the lear ning event (e.g., learning as participation or acquisition), or over the course of multiple distinct learning events (e.g., metrics or lasting impact). The types also indicate different units of analy sis for assessment, from individual patrons to patrons as a collective or community to maker educa tors. Importantly, the Typology reflects learning a s both participation and acquisition, a feature that is of ten missing from assessment tools and protocols. Th e inclusion of both types of learning might be considered epist emologically incompatible, but Sfard insists they a re productively contradictory: Each has something to offer that the other cannot p rovide… metaphorical pluralism embraces a promise of a better research and a more satisfact ory practice. The basic tension between seemingly conflicting metaphors is our protection against theoretical excesses, and is a source of power. (1998, p. 10) Ultimately, the Typology demonstrates that the lear ning outcomes and success library staff value and want to assess differs greatly, by timescales, units of analysis, and even theories of learning. By categorizing types of success that staff care about, this tool may hel p maker educators select or design assessments for their makerspaces. For instance, when engaging in domain analysis of their own sites, they might start with the Typology to identify the outcomes and models of success they value. Library staff might also conduct their own analysis and compare their findings with the Typology as a way to articulate the timescales, units of analysis, and models of learning they want to focus on. A precise  framework for the Typology and a comprehensive gui de is outside the scope of the paper but is forthcoming.

Evidence of success is a moving target In addition to the sheer variety of definitions of success in library makerspaces, our findings indicate that evidence of success, too, is difficult to identify because it can shift. For instance, one library staff asserted that the way they determine a patron’s progress “depends on what acti vity they’re doing.” In fact, six of the 17 library  staff interviewed, about 35%, were coded as responding with “it depends” when asked how they know when a program is working or successful. The staff member went on to say that in the case of a patron who wants to us e the recording studio, they are more likely to pose questions if they do not understand what to do, because their session time is limited. In contrast, when people visit the makerspace to explore materials and activities, they may not ask for assistance right away. It is then up to the mak er educator to observe their behavior and recognize  when a patron is using a tool without fully understanding it. Essentially, the library staff in this example implies that maker activities do not occur in a vacuum; they are socially and culturally embedded, and inherent in each activity are cultural practices around seeking support that facilitators need to be aware of while assessing, because those practices can inform how evidence of success looks.

Another staff member noted that certain maker behavior, like “coming in and just downloading one thing offline that they didn’t use or didn’t make,” may b e evidence that the makerspace is not successfully inviting patrons to “progress deeper.” However, they admit t hat such behavior might also communicate success if  the patron’s goal is to use the library for its availab le resources. Teasdale (2020) discusses this phenom enon of assessment as a moving target in her work as well, when she writes, “applying (assessment) criteria un iformly ICLS2022 Proceedings 835 © ISLS could, therefore, lead to a mismatch for some participants.” She calls for “individualized criteria” in assessment instead “to identify the criteria that [are] relevant to each participant” (p. 8). Moreover, our analysis indicates that evidence of s uccess not only varies by activity or the patron’s personal goals, but it can sometimes shift dependin g on the patron’s perceived dispositions. One libra ry staff stated that they could tell “things are well” or that a patron is enjoying themselves or engaged “if they’re asking more questions…and they’re wanting to find out more about what’s going on.” When pressed by the interviewer what specifically about the questions or number of questions indicates success, the staff member stated, Well, I think it really depends on the type of question. Some people might be the type of person that might not ask any questions, and if I see that they've been sitting quiet and all of a sudden, they start asking a couple of questions about somet hing, that indicates to me that they're interested. They're interest is more piqued. Wherea s somebody else might be just asking all kinds of questions about everything, "What is this?  What is that? What is that?" If they start ramping up their questions or start focusing their questions on a certain area, I can tell that they're more engaged in that area or they're getting more engaged. The quote suggests that even when a maker educator is looking for certain behavior or criteria to assess a patron’s experience or success, the meaning of that behavior  shifts depending on how that person normally behav es. Certainly, whether a make educator can really know in a short period how a patron “normally” behaves is question worth exploring in its own dedicated study. The key  point, however, is that evidence of success is som etimes a moving target, adding to the layer of complexity fo r assessment in public library makerspaces. While m ore research is necessary to fully investigate the notion that evidence of success can be dynamic rather than static, we share implications for assessing a moving target in the following section.

## Conclusions and implications

Across the literature on assessment in library makerspaces, scholars specifically note how difficult it is to design assessments for these settings, because of the numerous learning goals worth addressing and diverse public library stakeholders.  Teasdale (2020) comments on how the heterogeneity of patrons and maker educators, and t heir diverse goals for learning through making, make the challenge of developing or selecting assessment “particularly pronounced for public library makerspaces” (p. 1). Cun et al. (2019) recognize that assessment for lib rary makerspaces is connected to the assessment needs of  the library at large, and that part of developing authentic assessments for makerspaces requires compatibility between the two, or there is a risk of assessment b eing “viewed as having little or no value to a stakehold er of the library makerspace (e.g., a patron, libra rian, administrator, library board member, or municipal agency)” (p. 41). Wardrip et al. (2019) note that the vast array of stakeholders associated with libraries pose a ch allenge when it comes to assessment; the question o f “what should be assessed and for whom” is not as straightforward as it may seem (p. 34). This study confirms that success takes many forms. The Typology of Success introduces five different types of success that emerged from interviews with library staff. Additionally, the Typology begins to address the need to understand “success that accounts for makers’ and libraries’ varying priorities” (Teasdale, 2020, p. 1) and aid in the design or selection of new assessment tools to support assessment of these unique learning environments. For instance, the Typology might be used with maker educators to clarify the success type they most care about, thereby helping them identify the unit of analysis that they might target. Another implication of the Typology is that maker educators and their ability to facilitate learning in makerspaces may be a useful focus for assessment. Educators must make numerous inferences about patro ns, particularly when it comes to assessing a patro n’s progress and providing appropriate feedback to support their learning (Michalchik & Gallagher, 2010); it is worth exploring how well they make those inferences when it comes to assessment and what practices, tools, a nd professional development can help attune them to making stronger inferences. In their review, Lin and colleagues (2020) refer to assessment in any makerspace as a “moving target,” “with varying students, educators, activities, disciplines, arrangements, and learning outcomes” (p. 3). This study confirms that another layer of complexity in assessment of library makerspaces may be due to the shifting nature of evidence of success, which depends on the maker activity, the patron’s goals, and the patron’s disposition when it comes to facilitation of a program and their own participation. We imagine, too, that evidence of success may change as a maker educator continuously develops ex pertise for eliciting evidence from patrons, develo ping inferences of patrons’ learning, and making interpretations of those inferences. One implication for this finding is that library staff need support in identifying success and evidence of success with more consistency, so they might improve their informal assessment techniques (Gahagan & Calvert, 2020) and systematize the claims about their programs.

ICLS2022 Proceedings 836 © ISLS Another possible implication is that staff members’ penchant for saying, “it depends” when asked how they know their program is going well, may not an i ndication of uncertainty, but rather recognition th at what counts as evidence of success can shift in a moment ’s notice, especially as they take in more informat ion about the patron. This implication evokes several questions. If evidence of success shifts depending on multiple factors, what specifically are those factors? This study begins to answer that by pointing to the activity, the patron’s goals, and the patron’s disposition when learning, but mor e research is needed to address that provocation fu lly. Additionally, if evidence of success is not always static, are formative assessments sufficient to cap ture its dynamism? Or are other, more flexible assessment tools necessary to capture the evidence of success as it shifts? What do/would those tools need to look like? How wo uld they operate differently from current available assessment tools and practices? Moreover, if evidence of success can be in flux, what does that mean for assessing learners across learning settings? In describing the learning as participation metaphor, Sfard (1998) says, Being “in action” means being in a constant flux. The awareness of the change that never stops means refraining from a permanent labeling. Actions  can be clever or unsuccessful, but these adjectives do not apply to the actors. For the learner, all options are always open, even if he or she carries a history of failure…Today you act one way; tomorrow you may act differently. (p. 8) Perhaps our penchant for labeling students as a ref lection of their assessment results (e.g, gifted and talented, or below average) will be troubled by the finding that evidence of success is a moving target. If success evolves as the learner does, perhaps we will come to learn that current assessments cannot fully measure students’ ability to learn or grow and should not be used in this capacity. Maybe there will come a time when we abandon assessment practices that seek to define students by what they cando, and instead find ways to unders tand how students’ potential for learning expands with each new day.
