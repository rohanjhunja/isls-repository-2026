# Integrating Knowledge Forum to Support Knowledge Building among Adult Learners

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the integration of Knowledge Forum (KF) in face-to-face classes to support adult learners’ knowledge building. 10 early childhood educators joined this study while they attended a master course. Qualitative analysis of classroom observation showed how KF was integrated into face-to-face classes to foster knowledge-building among adult learners. The insights have implications for the future design of knowledge building for adult learners.

## Introduction

This is a work-in-progress case study, aiming to understand how to integrate Knowledge F orum to support adult learners’ knowledge building  in the classroom. Knowledge Building (KB) is a pedagogy proposed Scardamalia and Bereiter (2022). It aims  to involve students in the process of knowledge cre ation, akin to the practices of scientists. In KB, ideas are considered conceptual artifacts that can be constantly improve d, and students are expected to take collective cognitive responsibility for community k nowledge advancement. Knowledge Forum (KF), an asynchronous online discussion platform, has ofte n been used to support students’ knowledge building (Scardamalia, 2004; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2022). It provides a communal place where ideas can be collectively worked on in various ways (e.g., linked, reorganized, revised, integrated, etc.) for idea improvement. It also allows students to participate at their convenience, contributing from any location and at any time. KB has been widely studied in K-12 settings (e.g., Chen et al., 2015; Lin & Chan, 2 018; Zhang et al., 2020), and some have also explored its implementation am ong full-time university students (e.g., Hong et al., 2016; Lei & Chan, 2018), relatively less attention has been paid to the design of  KB for adult learners. Adult learners typically mingled between work and life, p ursuing part-time studies at university for further education. Given the numerous life and work commitments, adult learners typically have limited time for engaging in online discussions after class. Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to explor e how to integrate KF in adult learners’ face-to-face classes for knowledge building.

## Methods

Participants and context: This study was situated in a master’s degree course on digital workplace learning. 10 early childhood educators who enrolled in this course participated in this study. The instructor i s one of the researchers of this project, and she has extensive experience in KB research and practice. Data source and analysis: Classroom observations were collected  to understand the classroom process. Thematic analyses were conducted, including 1) identifying incidences of KB in the observation notes, 2) creating labels for each identified incidence, and 3) developing themes by combining the labels. Course structure: The course included three full-day sessions and one self-studied session. All sessions took place on Saturdays, with a one-week gap between each session. In the first session, students explored the role of technology in workplace learning through group discussions, facilitated by KF. They also worked together to analyze a journal article on e-learning at work and engaged in a hands-on activity on KF. In session two, they reflected on their discussion on KF, including whether they have been improving their understanding on KF, what are the  evidence of knowledge construction, and what are promising i deas worth further pursuing, etc. After reflection, students continued to contribute to KF. Students engaged in a group project in the end, which involved redesigning a technology-supported workplace learning scenario based on KB pedagogy, and posted their group ideas on KF.  Between sessions two and three, there  is a  self-directed learning session, students designed technology-supported workplace learning  in their own contexts and posted their designs and reflections on KF. In the third session, students presented and collaboratively improved their designs, providing feedbac k to each other on KF. They also engaged in a mind map activity to consolidate learning. The session concluded with reflections on their overall learning experience on KF.

## Results

Through content analysis of classroom observations,  we found a few themes related to how KF was integr ated into the adult learners’ face-to-face classes.

Recording and extending the collective ideas from small group discussion: KF has been used as a tool to support students’ small-group discussions, just like how they used other to ols (e.g., Padlet) to support group discussions during the Pandemic. Students used KF to document their group ideas during their small-group discussion. In a departure from conventional methods, they were also encouraged to collaboratively build upon each other 's contributions as a whole class. By doing so, studen ts' group conceptual artifacts were documented and made accessible in a public space, fostering continuous idea refinement and improvement. Complementing and extending the whole class discuss ion: In addition to supporting small group discussions, KF was also used to support and complement the whole class discussio n, including supporting idea brainstorming, articulation of prior knowledge, and building on and improving ideas. These roles complemented the traditional roles of whole class discussion, wh ich usually focus on teacher-lead discussion for consolidating ideas. For instance, in a discussion on good collaborative learning, students first shared their prior  ideas on KF, which were later enriched and expanded upon during the whole-group discussion. Afterward, students continued to work on KF, integrating new insights gained from  the class discussion and pursuing further inquirie s. In this process, asynchronous (supported by KF) and synchronous (cla ssroom) discussions played complementary roles in supporting students' knowledge-building processes.

Documenting the highlights of classroom discussion: To make KF more relatable for working adult students, the instructor introduced the concept of "organizational memory”. This concept emphasizes the value of articulating implicit knowledge into explicit forms, thereby creating a collective organizational memo ry for ongoing dissemination and refinement of ideas within the organization. In alignment with this, KF was positioned as a tool for preserving their community memory, where their explicit knowledge c an be shared and refined for continual advancement. This approach instilled in students a sense of collective responsibility to contribute to this shared memory through their engagement on KF.

Supporting collective reflection: KF was used to support reflection and peer feedback. After each session, students were encouraged to write reflection notes on KF, supported by pre-designed scaffolding (e.g.,  "my new learning," "how my new learning relates to my workp lace," and "what I want to know more”). These scaffolds were designed to support their connection of new learning to their workplace. KF also allowed the reflection ideas to be shared among the community.

## Discussion

KB has been widely studied in K-12 settings (Chen et al., 2015; Lin & Chan, 2018; Zhang et al., 2020), yet limited research has explored its application in adult learning settings and its potential synergy with classroom discussion.

The study extended the previous study by examining the integration of KF into in-person adult education, considering the distinct attributes of adult learners—such as their inability to contribute to KF beyond class time. While we focus on KF, this exploration also sheds light on ways of integrating asynchronous and synchronous discussion in post-pandemic educational settings. Furthermore, it offers practical insights for educators new to KB to seamlessly incorporate KF into their classrooms.
