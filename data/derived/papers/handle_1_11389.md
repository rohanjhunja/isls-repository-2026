# What are the Capacities Needed to be a Problem Solver?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Educators encounter an ongoing challenge in effectively preparing learners to become innovative problem-solvers for the future. Despite continuous advocacy for equipping youth with future-ready skills, significant changes in educational practices remain scarce. One contributing factor appears to be the absence of clear, consistent terminology, definitions, and indicators related to the capacities required for learners to develop inventiveness. To address this issue, we conducted a modified Delphi study with a diverse panel of experts to investigate the research question: What capacities do youth need to become inventive problem-solvers? Through multiple rounds of surveys and analysis, we developed a comprehensive set of 43 capacities and strategies relevant to problem-solving. This presentation will outline our methodology and findings and will encourage discussion on how these insights can inform future research and educational reform.

## Problem statement

Educators face a persistent challenge – how best to prepare youth for an unpredictable futu re. Multiple calls have been made to reform education to better prepar e learners for a technology-driven future (e.g., 21 st Century skills, maker education). Additionally, there have been myriad reform efforts to create and support ac ademic standards at the state and national levels (e.g., C ommon Core, Next Generation Science Standards) that  outline knowledge and practices that are meant to help lear ners and the U.S. be successful. In the last two decades, STEM (science, technology, engineering and mathemat ics) education has gained increasing importance as a way to develop a globally competitive workforce (De nson et al., 2015; European Commission, 2022; Maltese & Harsh, 2015). These efforts are based on the premise that individuals with expertise in STEM possess skills that lead to greater innovation and make them capable of  solving challenges (Wagner & Compton, 2015).   Despite decades of efforts toward change – going back to the launch of Sputnik – we argue that there have yet to be major shifts in instructional practice and assessme nts to support these reforms across the educational  landscape. We believe that one reason for the lack of substant ive changes in instruction, assessment and attainme nt may result from the lack of clear, consistent definitio ns as to what these necessary capacities are and su bsequent indicators to evaluate whether learners possess the m. Although some flexibility in definitions is nece ssary, it becomes problematic for both implementation and assessment when the se definitions  are too broad. For example, much of the focus on innovation involves t he ideas of creativity and problem-solving. However, defining and measuring creativity, especially in cl assroom settings and across multiple learners, pose s significant challenges (e.g., Plucker, 2016).

Various groups have developed frameworks that outli ne constructs deemed necessary for preparing learners to be future-ready, such as the 21st Century Skills, NGSS, and D efining Education 4.0. Some frameworks emphasize literacies and social/emotiona l constructs aimed at developing global citizens, w hile others focus on competencies for innovation and pro blem-solving. Although a comprehensive discussion is beyond the scope of this paper, there are overlaps and inc onsistencies in how these constructs are defined, with some frameworks describing them as skills while oth ers refer to them as practices or attributes. A nother notable issue across all of  these frameworks is the lack of consensus and consi stency in defining the competencies. Additionally, the level of specificity also varies,  with some frameworks identifying capacities at a s ub-construct level, some at a global level, and others somewhere in between. Finally, the frameworks vary in their process of development, with many existing frameworks lacking rigorous development through research. Objectives and approach Due to the aforementioned shortcomings, we initiate d a study aimed at describing and naming the capaci ties required for individuals to excel as inventive prob lem solvers. This research sought to identify the s pecific competencies necessary for graduates to address rea l-world problems, bridging the gap between existing frameworks and their practical applicability for educators. We believe this work provides a foundation for future research on optimal methods to assess these capacities and best practices for fostering them through education. For this project, we employed the Delphi method, a technique for gathering opinions and building consensus on a given topic or set of questions (Pol lard & Pollard, 2005). This method involves an iter ative process of data collection that captures participan t perspectives from diverse backgrounds and context s (Murry & Hammons, 1995). Before commencing data collection, our research team engaged in reflection and discussion to gain a better understanding of key capacities posited by existing frameworks as necessary for inventive problem solving, as well as the challenge s associated with data collection. This preliminary  step clarified the specific questions we intended to pose to our experts. We then identified a sample of potential participan ts with relevant and varied expertise in inventive problem solving. Our aim was to include individuals  from multiple fields, including education (both re searchers and practitioners), innovation-focused industries, invention and patent-related work, STEM hiring, and relevant government and policy initiatives. Recognizing the potential bias inherent in using traditional academic metrics (e.g., senior positions, publications, citations, a wards) to indicate expertise, we broadened our crit eria to increase the likelihood of engaging  a diverse panel of experts. We specifically recruit ed a heterogeneous group based on relevant experience, traditional indicator s where appropriate, and peer recommendations, ensu ring representation across U.S. regions, apparent race/e thnicity, sex, and professional background. To furt her minimize bias, we excluded close contacts, acquaint ances, and collaborators of any research team membe rs. This recruitment process yielded 170 potential part icipants, with 80 completing the demographics secti ons of our surveys. Our sample was evenly divided by gender, highly e ducated (95% holding at least a Bachelor’s degree), and representative of various demographics: 50% were first-generation graduates or STEM workers, 65% were aged between 35 and 55, and the racial and ethnic composition reflected the broader U.S. population. Utilizing a modified Delphi approach, we conducted multiple rounds of gathering expert input to build consensus on the capacities required for inventive problem solving. In the first round, we asked experts to provide definitions and terms for capacities  they believed necessary for inventive problem solvi ng, resulting in more than 550 instances of capacities. These respon ses were clustered using both human analysis and AI  tools (i.e., Claude and ChatGPT). We sought to condense the set of ideas based on similarity, w hile also trying to minimize overlap in the definitions. This process resulted i n 35 unique concepts and definitions. Next, we had our advisory panel review the concepts and definitions  for relevance and clarity, leading to further refin ements, which led us to separate out a few of the constructs. Thu s, in the second round, we presented experts with 4 0 definitions to rate for importance and sought suggested edits, resulting in 265 suggest ions. The research team considered each suggested edit, and did our best to incorporate them while maintaining the core idea of each definition. This process resulted in 43 concepts and definitions. In the third round, we asked  experts to evaluate each capacity, indicating how much they thought each capacity was associated with routine versus inventive problem solving or if a capacity was not important to problem solving at all.

## Findings

Our modified Delphi process  included three rounds of data collection and revisions, resu lting in a list of 43 capacities. Although this is an extensive list, we do not feel comfortable saying this is a “comprehen sive” list as we cannot be sure this is complete and bounded. The first round of da ta collection generated more than 500 ideas from the experts that ranged in length from a  few words to a few sentences. As we went through t he process of initial review and coding, we sought to keep each idea that was unique (without our judgeme nt of relevance or not), yet reduce redundancy. As we progressed, we realize d it was easier to evaluate the data if we parsed the larger set it into ideas that were connected to personal capac ities and another group that were associated with problem-solving strategies. This split made sense to us, and we maintain ed this distinction through the rest of the process as it seemed to hel p our advisors and experts break a large set of ide as into groups that were  more manageable. By the end of this process, we had a revised collection of 43 statements in two subsets: personal capacities (n = 20) and strategies (n = 23). Despite substantial emphasis on STEM education, extensive data from var ious sources suggest that these initiatives are not having a significant impact o r that it is challenging to determine what impact they are having.  Utilizing the general framework of a backward design approach, our research sought to identify the goals associated with problem-solving –  specifically, the capacities an individual requires and the strategies they should employ to be effect ive problem solvers. Given the central focus of science  and STEM disciplines on identifying and resolving problems, this work is pivotal to discussions on th e teaching and learning of these fields. Additionally, although science and STEM are not exclusively taught to fulf ill the requirements of the U.S. government, there is a widespread belief that these fields are vital to na tional economic stability. While we believe many of  these capacities and strategies can be acquired alongside  and through the study of science and STEM, we do n ot think they develop naturally without intentional effort  from educators. Therefore, cultivating a learner's ability to address problems of varying scales and contexts mus t remain a priority for educators and educational researchers. Although we understand that our list of capacities and strategies are not totally unique and that will be most valuable when evaluated in relation to exis ting frameworks, we have not yet been able to complete that part of this work.

We aim for this presentation to initiate dialogue w ith the audience regarding how this work can contribute to educational reform. In future endeavo rs, we seek to identify ways in which these capacit ies and strategies can: assist educators in informing their practice and as sessment; support researchers in shaping their conceptualizations and data collection methods; and  guide policymakers in determining whether these objectives are valuable for education, as well as h ow assessment systems might need to be adjusted to better capture learner progress. Ultimately, we hope this presentation will spark discussion that can inform ongoing and future work in this area.
