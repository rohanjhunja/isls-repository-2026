# Building Shared Meanings: Large-Scale Constructionist Professional Development in the Global South

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Implementing and sustaining large-scale technology-focused educational programs in public systems requires robust professional development (PD) programs. In this paper, we share our experience of creating shared meanings for teachers during a 17-week PD program as part of introducing a large-scale public policy that implements digital fabrication and maker labs in Brazil’s largest municipal public school network. We illustrate how a well-structured PD program, featuring constructionist hands-on investigative, creative, and reflective workshops, is key to instigating mindset changes and building shared meanings among teachers, which are essential for the implementation and sustainability of a large-scale educational reform centered on systems thinking, investigation, making, and project-based learning.

## Introduction

Educational reforms in the Global South face increa sing pressure to quickly and broadly integrate tech nology, driven by the promise of enhancing teaching quality and transforming students’ future job opportunities. However, this vision often adds extra burdens on Brazilian teachers, who predominantly work long hours in complex settings marked by social and economic vulnerabilities, incl uding violence, and earn low salaries compared to o ther professionals with equivalent years of experience i n the country. I n this context, familiarity with technology is not as prevalent among educators as it is in the Global North. Implementing technological innovations often occurs hastily and without adequate preparation, complicat ing the challenges of adaptation and adopti on in an already intricate environment. The lack of clarity and alig nment regarding what “technology” truly means and h ow it should be integrated to enhance learning underscore s the urgent need for a shared understanding among all stakeholders, especially educators, who are central to this transformative p rocess (Campos et al., 2019; Chen et al, 2024).

In Brazil’s largest municipal school network, intro ducing digital fabrication labs aligned with Constructionism (Papert, 1980) has become a corners tone of public policy. This initiative seeks to enh ance learning and skill-building in response to contemporary challenges by fostering digital culture, systems thinking, inquiry, maker, and project-based approaches. However, robust large-scale implementations of public policies involving project-based learning, Constructionism, and inquiry science practices alongside technology integration remain rare. For such technology-based educational reforms to succeed, it is crucial  to ensure that concepts and meanings are shared among all stakeholders, from th e school level to central administration (Chen et a l., 2024; Cobb & Jackson, 2012; Ioannou & Gravel, 2024). This p olicy aims to integrate maker labs in 500 schools b y 2028, and so far, 200 elementary and middle schools  have received these labs. Each lab is supported by  a Collaboratory Teacher (CT), responsible for integrating the labs into regular classroom activities. In 2022 and 2023, a collaboration was established between a North American research university lab and the municipality’s education department to launch a  professional development (PD) initiative supportin g the implementation of the newly established techn ology program. Over two consecutive years, more tha n 50 educators participated in a 40-hour PD program in New York, which also helped identify the network’s needs and the specific training requirements of its teachers. As part of the program’s implementation, a second in-depth PD program was designed to prepare the first 150 CTs f or their new roles. Created and implemented by the authors of this paper, who are part of the Brazilian team i n this lab, the PD program emphasizes hands-on experiences with Constructionism, critical reflections on the role of technology (Blikstein, 2008), and the creation of curricular units using backward design (Wiggins & McTighe, 2019). The course spans 17 weeks for two groups of 75 CTs. This paper seeks to contribute to the teacher profe ssional development literature by exploring how teachers in this program created shared meanings ab out Constructionism and technology integration, a c ore element of the public policy currently being implem ented, through “hands-on” and “minds-in” workshop experiences. Here, we use the term “minds-in” in alignment with Edith Ackermann’s idea that a  hands-on approach must intertwine opportunities for thoughtf ulness, reflection, emotional engagement, intellect ual presence, and the construction of meaning (Ackermann, 2001). This paper focuses on teachers’ reflections during one workshop centered on disassembling, exploring, and repurposing discarded CD-ROM drives. This workshop, like others developed for this professional develop ment program, incorporates an anchoring phenomenon (German, 2019) to spark curiosity and investigation, followed by productive conversations (Windschitl et al., 2021), hands-on activities, and reflective practices that connect the activity with constructionist prin ciples and learning goals for students. In the context described above, and considering that in Brazil most teacher education occurs online for both pre-service and in-service teachers (Todos pela Educaçã o, 2022), the professional development designed aimed to create an immersive e xperience for teachers where in-person workshops would model the type of learning envisioned for schools. Additionally, teachers would have in-depth opportunities to reflect and make connections between Constructionist theory, th eir fabrication labs, the Municipal Curriculum, and contemporary issues related to learning.

Design and implementation The professional development program aimed to help teachers gain confidence in creating their construc tionist curricular units, projects, and activities as they developed understandings related to Constructionism  and the critical use of learning technologies. To achieve this, the PD was designed to offer teachers immersive, hands-on workshops that modeled desired student experiences while also providing in-person and online spaces for reflection on how constructionist principles could apply to their contexts. The PD includes 45 hours o f in-person full-day workshops, 36 hours of real-time remote sessions (4 hours each), and 35 hours of asynchronous activities, primarily readings paired with reflective tasks suc h as visible thinking routines. Lastly, teachers en gaged in developing constructionist curricular units that in tegrated the city’s curriculum into activities in t he lab through creative technology use.

To further illustrate the principles underlying the PD, we focus on a specific workshop designed to model constructionist activities based on previous worksh ops by our lab and also the Tinkering Studio of the Exploratorium, which highlight how educators engaged in hands-on exploration and critical reflection to connect theory and practice. When designing a curriculum unit for a lab using a constructionist approach, it is essential to consider how materials and resources can enhance students’ epistemic agency and facilitate meaningful learning experiences. A key goal of maker and constructionis t education is to foster a design sensitivity —a mindset that perceives the designed world as adaptable and open to creation and re-imagination. In this process, teachers should create opportunities for students to closely observ e, explore complexities, and identify possibilities  in the world around them (Clapp et al., 2017).

Within this context, a workshop focused on disassem bling, exploring, and repurposing a digital artifac t was structured to demonstrate how hands-on learning can connect to students’ cultural conte xts and prior knowledge, spark curiosity regarding technology and  systems thinking, and facilitate the development o f creativity and problem-solving to achieve specific curricular learning outcomes. During two-and-a-half hours, 75 CTs were divided into fifteen groups based on Cohen  and Lotan’s (2017) criteria for heterogeneous clas srooms. This group work was intentionally organized to enco urage educators to engage with colleagues from diverse backgrounds, fostering the exchange of ideas and practices aligned with constructionist principles. The workshop aimed to build educators’ repertoires while prompti ng reflection on the potential and challenges of ha nds-on, minds-in learning within their newly implemented maker labs. The workshop began with an anchoring activity designed to activate prior knowledge, curiosity, and new questions. In the first 10 minutes, each group rece ived an old CD/DVD drive and was invited to draw and label the components and mechanisms they imagined  composed the interior of the artifact (Figure 1, le ft). This initial activity was followed by an investigation in which teachers spent 40 minutes exploring the insides of the object, disassembling the CD/DVD drives, categorizing the e ncountered parts, and  highlighting the imagined purposes and complexities. Throughout this inquiry-based activity, the labeling was done using Agency by Design’s Visible Thinking Routine (Figure 1, Project Zero, 2017), intended to provoke close and “slow” observation and reflection on what lies beyond the apparent features of the ob ject and the systems in which it is integrated (Cla pp et al., 2017). In the third part of the workshop, dedicated  to inspiring teachers to find opportunities and st imulate their creativity for proble m solving, teachers had 40 minutes to create someth ing new from at least one component from the CD/DVD drive using loose parts and other open-ended materials while also creating a switch (Figure 1, right). The choice to organize a repurposing worksh op as part of the PD sought to provoke reflections and demystify technology in light of materials and cult ural practices (repurposing), which is already comm onplace among teachers and students in Brazil (Blikstein, 2008). Once the groups finished creating their new artifacts and showcasing them to their peers, the workshop concluded with a reflective activity named “Thinking Together.” Inspired by the Project Zero thinking routine na med +1 (Ritchhart et al., 2020), each participant wrote a word on a piece of paper prompted by an idea from the workshop, then passed it to the person on their right. Upon receiving someone else’s paper, participants reflected on the word their colleague had written and added a new word to complement it. This process was repeated five times, resulting in a list of words. Then, keeping this list in mind, the groups of teachers wrote a synthesis based on their discussion of two questions: 1. What Constructionism ele ments did you identify in today’s activities? 2. What are the challenges and affordances of an activity like today’s workshop for integrating the Municipal Curriculum into the lab activities?

The 75 teachers were engaged throughout the worksho p, enabling the modeling of an activity that encouraged students to look closely, explore complexity, and find opportunities. Nonetheless, the highlight of the PD was the meaningful reflections that emerged during the “Thinking Together” part of the workshop, which will be discussed in the Lessons Learned section of this paper. ## Data collection and analysis

As part of the data collection process, we develope d a structured reflection protocol to ensure that teachers made their thinking visible and generated data relevant to their learning and the analysis phase. This incl uded prompts for identifying constructionist elements in activit ies and assessing the integration of the workshop w ith the makerspace and the local curriculum, as described i n the design and implementation section. Teachers d iscussed their responses with peers and recorded them in a digital mural. We also conducted systematic observat ions and took field notes to capture additional insights. Post-session reflections collected through online forms, which had a 50% response rate, asked teachers about their pri or knowledge, new learnings, and further inquiries. Additionally, we conducted in-depth interviews with two teachers using a semi-structured protocol to explore their teaching routines, school contexts, and expectations for the professional development (PD) program. Our analysis began with debriefs of the sessions fo cused on whether teachers met the learning goals through the workshop. During these meetings, we performed a thematic analysis of all data, including interviews, teacher materials, notes, and reflections. Our team  defined some of these themes based on the main ten ets of Constructionism (e.g., “mistakes and debugging”). O ther themes relevant to the PD also emerged from th e data, based on what participants said and did (e.g., “the  collaborative construction o f pedagogical activities” and “understanding how heterogeneous group work might b e generative for Constructionist activities”). Thro ughout these debriefing sessions and by using the identifi ed themes, our team noticed how and to what extent our PD activities support building shared meaning (Cobb & Jackson,  2012) among educators for technology-based programs such as the one discussed here. We present our main learnings and reflections in the next section section. Lessons learned The key takeaway from this process underscores the importance of modeling activities, where teachers actively engage in hands-on experiences while participating in structured me tacognitive moments designed to foster reflection on their thinking and learning during the workshop. Reflections documented on a digital “mural” reveal how these activities helped teachers connect their lived experiences with their understanding of const ructionist theory and imagine how this could be applied in the ir classrooms. This process highlights the need to rethink traditional conceptions of learning, encouraging teachers to embrace active, reflective, and meaningful educational practices that put students at the center of knowle dge construction. The workshop elements and stru cture served as guiding principles, enabling teachers to reflect upon and design personal constructionist practices that integrate technology while centering students in their knowledge-building journeys. For instance, one teacher reflected on the value of  “learning by doing,” mirroring the engagement they sought to foster with their students. Rather than replicating the workshop directly, they emphasized how it inspired them to conceptualize activities that involved stud ents in “hard fun,” building knowledge tied to real-world problems and the curriculum. Group 13 shared how wo rking with the repurposing of “a technological obje ct known as the CD-ROM drive” enabled an acti vity anchored in a phenomenon and the mobilization of prior knowledge that helped them “reduce the fear” of eng aging with making and technology. They noted that “the group engaged in a creative effort and developed a prototype artifact of a lock system by reusing parts and components repurposed from the disassembled object, ” linking their work to both constructionist principl es and their cultural context as they found a solution and  new way to use loose parts, something already part  of their cultural repertoire in a context where resources ar e scarce, and repurposing is commonplace. These ref lections suggest how the workshop modeled constructionist pr inciples while encouraging teachers to critically e xamine how these practices could be implemented in their c lassrooms. Groups also highlighted the importance o f designing meaningful artifacts and engaging in coll aborative processes aligned with constructionist th eory and their local teaching contexts. For example, Group 5  reflected on “recreating something meaningful from trash,” emphasizing how this approach fosters a sensitivity  to design by identifying opportunities to address real-world issues within their local environment. Similarly, Group 10 underscored the role of artifact creation as a process of interaction and negotiation, opening pathways tonew possi bilities for group learning. They emphasized the importance of hypothesis generation and thinking ro utines—not only “touching, exploring, observing, and experimenting before creating,” but also visualizing the cognitive processes involv ed in making. Group 9 added another dimension, highlighting how constructionist  activities connect with generative themes and enco urage reflection on the systems, function, and repurposing of objects while integrating technology. They noted aligning these activities with municipal curriculum goals, including subjects like science, geography, and mathematics. By emphasizing the repurposing of everyday material s, the activities in this workshop foster interdisciplinary learning and environmental awaren ess. Building on this, Group 6 described how the ac tivity inspired them to create “constructionist moments” in their own classes, incorporating sustainable practices aligned with city-wide educational goals. In an interview with a participant teacher, she noted how she has become more aware of practices where students—both her own and those from other cohorts—are not actively engaged in their learning. She cited an example of teaching support material made available for maker lab teachers that  she considered excellent before the workshops. However, since the PD, she has been more critical of how much these instructional maker projects stimulate students’ thinking and learning. She mentioned still using some ideas from this material but adapting them to create more oppo rtunities for investigation, question generation by  students, and autonomous group work with the creation of acti vity and resource cards that engage students in col lective investigation, discussion, and creation.

Broader value Reflections from our professional development sessi ons highlight the value of this approach, where tea chers actively engage in the learning experiences they ai m to foster in students. This is followed by metaco gnitive moments that allow them to reflect on their own practices and consider how to co-design and implement principles present in the hands-on and minds-in workshops within their own contexts. This proces s can be instrumental in scaling public education programs, as teachers’ ins ights, connections, and classroom applications reveal how the presence of investigation, hypothesis-building, reflection, collaboration, personal connections, and metacognition helps teachers build shared knowledge and meaning. Key elements to drive educational network reform focus on technology integration to enhance systems thinking, investigation, making, and project-based learning schools.

## Conclusion

The successful implementation and sustainability of large-scale educational policies—particularly those involving technology adoption, curriculum integration, and, m ost importantly, profound pedagogical transformatio n— require multiple measures, with transf ormative professional development (PD) at the core.  This paper illustrates how an immersive, hands-on PD program can ignite teachers’ deep reflection and mindset shifts—key factors for sustaining large-scale reforms that promote systems thinking, inquir y, creativity, and project-based learning in schools. We view constructionist workshops, such as  the one described here, as “objects to think with”  (Papert, 1980), fostering reflection on curriculum alignment  in digital fabrication labs. The core idea of this  PD model is twofold: teachers engage in a constructionist learn ing experience while simultaneously rethinking thei r own teaching practices. Through both collective and ind ividual reflection, they develop new approaches for  their classrooms based on these personal experiences. By immersing teachers in workshops and supporting the implementation of their curriculum units, we observ ed an increased awareness about their current practices and new possibilities for enhancing student engagement in knowledge construction.
