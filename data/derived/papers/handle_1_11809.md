# Prototyping Possibility: Leveraging Afrofuturism in the Design of Justice-centered Future Educator Learning Environments

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores justice-centered sensemaking and practice among future educators within the context of a redesigned future educator technology course at a Primarily White Institution (PWI) that brings together sociocultural theories of learning with Afrofuturist principles of learning and design. Specifically, this paper highlights justice-centered educator reflection and practice or Afrofuturist pedagogical prototypes within course final projects. These prototypes embody learning around four main Afrofuturist principles: justice-centered, future-oriented, tech-mediated, and collective. Findings from this study point to the importance of tech-mediated co-design with youth for the imagination and enactment of more just learning futures and technologies.

## Introduction

With an increasing number of students of color in s chools and a  largely white teaching population, we in the learning sciences (have and still) need to be think ing about how we study and design more inclusive and justicecentered learning environments for our young people  and their future educators (NASEM, 2024).  Afrofuturism as an aesthetic, pedagogy, and sensibility for desi gn, offers a critically innovative lens to study and craft justicecentered learning for all educators (Cann, 2016; Faison & McArthur, 2020; Love, 2019). An Afrofuturist approach to learning and learning design means looking at ho w race, institutions and oppression impact reality and knowledge, but also how, despite this, Black communities and other communities of color, reimagine and reinvent more just realities and futures (Baumann et al., 2018; Cortez et al., 2022; Holbert et al., 2020; McKoy & Cortez, 2022).

In this paper, I  investigate the conjecture that teacher learning ecologies organize d around Afrofuturist key principles and sociocultural theories of learni ng afford educators opportunities to iteratively im agine and enact pedagogical “prototypes” or critically innova tive tools, sensemaking, and practices for more jus t learning futures. Here, I point to an Afrofuturist orientation of prototyping that is more than a  process of technological design, rather it’s an iterative, intergenerational process of crea ting new models for tools, practices, selves, and futures of our freedom dreams (Baumann et al., 2018; Cortez & McKoy, 2022). This conjecture was embodied in the design of a future educator course on learning technologies, in which preservice educators were invited to create prototypes grounded in four main Afrofuturist principles, including justice-centered, future-oriented, techmediated, and collective; these principles also undergirded the design of t he course. Guided by a  social design experiment (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016) approach, this  paper examines evidence of these learning guides i n students’ final course projects. The research quest ion for this paper is: How do final design products  or educator “prototypes” reflect the four key Afrofuturist lear ning guides of the course (i.e. justice, futures, t echnology, and collective)?

## Background

This research builds from theories of learning that understand it as situated in the social and cultural activities that people engage in with one another towards particular shared goals and that are mediated by the cultural tools and artifacts people create and adapt (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003; Nasir et al., 2020; Vygotsky & Cole, 1978; Wertsch, 1991). Further, as is the case for work that marks a sociopolitical turn in Vygotsky-inspired scholarship, this work foregrounds learning and learning design in nondominant communities’ everyday experiences, practices, and tools (Nasir et al., 2014), especially as they organize t oward new futures and alternative ways to be and become (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016; Gutiérrez et al., 2019; Mirra & Garcia, 2020). This study extends current learning sciences resear ch by contributing to a small but growing body of literature that studies and employs Afrofuturism in learning and learning design context within higher education. Of the studies that leverage Afrofuturism as a lear ning and design lens, most focus on youth K-12 (after) school and summer programs to combat narrow, controlling notions of STEM and STEM identities (Dando et al., 2019), support a critical re visioning of the future (Toliver, 20 20), leverage techn ology and science as forms of socio-political resistance (Capers, 2019), and leverage expertise across contexts and disciplines (Dando et. al, 2019; Holbert et al., 2020).  Yet, there is an increasing interest in exploring A frofuturism within higher education contexts (Grue, 2020), particularly for pre-service educators (Cann, 2016). Afrofuturism can su pport future educators, particularly white educators, to better understand how race, oppression and deficit-thinking manifest in learning and how can they challenge and (re)imagine these constraints (Cann, 2016; Grue, 2020). An Afrofuturist framework for design Afrofuturist scholars have highlighted its epistemo logical intersections of (collective) imagination, technology, the future, and liberation, as well as its roles and possibilities in turning theory, imagination, and  literature into action (Womack, 2013). Inspired by these four epist emologies and a propensity for action, I integrated Afrofuturist principles and aesthetics into the des ign of an undergraduate course for future educators. I frame these principles and aesthetics as four design guides: justice-centered, future-oriented, technology-mediated, and collective. The following is a deeper dive into each of the guides that I conjecture are necessary for more liberatory learning futures and underscore the of structure the course. Justice-centered Borrowing from Sci-fi and speculative fiction, the concept of Afrofuturism parallels the plight of Africandiasporic people as we weather colonialism, capitalism, and technologies that continue to implicate Black and Brown bodies (Dery, 1994). Afrofuturism began mainly in literary contexts, but has since expanded into art, film, music, as well as learning and design to describe how nondominant communities endure lasting constraint by persistent ly reimagining and repurposing tools, practices, and spaces to protect, heal, and support communities of color (Eshun, 2003; Womack, 2013; Yaszek, 2006). Afrofuturism understands necessary justice work in exploring how race and diverse cultural experiences affect pedago gical reality, knowledge, and design  (Bell, 1992). It also recognizes how—in spite of racism—communities of color across the world continue to  reimagine and reinvent alternative worlds, technologies, and practices toward collective liberation (Baumann et al., 2018; Capers, 2019; Toliver, 2024). In the context of teacher education, an Afrofuturist justice-centered approach invites educators to specify the futures, technologies and collective relationships educators could enact toward the aim of freedom for us all (Cann, 2016; Love, 2019). In the course, future educators evaluat ed historic and current structures, tools, and practices  in teaching and learning, reflecting on how such educational systems have repeatedly  harmed communities of color, and  importantly, how educators may take steps to individually and collectively imagine and work towards just alternative ways to be and become. (Black) Future-oriented In Afrofuturism, looking to the past and across our  present is also looking towards the future (Yaszek, 2006). Understanding futures as dynamic rather than fixed, Afrofuturism propels  opportunity for Black and Brown agency in creating our futures rather than see ing them as something forced upon us or that excludes u s (Eshun, 2013). Afrofuturism grounds this fluidity, this ability to look back as if looking forward, as Sankofa, a t erm appropriated from its Ghanian proverbial origins to  best mean “to profit in the present from experienc es of the past to prosper in the future” (Jorgensen,  2001, p.120-121). Afrofuturism, then, helps us understand the h armful representation or absence of Black and Brown people  in dominant visions of the future.  Yet, through the notion of Sankofa, Afrofuturism  paints futures as flexible —we can write ourselves into futures, but they are always grounded in Black history and cultural tradition (Eshun, 2013; Yaszek, 2006). Guided by Afrofuturism’s dynamic futures, students in the course and I explored futures as something educators can change and more importantly, have the responsibility to jointly imagine and move towards equity and justice for all their students. Technology-mediated Dery (1994) indicated that Afrofuturism takes in s peculative visions that address African Americandi asporic concerns in the context of 20th century technoculture, and, more generally, African  American signification that appropriates images of technology and a prosthetically enhanced future. Afrofuturism, then, is a subject mediated by the fields of science and technology, borrowing language from their discourse and practices, to bot h critique but also reimagine our world in service of more jus t futures. Here, Afrofuturism offers a lens for s pecifically thinking about concerns in our everyday techno-rich environments, the role of technology itself (i n reimagining these environments), and why approaching these conc erns matter --a need for free and just futures. In the Afrofuturist tradition, prototyping is mo re than a process of technological design, rather i t’s an iterative intergenerational process of creating new models fo r the just tools, practices, selves, and futures of  our dreams (Baumann et al., 2018; Cortez & McKoy, 2022). Specifically, prototyping in  an Afrofuturist context is about developing both material (e.g., texts, curricular m aterials, digital media) and immaterial technologie s (e.g., theories, design, pedagogical practice) for new just futures that begins with a critical speculative look at our world, asking “what if'” and “what otherwise” of these con strained pasts and presents (Bauman et al., 2018) s o that we might iteratively and collectively design models for alternative just and equitable ways to be. Guided by Afrofuturism’s critically expansive view of technology and how we jointly prototype or design for them toward more just ends, the course design supports expanding an understandi ng of technology as moving past tools that we can plug in such as compu ters or robots, but as both physical and immaterial technologies grounded in the historic and everyday cultural practices of Black and Brown communities s uch as music (funk, hip-hop, jazz), fashion (braiding/hairstyling), pop culture (Black Panther, Iwaju) or freedom dreams (Abolition X podcasts) that deter and/or help us mo ve towards more just futures. In this way, the enti re course aimed to support educators in critically evaluating everyday technologies for how they might  harm communities of color (especially in education), but also, as th e final projects explore,  how we might reimagine and design for/with technologies toward the futures of our dreams. Collective Afrofuturism reminds us that freedom dreaming towar ds more just futures is seldom an individual  practice, and rather is a community endeavor with diverse l earners and expertise (Baumann, 2018, Holbert et. a l., 2020). Afrofuturism, specifically when leveraged with whit e pre-service educators can help them better access conversations on race, racism, and their role in im agining towards alternative and more just ways to b e and become. In this vein, the critical, yet speculative nature of Afrofuturism he lps white educators evaluate nondominant experience with enough distance to be r eflective, but close enough to realize their role i n reifying or changing the direction future s (Cann, 2016; Ellis & Donaldson, 2018)  Afrofuturism, then, helps support a collective examination and reimagination of our world with a variety of learners, but always with a responsibility to build towards futures where we all, particularly  marginalized communities, are free. In course desi gn, this meant privileging Black and Brown nondominant histories, experiences, and practices as foundational knowledge part in dreaming up and concretizing more just and equitable futures while simultaneously supporting white students in realizing their necessary  part. This involved creating numerous opportunities for future educators, through their final projects discussed here, to work with y outh, fellow educators, and community members to collectively surface, reimagine and combat issues t hat harm  communities of color through the design of pedagogical prototypes.

## Study context

The context for this paper was a Spring 2024 education technology course at a public university that is a Predominantly White Institution (PWI); like many preservice courses, the student population consisted of mostly white femmes. When deliberating my approach to teaching the course, I drew on social design-based experiment literature (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016)  to facilitate a learning space where future educators specifically explored justice-centered issues in learning. Social  design-based experiments allow for studying how educator participation, learning, relationships and sensemak ing transform over time, particularly through tool-mediated interventions (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016), an importa nt design feature for my work  and in Afrofuturism which deeply evaluates the role of tools and technologies  in supporting the joint creation of just new futur es (Dery, 1994). Here, this study might be best understood as a  social-design based experiment inspired  collective case study (Merriam, 1998) where I  make sense of learning as a result of multiple desi gns, tools, and participation structures within the (re)design and implementation  of this 15 week undergraduate education course but do  not yet focus on multiple iterations of that curriculum, although iteration is the ongoing goal of this work. This paper specifically elucidates future educator learning at the culmination o f the course, specifically in students’ final projects with young people.

Data sources and analysis The Mothership Connection Projects were culminating co-design projects in the course. The assignment  asked teams of 3-4 undergraduates and their youth partners to design  a technology that would address a justice issue. Future educators and youth took the lead in decidin g the justice issue they would address, the technologies they create, how this technology would work, the world in which this technology would live, and how they design for this technology. The projects took place over four weeks, including two weeks for future educators in the course to plan for designing with youth, a 1 –2-hour joint designing session with young people, and  a final time for reflection on their crafted technologies and design sessions. For this paper, I analyzed artifacts and videos rel ated to three Mothership Connection final projects. In particular, I analyzed all artifacts (n=21) and vid eo/audio (n=14) generated by future educators and youth throughout their collaboration on final projects to include Google Docs, PowerPoints,  digital whiteboards, photos/sketches, Zoom recordings, and voice memos. Since this paper explores how educator learning is reflected in the course’s final projects along the four learning gui des, I coded each data source for how they illustra te the four Afrofuturist learning guides, namely justice-centered, tech-mediated, futures-oriented, and collective.  The prototypes crafted during the Mothership Connection projects varied greatly —from a youth-designed in-school social media app to a public vending machine for ne urodiverse and dis/ability accessibility supports. I highlight one of the prototypes, a three-character interactive narrative, in the findings section of this paper as an ideal case because it embodies the notion of an Afrofuturist p rototype that helps imagine new and alternative jus t ways to be.

## Findings: A prototype for new futures in ‘Fix-It Fics’

The findings section of this paper explores one of three Mothership Connection final projects, an imagined storyworld around three characters: Willow, Arnold, and Shmee. This narrative prototype was designed by three undergraduate future educators in the course and th eir youth partners during the final project s. The joint session concluded with coding the beginnings of  a combined narrative around these characters into Twine—an open-source tool for telling interactive, game-like nonlinear stories—in order to bring their stories to life. To address the research question, how do the final projects reflect the four Afrofutu rist learning guides ?, I analyzed this narrative prototype and its co-design, paying attention to how it reflects the four learning guides. Descriptions of the characters Afrofuturism inspires  these character driven narratives  through how they reimagine and center characters that challenge dominant representation of nondominant folx and communities, situate these narratives in both real and speculative contexts that elucidate constraints communities of color face and how they navigate them, and lastly, present a complex relationship with technology and futures that both helps and harms nondominant communities. As part of their prototype, future educators and youth introduced their three characters in the form of a persona, a design tool used in the course. Below is a narrative synthesis of their characters, Willow, Arnold, and Shmee, coconstructed over the course of their time with youth. First, Arnold’s story exists in a world very much like our  own, crafted to tell the story of a person who often feels “invisible” in society. For this group,  it was important that Arnold’s story highlighted t he complex lives and histories of everyday people, particularl y people of color, as they navigate personal, commu nity, and societal belonging.

Meet Arnold, a young boy with lots of feelings that stay firml y inside his chest. Arnold, who just moved to a new US city, doesn’t really feel li ke he belongs. Growing up with a Brazilian mom and French-Canadiandad, his daily life involves speaking many languages and engaging in diverse cultural practices that honor both sides of his heritage, yet he feels like he doesn’t fit in anywhere or to any one culture. His loneliness g ets worse when his sibling passes away in a car accident. Now, the only time he feels less alone is when he posts his new fashion finds and creations to his social media. He finds that remaki ng and remixing fashion from everyday sustainable materials gives him purpose and meaning—that and his thousands of Insta followers who have become his most supportive and encouraging community. Next, Willow ’s story  is an attempt to center a complex femme villain, a narrative that counters stereotyped portrayals of femme and non-white characters in everyday media. This story aims to address an important question in Afrofuturism around nondominant representation: Who actually gets to be evil? Meet Willow, your typical teenage villain, except when she’s n ot. Willow is an average high schooler who lives in Evilsburg (formerly New Jersey) and has lots of friends. She misses her dad, though she’d never admit it—another mission to inconvenience the world the first thing on his to-do list this morning. See, Willow is in training to be the most powerful evil villain in the world, however that title currently goes to her dad, who single-handedly transformed the world they now live in. Evilsburg is the new capital of the universe, and Willow wants to be the most powerful human to ever exist. To do this, however, she’s been told by her father she must work on her evilness as she’s known for letting it falte r every now and then. So, there you have it, a whole story about a complicated “evil-ish” villain with a name that doesn’t help her street cred. Willow.

Lastly, in contrast to the first two human character-driven stories, Shmee’s  story tells of a non-human non-binary entity and their struggles with an increasin gly difficult future environment. This story  specifically leverages the speculative to connect our present with an increasingly disastrous future. Meet Shmee, cloud waterer. Shmee is a nonbinary platypus —they/them pronouns please — whose day starts every morning with watering the cl ouds. Shmee’s been tasked with watering the clouds so that it might rain because it doesn’t  really rain so much anymore. When it rains, though, all the city’s great potholes fill up with rainwater and citi zens can swim across the puddles; commutes are a breeze! But when it doesn’t  rain, everyone in the city, including Shmee, is busy trying to learn how to fly planes so  they won’t have to  worry about traversing potholes anymore. And when Shmee takes a break from  watering and flying and swimming they like to collect pieces of interstate, relics of a time and place before themselves. To Shmee, their life seems like one big contradiction.

(Session Videos and Notes) Justice-centered Afrofuturism situates necessary justice work in critically evaluating our world, particularly for how communities of color are oppressed and erased in daily life, yet, in spite of this, working to reimagine and create towards more just ways to be. While the descriptions of characte rs above might not immediately reveal themselves as  justicecentered, looking deeper into the design and complexities of this prototype, namely the narrative genre and join t character creation process, challenges this thinking.

The three-narrative prototype showcased above was named “The Fix-it Fics” by the group. In reflection on their final projects, undergraduate  future educators explained they were inspired to create this prototy pe after reading a text in the course entitled “Video Games as Ideological Projects” by Kishonna Gray (2014) (Final Reflection). Students were struck by Gray’s exploration of racial and gender justice within the context of everyday technology and her argument that narratives, rules, and designs  embedded within games  reflect the values and desires of their creators. Students’ reflection also delved into learning that  popular games and game narratives such as Grand Theft Auto, Tomb Raider, Street Fighter, and Call of Duty showcase the designers’ specific affinity for sexism, violence, and centering whiteness  and white masculinity (Gray, 2014). They explained that this text helped them see the power of narrative, most importantly, ho w race and gender could be intentionally and harmfully represented or erased in popular media, a nd moreover, inspired them to create a final prototype that attempts to challenge or reimagine these harmful narratives (Final Reflection). To illustrate, one group member, AJ, suggested the “fix-it fic,” as a popular narrative genre in fanfiction that aims to correct or reimagine a story, usually through a perspective that is less well represented  in dominant narratives (Final Reflection). This group directly named an objective for their final prototype to “ reimagine an existing character from popular media or create an original character in a way that pulls away from st ereotypical lenses portrayed in popular media”  (Final Group Presentation). E xpressing frustration with the ‘flat’ and stereotypical characters they saw in popular media, this group aimed to use their fix-it fic to “return control  and design of a narrative to readers” (i.e. youth partn ers) who hold many marginalized identities and (Sma ll group Video–AJ and youth).  Thus, leveraging Gray’s text on the critical, yet i maginative power of narrative, and subsequently designing their final project to reimagine problematic characters/narratives that limi t or erase nondominant folx, this group beg an the Afrofuturist justice-centered work of critically evaluating the world in order to imagine it anew.

(Black) Futures-oriented Although the stories of Willow, Shmee and Arnold seem to take place in alternative future(s), these futures are not untethered to real constraints that Black and Brown  communities face and work to navigate, a necessary orientation in Afrofuturism.

Arnold’s story exists in a world very much like our own, crafted t o tell the story of a person who often feels “some conflict about his true culture”. Arnol d’s story, “who’s mother is from Brazil, Dad is fro m Canada, but lives in the United States” highlights  the complex lives and histories of everyday people,  particularly people of Color (POC)  (Session Video), as they navigate personal, communi ty, and societal belonging across borders (both physical and virtual). His story is an Afrofuturist consideration of diasporic belonging and the role of digital technologies in bringing us together.

In contrast, Shmee’s future seems the most alternative, located in a time and place completely unlike our own. In a future where clouds must be watered to ma ke rain, our best modes of transportation are swimm ing in giant potholes or flying, and interstates are relics of an obsolete past, Shmee’s story speaks to a future that affects not just Black and Brown communities but all of us.  This future is riddled with contradiction, where s olutions to problems only seem to cause more problems. This gro up remarked in reflection that although Shmee’s story seemed “so farfetched it sometimes became funny”, this story actually reflected “the state of the world in a lot of ways” (Final Reflection). Here, this story leverages the speculative to connect our  present with an increasingly disastrous future  to highlight issues (like climate change) we all exper ience today. This story also calls into question current solutions to current issues, cauti oning us to think about whether they are even solut ions at all. Employing Afrofuturism’s Sankofa, which asks us to consider the past and present as we reimagine and work towards the futures of our dreams, this story asks us if we will indeed be like Shmee: stuck in a cycl e of creation and/or harm?

Technology-mediated Future educators  mentioned in their project reflections, that Afrofu turism as explored throughout our course helped them eventually understand narratives as imp ortant technologies that can both help and hurt peo ple of color in imaging and enacting the more just futures of our dreams (Final Reflection). Consequently, they decided to jointly redesign narratives with youth as prototypes for alternative futures that challenge deficit representation of nondominant communities. Here, narrative is used as a technology that supports educators and youth in jointly imagining alternative and just ways to be. Moreover, they created these narratives to be coded into Twine, a user driven, interactive text-based experience that would allow a reader to contr ol the narrative rather than being told how the story should unfold. The students said they wanted to input these stories into Twine specifically to “return control of narratives back to readers” (Final Group Presentation) in ways that we don’t often get to do in popular media. Thus, the reimagined narratives coupled with its coding in Twin e supports an Afrofuturist technological experience that is both immaterial and material, but all importantly aimed at resituating power towards the stories and voices of nondominant communities.

With the right modifications, I could turn hurricanes into weapons of destruction or create droughts to hold entire nations hostage”. Her words send a chill down your spine, the implications of her ambitions hitting you with full force.

(“ The Convention” in Twine) In this excerpt from the Twine interactive narrative, the three characters—Willow, Shmee and Arnold— and their futures become connected through the use of a weather controlling device. Willow’s desire to  control the weather device as a weapon to “hold entir e nations hostage,” foreshadows Shmee’s future, where the lack of water and deep erosion of the earth has caused very strange and difficult living conditions for beings in the future. But what’s also important about the unfolding of th is joint narrative in  Twine is that Willow hasn’t used the weather device yet. So, this narrative prototype, t hrough its use of the  speculative and interactive coding into Twine, gives participants the opportunity to change the future, embodying the core of collective Afrofuturist work. Here, the story, particularly through the last line, makes it clear that Willow’s decision around this  device is connected to the future Shmee lives in, and it is up to the participant to decide if the story moves toward a desolate future or can possibly change. Thus, in designing t his Twine prototype to center the connected futures  of the characters as well as how these futures can be altered by a decision made by the characters in this current moment, signifies the power of collective action in enacting more just futures. Specifically, this prototype exemplifies that we need each other, across space, time, and racial community to make choices now that will create better futures for us all.

## Discussion and conclusion

Over the course of this paper I have explored how Afrofuturism, particularly through four core principles, justicecentered, future-oriented, tech-mediated, and collective, supports future educators  in more justice-centered learning. From the exploration of one group’s final  prototype, “Fix-it fics”, we can see the origins of important justice work along these four guides, particularly in their impulses to design narratives that combat problemat ic stereotypes in popular media, create story worlds a nd futures that are deeply tethered to each other a nd the real historical and current issues that Black and Brown communities face, and ultimately, their expansive u se of technology to both critique and reimagine more just  futures. Students in the course remarked that these projects were extremely helpful for their learning overall and in future runs of the course, should take center stage to allow for robust opportunity to jointly develop and iterate on prototypes with y outh and community members. Ultimately, the A frofuturist redesign of the course, particularly around the joint  development of pedagogical prototypes, offers new windows into the design of a justice-centered future educator learning environment that is offered within the context of PWI university-based teacher education, a necessary step for creating more liberatory learning environments for all our future students.
