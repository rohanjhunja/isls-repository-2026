# Find It First, Don’t Cheat, Make Your Code Useful: Norming “Novelty” in Early Childhood Computer Science

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As young children now regularly learn to code in early childhood learning environments, computer science is becoming a new context for establishing social norms. Not only must children learn new disciplinary concepts (e.g., “code,” “loop,” “debug”), but they also collaboratively define what is proper conduct for creating code, what makes “good” code, and how to act and think like coders. This paper explores how children produce a sociocomputational expectation to create novel code. Through qualitative analysis of interactions in a second-grade classroom, we focus on three small groups who created novel solutions to integrated math-and-coding problems using a tangible programming robot. We found that while they were learning how to use a loop command to repeat units of code, children were conversely trying not to repeat the programs of their peers and other groups. We argue that this is reflective of innovation norms inherent in broader tech culture.

## Introduction

The social process of establishing disciplinary nor ms has been of interest to the learning sciences fo r some time (Rosebery et al., 1992; Yakel & Cobb, 1996). What constitutes legitimized and acceptable disciplinary activity is shaped by socially constructed goals  and values, which are continually formed (i.e., nor med) through discourse and social interaction. As such, disciplinary practice and the norms and beliefs about a discipline are reflexively related. Take the example of a practice important for engaging in science, like scientific argumentation. According to perspectives on disciplinary norming, to produce  valid scientific arguments, one must make claims in a way that conforms to the currently acceptable goals, be liefs, suppositions, and assumptions, of participan ts in the scientific activity. In this scenario, a scientific  argument is not valid or legitimate in and of itse lf; it becomes legitimized through a social process of establishing a “taken-as-shared sense” (Yakel & Cobb, 1996, p. 461) of what counts as appropriate contributions, a process known as norming. As young children now regularly learn to code in ea rly childhood learning environments, computer science is becoming a new context for establishing social norms. Not only must children as young as pr eschoolage learn new disciplinary concepts (e.g., “code, ” “loop,” “debug”), but they also collaboratively d efine what is proper conduct for creating code, what makes “good”  code, and how to act and think like coders. This p aper explores how children new to coding produce and inculcate a norm of “newness,” or a sociocomputational expectation to create novel code. Through qualitative analysis of children’s interact ions in a second-grade classroom, we focus on two groups who created novel solutions to math problems using a tangible programming robot and discuss findings in terms of a sociotechnical value for novelty that exists in broader tech culture.

## Framing perspectives on social dynamics of young children learning to code

We drew on three primary bodies of literature to inform our analysis: empirical studies of disciplinary norms in the learning sciences; emerging research on social interactions in (early childhood) tangible programm ing contexts; and sociotechnical perspectives on innovation and novelty in tech culture. Learning disciplinary norms Previous studies highlight how participating in mat h and science activities construct not only student s’ learning of domain specific knowledge and concepts, but also  disciplinary norms and practices in math (Hershikowitz & Schwarz, 1999; Sfard, 2007; Yakel & Cobb, 1996) and  in science (DeLiema et al., 2019; Herrenkohl, 2006; Stroupe, 2014). According to researchers of discipl inary norming, constructivist framings on activity are insufficient—we need sociological perspectives on activity that account for the social dynamics of learning (Yakel & Cobb, 1996). In their work on how elementary stud ents establish norms for what constituted mathemati cally different solutions, Yakel and Cobb (1996) found th at “sociomathematical norms are intrinsic aspects o f the classroom’s mathematical microculture” (p. 474). Norms for what counts as good mathematical practice are baked into the classroom culture and social interactions.  Sfard (2007) referred to sociomathematical norms a s “metadiscursive rules” and noted that “they are rarely made explicit and are usually learned from examples rather than from general verbal prescriptions” (p. 573). Research has begun to explore how novel integrated STEM and computer science learning designs provide new contexts for establish ing sociomathematical norms (e.g., Dickes et al., 2020). However, there are not yet many studies of how these norms relate to particular computational contexts (e.g., app-based or tangible programming), nor what is specifi cally socio-technical about establishing norms for computer science practice. In other words, we need studies that specifically focus on sociocomputational norms in K-12 learning environments, and it is this gap that the current study seeks to address (see Fong et al., 2023).

Social dimensions of coding robots Tangible programming represents the computational context of this study, and we draw on a body of research on the social aspects of learning to use coding robots. Papert’s (1980) innovative approach to developing  tangible robots for children to build interconnected spatial-mathematical-computational knowledge was rooted in constructionism, yet also spoke to the sociotechnic al dimensions of learning with new technologies (Tu rkle & Papert, 1992). According to this view, learning to think computationally is bound up with social interactions and social values that govern the how and why (and who)  of programming. A few recent accounts of how child ren develop computational thinking through tangible programming have emphasized social and interactional practices like teacher scaffolding (Wang et al., 202 0), promoting play (Bers, 2020), and caring relation ships (Silvis et al., 2022b). Kafai and colleagues (2019) called for putting c ognitive framings of computational thinking in dialogue with more socially situated and  critical accounts, advocating for a balance “of pro moting basic programming competence and understanding… with understanding ho w these skills can be used both for personal/social enrichment and to address issues within the world a t-large” (p, 104). We build from this expansive frami ng of computational thinking and also interrogate how the world at-large is increasingly impacted by a tech culture with its own norms that may be implicitly shaping computer science learning.

## Sociotechnical perspectives on innovation

In this study, we connect to broader concerns in te ch culture related to novelty, innovation, and “rei nvention” (Suchman, 2011). Sociotechnical theories of the rol e of technology in society have provided rich insig ht into the hubris of the incessant drive for novelty or a “cult of the new” (Deb & Homes, 2015). In their tre atise on the “innovation delusion,” Vinsel and Russel (2020) critiqued the ways in which tech culture—and Silicon Valley in particular—has created and sustained a desire for newness that  spreads through society, driving consumption, continuous production of material goods, and deplet ion of natural resources. According to Suchman (201 1), critiquing this “endless churning” (p. 11) is part of a feminist politics of technology that resists “ the universalization of novelty as a good” (p. 14) and instead promotes reusing prior materials, building from marginalized traditions, and amplifying others’ ide as as opposed to overwriting them with novel ideas.  In this paper, we show how young children are  already practicing and propagating norms of novelty  by socially constructing expectations to produce more code, produce it fast, and product it first. We share Suchman’s (2011) “interest in ways of theorizing change, breaks, ruptures, and the new that do not rely on singular origins, definite moments of invention, or trajectories of progressive development” (p. 11). These perspectives inform the ways in which we analyzed children’s development of sociocomputational norms while learning to code.

## Methods and data

This study adopted interaction analysis (Jordan & H enderson, 1995) to analyze the interactions among s mall groups of students using coding materials in an elementary classroom setting in the Rocky Mountain West region of the US. This study is a part of a l arger design-based research project that focuses on learning tra jectories of spatial mathematical thinking and computational thinking in early elementary grades. For the current analysis, we drew on three hour-long integrated mathematics-coding lessons tha t were video-recorded; one camera captured the whole class and other cameras recorded each of 3 small focal groups, resulting in ten hours of vid eo. Small groups worked in various locations around the class room where there were dry-erase writable surfaces, such as the floor with a mat, tables, and windowsills. Twen ty-five students participated in the lessons (9 total in the 3 focal groups; 4 males, 5 females). All focal studen ts were Caucasian, as was the classroom teacher, wh o had previous experience researching early childhood computational thinking using robot coding toys.

## Computational context and curriculum design

The three lessons analyzed in this study were desig ned to build upon repeated addition to teach multip licative thinking with a tangible programming robot called B otley 2.0. Students program Botley by entering code s in a remote control. Tasks were designed for students to program Botley to move along an open number line (created with a dry-erase marker on a writeable surface) using only two codes: the Forward arrow and the Loop command. For Botley 2.0, including a Loop within a program r equires two Loop codes, one on either end of the un it to be repeated, which in these lessons were the number of  Forward movements (Figure 1). As the children came t o understand it, Botley’s Loop codes surround a group of Forward arrows and then “lock in” those codes as the function, which can then be repeated a number of times depending on how many sequential Loop codes are used. After exploring how the Loop code operated as a whole group, their teacher Dr. H instructed children to find different ways of programming Botley to move Forward 8 moves using only the Forward arrow and the Loop command. Children were assigned to work in groups of three and instructed to rotate between three roles: a “robot technician,” who handled the robot; a “programmer,”  who wrote the program on a writable surface such a s a tabletop or window ledge and then transcribed correct solutions onto the group’s paper; and a “remote controller,” who entered the codes into Botley’s remote (see Silvis et al., 2022b). Individual groups worked in separate areas of the classroom and were often directed to reassemble to discuss particular groups’ solutions that Dr. H deemed important for students’ learning, a pedagogical pra ctice we call a “whole class call-back demo.” Dr. H  also rewarded unique program(er)s by having groups write  new solutions they devised up on the whiteboard at the front of the classroom. On the third day of the “different ways of making 8” lesson sequence, students were given pre-written list of eleven different pr ograms of increasing difficulty  (these were called “levels”) and were instructed to (a) write the mathematical notation c orresponding to the codes and (b) predict whether t he program would make Botley move 8 or not. Groups were approved to move to the next program (i.e., le vel) once their mathematical notation had been checked by a teacher.

## Analytic approach

Nine hours of video-recorded lessons were first reviewed and content lo gged (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). We then conducted a series of group video viewing sess ions of video recordings of 3 lessons implemented w ith 3 different groups of children. We identified a salie nt pattern related to how children were developing novel programs, and we thematically grouped instances tha t represented three forms of norming novelty that e merged from analysis: finding solutions first; (not) cheating; and making solutions count. We focus the following analysis on examples of each of these forms and show how groups of children and their teacher collaboratively established norms for what counted as “good” and valid programs. The microanalysis of their talk-in-interaction illustrates how sociocomputational practices involve producing novel algorithms and how novelty is socially and technically constructed. We then discuss ways in which these fi ndings represent broader values in tech culture and also how they reproduce-and diverge from-established norms for learning sociomathematical practice.

Example 3: Making programs count Fred’s group seemed to set the bar for creating novel solutions, getting them demoed, or being rewarded by writing their solutions up on the board. Yet Fred’s group was not immune from the pressure to create novel programs. Dr. H’s ultimate assessment, that “technically they are the same thi ng” (line 21) summarized what all children were implicitly attempting to avoid throughout Day 1 and Day 2 as t hey developed novel programs: sameness and repetition. Next, we discuss technical dimensions of sameness and how the value for computational difference was socially constructed as children established a norm of novelty in coding. Norming novelty in computer science and in tech culture This series of examples illustrates how children we re collaboratively establishing what counted as “good” and valid solutions to a computational problem. For Natalie’s group, this meant being the first to come up with a cool solution, one that was novel because it would “amaze” the class. For Diane’s group, this meant coming up with a unique solution, one that had not been thought of b efore. For Fred’s group, this meant coming up with a useful solution, one that utilized codes that served a pur pose. Each of these criteria conformed to a differe nt notion of their teacher’s prompt that they come up with “different” ways of making Botley move 8. These criteria were not explicitly stated as expectations from the outset, but instead arose through the process of learning h ow to use the Loop code. Furthermore, what qualified as “cool,” “unique,” and “useful” was defined through the children’s and teacher’s talk-in-interaction. For them, the process of teaching and learning to use the Loop code was interwoven with the social dynamics enacted while coding, whe reby children negotiated with one another and their  teacher what met the requirements of an appropriately novel  solution. In this way, learning how to program wit h Loops produced a sociocomputational norm of novel coding.

We understand this norming process in terms of orig inal findings related to socio mathematical norms but also as departing from these in important ways. In both Yakel and Cobb’s (1996) study of sociomathematical norms, and in our analysis here, establishing different solutions was paramount. Mathematical difference is  an important disciplinary concept, and one that is eme rging in early elementary grades. Our findings with  secondgrade students illustrate that computational difference is also an important concept; just as in math, it is important for novice coders to develop computer algorithms th at differ from one another. Another area of overlap  between socio-mathematical and socio-computational norms is that the practice of publicly presenting a solution is a vital part of knowledge sharing in the disciplines (Strou p, 2014). Yakel and Cobb showed how part of establi shing disciplinary practices is norming the notion of whe n it is appropriate to contribute to discussion. Ou r analysis demonstrates how one answer to this question is “on ly when you have a mathematically or computationall y different solution.” Given these requirements for establishing difference and only contributing when your solution is actually different, the question for children became learnin g what counts as different so that they could contr ibute in appropriate ways. As Yakel and Cobb found, and our findings reiterate, “issues concerning what counts as different, sophisticated, efficient, and elegant so lutions involve a taken-as-shared sense of when it is appropriate to contribute to a discussion” (p. 461). While math ematical solutions may  be more highly valued if they are sophisticated, efficient, or elegant, our findings show that, for computer science-math integrated activities like those we designed for children to learn to use Loops, solutions alsoneeded to be interesting (i.e., cool), ethical (i.e., not copied), and useful. How learners produc e this “taken-as-shared sense” constitutes the process of norming, and as for all social norms, these expecta tions or rules are implicitl y defined, not explicitly stated. By attending to multimodal interactions of children learning to use the loop code—rather than simply deferring to the simple prompt stated by the teacher —we were able to surface the class’s process for the  ongoing establishment of a taken-as-shared sense of computational difference. Furthermore, children were learning about what counts as computationally different even as th ey were working to constitute what counted as different. For example, when Dr. H refused to accept Fred’s group’s solution as different because of the requirement that novel codes be useful, he was teaching about a significant distinction between mathematical and computational principles. If we take Fred’s proposed solution LFFLFFFFL and translate it into mathematical notation—2+2+4+0—we see that the “useless” loop code at the end, in fact has the potential to stand in for a very useful mathematica l numeral: zero. No one would argue that a zero is useless; however, as Dr. H explained to the group, this extra code is “technic ally” useless. We can take Dr. H’s words literally; what he is helping them establish as a g roup, is that extra codes do not serve a technical function in a program. Like a zero in mathematics, extraneous cod es literally do not count! However, unlike in mathe matics, these novice coders demonstrate how novelty in computer science is defined in part by the expectation that code serves a purpose.

We see this implicit value for the purpose of code as reflective of values embedded in tech culture more broadly, where new devices, new apps, or new algori thms are often judged against a standard of utility. We find Dr. H’s question of “uselessness” at the level of the co de related in some ways to critical questions we ou ght to ask about technology in society, such as: Is this just a new gadget, or does it actually fulf ill a serious need?  Do we need an app for everything?  Is the race to create and refine a lgorithms like those used in AI technologies serving a purpose that is ultimately necessary and useful? In terms of Natalie’s group’s value of “cool” codes that are new and different, we might ask: Do we need more “next, best things? ” What is the value of a new tool with different bells and whistles, new features or user experiences, when the prior features and experience s were serving well?  Finally, turning to the ethical question raised by Diane’s group  we can ask: How is the tech industry’s innovation pr essure implicitly compromising the integrity of designers or condoning a culture of cheating to get to the next level, to be the first to the finish line (or to the capital investment)? These critical questions about tech culture may seem like a leap f rom the mundane classroom experiences we explored h ere. However, as long as we teach computer science to yo ung children, we must recognize the ways in which y oung children are already norming computer science and h ow these norms both reflect cultural values and will be reflected in culture-to-come.

## Implications for teaching math-CS integrated activities

In conclusion, we offer some implications for teach ing in computer science learning environments that integrate coding and math. For one, we echo Yakel and Cobb (1996) that “the teacher’s reactions  to a child’s solution can be interpreted as an implicit indicator of how it i s valued mathematically” (p. 464), and we suggest t his is also true for how solutions are valued computationally. Two, while their original work on sociomathematical  norms did not emphasize how establishing how to contribut e mathematically different solutions inadvertently contributed to a competitive environment in t he classrooms they studied, we sensed this was some times the case in our research setting (or was the case for some c hildren). This is not to say that the majority of c hildren in Dr. H’s classroom were not highly motivated and engaged in coding by the design of the lessons (they were),  nor do we mean to imply that a sometimes-contentious atmosphere was experienced negatively b y children (we saw no evidence of this). Rather, we suggest that inherent  in the novelty norm is a spirit of competitiveness, and thi s conflicts with an ethic of cooperation that is at the heart of Dr. H’s pedagogical approach. Teachers must strike a balance between on the one hand, motivation to achi eve individual or small group goals and, on the oth er hand, cooperation towards the whole class goal. This is important, not only for children to learn how cooperation reflects disciplinary practice in computer science but also to moderate the dog-eat-dog mentality to “move fast and break things” (Taplin, 2017) associated with the contemporary technology industry. We also see opening s for establishing norms and learning processes that ampl ify what came before, rather than erase it and claim it as one’s own. We are encouraged by the frequent repetition of common solutions in this classroom. We found it interesting that eve n children who themselves had already identified a particular solution would sometimes forget this and would present the same solution as novel later in a lesson or on subsequent days. How can we reframe practices like “write it up on t he board” so that rather than getting there first or claiming an idea as one’s own, a goal could be to add tally marks next to a solution you like or agree with. Within this framing, a “cool” program is not one that has never been thought of, but becomes “cool” because you and your group also thought of it, as in “cool, me too.” Listening to the ways that children incorporate each other’s ideas into their own computational thinking process es involves looking and listening for these process es of sociocomputational normin g, and fostering environments where social values c an reciprocally develop with valued disciplinary knowledge.
