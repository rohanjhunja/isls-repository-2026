# Reconstructing vs Constructing Explanations: Interview Interactions and Shifts in Epistemic Roles

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Constructing explanations is one of the eight recommended science practices outlined in the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS). To support participation in constructing explanations as a part of science learning, it is essential to move away from guiding students to targeted explanations and facilitate the development of explanations as plausible accounts of present or past observations. In this paper, we present an analysis of interaction dynamics, epistemic framing, and epistemic roles in an interview about natural selection to discuss how students’ shifts from reconstructing explanations to constructing explanations can be supported. This work has implications for supporting students in sensemaking during interviews and in classroom settings.

Consistent with the recent reforms in science education across the world, the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) in the United States promote learning of Science and Engineering Practices in addition to learning core disciplinary ideas and crosscutti ng concepts (Abd‐El‐Khalick, 2004; NGSS Lead States, 2013). Constructing explanations is one of the eight Science and Engineering Practices recommended by the NGSS. The description provided by NGSS for this practice includes making quantitative and/or qual itative claims, constructing and revising explanations, applying scientific ideas, principles, and/or evidence, and applying scientific reasoning, theory, and/or models. While the idea behind the practice-turn is to support students in participating in doi ng science as scientists do (Ford & Foreman, 2006), in practice, there is a paradox between asking students to participate in these practices in specific ways with the hope that they will learn how scientists do science (Miller et al.,2018). For example, scholars have pointed out that in the case of the practice of using and developing models, developing and using models of  target content doesn’t help students learn about how scientists use models for investigating and explaining phenomena (Gouvea & Passmore, 2017). We make a similar argument about the practice of constructing explanations by creating a heuristic reconstructing vs constructing explanations. Reconstructing explanations in a science classroom would involve guiding students to arrive at a targeted explanation for a phenomenon, whereas constructing explanations  would involve students developing ideas as plausible accounts that may or may not be accurate but involve the application of scientific ideas and reasoning. We argue that whether a student participates in the reconstruction of a target explanation or the construction of an explanation depends on their roles as epistemic agents in an interaction. How students participate in such interactions depends on how they interpret how knowledge is treated in the interaction, which is referred to as epistemological fr aming. Hammer and Elby define epistemological framing as “the cognitive activity underlying a student’s sense of “what is going on here” with respect to knowledge” (Elby & Hammer, 2010).  In inquiry-based learning activities, students’ epistemological framing influences how they see their roles as epistemic agents in those activities (Berland et al., 2016; Scherr & Hammer, 2009; Dabholkar & Wilensky, 2025). Whether  students see knowledge as propagated stuff that is to be accumulated, remembered and reproduced as a consumer of knowledge versus knowledge as constructed stuff that can be generated through sensemaking and reasoning as constructors and evaluators of knowledge could have implications on how students participate in learning interactions in inquiry-based learning settings. In this short paper, we present an empirical account of a student interview to showcase the contrast between reconstructing vs constructing explanations and discuss the implications for supporting student agency in constructing explanations. We discuss how shifts in students’ epistemological framing and how shifts in the roles they take as epistemic agents in an interaction can influence how they engage in constructing an explanation of a phenomenon.

## Conceptual framework

Elby and Hammer argue that whether students see  knowledge as propagated stuff  or constructed shapes their epistemological framing. When students see knowledge as something that is established by experts and learning 1862 as an accumulation of that knowledge, they are more likely to frame an interview interaction as recall and participate in the reconstruction of an explanation which they would see as providing the “right answer” (Elby & Hammer, 2010; Russ et al., 2012). Whereas when students see knowledge as constructed based on prior knowledge, they are likely to participate in figuring something out, thus engaging in sensemaking to construct an explanation.

Framing can shift from moment to moment depending on context. Prior work has focused on the interviewee’s framing, independent of the interviewer (Russ et al., 2012). Russ and colleagues explain this choice as related to the “interactional asymmetries" of traditional clinical interviews; the interviewer may often have a relatively set agenda (i.e. to have the student explain their thinking) which leaves little room tonegotiate epistemological framings. However, in a less structured interview interactions b etween an interviewer and a student could support shifts in epistemological framings. In this study, the interviewer was sensitized to the possibility of shifts in epistemological framing and made intentional bids to allow for shifts in roles. These bids were intended to make possible shifts in positioning (vande Sande & Greeno, 2012). For example, positioning the student as the one who was responsible for providing an explanation could activate a shared framing where recalling knowledge was considered acceptable. In contrast, positioning the student and the interviewer as mutually working to construct knowledge would activate a shared framing of sensemaking. Odden and Russ

(2019) define sensemaking as “a dynamic process of building or revising an explanati on in order to ‘figure

something out’ – to ascertain the mechanism underlying a phenomenon in order to resolve a gap or inconsistency in one’s understanding”. This definition helps clarify the difference between constructing an explanation, which is done to resolve a gap in one’s understanding and reconstructing an explanation which is done to demonstrate what one knows.

In this paper, we study these interactional shifts to address the following research question:  How do students’ epistemic roles shift from reconstructing explanations to constructing explanations in an interview? Data sources, knowledge analysis and interaction analysis The data used in this paper comes from an NSF-funded research project aimed at understanding students’ use of conceptual and epistemological resources in reasoning about natural selection. As a part of this project, we interviewed five students enrolled in an introductory biology course at a community college. The instructor discussed evolution and natural selection briefly during the first week of class, and students in the class wrote responses to a question asking them to choose from among four organisms (bat, dandelion, mouse, trumpet flower) and write out an explanation for the evolution of a trait (wings, parachute seeds, fur color, flower color). We collected students’ written answers and later presented them back to the students in the interviews. We conducted semi-structured interviews in the middle of the semester (weeks 7-9). These interviews were designed to elicit what students found puzzling or confusing. This approach was used intentionally to create opportunities for shifts in positioning and epistemological framing. The interviewer hoped to communicate that she was interested not only in explanations already known to the students, but also possible explanations constructed in the moment to address puzzles or gaps of interest to the student. Of the five interviews, we chose one interview as a case study that was the most “information-rich” case to develop an understanding of the dichotomy of reconstructing vs constructing explanations and how these activities emerged from interview dynamics (Merriam, 1998). Interviews were transcribed first by automated transcription and then edited by researchers. The analytical characterization of knowledge-related interactions within the interview was similar to an approach by Russ et al. (2012), which used verbal, nonverbal (gaze, gesture, and body posture), and para-verbal behaviors (pacing, volume, and clarity) from video-recorded interviews to infer shifts in students’ epistemological framings and their roles. We also attended to the moves made by the interviewer and how these moves may have functioned to frame the purpose of the interview and the respective roles of interviewer and interviewee with respect to knowledge construction and evaluation.

Finally, we conducted a knowledge analysis to trace the disciplinary ideas that arose in the interview. Our analysis included the first two stages of observation, schematization, and systematization (OSS) cycle (Parnafes & diSessa, 2013). The research group has collectively analyzed more than ten interviews with multiple rounds of OSS to establish a shared understanding of the operationalization of constructs like epistemological framing and participation. For the dataset presented in this paper, Observation included identifying episodes with specific interactional dynamics and listing disciplinary ideas within those episodes; Schematization included inferring and characterizing Epistemological Framing and Epistemic Participation within the episodes. We 1863 identified seven episodes based on the changes in the interactional dynamics in the interview. Nikita’s use of phrases and words like you know, I don’t know, I mean, I feel like, maybe are indicators of her engagement in sensemaking by imagining and speculating. Nikita participated in these episodes as a consumer, evaluator and constructor of the knowledge as someone who believes that there may not be a right answer to this question and  they are speculating using their knowledge of biology and evolutionary change. She first proposed a plausible account for a gap that she had identified in her explanation about formation of wings. She then evaluated her explanation to further identify a gap in learning to use wings to fly. In these episodes, with moves by the interviewer to frame the interview as about “speculating” and to validate Nikita’s tentative ideas, Nikita shifted between epistemic roles as evaluator, consumer and constructor of knowledge to propose, evaluate and compare plausible accounts related to evolution of wings in bats.

In this paper, we studied Nikita’s epistemic roles that emerged through the negotiation during the interactional dynamics with the interviewer. During the interview, Nikita’s epistemic participation showed shifts between recall and sensemaking. When Nikita started engaging in sensemaking to construct an explanation rather than recalling to reconstruct one, her use of disciplinary ideas changed. While Nikita’s use of disciplinary ideas during the reconstruction phase was accurate, we see distinct, and arguably more critical, aspects of evolutionary reasoning in the construction of her explanation through sensemaking. While sensemaking, Nikita speculated about intermediate morphological forms of bat-wings to argue about the advantages due to specific morphological changes leading to changes in the phenotype in a population. These episodes in the interview show Nikita’s productive use of disciplinary ideas regarding the frequency change over generations and evolutionary developmental (evo-devo) biology. Towards the end of this part of the interview, Nikita evaluated her idea by invoking connections between morphological and behavioral modifications. We argue that these shifts in Nikita’s roles as an epistemic agent were mediated by the shifts in her framing of her participation as reconstructing explanations versus constructing explanations. The interactional were unfolding during the interactional dynamics. As Nikita started seeing that her role as a constructor and evaluator of knowledge is being valued in the interview, her participation as an epistemic agent changed. These findings also underscore the importance of interactional dynamics in epistemological framing. Beyond interviews, we see the potential for classroom instruction to create openings for students to be knowledge constructors rather than knowledge reproducers.
