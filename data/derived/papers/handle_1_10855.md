# Learning for Every Body: Intersectional Dimensions of Embodied Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As embodiment research gains popularity within the learning sciences, this symposium both leverages and critiques embodiment perspectives as they reveal the learning of marginalized students. We highlight the manifold ways in which bodies that do not conform, subscribe, or satisfy the imagined, normative ‘ideal’ standard in educational settings—be they racialized, gendered, disabled, indigenous, multilingual, queer, and/or politicized—construct and are constructed within explicitly embodied learning settings.

## Overview and objectives

In alignment with the ISLS 2024 theme of “Learning as a Cornerstone of Healing, Resilience, and Commun ity,” we seek to facilitate a dialog among those conducting critical, sociocultural, and intersectional research on bodies within the context of embodied learning activities. This symposium draws into dial ogue diverse critical perspectives on the body as they come into play in the design and theorization of embodied learning ac tivities, towards the following aims:

1. To instigate interdisciplinary conversations cuttin g across axes of bodily non-normativity, towards a

holistic, intersectional view of the learning body.

2. To analyze issues and opportunities with embodied a pproaches to instructional design for diverse

learners' well-being, identity development, and learning outcomes.

3. To illustrate the centrality of marginalized learne rs’ experiences to fundamental theoretical and

methodological questions in the learning sciences.

Cross-cutting themes The work in this symposium interrogates the body as  both subject to marginalization and domination, and as a site of promise for imagining more equitable and inclusive pedagogical futures. To do so, we center non-normative bodies at the intersection of multiple grounds for marginalization (Annamma & Booker, 2020), drawing u pon critical theoretical perspectives including epistem ological pluralism, critical race and disability theories, sociocultural learning theories, indigenous perspec tives of the self and land, trauma-sensitive pedagogies, and philosophies of the thinking-moving-feeling body in sites of learning. Across populations and learning contexts, this work  troubles hegemonic ideals for what constitutes a learning body. We analyze the affordances and constraints of bodies as tools (for expression and meaning-making across time and space; Tancredi et al., 2024; Champion & Solomon, 2024), with tools (extended through creative and material interaction with artifacts and land; H ussain, 2024; Marin et al., 2024), and beyond tools (as agents with sensory, affective experiences; Vickery et. al. 2024). On the one hand, we show bodies as they ar e read, suppressed, and circumscribed within extant educational practices. On the other, we investigate bodies as sites of creativity, a gency, and possibility. Centering marginalized popu lations, we surface neglected and suppressed bodily practices and semiotic modes and demonstrate  how these can offer unrecognized resources for lea rning, from the multimodal expressions of Black girls (Cha mpion & Solomon, 2024) to the signed languages of Deaf communities (Hussain, 2024). We reflect on how embracing marginalized children’s embodied resources can give rise to novel pedagogical practices and ultimately, alternative epistemologies. Rooting in work with marginalized learners, we further present methodological critiques for educational research, challenging normative assumptions that pervade learning objectives, design, and data analysis (Gholson & Ma, 2024). We call upon embodiment researchers to engage in critical reflexivity of their theories, m ethods, and design praxis and recognize the stakes of pedagogical bodily engagement for non-normative bodies. This requires researchers to attend to their unit of analysis, po sitionality, and data collection methods with an ey e towards the complexities of learners’ diverse felt experiences,  as well as multimodal means of participation and e xpression. We must expand notions of ‘learning’ beyond formal learning envi ronments, attending to emergent embodied meaning-making in-situ. We outline proactive and critical design approaches that privilege the voices of learners, including participatory and co-design processes. Ultimately, this symposium sketches an intersectional vision for how embodied design practices might become more sen sitive to and empowering of learners' sensory, affe ctive, social, and cultural identities and bodily agency.

Re-imagining physics: Designing liberating spaces of d iscovery for Black girls by attuning to bodies, imagination, and cultural identity Dionne Champion, and Foloshadé Solomon Black bodies are often positioned as non-conforming in physics learning spaces. Black girls, who have struggled to gain access to physics, are often subjected to l earning environments where their resources and cont ributions are easily dismissed, erased or excluded, their moving bodies more harshly managed,  and their vocally and physically expressive cultural practices are more likely to be seen as behaviors to be policed than as resources for science learning (Hartman, 1997). They face harsher  disciplinary actions for non-conforming behaviors such as expressing opinions or discouragement from particip ating in STEM learning opportunities. This paper ex plores the possibilities for learning when bodies that are  typically framed as problematic are re-positioned as resources for physics learning. We examine the ways that Blac k girls embody their ideas utilizing multiple modes  of expression – words, vocalizations, facial expressions, gestures,  and full body movements – as they use dance to explore concepts in physics. Through the Embodied P hysics Learning Lab, a DBR project that takes a cul turally responsive, asset-based approach to the design of physics learning environments, we have seen youth experience dance settings as liberatory spaces (Barton, 1997) for physics exploration. Liberatory learning spaces, rooted in critical peda gogy, emphasize the dismantling of oppressive structures within educational contexts. These spaces prioritize inclusivity and acknowledge and celebrate diverse identities, cultures, and perspectives (Barto n, 1997; Jefferson et al., 2018; Farrington, 2021).  The Embodied Physics Learning lab, with its explicit design to u se bodies and cultural identities to explore physic s, prioritized creative embodiment as a primary resource for explo ration and explanation,  validating students' cultural backgrounds, fostering a sense of belonging, and bridging the gap between physics and the familiar practices and lived experiences of youth. The lab used many essen tial elements, including embodied imagination (Solo mon et al., 2018) and re-presentation (Champion, 2018). In this paper, we ex amine one essential embodied practice of the lab, listening palettes, and the liberatory imp act on (1) empowerment and agency, (2) inclusivity and equity, and (3) holistic development (Table 2).

Our exploration of physics concepts like energy, pa rticle and wave behaviors, superposition and polyrhythm, vibration and resonance included opport unities for youth to discuss their own real-life experiences and connections to a topic, hear from scientists ab out each topic, and reflect on their i nitial interpretations by crafting multimodal responses. Given the choice to respond to ideas and images that were salient to th em, youth collaborated to develop and share sketches, choreog raphy, even poetry and raps that reflected what the y saw, heard, fe lt, and remembered from each scientists' presentati on. These listening palettes, a concept initially developed by choreographer Liz Lerman through her c ollaborative work with scientists and choreographer s, became a critical part of their learning experienc e, offering them ways into the discussion of comple x physics ideas that did not require them to be content experts.

Arts-based research: Knowledge co-construction with young adults who are Deaf/Hard of Hearing and Racialized, Black, Indigenous, and/or People of Color (YA DHH RBIPoC) Farha Najah Hussain This paper argues for arts-based research as an equity-driven, and embodied methodology of knowledge co-construction of learning environments and interacti ons with young adults who are Deaf or Hard of Heari ng and also Racialized, Black, Indigenous, and/or People of Color (YA DHH RBIPOC). The lived experiences, ideas, and knowledge of RBIP oC have been treated as inferior due to systemic racism and white supremacy, including within the speech-language pathology profession (Yu et al., 2022). While the predominantly White Deaf community in the  global north has historically been marginalized due  to the disabling premise that hearing/spoken languages are better thandeafness/signed languages, it has simultaneously perpetuated eurocentrism and racism, and ignored intersectional DHH realities (Smith-Warshaw & Crume, 2020). As such, it is important to focus on YA DHH RBIPoC’ s perspectives of preferred interactions in their l earning environments, including healthcare contexts (e.g. their interactions with speech-language pathologists). As a transdisciplinary and multidimensional paradig m, arts-based research engages in knowledge building that combines the tenets of the creative a rts in research (Leavy, 2020). Instead of perpetuat ing binaries, dominant ideologies, and hierarchies, Leavy conveys  that arts-based research has the possibility to center our intersubjectivities. Visual art can tap into what may not yet be conscious in an indivi dual (Leavy, 2020). Visual art research can enhance our understanding of a soc ial phenomenon (Leavy, 2020), such as revealing YA DHH RBIPoC’s preferred interactions with professionals in their learning environments. Visual arts –based research, including through digital collage-making, can allow a synergistic prac tice facilitating a holistic view of research on learning environments and interactions, particul arly for marginalized people (Leavy, 2020). The app lication of arts-based research through digital collage-making can result in the co-construction of knowledge that centers YA DHH RBIPoC’s expression of preferred interaction s with professionals in learning environments, incl uding those that intersect with healthcare contexts.

Research in Communication Sciences and Disorders (CSD) points to the necessity of critically examining knowledge construction to better understand how rea lities of marginalization, societal power imbalance s, and positivist approaches in tandem with biomedical and deficit-oriented frameworks constrain the profession (Pesco, 2014; Pillay & Kathard, 2018). Pathways to equitabl e learning can be created once approaches to knowle dge construction are re-imagined. Applying arts-based research can be an equity driven methodology when centering YA DHH RBIPoC’ knowledge of learning environments a nd their interactions within these environments. Specifically, visual art research decenters spoken language. Instead, participant’s perspectives in th e form of digital collage  afford visual expression as the central means of co mmunication. The digital collage-making process in response to prompts/questions posed to Y A DHH RBIPoC about their experiences in learning environments (e.g. interaction with speech-language pathologists) serves as embodied knowledge creation that can be supplemented with interviews in the YA DHH RBIPoC’s primary language (e.g. American Sign Language) (Leavy, 2020).

Learning scientists can use arts-based modalities, including digital collage as a me ans to engage in knowledge co-construction with YA DHH RBIPOC in service of more equitable learning and healthcare environments.

Can we see him now? Possible futures of race-critical interaction analysis Maisie L. Gholson, and Jasmine Y. Ma In this paper, the tragic death of Tamir Rice, a 12-year old Black boy, serves as a grounding case of an embodied learning activity for exploring how the racialized body takes on meaning in social interaction theory and analysis. Tamir’s murder occurred in  Cleveland, Ohio, US on November 23, 2014. Surveilla nce video captured the Cleveland police arriving on the scene by car at high speed and abruptly stopping, before a police officer jumped out of the car and lethally shot Tamir from a dista nce of less than  10 feet. The incident spanned two seconds in length, suggesting the signifying and semiotic power of the racialized child body in possession of a black toy gun. We treat this event and the local setting, near a g azebo in Cudell Park and Recreational Center, as a case of informal learning; one where any possibility for le arning was sharply truncated due to Tamir’s Black b ody and the police response to it. While increasingly attending to both racialization and embodiment in recent scholarship, the learning sciences has stopped short of construc ting a theory of social interaction that captures a nd links violence rooted in white supremacy with learning; f urther, how Black and other racialized bodies becom e resources for action is unaccounted for in theories of embodied learning in the field. As learning is part and parcel of social interaction, it follows then, that our theories also fail to capture Black children’s learning and becoming in formal and informal learning contexts built in a white supremacist society. However, we contend that theoretical tools are alre ady available. We offer three examples to show how existing concepts and approaches bring into analyti c relief different ways of making sense of the raci alized body in interaction and learning. First, Goodwin’s (2007) environmentally coupled gestures illuminat es how Tamir’s Black body and the toy gun he held mutually elaborated each other as a threat that justified immediate and targeted gunfire by police officers. Next, the concepts of s emiotic fields and substrates (Goodwin, 2013) draw analytic attention to the various laminating semiotic fields (e.g, the context of the park, the Black body with what appeared to be a gun) that provided substrate for the culmin ating action–shooting–of Tamir Rice. Finally, the approach of relational ontologies and assemblages makes an impo rtant distinction from the aforementioned concepts (de Freitas & Sinclair, 2014; Spyrou, 2018), insofar as  Tamir’s, as a human, is not distinct and separate from the toy gun but intimately entangled with intra-acting agencies. Each of these appr oaches holds various affordances and constraints in their conceptualization of human agency, race, and its relationship to the body, as wel l as different analytical challenges in determining what analytic resources are necessary to show demonstrable relevance. Through these three analyses, we deconstruct the Ta mir Rice incident using the aforementioned perspectives to locate analytical openings and fissures that all ow for deeper consideration for how race and racialized bodies are shaping social interactions. We conclude with a discussion of how, while Tamir’s  may be seen as an extreme case, the learning of children of color is always made tenuous by their racialized bodies; the 2 seconds in which any possible futures for Tamir’s life (not to mention learning) was obliterated is not an anomaly, but instead an example of how the temporalities aro und how learning is shaped, supported, produced, or  even allowed to occur for children with racialized bodies shifts and narrows as soon as racialized bodies bring attendant meanings into a learning setting.

Can you feel it?: Following the embodied trails within improvisational jazz performances Ananda Marin, Lindsay Lindberg, Brenda Lopez, and Shivani Davé In 2020, we began collaborating with a group of fiv e Chicago-based improvisational jazz artists (two percussionists, a bass player, a harmonium player, and a vocalist) whose creative work exemplifies the  great tradition of Americana, primarily drawing from  African American and Indigenous traditions includin g jazz, rhythm, and blues. Our work together is motivated b y a desire to better understand how collaboration a nd improvisation, two practices that are central to le arning, are supported by histories of re lationships with others, materials, and tools. Our focus on relationality is grounded in writings by Indigenous scholars who recognize that one’s being and reality are constituted, enacted, and practiced through relationships (e.g., Brayboy et al., 2012) as well as sociocultural scholars who see relationship s as foundational to learning (e.g., Vossoughi et a l., 2021). Importantly, within Indigenous ways of knowing, rel ationships are bound with a set of responsibilities  to self, community, and lands/waters (Tynan, 2021).

Our collaborative work is organized around an inter related set of questions about the histories and geographies of learning and learning as shifts in p articipation. First, how do these artists understand their relationships with others, materials, and tool s to be consequential to their collaborations and i mprovisational performances as ensembles? And second, what resources and communicative channels do these artists draw upon in order to improvise and collaborate? More specifi cally, how do they coordinate attention in order to  make resources available to others? To answer these ques tions we draw on multiple methodologies including  participatory design research (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) and the principles of genealogy (i.e., kinshipbased and activity-based family), relationships, reciprocity, and guardianship and care (Jahnke & Gillies, 2012).

In this paper, we explore how the relational aspect s of collaboration and improvisation are entwined within the learning body. Of interest to us are the  many ways in which live, improvisational jazz perf ormances are both grounded in histories of relations and are  a context for embodied learning for both the perfo rmers and audience members. We draw on data from over 10 co-design meetings, two live jazz performances, and po stperformance conversations. Our analyses detail how ideas and words from co-design meetings traveled to an initial jazz performance and shaped the improvisational space. For example, we attend to how discussions from co-design meetings led two of the performers, a harmonium player and a voc alist, to bring their musical forms alongside their visual/material arts practices. For example, the ha rmonium player decided to alternate her movements b etween painting and playing her musical instrument during both performances. We also trace how the vocalist used both her body and tools to engage with the audience to c ollectively reflect on the meanings of particular w ords and phrases that she selected from transcripts of the c o-design meetings (e.g. “Can you see it? Can you hear  it? Can you feel it?” “Get out of the way” “How’d you do th at? “The breath”). In doing so, she transformed lan guage from the co-design meetings into an invitation for an embodied,  interactional experience for both co-designers and audience members. During the performance, the vocalist also began working on a quilt that integrated pictures of the performers relatives and ancestors. She continued working on this quilt in the second performance, passing the artifact through the audience. In both cases, we trace key moments from the performances to post-performance conversations in order to illuminate the improvisat ional and collaborative nature of mind/body experie nces. Finally, we address our own embodied practices as researchers by attending to how our experiences with watching and listening to video recordings of research activ ities shifted as we used our bodies to engage with multiple perspectives. We close by reflecting on the ways in  which our relationships as researchers entwine us with a set of responsibilities to self, community, and lands/waters. From reactive to proactive: Considering socio-affective experiences in inclusive embodied activity design Morgan Vickery, Nitasha Mathayas, Selena Steinberg, and Megan Humburg This paper examines how learners’ socio-affective expressions during embodied learning acti vities can expose marginalizing aspects of activity design and how su ch revelations might prompt proactive rather than r eactive approaches for disability-inclusive and trauma-informed designs. Educational philosophers have attended to the body both as an object of social inscription and as a subject with the possibility of action (Csordas & Harwood, 1994). Within this exploration, embodied action ari ses as a means for expressing and discerning internal states in the external world (Humburg, 2022). In embodied learning research, the focus has often been on understanding  the expression of cognitive processes via the body, such as the negotiation of and reflection on disciplinary i deas as they are projected through and represented by the body (Mathayas et al., 2019). However, these approaches often overlook the feeling body  in their analysis of the learning-moving-thinking body; they frame the body as a malleable tool or artifa ct under observation and construction. We challenge this framing and highlig ht how bodies are viscerally and personally connect ed to learners in ways that other tools are not. Socio-affective dimensions of the body in embodied learning activities (e.g., dis/comfort, belonging, bodily autonomy, psy chological safety) are too often deprioritized in l ieu of conceptualizations of the body as a ‘terrain’ in wh ich we ‘work’ under the scrutiny of others to attai n some standard of desirability (O’Loughlin, 1998).

The affective dimensions of embodiment are particul arly imperative for designing disability-inclusive and trauma-informed experiences as the highly visible and physical aspects of activities may inadvertently disable and (re)traumatize learners with non-normative bodies. Stiker argues “We organize the world—that is, space and time…—for a kind of average person, designated normal. Th is is a world that the person who cannot, or can no longer, move there comfortably threatens to modify and remake” (2019, p. 36) - embodied activity designs are no exception. The revelation that a design is inadequate evokes a fear and discomfort in designers of that which is in tension with the mental and physical ‘order.’ Thus,  learners' embodiment is divorced from their socio-affective experiences to deny, invalidate, and erase the expe riences of disabled bodies and minds. This privileg ing of the ‘observable’ over the ‘felt’ manifests in reactive approaches to embodied activity design, where short comings in design are only unvei led as students respond to emergent inaccessibiliti es with subtle acts of resistance. Historically, such acts of resistance have been sub dued to minimize disruption to the expected activit y sequence using segregative, punitive, or assimilatory measures.

Thus, we advocate for a shift towards proactively d esigning embodied learning activities that anticipate the exposure of inadequate and exclusive aspects by  validating students' affective expressions. We ada pt Goodwin’s “principle of universal trauma” (2020) as  a standard for proactively designing under the ass umption that all learners will be made vulnerable or disabl ed by exclusionary designs such that learners with ‘invisible’ experiences do not fall through the cracks. We enga ge in a humble reflection o f an array of instances across diverse implementations where students expressed di scomfort, distress, or frustration in response to e mbodied activity implementations. We then share how we inte rpreted, responded, and reflected upon these experi ences to inform subsequent embodied activities that better atten ded to learners’ socio-affective needs of comfort, safety, and autonomy.
