# Collaborative Virtual Reality Worlds, Cultural Play: Re-imagining Game Design through Interactional Tool Exploration

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This case study explores how interactional tools can support the development of design ideas within a collaborative virtual reality (VR) -based studio. Six participants explored multiplayer VR applications to reflect on existing VR designs and co-design an LGBTQ+ cultural mini-golf game, while documenting their experiences in reflective journals. Our findings extend sociocultural constructionist and socio-materialist perspectives, demonstrating how VR pens, viewed as “non-neutral actors,” shape learners’ design idea generation, refinement, and decision-making.

Virtual reality (VR) enables users to step into computer-generated, three-dimensional environments where they can interact with objects and experience a strong sense of “presence” (Mayer et al., 2023). Previous work has demonstrated the unique affordances of VR for learning, such as supporting experiential learning tasks, making abstract concepts tangible, and enhancing learners’ spatial knowledge (e.g., Silseth et al., 2024). Moreover, VR enables learners to use their bodies and gestures to manipulate objects within 3D spaces, which supports embodied learning and enhances their sense of agency over the learning process (Johnson-Glenberg, 2018, 2019). Researchers have shown growing interest in how VR can support collaborative learning across disciplines (Fortman & Quintana, 2023; Paulsen et al., 2024). Paré et al. (2019), for example, demonstrated how creating 3D sculptures and customizing avatars in multiplayer VR settings supported playful inquiry, enabling participants to collaboratively explore and share queer experiences. Yet, immersive VR is still often perceived as an individual technology, with many learning environments designed for the individual (e.g., Enyedy & Yoon, 2021). Much of the existing research also focuses on evaluating learning outcomes, rather than on the learning processes themselves (Radianti et al., 2020). Few studies employ qualitative methods to examine how learning occurs in collaborative VR, and how participants utilize embodied and interactional resources to co-construct meaning (Paulsen et al., 2024). Furthermore, most research focuses on learners engaging with pre-designed VR content to acquire specific procedural or declarative knowledge (Radianti et al., 2020), with far less attention paid to learners’ inventive and design-oriented practices as creators in collaborative VR environments.

## Theoretical background and objectives

From a constructionist perspective, learning occurs when learners actively engage in constructing a “public entity” that can be shared with others. We thus conceptualize the objects learners created in the VR worlds as not merely knowledge application products, but as powerful “objects-to-think-with” (Papert, 1980) created through embodied VR learning experiences that support their decision-making. We also draw upon socio-materialism (Barad, 2003), which argues for a shift in the transitional view from “how  humans use tools” to “how humans and materials act together” (Peppler & Thompson, 2024). It challenges the traditional view of tools and materials as passive intermediaries, which posits that tools and materials are “non-neutral actors” possessing their agency, historicity, and identity (Peppler & Thompson, 2024). That is, these tools are not just passively utilized by human beings, but rather proactively co-shape learning and creative processes alongside them. This process aligns with Barad’s

(2003) notion of “intra-action,” emphasizing that humans and tools co-produce meaning and outcomes through

mutual interaction, rather than one unilaterally acting upon the other. We focus on a specific design project to understand how individuals learn about VR and cultural heritage design more broadly through participation in a collaborative VR studio. We then ask: In collaborative VR exploration, how do interaction tools support individual idea development when designing a VR cultural mini-golf game? We employed a qualitative case study (Yin, 2018), situated within a larger participatory design research project. The case focuses on a six-week VR studio as a particular, bounded design context where six participants collaboratively explored VR design with the goal of designing an LGBTQ+ historical and cultural mini-golf game. Participants included three graduate students and three VR industry professionals from Canada and the United 656 States. They utilized VR headsets to explore multiplayer applications (e.g., VRChat) and engage in design prototyping (e.g., ShapesXR). We specifically examined the use of VR pens as interactional tools. Unlike 2D drawing, VR pens allow for dynamic computational geometry creation in 3D space, heavily relying on learners’ physical movements and gestures. The data comprised forty-four reflective journals, eight video recordings, seventy-one participant photographs, and thirty-three-dimensional artifacts creat ed within the virtual environment. For this analysis, the individual’s interactional tool-use episode and its support of collaborative design serve as the primary unit of analysis. We then followed Braun and Clarke’s (2006) thematic analysis, coding specifically for how interactional tool affordances shaped design development. We found three main themes indicating that VR pens function as “non-neutral actors” that explicates how VR pens supported participants’ design idea development: (1) Making the “invisible” visible; (2) Sketches as “Prisms”; and (3) “Anchors” as Design Stages. We present one of the participants, Jazlyn, to illustrate these themes, as it provides a rich vignette showcasing how pens facilitated her design thinking across different design stages, from individual to collaborative processes. First, the tools made participants’ thinking visible by externalizing intangible ideas into concrete 3D sketches. For example, Jazlyn used the VR pens to materialize personal memories into visible artifacts (see Figure 1, left-hand column), transforming internal thoughts into s hareable “objects-to-think-with” (Papert, 1980) as entry points for subsequent game design generation. Figure 1 Demonstration of Design Development Second, sketches functioned as “prisms” rather than mirrors of thought, refracting design ideas into multiple perspectives that were not apparent in verbal discussions alone. In Jazlyn’s reflective journal discussing her experience, for example, the act of  her drawings unfolded into different lenses (e.g., personal story sharing, co-creation, and freedom of expression), refining her understanding of how ideas could be represented in VR space. Through a socio-material lens, these refractions emerged from the  intra-action between learners and the tool, where the virtual material properties of the pen co-produced new design dimensions (Barad, 2003). Third, sketches served as potential “anchors” for group discussions and design alignment. Jazlyn’s sketches of the minigolf game design (see Figure 1, right-hand column, number 1) served as reference points that support shared, iterative design stages, enabling the group to converge on collective decisions. Through Jack’s integration of game mechanics, Andi’s historical narratives, and Skye’s refined sketch, the artifact evolved from a personal expression into a “public entity” (Papert, 1980). This showcases a practice of VR co-creation, where the modifiability of VR pens allowed for the visible integration of diverse contributions within the shared space. Our case study shows VR interactional tools within collaborative VR environments actively shape the trajectory of design development rather than serving as passive instruments. By enabling learners to externalize invisible concepts, refract ideas through multiple perspectives, and anchor collective iterations, VR pens function as “nonneutral actors” that fundamentally alter the design process. From constructionist and socio-material lenses, our spatial freedom and lack of physical resistance, co-produces unique forms of “reflection-in-action” (Schön, 1983) and collaborative knowledge building.
