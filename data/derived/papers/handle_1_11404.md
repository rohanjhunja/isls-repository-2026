# “Kind of Like a Chemical Reaction”: Leveraging Structural Similarities between Models to Support Teachers' Growing Expertise with Computational Modeling for Science Teaching

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
There is consensus that computational modeling can support integration of science with computing for student learning. However, it can be challenging for teachers to gain comfort with this relatively new practice, reconcile how it relates to science, and recognize its pedagogical value. In this paper, we illustrate how teachers working with a (serendipitous) analogy between a familiar process and a topic covered in chemistry class–frogs catching flies and reaction rates–enabled them to take up computational modeling in their science teaching. Drawing on notes from co-design sessions, teacher interviews and student worksheets, we illustrate how a focal teacher shifted from initial reluctance to taking ownership of designing and teaching a computational modeling lesson sequence. We also briefly show how her students took up this work. Finally, we reflect on the importance of leveraging teachers’ existing domain and pedagogical expertise as we bring these new practices into their classrooms.

## Introduction and theoretical background

There is consensus that computational modeling (CM)  supports integration of science with computing (e. g., Sengupta et al., 2013; Weintrop et al., 2017; Hurt et al., 2023), and can enable students to engage wi th scientific ideas while engaging in computational thinking (CT) practices (e.g., Arastoopour Irgens et al 2020; Dickes et al., 2020; Hutchins et al., 2020; Peel et al., 2021; Wag h et al, 2017). However, the field is grappling wit h questions around how to support teachers to successfully inte grate the two in their teaching (e.g., Yoon et al., in press). In particular, teachers need support with developing c omfort and competence with CT practices such as programming (Liu at al., 2024). Moreover, teachers can see science and computing as incongruent with each other, and need support with knowing how to integrate the two in their teaching (Bain et al., 2020). One reason CM supports the integration of science a nd computing is that the science and the code are reflexive (Guzdial, 1994). The code provides a stru cture to represent and explore rules and relationsh ips underlying the target scientific phenomenon. For in stance, the code structure in an agent-based model (ABM) involves specifying rules for agents in a system, their properties, and interactions with each other (e.g., Wilensky & Reisman, 2006). Running these rules enables stude nts to investigate emergent trends at the populatio n level. Interestingly, phenomena that are distinct in appea rance or seem unrelated to one another can have ver y similar or identical rules at the agent level. For instance, the transmission of disease and spread of rumors bear many similarities in the underlying mechanism or the rules that the entities in the system follow. Research in Cognitive Science and the Learning Sciences has examined how to support learners to attend to the similarity in mechanisms underlying seemingl y unrelated phenomena. For instance, in structure-mapping theory, scholars proposed an analogy as having an “overlap in relationships but not objects” (p.161) by giving the example of how a hydrogen atom can be seen as analo gous to the solar system (Gentner, 1983). While the  two bear no literal similarity, the rules followed by the entities in each are similar to each other. Others have proposed leveraging CM to support learners to generalize rea soning across seemingly unrelated phenomena (Goldst one & Wilensky, 2008).

In this paper, we build on this work to illustrate how attending to the underlying structural similarities or the analogy between two seemingly unrelated phenome na led high school teachers to adopt the practice o f programming computational models in their science class. We also illustrate how students took up the analogy in their work, and reasoned about reaction rates, a to pic that is otherwise challenging (Cakmakci et al.,  2007). Ultimately, we demonstrate how leveraging teachers’ existing domain and pedagogical expertise can enable them to recognize the value of computational modeling using code for science learning.

The study Our work takes place in the context of a Research-Practice Partnership (RPP) (Coburn et al., 2013) with a district that intends to integrate CM into every core scienc e subject as a way to give every student some basel ine CT experience. This RPP aimed to develop at least two CM-integrated science units each for biology, chemistry, and physics, using the StarLogo Nova (Wendel, 2013) blo cks-based ABM platform. All participating teachers were invited to three in-service and two summer PD days, where a significant  portion of each day was devoted to reviewing, refining, and preparing to teach the draft units. An explicit design goal was to incorporate one or m ore of five Computational Modeling Practices (CMPs) we identified by drawing from literature on CT and scientific modeling in general (Wagh et al.,  2024), into the units. These CMPs are: 1) identify core m echanisms of phenomena, 2) create computational representations (i.e. code) to model the phenomena, 3) read and interpret others’ code for scientific sensemaking,

4) experiment and discover relationships between variables, and 5) validate, refine, and extend a model.

Catching Flies and the analogy to reaction rates The majority of the participating teachers had litt le or no prior experience with CM or coding. We hav e written separately (under review) about the effectiveness of using simple modeling activities of familiar phenomena that we call code-the-familiar activities to develop teachers’ comfort and competence with mo deling. One of these activities is called Catching Flies (Zhuang et al., 2013), a StarLogo Nova tutorial that has been tested and refined over 11 years of working with novice programmers in  a variety  of contexts. It uses a step-by-step format with visual and text-based scaffolds to guide learners through the build ing of a simple model. The product of the Catching Flies tutorial is a simple predator/prey m odel in which frog agents move around the simul ation space and “eat” (i.e. remove) fly agents with which they collide. At a summer modeling workshop unrelated to this RPP, a teacher participant was sharing his extensions to the Catching Flies tutorial. He shared that he “noticed that this is kind of like a chemical reaction”, and discussed how he changed his model to reflect this alternate interpretation. We were struck by the similarities: two types of agents, moving randomly around a confined space, in teracting via collisions, with one (or more) type b eing consumed in the interaction.

Indeed, each of the three factors of reaction rates  that our chemistry teachers cover –temperature, concentration, and surface area –has a simple analog in the model of frogs and flies. Temperature, i.e. average kinetic energy, is analogous to the distance frogs can hop each time step. And, just as a frog that ho ps faster can catch flies more quickly, the particles of a hot su bstance collide with other particles more frequentl y, providing more opportunities to react. Similarly, adding more  frogs to StarLogo Nova’s fixed-size world is analogous to increasing the concentration of a reactant; and, in tuitively, more frogs can eat more flies more quick ly. Lastly, surface area in a heterogeneous reaction is easily represented by constraining the frogs to a smaller area of the space; because the frogs are competing in shared territory, they are not able to catch as many flies each. Figure 1 shows the StarLogo Nova code for the dually-interpretable model. This paper documents the development and piloting of a chemistry unit that leaned into this analogy and was designed by an initially reluctant high school chemistry teacher. We focus on the ways in which this analogy was taken up by our partner teachers in design sessions, and argue that it helped them consider multiple CMPs in design. We also show how the focal teacher’s students took up the analogy in chemistry class. Figure 1 StarLogo Nova Code for the Catching Flies / Reaction Rates model

## Research questions

In this paper, we examine the work this analogy did for teachers and students in our study to ask:

1. How did teachers take up the frogs and flies analogy in modeling of reaction rates?

2. How did students take up the frogs and flies analogy in sense-making of reaction rates?

## Data collection and analysis

To answer our research questions, we draw on both t eacher and student data. The teacher data comes fro m five partner teachers who participated in chemistry co-design sessions. We used observation notes from co-design sessions, and a post-implementation interview with the focal (pilot) teacher. Student data comes from observation notes from the pilot implementation, and worksheets of 19 students who consented to participate. Analysis began with the research team noting the sh ift in teacher comfort with programming after they worked on Catching Flies. This observation led us to revisit the observation notes from co-design sessions before and after this activity. We looked for themes (Braun & Clarke, 2012) in teachers’ considerations of kinds of CMPs to include in the activity and related design modif ications, their engagement in target CMPs, as well as any articulated hesitancy, enthusiasm or other affect. We also used thematic analysis to analyze one focal  teacher interview by identifying and refining emergent them es around the uptake of this analogy (e.g., reflect ions about how students took up the structural similarity betw een “catching flies” and “reaction rates”). We also  analyzed student worksheets to identify the connections stud ents made (or not) between the conceptual topics and their representation in code.

## Findings

Teacher uptake: From veridicality and hesitancy to CMPs and ownership The early drafts of the Reaction Rates unit used a model in which there were two types of molecules, represented as colored spheres, and each molecule had a kinetic energy property. On collisio n, molecules of distinct types would produce a “product” molecule and delete both initial molecules. We presented this model to the w hole chemistry group during an early PD session for feedback and lesson ideas (March 2023). Having recently completed a “code-the-familiar” activity about epidemics, the teachers ag reed that “students generating some part of the model” may be  important. However, initial ideas in the co-design session for student work leaned toward complicated veridicality. One suggestion was to model the molecules as grou ps of connected atoms, and have students rearrange tho se atoms to form product molecules. This might have  aided in the study of stoichiometry, but neither this, no r other suggestions from the session, would provide  insight into the target core ideas: that temperature, concentration, and surface area affect the likelihood of molecules colliding, and hence the reaction rate (i.e. Collision Theory).

The introduction of the Catching Flies activity was  a turning point for many teachers’ comfort with programming a model in StarLogo Nova (August 17, 20 23). Our focal teacher, Ms L, pointed to Catching F lies as the moment it “really clicked” for her. However, she was still hesitant to have students program models in her chemistry class. Responding to a colleague’s plan to do the Catching Flies activity prior to a different unit,  Ms L expressed concern that the programming would overwh elm and distract students from the science: “I don’t want to teach programming-I don’t want to spend two days teaching something that isn’t chemistry”. Later that day, in a co-design session with the chemistry teachers, the first author relayed the observation that the Catching Flies model is structurally similar to a simple chemical reaction. Immediately the tone and tempo of conversation shifted, with the facilitator (the first author) no longer leading the discussion as the teachers shared and debated ideas about how to bring this to  their students, engaging in several CMPs. In particular, the group loved the “simplicity of using [this frogs and flies] model for kinetics”. In contrast to their previous ideas for model extensions, the group now focused on and debated how simple the model could be as they ident ified the core features and mechanisms (CMP 1), and then mapped them to representations in the co de (CMP 2). The teachers also discussed how easy it was to “collect  a lot of data very quickly”, which would enable students to “do repeated experiments and see the variability but also trends” (CMP 4). They contrasted this to wet labs, where “sometimes the data isn’t quite ‘as expected’ and it’s hard to explain” because repeated trials are impractical. The group engaged in CMP 2 as they debated whether to reskin the activity, replacing frogs and flies with molecules, and whether to create “product” agents on collision and delete both reactants to increase fidelity. However, one teacher argued, from an engagement perspective, for leaving the activity as frogs and flies, saying it was “more fun” and “not as ‘schooly’”. He added that, from a modeling perspective, it provided an opportunity for students to validate and extend the model (CMP 5) as they considered how frogs and flies supported learning about collision theory, and how they might change the model to improve that support. Ms L further argued, again employing CMP 1, that “once it becomes real molecules, we introduce stoichiometry and other real factors there. But keeping it abstract lets us sidestep that-we can just focus on temperature, concentration, and surface area in the abstract, so it’s actually a little less muddle d.” In a followup conversation (Oct 2023), another teacher characterized the decision to start with frogs and flies as being “intentional and important” for teac hing computational modeling practices.

By the end of this PD, Ms L had determined to develop her own lesson sequence. In this sequence, the first lesson was the Catching Flies activity using the guide as provided during the PD; students built  a model in which frogs and flies moved around randomly, and fr ogs caught flies when they collided against them. I n the second les son, students were invited to modify their model co de to represent temperature, concentration and surface area, and asked to explain why their modifi cations were mechanistically appropriate. For each modification, they were also asked to run experimen ts and collect data to examine the impact of the fa ctor on reaction rate. This lesson sequence replaced our prior unit designs for Reaction Rates, and became the foundation and first draft of our new unit that is being distributed to the district. Students successfully drew on structural similarities between the two processes Students in Ms L’s class were able to make connections between the two different processes–frogs catching flies and factors affecting reaction rates –by programming and modifying their own models. The student worksheet defined temperature as being molecular  movement speed, and, based on this, all 19/19 stude nts were able to modify the appropriate code in their models and run  trials at different “temperatures”. When asked to describe how temperature affects the rate of a chemical reaction, one pair wrote, “As the temperature increases, the amount of times it takes for the flies to be caught decreases. Because you increased the flies speed. As the temp gets hotter the chemical reactions is faster.” This indicates that the pair made a causal connection by analogy, reasoning that just as faster flies are caught more quickly [because they encounter flogs more frequently], the rate of a chemical reaction will also be faster if the temperature [hence molecular movement speed] is higher. Similarly, 15/19 students were able to modify their  model code to run a series of trials with differen t “concentrations”, linking across the two different processes. One pair wrote, for example, “Concentration affects the rate of a chemical reaction. When you increase the concentration The time decreases because you're  adding more frogs”.

The section of the lesson about surface area was le ss successful in this first iteration, as limiting the extent of frogs/flies range was not part of the initial model code, and most students were not able to add it before the end of class. Nevertheless, 7/19 students were able to reason about the effect of surface area on reaction rate. In her interview after piloting the lessons in her class, Ms L noted that “the analogy was definitely like a win” and that “running this again will be more successful” because she could “pull that reasoning out of students more.” Though she thought it had “a lot of potentia l”, she also thought that “students need a lot more  guidance with it” because it was “very easy to look at the c ode and just see frogs wise and be like, oh, I’m ch anging the speed, but like not take it back to chemistry.”

## Discussion

In this paper, we showed how attending to the struc tural similarity between the two processes–a notably familiar and accessible one, and one targeted in high school  chemistry–enabled teachers to not only gain comfort with programming models but also to integrate science and CT into their teaching. For our teacher partners, the analogy of frogs to reactants allowed them to carry the com fort they had gained with coding over to their peda gogy, as they began to identify areas of value of this work for their students. For the students, intuitions ga ined from reasoning about a familiar phenomenon provided insi ght into an otherwise abstract and unfamiliar conce pt in chemistry.

Goldstone and Wilensky (2008) proposed leveraging CM to support students to generalize across distinct but mechanistically related phenomena, and we demon strated the value in this approach (albeit serendip itously) even across vastly different domains for teachers. Our work highlights the need to focus both on devel oping teachers’ comfort with programming, and providing g enerative experiences that encourage and enable the m to draw on their resources (e.g., Smith et al., 1994; Warren et al., 2001) in ways that relate this new practice to their subject area. That is, while teachers may lack peda gogical content knowledge in new domains and practi ces (e.g Yoon et al. in press), tapping into teachers’ existing domain and pedagogical expertise (in this case, by using this analogy), ultimately enabled them to recognize (and indeed generate) pedagogical value for their students.
