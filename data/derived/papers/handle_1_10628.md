# Emergent Design for Developing Undergraduates’ Understanding of Research in a Knowledge Building Community

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explored the profound significance of undergraduates’ engagement in research activities for fostering innovation and practical skills. Focused on the elective course Introduction to Research Methods at a university, we employed an emergent design in a knowledge-building community. Through dynamic collaboration between teachers and students, we aimed to elevate undergraduates’ understanding of research and facilitate their active involvement in research pursuits. Quantitative findings underscored the marked improvement in students’ research understanding facilitated by this design. Qualitative analysis identified five pivotal themes in students’ research engagement. These insights offer valuable guidance for educational practices, contributing to the cultivation of students’ innovative spirit and practical prowess and advancing the nuanced evolution of scientific research education.

## Introduction

Undergraduate engagement in research is a pivotal a venue for cultivating innovative talents in higher education. Knowledge building underscores the cultivation of students’ epistemic agency, fostering active participation in the learning process and contributing to the contin uous advancement of community knowledge  (Nasir & Lee, 2014; Paavola & Hakkarainen, 2005). This paradigm shifts from passive knowledge reception to active engagement in knowledge creation and sharing collectively fuels the development of community knowledge. The concept of emergent design aligns with these princi ples, emphasizing collaborative co-design between students and teachers. In this joint process, participants e xplore, experiment, adjust, and optimize the learni ng journey, mirroring the dynamics of a research expedition  (Cavallo, 2000; Thompson & MacDonald, 2005). These experiences facilitate a progressive comprehension of the essence of research, providing students with  firsthand exposure to the research process and a continual enhancement of their research outcomes. Therefore, this study is dedicated to empirically exploring the effectivenes s of the Emergent Design of Formulation-IdentificationConceptualization-Peer-Assessment (FICP) within a knowledge-building community to enhance undergraduates’ understanding of re search. The research questions guiding this explora tion are: (1) What characterized undergraduates’ understanding of research during th e emergent design of FICP, and did they demonstrate improvement in their understanding of research? (2) How did undergraduates engage in research activities during the emergent design of FICP?

## Participants

Twenty-five students across majors and grades from a science and engineering university in Shenzhen, Mainland China, participating in the course Introduction to Research Methods, were involved in this project. Data source and analysis We measured students’ understanding of scientific r esearch with a detailed questionnaire that explored  the definition of research, characteristics of high-quality research, and effective methodologies. We e valuated the impact of the course design on undergraduates’ understanding of research by analyzing content ch anges before and after the course, yielding a Cohen’s kappa coef ficient of 0.78. A thematic analysis of Knowledge F orum data—encompassing views, notes, collaborative artifacts, and teaching assistant observations—revealed practical aspects of student engagement and elucidated core t hemes in the learning process, providing insight in to their research approach.

Through content analysis of questionnaire data, the  study identified five levels of students ’ understanding of research, as outlined in Table 1. At the initial le vels (1 and 2), understanding was relatively limite d, focusing on experimental conduct and the presentation and valid ation of viewpoints. Progressing to levels 3 and 4,  students recognized research as a process involving problem-solving, boundary exploration, and emphasizing cont inuity and innovation. At the advanced Level 5, students highlighted the systematic nature of research, emphasizing the need for innovation and possessing both systematic and comprehensive attributes. The quantitative analysis results (see Figure 2) revealed that, befo re the FICP emergent design, 60% of students had an understanding at Level 3, with only 16% reaching Level 4, and none reaching Level 5. However, following the FICP emergent design, 72% of students  achieved Level 5 in their research understanding. Further paired-samples t-test results reveal significant differences between students’ pre-test (M = 2.76, SD = 0.93) and post-test (M = 4.56, SD = 0.77), t(24) = 22.05, p <.001. The findings suggest that undergraduates enhanced their understanding of research during the emergent design of FICP. The qualitative analysis of students’ engagement in research activities, encompassing both Knowledge Forum and offline classroom interactions within the emergent design of FICP, reveals five key themes. Theme 1: Formulating interdisciplinary research groups and articulating research directions In the initial phase  of FICP, undergraduates formed seven interdisciplinary groups based on th eir research interests, defining preliminary research directions across fields such as chemistry, medicin e, and mechanical engineering (see student research groups in Figure 3(a)). Employing diverse research methods, students collaboratively refined their understanding of rese arch topics. For instance, Group 4  focused on “Intelligent Prediction of Risk for Pulmonary Nodule Growth,” utilizing multivariate analysis to explore factors and establish an intelligent prediction model, offering crucial insights for medical decision-making. Theme 2: Navigating research inquiries on Knowledge Forum and identifying key elements of research While exploring projects, group students raised critical research questions on the Knowledge Forum, categorized into nine themes. These encompassed identifying top ics with research potential, refining research ques tions, conducting literature reviews, selecting research m ethods, interpreting res ults, drawing conclusions, addressing unforeseen challenges, structuring research, and preparing research outcomes for publication. For instance, within the “Research Structure” theme, students critically examined the flexibility  of research paradigms, recognizing that a well-designed study need not conform to a fixed framewor k but should align with specific research goals and field characteristics (see inquiries for conducting research in Figure 3(a)). Figure 3 The Welcome Knowledge Forum View of the course (a) and a group view with the OCAR model (b) Theme 3: Enhancing group research narratives through reconstructing storylines Applying the OCAR (Opening, Challenge, Action, Reso lution) funnel model, students meticulously analyze d logical connections within group storylines, refining their research narratives (see Figure 3b). For instance, Group 3 identified a logical gap in their research problem, introducing the “negative capacitance effect” as a new element. This refinement was incorporated into the research question and subsequently reflected in the project ’s title: “Research on the Negative Capacitance Effect of Hafnium Zirconium Oxide Ferroelectric Films.” Theme 4: Dissemination and peer review at an academic symposium After completing initial research projects, the  course instructor organized an “ Shaping the Future ” academic symposium to facilitate research exchange. Using posters and oral presentations, students showcased their research background, objectives, methods, results, and conclusions (see Figure 4(a)). The symposium included a peer-review process where participants critically assess ed group’s research content, fostering valuable feedback and collaborative learning. For instance, Group 3 refined their topic to “Stress-Regulated Ferroelectric Properties in Hafnium Zirconium Oxide Thin Films” based on these discussions, highlighting methodological nuances. Theme 5: Reflective journey and project narratives In the culminating phase of FICP, students engaged in reflective practices, summarizing their research  journey through narratives. They navigated challenges, emph asized growth in critical thinking, problem-solving, and communication, providing insights into individual a nd collective progress. For instance, a Group 3 mem ber employed scaffolds like “My idea,” “My previous idea, ” “My current idea, ” and “Why I changed my idea ” for sustaining research ideas, also utilizing “My question,” “My new question, ” and “My solution” for progressive problem-solving (see Figure 4(b)). Their reflections encompassed perspectives on viewpoint generation, solution gaps, implementation challenges, recognition of research’s systematic nature, and field innovation. ## Discussion

This study investigates the impact of the emergent design of the FICP on undergraduates ’ research acumen and capabilities. The outcomes demonstrate a marked enh ancement in students’ comprehension of research, wh ich evolved from a limited focus on experimentation and validation to recognizing research as an iterative process of problem-solving and extending boundaries, highlighting its systemat ic and expansive nature. Five principal themes were identified in the students’ research engagement, offering valuable insights for educational strategies and reforms. These insights are pivotal for fosteri ng scientific literacy and innovation skills among students, thereby propelling the progress of research educati on. Future research should refine teaching approach es and tactics that bolster students’ understanding and active engagement in research.
