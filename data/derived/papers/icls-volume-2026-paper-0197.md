# Understanding History through Data: Inviting and Supporting Student Personal Negotiation of Historical Data

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Negotiation is a key practice when engaging with both history and data. Drawing on views of history as interpretative and on critical data literacy frameworks that position data as inherently subjective and personal, we argue that through constructing network visualizations in history class, students may begin to experience these views. To explore this argument, we draw on data from two eighth-grade history classes who created network visualizations representing relationships between social roles across ancient civilizations and their modern-day home state. We conducted qualitative coding of students’ negotiation processes and Interaction Analysis of a case episode to understand students’ negotiation. We found that students engaged in a range of negotiation moves about the validity of data in the network, with implications for helping students to make sense of multiple perspectives in history.

History is often presented in schools as a single, fixed narrative, leading students to treat the discipline as a set of names, dates, and events that they need to memorize (Craig et al., 2021; Wineburg, 2010). This view of history contradicts the interpretive view of history held by historians, in which context, authorship, and perspective are important components of understanding historical documents (Barton & Levstik, 2004). In order for students to take up the disciplinary practices of historians and move towards an understanding of history as multi-voiced and subjective, lessons should prioritize the interpretation of historical evidence and consideration of multiple perspectives, including those of the students themselves. Centering student interpretation in this way can help make history feel more relevant to students, as they use their own lenses to make sense of and understand relationships between the past and present (Van Straaten et al., 2016). We propose that engaging with history through data, and specifically, creating their own network visualizations of that data, can help students to take up this interpretive view of history. A network visualization is a collection of nodes and edges that is used to display relationships between data points. Nodes  (circles) refer to the things in the network; edges  (lines) represent a relationship between those nodes  (Figure 1). Through deciding what data to include, determining how different historical data points relate to each other, and justifying those decisions to their peers, we believe that students can experience the subjective and personal nature of presenting and making sense of history (Craig & Danish, 2025). Engaging with historical data allows historians to “look at old evidence in new ways," specifically through identifying interactions between data points (Shreiner, 2018, p. 204). We take a critical data literacy approach to data, viewing it  as inherently personal (Eckert et al., 2022; Kitchin, 2014).  As Pangrazio & Selwyn (2019) explain, “any piece of digital data is subject to ongoing (re)interpretation, (re)use, and (re)application” (p. 420). When  working with  historical data, students should understand that no data are objective and that data visualizations can be imperfect (Polman et al., 2022; Shreiner & Guzdial, 2022). In line with these ideas, the disciplines of history and data literacy share negotiation as a key practice. From a historical perspective, negotiation is required as students understand, accept, and reject information through the lens of their own perspectives (Dikawati et al., 2018). From a data literacy perspective, students must negotiate the meaning of data in various contexts (Dangol & Dasgupta, 2023). In thi s work, we invited students to curate their own historical dataset via network visualization, connecting the past to their present communities. We ask: How do students negotiate their personal understanding of social roles across history and their own communities through network visualization?

This work is part of a multi-year, design-based research study, in which we have partnered with a public school district in the rural, southeastern United States. The school district contains 16 schools that serve approximately 1467 seven thousand students, of which approximately 75-80% qualify for free and reduced-price lunch. In this study, we focus on our collaboration with one middle school history teacher and his two eighth grade class periods that participated in the study during the 2024-2025 school year. Fifteen students in first period and six in second period consented to participate in the research; an additional 32 students participated in the lesson s but did not consent to participate in the research; their data was not analyzed. Students created network visualizations using Net.Create, an online, collaborative software tool designed for the real, time, simultaneous entry and visualization of data (Criag & Danish, 2018; netcreate.org). Net.Create has been used to support students to make personal connections to historical content and their peers in undergraduate (Craig, 2017; Craig et al., 2020) and middle school (Steinberg et al., 2025) history classrooms. The joint co-construction of a dataset requires that students engage with each other’s ideas through negotiation as they display and refine their ideas towards creating a joint class network. As  Craig et al.  (2020) explain, creating network visualizations in Net.Create enables “a multi-voiced interpretation of history that [students] could not have built alone” (p. 534). Net.Create has several features that support negotiation. First, it supports real time updating and display of the data, instantaneously displaying new nodes and edges as they are added and increasing the displayed size of the nodes as their number of edges increases. This feature enables students to notice and debate data added by classmates across the room. Students are also able to add descriptions and types to nodes and edges they add, enabling them to justify and describe their data entry. Additionally, students can leave comments on both nodes and edges, enabling them to express disagreement or ask questions about the data. Finally, provenance tracks who created and updated nodes, helping students to see where data comes from. See the network at https://samplenetworks2025.kalanicraig.com/?dataset=ConsentedModernToAncientSocial#/ Throughout the school year, students in each class period collectively built a network that demonstrated connections between the time period they were learning about and their modern-day home state (Figure 1). The network focused on social roles and hierarchy, asking students to consider 1) what social roles / careers were available at each time (entered as ‘nodes’), 2) where these roles fell in a perceived social hierarchy (entered as an ‘attribute’ to the node), and 3) how the roles related to each other within and across time periods (entered as ‘edges’ between nodes). When students created the edges, they could select ‘relies on’, ‘works with’, ‘works for’, or ‘is similar to’ as connection types, denoted by color, and explain the connection further in a notes field. For example, one student said that History Teacher ‘works with’ Special Ed Teacher because they ‘plan and connect lessons’, while another student specified that a Special Ed Teacher ‘is similar to’ a Muslim Teacher because ‘both teachers, they  are special teachers, they work with children.’ Students considered the first two time periods (students’ own social context: Modern [Home State] and Ancient Mesopotamia) at the beginning of the year (days 1 and 2), and then returned to the network again several weeks later as they learned about Vedic India and Ancient China (days 3 and 4).  They were tasked with adding social roles from each time period/location and connecting 1468 them to jobs in their modern home state. The facilitators (teacher and researchers) aimed to 1) validate disagreement and negotiation as part of historical practice and 2) position history as multi-voiced. Each lesson was video-recorded via Zoom, capturing the teacher and projected screen. Additionally, a researcher was present in the classroom for the first two days of instruction. On those days, in addition to the zoom video, we were able to capture classr oom video footage of students. GoPros were placed on two tables of consented students in each of the two periods (resulting in 4 total focal groups). In this paper, we focus on days 1 and 2 of the implementation, in order to analyze student conversation and negotiation. Additional data sources included the network visualizations and a teacher post interview. We began this analysis with an interest in how, through network visualization, students could connect content learning to their identities and personal experiences (Steinberg et al., 2025). We turned to this dataset because we thought the task of making past and present connections, and how students were positioning themselves, their families, and their communities in relation to the historical data would be analytically rich. We began our analysis process by watching classroom GoPro video of a few small gr oups, looking for evidence of how students seemed to bring the personal into the data. Throughout this process, we identified negotiation as something that seemed to support students in treating data as subjective and personal, and began to categorize the kinds of negotiation that students engaged in, including around nodes and edges. We reviewed the remaining video data-corpus, starting with the full class videos (as a way to understand the negotiations that became salient to the whole class), and then moving into GoPro footage for each of the four small groups across the first and second lessons. During this process, we transcribed talk that appeared to involve negotiation, as defined by reactions and discussions about what was represented in the network a nd how. Using a process inspired by Glaser’s (1965) constant comparative method, we created an emergent, iterative taxonomy of the content, mode, and context of each negotiation, returning to the data as we merged, added, and cemented categories. By content, we mean what in the network visualization students’ negotiations centered around (e.g. nodes/edges). By mode, we mean the kinds of discursive moves that students made while negotiating, and by context, we mean where the negotiation occurred (in a small group, with an adult, or in a whole group discussion). We also attended to overlaps in each category, trying to understand how negotiation was invited and supported. We identified and share a case that cleanly highlights how students engaged in negotiation, treated historical data as interpretive, and engaged in personal negotiation, and unpack it using Interaction Analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). Students engaged in negotiation about different features of both nodes and edges in the network through a series of modes (Table 1). Their negotiations about nodes often entailed conversations about the validity of the node; students called out nodes that did not seem to represent social roles, like “grandpa”, “PTSD”, “or Arby’s.” The resolution of these kinds of negotiations either resulted in the deletion of the node from the network, or a change (e.g., from Arby’s to Arby’s worker). Their negotiations about edges often entailed wondering why certain edges were present. Students asked questions (e.g., “How does the White House have anything to do with construction?”), and expressed disagreement (e.g., “bus driver and bus inspector, okay, but not airplane pilot. That shouldn’t be connected!”) with edges that other groups had entered. In some cases, group members offered a justification (e.g., “airplane pilot is a bus driver in the air”), while in others, they called over an instructor. The students ultimately resolved their negotiation by accepting a suggestion from Lia to include fisherman, and not slaves, because they could all relate to it.

As students engaged with the network visualization, they treated historical data as negotiable. Through their negotiations, they took up several ideas from critical data literacy. First, data are contextualized (Rubin, 2019). That is, different authors of datasets might make different decisions about what to collect and include, just as Zahra wanted to include slaves and Bryanna did not. Data are messy (Wise, 2020). As students constructed their collective network visualization, they had tonegotiate multiple perspectives on the data. Data are personal (Lee et al., 2021). As students engaged in this negotiation, they drew on their own perspectives and values (e.g., “we’re not doing a slave”). Data are not always static (Craig et al., 2021). As a result of their negotiation, students updated and deleted nodes/edges present in the network. Taken together, we saw students engaging in negotiation that leveraged their personal histories and feelings; future work should unpack how engaging in that negotiation informed students’ views of history as multi-voiced and data as central to the study of history. 1470 Barton, K., & Levstik, L.
