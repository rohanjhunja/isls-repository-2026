# STEAM in the Learning Sciences: Foregrounding Heritage Algorithms in the Design of Learning Tools

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
My research examines how cultural practices (e.g., barbering, cosmetology, fashion design) and epistemologies (i.e., ways of knowing) can be learned and sustained while acquiring computer science (CS) skills. I draw on ethnocomputing and leverage heritage algorithms— hybrids of computing and culture—to create simulations of cultural practices using algorithms. These simulations can be used to teach cultural ways of knowing and making as well as CS skills. Overall, my research focuses on sustaining cultural making practices and cultivating the creative aspects of CS education through community-based participatory design research. In this statement, I share my previous work in CS education and ethnocomputing and situate future strands of my work in STEAM and the learning sciences.

Earlier this year the editors of the Journal of the Learning Sciences, Erica Halverson and Leema Berland, asked for the field to expand “what counts as the learning sciences” (2025). Drawing on the field’s commitment to bring together diverse scholars, tools, and disciplines, Halverson and Berland point to this range as the source for continued growth and success in understanding learning and supporting all learners. While the field is fundamentally concerned with learning, what it means to learn, and how to design for learning, there is growing interest and participation in the maker movement and making in educational contexts that is coupled with the large role that the arts play in reimagining teaching and learning (Halverson & Sawyer, 2022; Lachney & Foster 2020). Given this shift to include arts, making, and creativity as central parts to learning, an integrated approach to science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) education has increasingly drawn on the mutual benefit of including the arts to improve STEM pedagogy and create learning environments that foster inclusivity and sociocultural knowledge production. This inclusion of the arts creates “STEAM”, or an integrated approach to science, technology, engineering, art, and mathematics. Technologists and proponents of computing education have long argued that computing serves as a gateway or bridge for creativity in learning contexts by connecting creative expression and STEM learning (Papert,1980; Kafai et al., 2014; Bennett et al., 2016; Bennett, 2021). In early work on ethnocomputing, Eglash and colleagues (2006) demonstrate the affordances of this relationship by creating a suite of tools called Culturally Situated Design Tools—digital tools that explore the computational and mathematical knowledge of textiles, architecture, stamps, and other practices from various Indigenous and vernacular cultures. My work lies at this intersection of culture, arts, learning, and technology and is situated in computer science (CS) education. Through the lens of ethnocomputing, I explore how we can sustain Black cultural ways of knowing, being, and doing while teaching and learning computational skills, highlighting the bidirectional relationship between computing and culturally situated making (Allen Kuyenga, Gladney, et al., 2023; Allen Kuyenga, Lachney, et al., 2023).

General Framework: Ethnocomputing in STEAM Ethnocomputing recognizes the ways in which computing knowledge exists outside of Western assumptions of what counts as computing knowledge and challenges the dichotomies between cultural and technical knowledge and knowledge production. I explore the unique relationships between ethnocomputing and STEAM concepts like computational thinking (CT) through “contact zones” (Lachney et al., 2021). Contact zones are those epistemic encounters between different cultures where learning and creating are done in light of histories of violence and extraction (Allen Kuyenga, Lachney, et al., 2023). These zones allow for multidirectional translations between computing and cultural ways of knowing, being, and doing. These translations offer a buffer against CT and computing’s tendencies to subsume cultural lifeways and reproduce logics of imperialism, including assimilation and extraction (Lachney et al., 2021). Ethnocomputing offers an alternative way of thinking about how we engage historically excluded groups of people and their knowledge in computing and computing education. One way I do this is by leveraging “heritage algorithms.” Bennett (2016) defines heritage algorithms as hybrids of computation and culture that circulate intergenerational value within communities. Indeed, heritage algorithms demonstrate how Indigenous and vernacular communities have developed and maintained technological creativity and ingenuity. E xamples of heritage algorithms can be found in cornrow braiding (Bennett, 2016), cosmetology (Lachney et al., 2019), barbering (Allen Kuyenga, Gladney, et al., 2023), and textiles (Bennett, 2016; Allen Kuyenga, 2025). These examples have offered a systematic way to analyze computational representations of creative expressions and cultural knowledge systems related to making and creativity. In this research, I ask two main questions. First, I ask: how can we leverage CS education to sustain the making, communal, and relational knowledge practices found within Black communities? Second, I ask: what computational knowledge, embedded within the making and communal practices of Black peoples, can be leveraged to teach CS skills? These questions guide my collaborations with artists and learners, my design of learning environments and tools, and the analysis of teaching and learning that occur in these spaces and/or with these tools. To answer these questions, I employ Design-Based Research (DBR) as a primary methodological framework, because its iterative pattern of design and reflection is useful for understanding how, when, and why innovations work in complex contexts, like classrooms (The Design Based Research Collective, 2003; Kafai, 2005; Hoadley & Campos, 2022). DBR in conjunction with participatory methods support my commitment to design with, rather than for, communities, positioning students, teachers, and artisans as co-designers and coresearchers (Allen Kuyenga, 2025). These collaborative processes also allow me to investigate what happens when knowledge is exchanged within contact zones using ethnographic methods to attend to specific cultural and relational dimensions of learning and design. Lastly, I leverage narrative analysis to trace participant meaning making and creative expression wile engaged in STEM education. Future research At the start of my career and as I formally enter the Learning Sciences community, I bring a research agenda that foregrounds computational learning as culturally grounded and relational in nature. I position my work in this community as part of a broader effort to “expand what counts as learning” and more specifically to expand what counts as CS learning and CS knowledge. To this end, I and my colleagues (2023) have previously presented pathways as an agentive alternative to the pipeline metaphor common in computing education. The concept of pathways constructs alternative understandings of what counts as computing that privilege student identities and desired paths—the expansive routes to engaging with computing through various entry points like barbering, cosmetology, and fashion design.

I will continue pursuing new pathways by developing other community-based participatory design research projects to create spaces and tools from the knowledge and perspective of community members, like artisans and designers. While it is still in the earliest stages, I am developing this work in community with others who share the priorities of bringing Black epistemologies and making practices to bear on how we teach, learn, and imagine CS education. I aim to co-design a series of virtual tools and curricular materials that integrate making practices, computational knowledge, and community perspectives on computing to provide robust educational materials for youth and adult learners in and around the U.S. state of Pennsylvania in cities like Pittsburgh, Harrisburg, and Philadelphia. These projects will work to meet the need of culturally sustaining CS education and provide opportunities for intergenerational learning in CS learning contexts. I hope that this work also brings together researchers, communities, and practitioners to have generative dialogue around what it means to do this work and commit to sustaining cultural making practices in a climate that increasingly devalues and erases these practices, their relevant knowledge systems, and their histories.
