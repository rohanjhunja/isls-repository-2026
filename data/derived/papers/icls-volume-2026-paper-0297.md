# Linguistic Cues in Instructor and AI Feedback: Linking Language to Student Motivation

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examined how linguistic cues in instructor feedback relate to students’ motivation and how these patterns differ between human and AI-facilitated feedback. Feedback from 123 undergraduates in an introductory educational psychology course was analyzed using the General Inquirer and Lasswell dictionaries. Correlations between linguistic features and students’ perceived attainment, interest, and utility values revealed that cogn itively rich and action-oriented language (e.g., problem-solving, active verbs) enhanced motivation, while doctrinal and ideological language reduced it. Relational cues expressing affiliation fostered interest value, whereas loss-focused phrasing undermined attainment value. Comparisons showed that AI feedback used more cognitive and affective expressions but also more abstract ideological framing. These findings highlight the motivational importance of feedback tone: effective feedback should be confident yet collaborative and specific yet empathetic. Designing AI systems that balance analytical precision with relational authenticity may better sustain student motivation in feedback-rich environments.

Feedback plays a central role in shaping students’ learning and motivation. It guides reflection, fosters self-regulation, and communicates expectations and values between instructors and learners (Hattie & Timperley, 2007; Nicol & Macfarlane‐Dick, 2006). Beyond its informational function, feedback language conveys subtle psychological cues —about competence, effort, and relational support —that influence students’ affective and motivational responses (Carless & Boud, 2018).

With the emergence of generative artificial intelligence (AI), instructors increasingly use AI tools to generate or refine feedback (Henderson et al., 2025). While AI offers efficiency and linguistic fluency, it remains unclear how its feedback differs fro m human instructors’ in motivationally relevant ways. Recent studies show that linguistic tone and stance strongly affect how students perceive feedback credibility and emotional tone (Derham et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025), yet few have systematically analyzed how such linguistic cues relate to students’ motivational values or compared them across human and AI-generated feedback. Grounded in the expectancy –value theory of motivation (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000), this study investigates how specific linguistic features in instructor feedback predict students’ perceived attainment, interest, and utility values. It further examines how these motivationally relevant linguistic patterns differ between humanwritten and AI-facilitated feedback. By combining computational linguistic analysis with motivational measures, the study seeks to illuminate how language in feedback supports —or hinders —student motivation in AI-assisted learning environments. This integration advances understanding of how linguistic tone functions as a psychological signal in educational communication, bridging learning analytics and motivational theory. The research questions are:

1. Which linguistic features of instructor feedback are associated with students’ attainment, interest, and

utility values?

2. How do human feedback and AI-facilitated feedback differ in the linguistic features that influence

students’ motivation?

## Participants and context

Participants were 123 undergraduate students enrolled in an introductory educational psychology course at a large Midwestern university in the United States. The course emphasized foundational theories of learning, motivation, and their classroom applications. Six instructors participated in the study. Within each class section, instructors randomly assigned half of the students to receive human-only feedback and the other half to receive AI-facilitated feedback on their reflective writing assignments. For the AIfacilitated condition, instructors first generated initial feedback drafts using Custom ChatGPT developed by the 1977 researcher (He, 2025), which were then reviewed and refined by the instructor before being returned to students. This procedure ensured consistency in feedback quality while allowing comparison between human-only and AIfacilitated feedback conditions. Following the feedback experience, all students completed a motivation survey designed to measure changes in their motivation and perceptions of feedback credibility. Linguistic analysis To examine linguistic characteristics, feedback texts were analyzed using the General Inquirer (GI) and Lasswell Value Dictionary,  which capture a wide range of linguistic dimensions related to cognition, affect, social interaction, and agency. Linguistic features were computed using the SEANCE (https://www.linguisticanalysistools.org/seance.html, Crossley et al., 2017) tool. A total of 182 features (119 GI dictionary, 63 Lasswell dictionary) were extracted for each feedback file. Features with fewer than 30 nonzero data points were excluded to ensure sufficient variation. For Research Question 1, Pearson correlations were computed between each linguistic feature and students’ motivational variables (attainment, interest, and utility). Given the exploratory nature of this analysis and the large number of features relative to sample size, uncorrected correlations were used to identify candidate features for further conceptual analysis. Results should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Features showing meaningful effect sizes were retained for review. Two researchers independently examined these features and grouped them into three conceptual categories—cognitive, relational, and agency-related—based on their psychological meanings and theoretical alignment with the feedback literature. Disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. For Research Question 2, Welch’s t-tests were used to compare the mean frequencies of these motivationally relevant linguistic features between human feedback and AI-facilitated feedback, allowing for unequal variances between groups.

Student motivation survey Students’ perceived task value of feedback, adapted from Eccles and Wigfield’s (1995) expectancy–value model, was used to measure motivational outcomes. The construct included three components: interest, utility, and attainment values. Interest value, refl ecting how engaging students found the feedback, was measured with two items such as “In general, I find reading this feedback (very boring –very interesting).” Utility value, which captures the perceived usefulness of the feedback for future learning goals, was assessed with two items including “In general, how useful do you find the feedback you receive in this course? (not at all useful –very useful).” Attainment value, which focuses on the importance of performing well and receiving high-quality feedback, was measured with two items such as “For me, receiving high-quality feedback on my work is (not at all important – very important).” All items were rated on 7-point Likert-type scales, and higher scores indicated greater motivational value. Each subscale demonstrated strong internal reliability (Cronbach’s α >.80). RQ1: Linguistic features associated with students’ motivation The correlation analysis revealed distinct linguistic cues linked to students’ motivational beliefs (see Figure 1). Among cognitive cues, feedback containing problem-solving language (Solve_GI, r  =.25, p  =.008) and enlightenment or learning-outcome terms (Enlends_Lasswell, r =.19, p  =.040) were positively related to attainment values, suggesting that cognitively rich language helps students perceive mastery and growth. Comparison framing (Compare_GI, r  =.19, p =.039) correlated with utility values, indicating that comparative reflection enhances perceived usefulness. In contrast, doctrinal or rigid language (Doctrin_GI, r = −.19, p =.043) was negatively associated with attainment, implying that an authoritarian tone dampens motivation. Relational cues showed a similar pattern. Affiliative affect (Afftot_Lasswell, r =.21, p =.025) positively associated with interest, whereas ideological or political framing (Polit_2_GI, r  = −.21, p =.023; r  = −.18, p =.047) was negatively associated with both interest and attainment. Among agency-related features, action verbs (Active_GI, r  =.21, p =.027) were positively associated with attainment, while references to loss or regression (Trnloss_Lasswell,  r = −.20, p =.039) were negatively associated with it.

Collectively, these findings suggest that feedback using cognitive guidance, relational warmth, and action-oriented cues tends to support students’ motivational values, whereas abstract or deficit-focused phrasing may undermine them.

Overall, AI feedback was more cognitively and affectively expressive, emphasizing problem-solving, action, and positivity, but also more ideological and abstract, consistent with the stylistic tendencies of large language models. These findings highlight that linguistic features in feedback—whether human or AI-generated—carry significant motivational implications. Though no previous studies have directly examined the relationship between specific linguistic features of feedback and  distinct motivational values, the present findings align with broader research linking feedback tone to student engagement (e.g., Fong & Schallert, 2023). Feedback that affirms competence and supports autonomy tends to enhance student motivation, whereas feedback perceived as controlling, unclear, or disrespectful has been shown to undermine motivation and engagement (Weidlich et al., 2025). The human –AI comparison reveals a dual pattern: AI feedback tends to amplify both strengths and weaknesses of motivational language. It enhances cognitive clarity and positivity but risks sounding formulaic or impersonal, which candecrease social connect ion. These results extend recent findings that emphasize the need to align AI-generated feedback with human communicative and relational practices (Author, 2025). From a pedagogical perspective, feedback should be linguistically confident yet collaborative, specific yet empathetic, and motivationally supportive rather than prescriptive.  Instructors using AI tools should review and refine AI-generated feedback to maintain warmth and contextual relevance. Future work should explore adaptive AI systems that dynamically calibrate tone to student needs, supporting both the cognitive and affec tive dimensions of learning.
