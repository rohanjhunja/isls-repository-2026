# How Transactivity and Regulation of Learning Co-Construct Socio-Emotional Climate in Collaborative Groups

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The study explores how transactivity in different phases of regulation of learning unfolds in a six-week online collaboration and their association with the socio-emotional climate of the group. Video recordings of two case groups were coded and analyzed using qualitative content analysis and Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA). The findings indicated the role of quick consensus building in regulation of learning, suggesting associations between transactivity and regulation to be meaningful in constructing a group’s emotional climate.

## Background and aims

Computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) processes have been widely studied for  several decade s. Social-cognitive aspects, such as transactive quality of the discourse (Wei nberger & Fischer, 2006), have been regarded as critical for deepening the shared under standing among learners. Besides, shared metacognitive processes, namely socially shared regulation of learning (SSRL), also play a key role in CSCL by enabling learners to coordinate cognitive tasks and maintain a positive relational climate to strive for a shared learning goal (Järvelä & Hadwin, 2013; Molenaar & Chiu, 2014). Both cognitive and metacognitive aspects of collabo ration relate to the quality of social interactions and altogether c ontribute to a group’s socio-emotional climate (Kirschner et al., 2015; Sinha et al., 2015). However, the relationship between these elements and socio-emotional aspects in collaborative learning remains underexplored. This study addresses t his gap by exploring how transactivity unfolds across different SSRL phases in CSCL and how it relates to the group's socio-emotional climate.

## Participants, data, and case group selection

Participants were international master’s degree  students (N = 38; 11 men, 27 women) who enrolled in a CSCL course conducted as a collaboration between universities in Finland, Germany and the Netherlands in 2021. They worked in small groups of five for six weeks to collaboratively write a scientific article. Group work was organized (a)synchronously using Microsoft Teams, and students’ meetings were video recorded. Additionally, after each online meeting, students completed questionnaires evaluating their collaboration process. To observe varying socio-emotional moments, two case groups were selected with the following criteria. First, questionnaire responses (rated on a four-point Likert scale) were analyzed to assess student s' subjective evaluations of their learning. Second, final course grades (ranging from 0 to 5)  provided an objective measure of learning outcomes. Based on these criteria,  two groups were selected: Group A (questionnaire M = 3.66, SD = 0.47; grades M = 5, SD = 0) and Group B (questionnaire M = 2.22, SD = 0.41; grades M = 2.2, SD = 0.97). Analysis Online meetings (6,4h and 6,52h each) were coded at  utterance level (5,360 turns) using three coding schemes: Transactivity, SSRL phase and Socio-emotional climate. In this study, we focused on dif ferent modes of transactivity, namely, consensus building: quick consensus building (QC), integration-oriented consensus building (INT), and conflict-oriented consensus building (CON) based on the framework of Weinberger & Fischer (2006). SSRL phase coding refers to Zimmerman’s cyclical model of self-regulated learning (2008), including forethought (FORE), performance (PERF), and reflect ion (REF) phases. Other task-related interactions without regulation (No-Reg) and off-task interactions were additionally coded. Socio-emotional climate (positive, negative and mixed) was identified from students’ verbal and non-verbal cues (see Järvenoja et al., 2018). A Chi-square test of independence was computed in SPSS (v ersion 26) to examine the distribution of transactivity modes across SSRL phases. Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA; Shaffer et al., 2016) was conducted using the ENA Web Tool (version 1.7.0) to capture the association of transactivity and SSRL with socio-emotional climates. The window stanza was set to three considering the nature of interaction among the members as QC can quickly interrupt interactions. Differences between the case groups were tested with a Mann-Whitney test.

## Discussion and conclusion

This study explored how groups with varying performance levels interacted during online CSCL, with a focus on transactivity in groups’ regulatory phases and its association with socio-emotional climate. While integration-and conflict-oriented consensus building is regarded important in knowledge construction, qu ick consensus building supports SSRL. Conflict-oriented discussions during the forethought phase  may trigger negative emotions, but they could enhance group performance if members engage in critical yet constructive dialogue that  fosters joint coordination and mitigates negative emotions. The findings highlight the role of transactivity in regulation to shape positive socio-emotional climates. Further qualitative research is needed to fully explore these phenomena.
