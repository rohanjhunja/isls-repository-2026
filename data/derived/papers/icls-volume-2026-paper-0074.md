# Exploring the Mediating Role of Motivation in the Relationship Between Sense of Belonging and Intention to Leave STEM Majors

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Intention to leave STEM majors remains a challenge, particularly in Latin America, where psychosocial predictors of persistence are underexplored. Grounded in Self-determination Theory, this study examined whether motivation mediates the relationship between sense of belonging and inte ntion to leave a STEM major. Participants were 945 undergraduates from a private Mexican university who provided self-reports on sense of belonging (SB), intrinsic motivation (IM), extrinsic motivation (EM), and intention to leave STEM (ITL). Structural Equation Modeling indicated that SB was positively associated with IM and negatively associated with ITL, while showing no significant association with EM. IM was negatively associated with ITL and partially mediated the relationship between SB and ITL. EM showed no significant associations with ITL. Overall, the model explained 9% of the variance in ITL. Findings highlight the importance of socially supportive learning environments for fostering intrinsic motivation and persistence-related intentions in STEM majors.

Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) programs are widely recognized as engines of innovation, economic growth, and technological advancement (World Economic Forum, 2020). However, despite their importance, STEM programs globally face persistent challenges in student retention. Comparative studies indicate that between 40 % and 60 % of STEM students worldwide drop out during the first two years of their programs, and that attrition in these fields remains among the highest across higher education disciplines, particularly in engin eering and technology,  highlighting a critical vulnerability in the early stages of higher education (Casanova et al., 2023; Chen, 2013; Geisinger & Raman, 2013). This global trend is also reflected in Latin America, where an enduring structural imbalance persists: although opportunities to enter STEM programs have widened, completion and retention rates continue to lag, especially for students from historically marginalized groups (Comisión Económica para América Latina y el Caribe [CEPAL], 2024; Organization for E conomic Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2025).  This challenge has direct implications for the development of skilled human capital and for national competitiveness in science and technology. Reflecting this broader regional trend, Mexico exhibits similarly low persistence rates, with engineering and technology programs showing especially modest levels of degree completion—only about six in ten students finish their degrees (Asociación Nacional de Universidades e Instituciones de Educación Superior [ANUIES], 202 4)—while OECD indicators show that Mexico’s tertiary completion rate (31%) continues to fall below the average of member countries (OECD, 2021). Although national statistics typically focus on public higher education, the private sector— which enrolls nearly one-third of Mexican university students (ANUIES, 2023) —also faces significant challenges in retaining students in STEM majors. However, evidence from these institutions remains limited. Preliminary data suggest that attrition rates in private universities may be comparable to those in public institutions. For example, a private university in Puebla reported that more than 45% of first-year engineering students dropped out during their first year (Cuautle, 2023), while in public institutions such as the University of Guadalajara’s Engineering Center (CUCEI), cumulative dropout across cohorts from 2014 to 2019 reached approximately 47% (Orozco-Rodríguez et al., 2025). These figures show that private universities also face barriers to STEM persistence, highlighting the need for retention strategies tailored to their students’ academic, socioeconomic, and cultural contexts. To fully understand why students  stay or leave their STEM major, it is alsonecessary to examine the psychological and social processes that shape their engagement and commitment. Beyond structural factors (e.g., socioeconomic background or institutional conditions), psychological and social determinants have gained attention as critical for persistence in STEM (Kuchynka et al., 2019; Ribeiro et al., 2025). Among these, the sense of belonging—the feeling of being valued, accepted, and supported within an academic community—emerges repeatedly as a strong predictor of persistence. Students who report higher levels of belonging tend to show greater engagement, motivation, and academic success, whereas perceptions of exclusion or isolation are linked to a higher risk of dropout (Fong et al., 2024; Hausmann et al., 2007; Pedler et al., 2021; Walton & Cohen, 2011). 632 Self-Determination Theory (SDT) offers a robust framework for investigating how motivation mediates the relationship between a student’s sense of belonging and their academic persistence (Deci & Ryan, 1985). This study adopts SDT as its grounding framework primarily because the theory identifies relatedness—the innate need to feel connected to and accepted by others—as a fundamental “nutrient” for psychological growth and behavioral stamina. Furthermore, the adoption of a mediational structure is grounded in Vallerand’s (1997) “sequence of motivation,” which posits that social-contextual factors influence behavioral outcomes through the satisfaction of basic psychological needs and the resulting quality of motivation. Within this mediational sequence, the specific quality of a student's motivation is best understood through a continuum of internalization rather than a simple binary. At one end of this continuum, intrinsic motivation involves engaging in learning out of genuine interest or inherent satisfaction. This state is consistently associated with higher-quality engagement and is  a primary driver of long-term persistence (Howard et al., 2021; Hwang, 2024; Lavigne et al., 2007). In contrast, extrinsic motivation refers to performing an activity to obtain a separable outcome, such as grades, career recognition, or social expectations. While external drivers are often viewed as less stable, SDT suggests they can be internalized into personally valued goals. This makes extrinsic motivation a critical  factor in performance-oriented STEM environments where external benchmarks are prevalent (Bayanova et al., 2023; Howard et al., 2016). Exploring this full motivational continuum provides a granular lens to observe how external social support becomes an internal driver of academic persistence. The practical importance of this internal process is particularly evident when examining the evolving research landscape in Mexico. In this context, recent studies have begun to examine contextual factors in STEM retention—such as faculty support, institut ional culture, or classroom climate (Melendez-Anzures et al, 2025; Morán-Soto et al., 2022; Ortiz -Martínez et al., 2023). While these studies have advanced understanding of contextual influences, the psychological processes through which students decide to persist or leave remain underexplored. By applying the SDT framework to this specific population, the present study addresses this gap, offering a necessary bridge between institutional conditions and the psychological dimensions of persistence in STEM education.

Accordingly, this study examines whether motivation mediates the relationship between students’ sense of belonging and their intention to leave STEM majors within a Mexican higher-education context. The central question driving this study is: Does motivation mediate the relationship between students’ sense of belonging and their intention to leave STEM majors?

Based on prior evidence, the following hypotheses were proposed:

• H1. Sense of belonging will be negatively related to intention to leave STEM majors.

• H2. Intrinsic motivation mediates the relationship between sense of belonging and intention to leave

a STEM major.

• H3. Extrinsic motivation mediates the relationship between sense of belonging and intention to leave

a STEM major.

## Participants

A total of 945 undergraduate students from a private higher education institution in Mexico were recruited using non-probabilistic sampling across four STEM disciplines. The sample included 613 males (64.9%) and 332 females (35.1%). Participants ranged in age from 18 to 23 years, with a mean age of 19.88 years (SD = 1.51). Although the sample presented a gender imbalance with a predominance of male participants, this distribution reflects the national trend in Mexico, where men continue to represent the majority in STEM enrollment (ANUIES, 2024).

## Measures

Sense of belonging The Sense of Belonging in Major Scale (London et al., 2011) was adapted to measure students’ feelings of acceptance and comfort within their academic major. The scale consists of six items (e.g., “I feel well received in this major”; Cronbach’s α =.86, McDonald’s ω =.87) rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) showed an acceptable fit to the data (Satorra–Bentler adjusted chi-square [SBχ 2] = 24.42, df  = 5, p <.001; Standardized Root Mean Square Residual [SRMR] =.02; Tucker–Lewis Index [TLI] =.98; Comparative Fit Index [CFI] =.99; Root Mean Square Error of Approximation [RMSEA] =.06, 90% Confidence Interval [CI] [.04,.08]).

## Motivation goal orientation

The Motivation Scale from the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (Duncan et al., 2015) was used to measure two dimensions of goal orientation: Intrinsic Orientation (four items; e.g., “I prefer class material that really challenges me so that I can learn new things”; α =.66, ω =.68) and Extrinsic Orientation (four items; e.g., “If I can, I want to get better grades than most of the other students”; α =.77, ω =.78). Responses were provided on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The initial two-dimensional model showed inadequate fit to the data (SBχ 2 = 165.96, df = 19, p <.001; SRMR =.04; TLI =.88; CFI =.92; RMSEA =.09, 90% CI [.07,.10]). Therefore, model fit was improved by examining the item factor loadings. One item from each dimension was eliminated for failing to meet the ≥.50 criterion established in the literature (Hair et al., 2019). After these adjustments, the CFA indicated good model fit (χ2 = 21.47, df = 8, p =.006; SRMR =.03; CFI =.99; TLI =.98; RMSEA =.04, 90% CI [.02,.06]). Intention to leave stem This variable was measured using the scale developed by Findley-Van Nostrand and Pollenz  (2017). The instrument consists of four items assessing students’ expressed likelihood or consideration of leaving their major in STEM fields (e.g., “I have considered changing to a major that is NOT in STEM areas”; α  =.93, ω  =.93). Participants responded on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The CFA indicated excellent model fit (SBχ 2 = 3.33, df = 1, p =.068; SRMR =.01; TLI =.99; CFI =.99; RMSEA =.04, 90% CI [.01,.06]).

## Procedure

The study was conducted in accordance with high ethical and methodological standards. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee, which granted exemption status (ID: EHE-2023-05) based on its classification as low risk. This approval ensured full compliance with institutional, national, and international ethical guidelines, safeguarding participants’ rights, confidentiality, and informed consent. As part of the approved protocol,  the research team was authorized by the ethics committee to contact students through the institution’s official academic email database, in accordance with data protection and privacy regulations. Participation was entirely voluntary, and no financial, ac ademic, or material compensation was offered. Before accessing the questionnaire, participants were presented with an electronic informed consent form integrated into the Qualtrics platform describing the study’s objectives, its voluntary nature, and participants’ right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Only those who provided digital consent could proceed to the survey. Before large-scale deployment, a pilot test was conducted with a small subset of students to verify item clarity and contextual relevance. After minor adjustments, the finalized questionnaire was distributed via institutional email to all eligible undergraduate students enrolled in STEM programs, accompanied by a concise explanation of the study’s purpose and ethical assurances. Reminder emails were sent at predetermined intervals between November and December 2023 to improve participation rates. All responses we re anonymous and securely stored on encrypted, password-protected institutional servers with restricted access to the research team. The data collection process was systematically monitored to ensure completeness and reliability, and only anonymized and cleaned data were used for statistical analyses, with all results reported exclusively in aggregated form to preserve participants’ privacy.

## Data analysis

Preliminary analysis Means, standard deviations, skewness, kurtosis, and Pearson correlations were calculated for all variables included in the study. According to Hancock, Mueller, and Stapleton (2010), values of < |2.0| for skewness and < |7.0| for kurtosis were considered indicators of normal distribution. Pearson correlation coefficients were interpreted based on effect size criteria proposed by Funder and Ozer (2019), where correlations of.10 represent small effects,.20 moderate effects, and.30 relatively large effects in social science research. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) A structural model with latent variables was estimated using JASP 18.3. The Robust Maximum Likelihood estimation method (MLR) was employed to ensure that parameter estimates were not affected by violations of multivariate normality (Byrne, 201 2; Hancock & Liu, 2012). Based on prior literature, the following fit indices were used: SB χ 2 (p >.001), TLI (≥.95), CFI (≥.95), SRMR (≤.08), and RMSEA (≤.08) (Brown, 2015; Byrne, 2012). The model tested the direct and indirect effects of sense of belonging on the intention to leave STEM, mediated by intrinsic and extrinsic motivation.

To better contextualize these findings, the following sections examine the role of each proposed relationship in shaping students’ intention to leave STEM. 636 The relationship of sense of belonging on intention to leave STEM The results revealed that a sense of belonging was negatively associated with students’ intention to leave their STEM majors, supporting hypothesis H1. This finding underscores the protective role of belonging in academic persistence, suggesting that students who perceive themselves as valued members of their academic community are less likely to consider leaving their programs. Within the framework of SDT, this result is coherent with the notion that satisfaction of the relatedness need enhances psychological well-being and academic engagement, thereby reducing intentions to leave (Deci & Ryan, 2012). A strong sense of belonging may buffer students against feelings of isolation and self-doubt—common deterrents to persistence in STEM contexts —by fostering a sense of purpose and internalized commitment to their academic goals. Previous research has similarly demonstrated that sense of belonging is a critical determinant of persistence and retention among underrepresented and general STEM populations (Hansen et al., 2024; Johnson et al., 2020; Pedler et al., 2021), supporting the interpretation that fostering inclusive and supportive academic climates can meaningfully decrease attrition risk. Mediating role of intrinsic motivation Mediation analysis revealed that intrinsic motivation partially mediates the relationship between sense of belonging and intention to leave STEM, providing partial support for hypothesis H2. This suggests that part of the association between perceived sense of belonging and intention to leave STEM operates through the activation of intrinsic motivational processes. In other words, higher education students with a greater  sense of belonging tend to report higher levels of intrinsic motivation and lower intention to leave STEM majors. This finding suggests that students who feel accepted and connected within their academic environment tend to exhibit a genuine interest in learning and a commitment to personal growth. This pattern aligns with the principles of SDT and other scholars, who posit that satisfaction of the need for relatedness fosters autonomous forms of motivation (Deci & Ryan, 2012; Pedler et al., 2021; Ryan & Deci, 2000). The items assessing intrinsic motivation in this study captured curiosity-driven and mastery-oriented engagement —for instance, students’ preference for challenging material and their enjoyment in understanding content deeply—characteris tics consistent with autonomous and self-determined learning behaviors.

In addition, we confirmed a negative association between intrinsic motivation and intention to leave STEM majors. This indicates that students motivated by curiosity, enjoyment, or personal interest show lower intentions to abandon their studies. This evidence converges with prior research linking intrinsic motivation to academic persistence and student well-being (Deci et al., 1991; Howard et al., 2021; Taylor et al., 2014). Our research focused on private higher education students and confirmed that a sense of belonging was associated with intentions to leave STEM, mediated by intrinsic motivation. Therefore, how to increase individual intrinsic motivation should receive more attention.

Mediating role of extrinsic motivation Conversely, extrinsic motivation did not show a significant mediating relationship, suggesting that controlled forms of motivation do not constitute a key mechanism linking sense of belonging to intentions to leave STEM majors, indicating that hypothesis H3 was not supported. The results of this study showed that a sense of belonging is not significantly associated  with extrinsic motivation. This result indicates that the experience of belonging does not necessarily heighten interest in external rewards such as grades or recognition. Similar findings have been reported in prior studies (Cerasoli et al., 2014; Howard et al., 2021), showing that sense of belonging is more strongly linked to intrinsic motivation, whereas its associations with extrinsic motivation are weak or inconsistent. Furthermore, extrinsic motivation did not show a significant relationship with intention to leave STEM majors. This finding aligns with evidence indicating that external rewards or pressures may enhance short-term performance but do not sustain long-term persistence when motives are not internalized (Cerasoli et al., 2014; Deci et al., 2001; Howard et al., 2021; Lavigne et al., 2007). Nevertheless, some research has observed positive effects of extrinsic motivation on persistence when rewards are perceived as informative rather than controlling, particularly in rigorous evaluation contexts (Deci et al., 2001; Kusurkar et al., 2013). These discrepancies suggest that the institutional environment may modulate the strength of these relationships. It is worth noting that the extrinsic motivation construct used in this study primarily captured external and introjected regulation—forms of motivation characterized by grades, competition, and the desire to demonstrate ability to others. These represent the most controlled forms of extrinsic regulation within SDT. Consequently, their non-significant relationship with intention to leave STEM may be attributed to this operationalization, as such externally driven motives typically predict short-term engagement rather than enduring persistence.

## Implications for STEM education and learning

This study underscores that fostering students’ sense of belonging is fundamental for sustaining intrinsic motivation and persistence in STEM education. Beyond their psychological relevance, these constructs have direct implications for how learning is des igned, supported, and experienced in higher education. From a learning sciences perspective, sense of belonging and intrinsic motivation function as key enablers of deeper cognitive engagement, self-regulated learning, and collaborative knowledge construct ion—processes essential for meaningful learning in STEM contexts.

Extending SDT to the Latin American context, the findings reveal that intrinsic motivation mediates the relationship between sense of belonging and intention to leave STEM majors, emphasizing the importance of supporting students’ relatedness through instructional design and pedagogical practice. Instructors can translate these mechanisms into concrete strategies by fostering inclusive classroom climates that encourage participation and normalize academic struggle as part of learning. Learning environments that privilege mastery goals and peer collaboration are likely to enhance both a sense of belonging and intrinsic motivation. For higher education institutions, this underscores the need to cultivate autonomy-supportive and socially connected learning ecosystems in which students feel valued, competent, and self-directed. Faculty development programs should prioritize training in mentoring, formative feedback, and collaborative learning practices that promote relatedness and autonomy (Niemiec & Ryan, 2009; Reeve, 2016; Walton & Cohen, 2011). Similarly, universities candesign peer mentorship networks, learning communities, and motivational workshops that intentionally integrate social and academic support, particularly for women and first-generation students who may experience a weaker sense of belonging in competitive STEM environments (Melendez-Anzures et al, 2025; Morán-Soto et al., 2022; Ortiz-Martínez et al., 2023).

In STEM programs across Mexico and Latin America—where dropout rates remain high—localized, culturally grounded interventions that combine motivational and pedagogical dimensions are essential. Examples include project-based learning anchored in real-world challenges, group-based problem solving, and structured peer feedback sessions that build both competence and connection.  To implement such interventions effectively, teacher education and faculty development programs should emphasize relational and motivational competencies, helping instructors design learning environments that foster students’ sense of belonging, autonomy, and purpose as integral components of effective STEM teaching. Ultimately, these findings suggest that long-term persistence in STEM is best achieved through learning environments that nurture connection, autonomy, and purpose, rather than through reliance on external incentives or performance pressure (Cerasoli et al., 2014; Vansteenkiste et al., 2004).

## Limitations and future research

Although this study offers valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. Its cross-sectional design limits causal inference among the variables analyzed; longitudinal or experimental research is needed to trace how belonging and motivation evolve and influence persistence over time. Additionally, reliance on self-reported measures may introduce perceptual biases; future studies should integrate objective indicators such as actual dropout rates or academic performance to enhance validity. Mo reover, given the relatively large sample size, statistically significant results should be interpreted with caution, as some observed effects were modest in magnitude. The sample, drawn from a single private Mexican university, limits generalizability; therefore, replication across public institutions and other Latin American contexts is necessary to examine whether these motivational dynamics vary across cultural and institutional settings. Motivational structural-equation modeling remains underexplored in the region, making its further development essential for designing evidence-based retention strategies. Future research should also differentiate among subtypes of extrinsic motivation (identified, introjected, and external) and examine contextual variables such as self-efficacy, teacher support, and institutional engagement to deepen understanding of the motivational mechanisms linked to STEM persistence. Additionally, future studies could test moderated mediation models, with gender or socioeconomic status as moderators, to examine whether the relationships among belonging, motivation, and persistence vary  across student subgroups. The findings provide empirical evidence of the motivational processes associated with the relationship between sense of belonging and intention to leave STEM programs in the Mexican higher education context. Although the mean level of intention to leave STEM was relatively low (M = 1.62), the structural model  showed significant associations between psychological variables and this outcome. This suggests that even in contexts where students generally intend to persist in STEM, individual differences in variables such as sense of belonging and motivation still play an important role in explaining variations in intention to leave. The structural model showed that sense 638 of belonging was positively associated with intrinsic motivation and negatively associated with intention to leave STEM majors, with intrinsic motivation acting as a partial mediator. In contrast, extrinsic motivation did not show significant effects, which may reflect contextual characteristics of the institution studied or measurement limitations rather than a complete absence of influence. These results suggest that internalized forms of extrinsic motivation could still support persistence when external incentives align with students’ personal values and professional aspirations. In contrast, more  controlling rewards appear less effective for sustaining long-term commitment. Theoretically, this study expands the understanding of motivational dynamics in Latin American STEM education by showing that a sense of belonging and intrinsic motivation jointly explain patterns of persistence. In practice, it underscores the importance of creating learning environments that reinforce  a sense of belonging, autonomy, and purpose,  and integrating incentives that are perceived as developmental rather than controlling. Future research employing longitudinal and multi-site designs with refined measures of extrinsic motivation will further clarify the psychological determinants of STEM per sistence across diverse educational systems in Latin America.
