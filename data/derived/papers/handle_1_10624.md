# Examining Settled Disciplines in Dual Language Immersion

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Settler-colonial ideologies shape disciplinary norms to privilege dominant ways of knowing and being, excluding and erasing perspectives of minoritized learners. This analysis focused on multiplicity, or an attunement to multiple ways of knowing and being in the world, as a resource to surface and examine settled disciplinary norms in a Dual Language Immersion (DLI) teacher education context. Using interaction analysis, we present a moment from a methods course that illustrates how teacher candidates attuned to multiplicity to surface and examine disciplinary norms in science and language learning. We conclude by considering the benefits of integrating theoretical perspectives across the disciplines of Learning Sciences and language education within contexts like DLI.

## Introduction and theoretical framing

In U.S. educational contexts, settler-colonial ideologies privilege and center disciplinary knowledge and practices based in whiteness that are visible in classroom curricula and participation norms. These “settled expectations” result in learning spaces that harm minoritized learners by marginalizing, excludi ng, and erasing their ways of knowing and being as valuable in schools  (Bang et al., 201 2). While the influence of dominant ideologies on disciplinary learning is well documented, less is k nown about how to challenge prevailing norms and re imagine more equitable learning.  This paper draws on the notion of multiplicity as a possible resource to surface and challenge settled disciplinary norms. Within a Dual  Language Immersion (DLI) teacher education context, we asked: How did attunement to multiplicity serve as a resource for DLI teacher candidates to surface and examine disciplinary norms?

Settled practices  and histories  are evident across disciplines. Here we highlight norms in lang uage education and science education as the focal discip lines of this analysis. Settled norms in language e ducation transform language into capital (Macedo, 2019). Those with privileged identities (i.e., White, European-heritage, monolingual English speakers) easily access opportu nities to obtain additional linguistic capital by b ecoming multilingual, while minorized learners’ linguistic resources are often framed as problematic, even within programs like DLI (Freire & Delavan, 2021). Similarly, settled norms of language as an abstract  object of study mean that language is often divorced from culture in language education. Yet, when culture is included in DLI, for example, it is often with a Eurocentric focus that privilege s language varieties of European nation-states with colonizing pasts. This Eurocentric approach results in an erasure of the settled histories of language learning that  are a fundamental reality in U.S. settings (Macedo, 2019).

Settled norms position science  as a discipline  from a positivist orientation, claiming objectivity  and neutrality. Acquiring (academic) English proficiency is typically framed as prerequisite to engaging in meaningful science sensemaking, despite evidence of rich science engagement when students use their full linguistic resources to contribute to collective sensemaking that then goes beyond what is possible when limited to English alone (e.g., Hudicourt-Barnes, 2003; Pierson et al., 2021).  Similarly, students’ cultures tend to be taken up in science only when they reinforce canonical science knowledge and practices (Bang et al., 2012). Yet recent efforts have shown that cultural knowledge can serve as an essential resour ce for science learning that benefits all students (e.g., Hudicourt-Barnes, 2003).

Learning sciences scholars have proposed multiplicity, or an attunement to multiple ways of knowing and being, as a resource that can support delinking  from settled disciplinary norms to move toward learning that legitimizes and welcomes non-dominant forms of knowledge and ways of being withi n classroom spaces (Bang et al., 2012; Warren et al., 2020). Warren and coll eagues (2020) note that “all disciplines are in need of a critica l re-reading” (p. 278) to understand how settled expecta tions and histories shape disciplinary knowledge and practices. We take up their invitation within the context of Dual Language Immersion (DLI) teacher preparation, a context less commonly examined in the Learning Sc iences. While program models and approaches vary, D LI teachers are responsible for developing students’ l inguistic and cultural proficiency by “immersing” t hem in another language (e.g., French, Mandarin, Spanish, etc.) while simultaneously providing learners with grade-level content instruction (e.g., language arts,  math, science) in that same language (Howard et al., 2018). During part of the day, students also receive instruction in English. DLI teachers consistently engage with multiple disciplines across multiple languages as part of their work. Furthermore, because DLI inherently invokes multiple disciplines, it is also possible that much can be learned by crossing traditional disciplinary and theoretical boundaries.

## Context and methods

The data presented here came from a DLI biliteracy methods course taught at a large, private university in the western United States. Six teacher candidates who were part of the univers ity’s DLI K-12 Teaching Minor participated (all names are participant-selected pseudonyms). Four were preparing to teach Spanish immersion [Anne, Luna, Maria, and Mia (who also had some prio r experience with French)].  One (Abby) was prepari ng to teach German immersion and also had some prior expe rience with American Sign Language and Spanish. The final participant (Melody) was preparing to teach C hinese immersion and her language experience with C hinese included the Shanwei dialect, Cantonese, and Mandarin. Melody was also studying Japanese. Some teacher candidates were L1 or heritage speakers of their immersion languages, some had studied the immersion language formally in school, and some had learned it as full-time religious volunteers. This diversity was not i ntentionally planned, but it was a marked feature of the course space. The instructor, Emily, took an experiential approach to the course, designing it  to allow teacher candidates to experience as learners the methods th ey were studying.  Emily emphasized science as a site for biliteracy development since teacher candidates were expected to teach this discipline in the immersion partner language rather than in English according to state DLI program models. Bethany, the  first author, supported this effort by leading two demonstration lessons using  phenomenon-centered science app roaches (Windschitl et al.,

2018) that were situated within French cultures and taught in French to model DLI pedagogical practices (e.g.,

making linguistic input comprehensible, scaffolding; Lyster, 2019) in a language unfamiliar to teacher candidates. During the methods course, teacher candidates also had regular opportunities to enact teaching practic es through teaching demonstrations given to their peers, resulting in them teaching peers who did not speak their immersion language and teacher candidates being taught in languages they did not speak. This environment created many possibilities for attuning to multiplicity.

We analyzed nine 3-hour class sessions to identify when participants attuned to multiplicity as a resource to surface and examine settled disciplinary practices related to language and science learning. To accomplish this, we content logged video and audio recordings of cla ss sessions  and reviewed each session to identify “interactional hot spots” where participants attune d to multiplicity (often connected to  multiple languages or cultures) as part of their sensemaking about disciplinary learning in DLI. Here, due to space constraints, we present one case representative of those moments. We analyz ed this case using Interaction Analysis methods (Jo rdan & Henderson, 1995), attending to how interactional elements  such as turns of talk, joint shared attention, and physical resources (e.g., materials from the lesson) were connected to multiple languages, cultures, o r science understandings. We then considered how participants  coordinated these elements to surface  settled norms and engage in sensemaking about disciplinary practices (Erickson, 2004; Goodwin, 2018). We viewed the data multiple times individually, together as authors, a nd in community with an Interaction Analysis Lab (J ordan & Henderson, 1995).

## Findings

The moment we highlight here demonstrates how multiplicity served as a resource to surface settled norms related to disciplinary learning of science and language bu t stopped short of engaging in histories that exami ned and disrupted these norms. Our focal moment occurred in  the context of an immersive learning experience th at considered the science phenomenon of how oak trees reproduce.  During the science lesson taught in French, the group examined species of trees that grow in French-speaking countries (e.g., maple trees, baobab trees, mango trees) before focusing on oak trees specifically.

Afterward, Bethany, Author 1, facilitated a conversation that invited the teacher candidates to reflect on their experiences with the French  science lesson. Abby, a teacher candidate, immediately attuned to multiplicity related to the science content, expressing surprise  that “I have just never really thought about the p ossibility of different types of trees as a cultural connection.” Abby’s response acknowledged how science standards establish settled disciplinary expectations (“Okay, I’ve gott a talk about  oaks”) but was surprised by Bethany’s cultural connection to “talk about trees in all these places”. She named her desire to integrate culture to support immersion language learning (“how am I supposed to culturally  connect this?”). As Abby attuned to multiplicity, she began to grapple with disciplinary practices in science t hat tend to erase culture to maintain settled norms  of neutrality and objectivity.  She also considered how to achieve one of the course emphase s, that of integrating culture into DLI instruction, despite disciplinary norms in DLI that tend to omit cultural explorations in favor of preserving more time for content knowledge acquisition.

Maria, another teacher candidate,  built on Abby’s contribution by highlighting how mu ltiplicity that attunes to scientific phenomena across cultures cou ld facilitate student learning by “build[ing] backg round knowledge for students” who have connections to tre es from around the world. Maria’s reflection opened space to mediate relationships between students’ identities and experiences and academic disciplines (science, language learning). For students “who maybe just moved here, ” Maria framed multiplicity, including diverse cult ural experiences and knowledge, as a resource that could  contribute to sensemaking about science phenomena (“they’re used to seeing fruit trees... you’re buildi ng those connections”). Maria highlighted that draw ing on multiplicity (“using the multiple trees…[to] provid e the cultural connections”) could support students  to access settled science content in new ways. Maria’s contribution stopped short of identifying how students’ background experiences and cultural practices could support a reimagining of science as a discipline to resist settled framings of science as static. However, it did push against settled norms that exclude relationality in favor of objectivity (Bang et al., 2012).

Bethany built on Maria’s contribution by explaining, “I don’t have palm trees where I’m from.” Bethany noted that even culture within the United States varies widely, a p henomenon that could be represented by trees in different local environments. As Maria and Betha ny recognized that trees could serve as cultural representations, they invoked a closer examination of what “counts” as culture.  Emily, the instructor,  added her experience to this examination to push the group further in thinking about settled constructions of culture:  “Like I didn’t know they had mangoes in certain French-speaking countries. I don’t think of France and go,  ‘mango.’” Emily’s stance connected Maria and Bethany’s sensem aking about multiplicity within the United States a nd expanded it to consider multiplicity within and across cultures. As Emily expressed surprise about the new cultural connection she had made between varieties of trees and cultures (“I didn’t know they had mangoes in ce rtain French-speaking countries”), she identified the settled disciplinary norm of associating a language with European nation-states and their cultural products (“I don’t think of France and go ‘mango’”). Together, the group surfaced and engaged with settled disciplinary conceptualizations of culture. Abby expanded on Emily’s contribution by drawing on additional examples of multiplicity from her own experiences as a GermandLI teacher candidate and her husband’s  experiences learning to speak French while living as a volunteer in Tahiti. Abby’s attunement to multiplicity deepened the group’s examination of disciplinary practices for teaching culture in language learning. Abby probe d at the settled expectation that Emily raised, naming how German is not as widely spoken as other languag es (“you don’t have  quite a broad…if you give a map of where all the places are that speak German”). Abby did not delve into the history that resulted  in this reality (i.e., that German was once spoken more widely because of colonization, but that Germany lost colonial territories in wars of the 20 th century). However, she did name that the norm of framing culture in limited ways held true in German language education as well as F rench (“right now in the German world  [i.e., the German language teaching world], they’re being like, well,  we always focus on Germany and not Switzerland”).  While the histories that shape settled disciplinary expec tations for teaching culture were not explored, att uning to multiplicity allowed for these realities to surface as the group examined disciplinary practices in language learning related to culture teaching.

Abby also drew on her personal experiences with Fre nch to further highlight how colonizing, European cultures are centered in language learning. She shared how her husband had served as a voluntee r in Tahiti, then offered a template for a typical conversation that demonstrated that these types of cultural assumptions exist more broadly (“If you’re like, ‘Oh, I served [as a] Fren ch-speaking [volunteer],’ they’d be like, ‘Oh, to Fran ce or to Canada?’”). Abby’s tone as she retold the conversation implied that it was a familiar discussion that her husband had had multiple times. On learning that her husband spoke French, people assumed that he must have volunteered in France or Canada, drawing on settled expectations that center European/Europe an-heritage countries and cultures. However, Abby noted how her husband’s una nticipated response disrupted these assumptions (“A nd he’s like, ‘Nah. To an island.’”). Abby summed up how seeing trees used as cultural representations expanded her perspectives on culture (“So that was really cool to also just see actually, French is spoken in a lot of places”). While this conversation and participants’ attunemen t to multiplicity allowed them to surface and (re)examine disciplinary practices in science (inco rporating culture as a resource for science sensema king) and language learning (considering expansive definition s of what counts as culture and from where), it is crucial to note that the conversation stop ped short of explicitly naming histories of settler-colonialism that shaped where, why, and how French and German are spoken around th e world today.  While attuning to multiplicity opened space to surface and examine disciplinary histories, in this moment, it fell sho rt of its full potential as a resource to delink from settled assumptions (Bang et al., 2012; Warren et al., 2020).

## Discussion and implications

One of the primary contributions of this paper has been an exploration of how a Learning Sciences pers pective can support teacher candidate learning in a less-commonly explored context, that of DLI teacher educ ation. We found that Warren et al.’s (2020) invitation to critically re-read disciplines through the lens of multiplicity allowed us to examine individual disciplines taught in DLI (e.g., science, language learning) and the unique relationships across those disciplines that emerge in a DLI context. As teacher candidates attuned to multiplicity, they surfaced connections that allowed them to consider disciplin ary norms of science and language learning within a DLI context. Fully disrupting disciplinary norms would require time to unpack settled histories in science, in language learning, and in DLI programs. This complexity goes  beyond the bounds of a single course. Our analysis highlights how doing this work requires establishin g linkages across departments and programs that cross disciplinary boundaries and make interdisciplinary connections. Ideally, supported by parallel conversations in other courses, the conversations presented here would have directly named settler-colonialism as force that shapes language education in the United States. Teacher candidates might have engaged in collective sensemaking around the historicity of their different immersion languages. Grounded in that background, the course might have then engaged in a critical analysis of science and langu age curricula, considering whose voices and perspective s are included, missing, or silenced. Another key element would involve exploring with teacher candidates how settlercolonial histories and settled disciplinary assumpt ions marginalize culturally and linguistically minoritized learners and how to resist that reality through intentional pedagogy that foregrounds multiplicity (Warren et al., 2020). Future work might then include developing classroom norms and practices with teacher candidat es that create humanizing spaces that welcome onto-epistemological heterogeneity. Similarly, future resea rch might consider how to intentionally design for classroom experiences that disrupt settled disciplinary norms  (Bang et al., 2012), something especially important for DLI contexts where those norms  cross multiple cultural and linguistic boundaries. By breaking down disciplinary silos, we may be able  to desettle disciplinary expectations and center multiplicity in new ways to support onto-epistemic heterogeneity across and within multiple disciplines and multiple learning contexts.
