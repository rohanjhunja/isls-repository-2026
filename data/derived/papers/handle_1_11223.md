# “My Body was Trapped by the Wall”: A Longitudinal Studyof Teachers’ Sensemaking of Embodiments of ResponsiveTeaching

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Recent attention has focused on the multiple strategies teachers use to create responsive classrooms. However, little is known about the embodiments of responsive teaching. Grounded in situative and embodiment learning theories, this study situates teacher learning within dilemmatic spaces. Using a longitudinal exploratory case study, we explored how Kasey, an experienced mathematics teacher, made sense of the embodiments of responsive teaching over time. This case emerged from a multi-year research-practice partnership in a large urban school district in the United States, employing video-based feedback (VFF) and the Interaction Geographic Slicer (IGS) to support reflective teaching practices. Findings reveal that embodiment is a central element in responding to students’ learning needs. Responsive teaching is filled with embodiment dilemmas, which should be recognized as critical resources for teacher learning and development rather than obstacles. This study offers valuable insights for teachers and teacher educators aimed at fostering responsive teaching practices.

## Introduction

Responsive teaching, which is essential to create a diverse and inclusive learning environment, requires teachers’ adaptability in making instructional decisions based on students’ various and dynamic learning needs (Lampert, 2001; Parsons et al., 2018). Researchers have ext ensively explored strategies to support responsive teaching, such as adapting curricular materials, employin g questioning and feedback techniques, and orchestrating classroom discourse (Gallagher et al., 2022). Furthermore, the embodiment of teaching has recently gained attention as a powerful, yet under explored, resource for responsive teaching, requiring further exploration (Abrahamson & Bakker, 2016; Goldin-Meadow et a l., 2019). We argue that responsive teaching should be understood as an embodiment practice and is inherently filled with embodiment dilemmas. Embodiment is conceptualized as physical and artifact-me diated dimensions of human interactions and is important for teaching as vario us pedagogical values are expressed through such interactions (Norris, 2004; Vossoughi et al., 2020). Respo nsive teaching is uncertain and value-laden, requiring teachers to continuously balance and prioritize competing values. This often results in tensions and conflicts related to teachers’ bodily presence and actions in the clas sroom, giving rise to what we term embodiment dilemmas (Cuban, 1992; Lampert, 2001; Windschitl, 2002). I magine this scenario: Group A, on one side of the classroom, is struggling with a challenging problem and cle arly needs guidance, while Group B, on the other side, has just proposed an innovative idea that warrants imm ediate attention to encourage further discussion. Ideally, the teacher wishes to meet both groups’ needs equal ly. However, constraints of time and space make it impossible for the teacher to achieve all valued goals simul taneously. Moving toward Group B might signal a preference for fostering creativity, while moving toward G roup A could indicate a commitment to direct support for students. Here, the teacher is not merely deciding where to move physically –her proximity to students – but is also navigating competing pedagogical values. Together, the embodiment dilemmas underscore the complex interplay between teachers’ bodies and the pedagogical val ues embedded in responsive teaching.

In this paper, we explore how teachers and teacher educators can use embodiment dilemmas as sensemaking tools for teachers’ professional learning. To that end, we conduct a case study analysis of an experienced teacher’s, whom we call Kasey, professional le arning interactions during an intervention called video-based formative feedback (VFF) enriched with the Int eraction Geographic Slicer (IGS) tool, providing visible representation of teaching and collective discuss ion opportunities. Through our partnership with public high school mathematics teachers in the Western United Stat es, we identified Kasey’s case as significant, demonstrating how teachers’ sensemaking of embodiment dil emmas within responsive teaching serves as a form of teacher learning. Our analysis is guided by the follo wing research question: How did one experienced mathematics teacher make sense of embodiments of responsiv e teaching over time?

## Theoretical framework

A situative view of learning By taking a situative view perspective (Greeno, 1997), we emphasize the contextual and ambiguous nature of teaching, as well as the characteristics of teacher learning as embedded within sociocultural and historical dynamics (Author, 2022). We trace teacher learning through shifts in teachers’ pedagogical judgment – the management of interactions between pedagogical action, pedagogical reasoning, and pedagogical responsibility (Author, 2022). Pedagogical responsibilities include the ethical, legal, institutional commitments that inform what pedagogical actions a teacher chooses to do. Teachers’ emergent understanding of how their pedagogical actions (mis)align with their pedagogical responsibilities is mediated by their pedagogical reasoning, which ultimately contributes to the development of pedagogical judgment. Professional learning spaces are one site where teachers can explore and reflect on dilemmas, which are “endemic to the development of pedagogical judgment” (Author, 2022, p. 63).

Learning as embodiment processes We conceptualized human learning as an embodiment process, where we define embodiment as the physical and artifact-mediated dimensions of human interaction (Norris, 2004; Vossoughi et al., 2020). The physical aspects include embodiment modes such as gesture, posture, gaze and proximity (Norris, 2004). Artifacts refer to various material tools that rely on participants’ engagement to become instruments of meaning-making. The publicly visible nature of embodiments enables participants to perceive and negotiate their meanings through mutual orientation, constructing shared understandings and actions within the interaction (Goodwin, 2000). For example, a mathematics teacher can use gestures simulating a right angle, an encouraging gaze, and a protractor to engage students in collaboratively constructing an understanding of a right angle. Here, embodiments are not merely instrumental; they also convey the teacher’s ethical and pedagogical values (Vossoughi et al., 2020). The gestures reflect a commitment to clarity and accessibility, the gaze demonstrates attentiveness and support, and the use of the protractor emphasizes the importance of connecting abstract concepts to tangible tools, creating a responsive learning environment.

In addition to being a resource for student learning, teachers’ bodies also serve as vital sites for their own ongoing sensemaking and professional growth. Far from being passive tools, teachers’ bodies operate as embodied minds with their own capacities for perception, cognition, and sensemaking, making embodiment experiences foundational for understanding and development (Gallagher, 2005; Merleau-Ponty, 1962; Nguyen & Larson, 2015). In other words, the body is an active place, where teachers internalize, reinterpret, and express their pedagogical values through embodiment experiences, which significantly shape their professional growth and identity (Perry & Medina, 2011, p. 63). Compared to disembodied cognition, these embodied experiences enable teachers to develop nuanced insights into responsive teaching that transcend purely cognitive reflection. The embodiment of teacher learning also aligns with another critical aspect of reflective teaching practice: navigating the complexities of teacher dilemmas.

Dilemmatic space as a site for learning We take up Cuban’s conceptualization of a dilemma and define it as “conflict filled situations that require choices because competing, highly prized values cannot be fully satisfied” (Cuban, 1992, p 6). As such, we also conceptualize embodiment dilemmas as the tensions that arise from conflicting values specifically related to teachers’ physical presence in responsive teaching. We extend Cuban’s definition by arguing that these moments of embodiment dilemmas exist within the larger existence of dilemmatic spaces, emphasizing the contextualized and situated nature of dilemmas (Honig, 1996; Fransson & Grannäs, 2013). Dilemmatic spaces are “social constructions resulting from structural conditions and relational aspects in everyday practices” (Fransson & Grannäs, 2013, p. 7). While a specific dilemma can be experienced by teachers teaching in different contexts, the dilemmatic space of the same dilemma is unique, changing according to various contextual variables. For example, a female teacher might naturally express care through gestures like hugging or patting a student’s shoulder, aligning with certain societal expectations of women as nurturing figures. In contrast, if a student is crying, a male teacher might hesitate between gently patting their shoulder to offer comfort or keeping his distance to avoid potential misunderstanding. We center the space of an embodiment dilemma as a critical sou rce for teacher learning and development. According to Caspari-Gnann and Sevian (2022), “dilemmas that teachers face in their practice can be growth points for teacher development” (p. 1). This persp ective is further clarified through the concept of dilemmatic space, which illustrate how dilemmas interact a cross individuals, their contexts, and broader systems (Fransson & Grannäs, 2013). In other words, dilemmatic space s offer teachers an opportunity for deep sensemaking, where they can critically examine their pedag ogical actions, reasoning and responsibility, competing demands and values within teaching, and broader s ociocultural and historical contexts. Teachers are prompted to reflect on the contradictions and conflicts wit hin their everyday teaching practices, creating potential for the emergence of new, responsive practice. In addition, teachers’ dilemmatic space sensemaking processes are also shaped by collaborative interactions wi th colleagues, institutional norms, and systemic forces. To better understand how dilemmatic spaces can serve as rich resources for teachers’ learning over time, we analyze Kasey’s sensemaking of the embodiment dile mmas of responsive teaching, as facilitated through the VFF sessions.

## Context

To explore the varied ways that a teacher makes sense of the em bodiments of teaching, we draw from a larger, multi-year research-practice partnership between univer sity researchers, teachers, professional development organization (PDO) in a large urban school district in the We stern United States. The focus of this partnership is on career-long learning of secondary mathematics teachers in urban schools as they work toward enacting equitable and ambitious mathematics instruction. We worke d closely with 12 experienced high school mathematics teachers to support their reflection and inqui ry into their practice using a VFF cycle. The VFF cycle unfolds as follows: (1) a focal teacher identifies a qu estion of interest (e.g., am I intervening with groups equitably?); (2) the day of the lesson, the teacher identifi es three focal student groups to record; (3) the research team documents a lesson with the question in mind, using a who le-class camera, GoPro 360o cameras for the three student groups, and a point-of-view camera attached t o the teacher; (4) with the teacher’s question in mind, the team reviews the video and creates a visual representati on of the teacher’s movement and talk with a tool called the IGS (Shapiro et al., 2017); (5) the teacher, their colleagues, and researchers discuss the teacher’s questions using the video and IGS representations in a subse quent, video-recorded debrief. This analysis focuses on the debriefs. After each VFF cycle, research team members wrote reflective memos that summarized the data collection events (e.g. classroom and debrief filmings). I n particular, these memos summarized notable moments from the debrief, what clips we shared with teachers, how teachers responded to the video clips and IGS, and what questions, tensions, and ideas were raised in t he resulting conversations. Across these debriefs, we noticed that conversations amongst teachers and researc hers often surfaced dilemmas. Case selection Kasey is an experienced teacher at Vaughan High School, whic h is an arts high school serving a diverse population; about 40% of students are identified as Hispani c, 25% white, 20% biracial, and 15% Asian. Kasey’s colleagues from the same school, Nadine and Vince, are also p articipating in our project and join each other’s VFF debrief sessions. In the year the data analyzed in this pa per was collected, Kasey had 24 years teaching experience, and taught Mathematical Modeling and Algebra 2. Over two years, we conducted a series of detailed observatio ns in Kasey’s classroom and held conversations with Kasey. This sustained engagement enabl ed us to develop a nuanced understanding of Kasey’s pedagogical commitments and ways that he makes sens e of his embodiments of teaching practices. Kasey is dedicated to creating a respectful and warm learnin g environment where students feel acknowledged and supported. He adopts reflective and critical approache s in teaching, often considering how his physical positioning, movement, and presence shape his interaction s with students and the overall classroom dynamic. As he engaged in the VFF cycle and debriefs, Kasey became incr easingly aware of the embodiment dilemmas inherent in teaching, responding with new insights and prac tices. Kasey’s case serves as a compelling example because of his su stained reflection about the embodiments of teaching, which he frequently raised during debriefs. Compared to other teachers, he demonstrated a more consistent and frequent engagement wit h this topic. More importantly, his ongoing sensemaking around embodiment dilemmas demonstrates how n avigating these challenges with professional development supports like VFF enriched with IGS demonstrat ions can be a potential source for professional growth. We therefore conceptualize Kasey as an exploratory case (Yin, 2017) whereby we look longitudinally at how Kasey’s sensemaking evolves and changes over time. Becau se dilemmas inherently have no solution, looking at how a teacher’s sensemaking evolves is a potentia lly fruitful way to look at the varying ways teachers manage their dilemmas in their everyday lives.

Data sources Table 1 summarizes the data sources used in this study. To dee pen the analysis, data were primarily gathered from three VFF sessions and three one-on-one interviews. Th e data was collected from October 2022 to May 2024.

In the first and third VFF sessions, Kasey was the focal teach er, enabling an in-depth look into his sensemaking of embodiment in teaching practice over time. I n the second VFF, Kasey was not the focal teacher, but he extensively reflected on his own embodiment dilemmas during the debrief of his colleague Nadine’s session. In addition, the interviews were conducted at the e nd of year (EOY) and the beginning of the year (BOY). These interviews provided additional insights into the teacher’s evolving perspectives on embodiments of responsive teaching.

## Data analysis

This study employed a comprehensive data analysis approach. The first round of analysis involved a thorough review of six transcripts, where the authors identified and extracted episodes reflecting embodiment dilemmas. These episodes were selected based on key terms frequently e merging in teacher discourse, such as “body,” “movement,” “hand,” “eye”, and “space.” Building on this initial review, in the second round, an open coding framework was developed (Saldañ a, 2016) to capture emergent themes. For example, t he open codes include “the impact of the height,” “hesitant about the way to approach students closely,” “sen sitivity to gender dynamics,” “directing attention through movement,” “using eye contact” and “changing the cl assroom layout”. Each code provides qualitative evidence of his ongoing developmental trajectory.

Next, discourse analysis (Gee, 2010) was applied to the iden tified episodes to investigate how Kasey’s language choices revealed shifts in his sensemaking of embo diment of responsive teaching over time. This analysis involved zooming in on the nuances of his phrasing, tone, and conversational patterns. For example, his shifting use of terms, from describing “close” interaction as merely physical proximity with students to emphasizing relational closeness that fosters meaningful teaching moments, reflects a deeper transformation in his perspective.

We employed several strategies to ensure the trustworthine ss of this study. First, the diversity of data sources and analytical methods enhanced the breadth and dep th of our analysis. Second, the authors, bringing varied areas of expertise, convened regularly from Februar y 2024 onwards to refine the analysis. For example, the authors independently coded the embodiment dilemma epi sodes to identify the key elements constituting the dilemmatic space, reducing the limitations of individual r esearcher perspectives. Furthermore, we sought feedback from colleagues outside the research team to stren gthen our interpretation.

## Findings

The analysis showed that Kasey made sense of the embodiments of responsive teaching through three developmental shifts: (1) expanding awareness of the embod iment elements within the dilemmatic space of responsive teaching; (2) reconceptualizing the body as a re source for engaging students to meaningful mathematics learning; and (3) navigating embodiment dilem mas within teaching in a more conscious and strategic way.

Expanding awareness of the embodiment elements within the dilemmatic space of responsive teaching We found that Kasey’s increasing awareness of embodiment dil emmas serves an essential role in his sensemaking of the embodiments of responsive teaching. In t he VFF 2 in February 2023, researchers shared the IGS of Kasey and two colleagues. Through this comparison, Ka sey observed that, unlike his colleagues, he tended to remain stationary in the lesson for extended perio ds and had a fixed movement route. This observation prompted Kasey to reconsider how his body positioning and mo vement impacted interactions with students (see Figure 1).

As discussions progressed, Kasey expressed his dilemma wit hin responsive teaching between respecting student space and privacy and maintaining effec tive interactions. Initially, he identified two elements of the dilemmatic space of this dilemma, embodiment interac ting with his pedagogical ideals or commitments on the dilemmatic space, giving rise to the embodiment dilem mas. First, Kasey described how the fixed seating arrangement along the wall restricted his ability to intera ct without encroaching upon students’ personal space. As he noted, “I’m sorry... it’s the wall thing, and the fact th at they’re pinned on one side by the wall” (VFF 2, Feb 2023). Second, Kasey’s height complicated close intera ctions, as he believed his stature might unintentionally evoke feelings of dominance in students: “ It’s a height thing...I get really close to them, actually on the chair, but then we’re eye to eye. I feel like, this is a ve ry dominating…” (VFF 2, Feb 2023). This concern was especially pronounced in interactions with female stud ents, where he feared that his presence might exacerbate feelings of discomfort. His commitment to ethic ally and pedagogically appropriate student engagement thus positioned him in a constant conflict betwe en the demands of effective teaching and the goal of minimizing personal and student discomfort.

Later in VFF 2, the group engaged in a collective inquiry that supported Kasey’s deeper sensemaking of the embodiments of his teaching. For example, when Kasey m entioned the dominating dynamics caused by the height, his colleagues highlighted the implications of classroom layout. This prompted Kasey to describe his concerns about certain students’ seating arrangements, whe re limited movement options created tight, uncomfortable spaces. As Kasey noted: There’s two students in particular, that I'm worried about. Because, from the outside, I feel I’ve got enough room, and space to come in from the outside. I s till feel weird, because they have to do this to get to me. But then, it's these two on the insi de, where I have to... Now, I'm between four people, and…(VFF 2, Feb 2023) After several VFF sessions, Kasey began to recognize the complexity of the dilemma of respecting privacy and maintaining interaction than he recognized initially and to identify other elements of it. First, Kasey’s attention shifted from the static “wall” to the broader interpretation of embodiments, including classroom spatial layout, desk arrangements, podium placement, and even the depth of his positioning in the room, and their interactions with subjects in the classroom. For example, rather than focusing on whether he was positioned to the left or right of the whiteboard, he started to consider how the depth of his location could impact student engagement. As he put it, “To me, the more interesting thing is, where are y ou depth-wise in the room?” (Interview 3, May 2024).

Second, Kasey came to understand that the key to managing stu dents’ feelings in interactions was not solely his height, but rather his “presence”—a blend of his i nteraction style, posture, and overall classroom ambiance. By observing other teachers, Kasey noted that a te acher’s “presence” could convey authority regardless of height, for example, that a shorter teacher mi ght command respect through a strong presence while a taller teacher could alleviate student tension by adoptin g a gentler demeanor. He concluded: “So I mean, it’s not just height, it’s so much about presence” (Interview 3, M ay 2024). This nuanced understanding of presence reflects Kasey’s growing sophistication in using embodime nts to foster a supportive and responsive teaching environment.

Third, Kasey began integrating students’ authentic needs w ithin the dilemmatic space, moving beyond his initial assumptions about students’ discomfort with his height. He aligned his gesture and movement with students’ actual reactions, such as their body language, att ention and engagement levels, Kasey shifted from relying on his assumptions to tuning into students’ actual re actions. He emphasized the core of teaching is to enable students to feel care and support: “I want you to feel l ike I’m here, I’m with you. Not just from an accountability standpoint, but also just like a, ‘Hey, I car e about you,’ and giving you that face time, that's important” (Interview 3, May 2024). This shift reflected Ka sey’s evolving pedagogical responsibilities and actions, where identifying and understanding students’ rea l needs became a central consideration in his navigation of the embodiment dilemma.

Reconceptualizing the body as a resource for engaging students to meaningful mathematics learning The embodiment dilemmas facilitates Kasey’s deep sensemak ing about the role of bodies in the classroom. He reconceptualized the role of physical presence in responsi ve teaching, shifting from viewing his body as a constraint to understanding it as a resource for actively en gaging students in interaction. For instance, in Interview 2, Kasey describes his adjustments in positionin g and posture to foster peer engagement among students: “So I mean moving around. A lot of times I’m trying t o direct their eyes and sometimes I get as explicit as going where I'm behind. Look at her, I'll do this” (Interview 2, Jan 2024). Moreover, Kasey has also reflected on managing his stance, p roximity, and eye contact with students to avoid projecting an “authoritative” image. He mentions tha t, especially this year, he has intentionally used artifacts to the blackboard to promote student autonomy: And you know what, I did that more this year than almost ever be fore, because I had in the mathematical modeling class, a lot of PowerPoints prepared, like slideshows, and I had a clicker, and it has a laser pointer. And so I was able to use the laser pointer to point at stuff and not be near the board. I need to have less control, and I giv e the markers to the kids, more (Interview 3, May 2024).

This excerpt illustrates the integration of Kasey’s physic al and artifact-mediated dimensions in his teaching. By using a laser pointer, he altered the spatial dynamic betwee n himself, the board, and the students, redistributing the attention and authority within the classroom. Also, handing markers to students further exemplifies a shift from direct control to a more participatory approach.

On the other hand, Kasey has come to recognize that students’ b ody language serves as a crucial resource for assessing their engagement and learning needs. He realized that seating arrangements significantly influence students' posture, which in turn affects their fo cus and interaction patterns. For instance, a seating layout with students facing the board helps maintain classr oom focus but may elicit discomfort or complaints from students about feeling constrained. Conversely, in a n orth-south arrangement, students must turn their heads 90 degrees to view the board, which increases the cost o f physical rotation. Consequently, Kasey experimented with designing a central loop arrangement to b alance comfort with effective participation, noting, “I was trying to go to a central loop, and the central loop woul d allow me to get to everybody easier. So the alleys kind of went away, but they became streets now instead of just alleys” (Interview 1, June 2023). In addition to his own positioning, Kasey also became attune d to how students' postures, eye contact, and seating orientations could reflect their emotional sta tes, engagement levels, and comprehension. For example, he observed students' body language to gauge group consensus and engagement: I would look to see if there is consensus at the groups. Does everybody at this group agree? I would look to see, honestly, at the way the kids are sitting. I f they're leaning back in a chair like this, or away from the table, to me, for sure, the leaning in, or I think we call it slanting (VFF 3, Feb 2024).

This focus on students’ non-verbal cues reveals Kasey’s evolving understanding of embodiments in responsive teaching, highlighting how he positions students’ bodies as dynamic resources that shape his teaching. Navigating embodiment dilemmas within teaching in a more conscious and strategic Way Through ongoing sensemaking of embodiment dilemmas within teaching, we found that Kasey gradually developed more conscious and strategic approaches in managing his dilemmas. In the initial emergence of Kasey’s embodiment dilemmas, his reflections on space, body, and movement were marked by a noticeable sense of uncertainty. Early on, he often conveyed hesitation and a lack of control, frequently using phrases like “I don’t know…” and “I am sorry…” to express his uncertainty. This self-doubt became especially evident in Interview 1, where Kasey described his feeling as “frustrated” and even “disgusted” when reviewing his IGS recordings. He acknowledged his difficulty in envisioning a clear path forward to modify his spatial engagement and was candid about the challenges in changing his habitual movement patterns: Ideally, I should be thinking about how I am moving about the s pace and facilitating the way the students are engaging with the material, that's what I sh ould be doing. That is not what I do in practice though, but I feel like I should. But then to be r eal, I just don't feel like I have enough time to do that... I’m trying to envision how that coul d happen, and I don’t see a path forward with that. And it’s also really hard, my neural pathw ays are so... The groove is so etched into my mind. It sounds intellectually lazy to say thi s, but I don’t know how to change (Interview 1, Jun 2023).

In this reflection, Kasey frequently uses the word “should,” indicating his awareness of the expectations he holds for himself in responsive teaching. However, he felt s enses of anxiety, uncertainty, and helplessness, as seen in phrases like “feel like I should,” “it’s really hard,” and “I don’t know how to change.” He attributes these struggles to a perceived lack of time. Such cues demons trate the tension Kasey experiences between his ideal aspirations and the realities of his current circumst ances. Through the VFF sessions, Kasey recognized the need for subs tantial adjustments in his classroom layout to enhance his interactions with students. As he bega n identifying kinds of elements (e.g., physical, spatial, cultural) within the dilemmatic space, Kasey’s ima gination expanded to envision ways to restructure the physical layout. For example, he mentioned that if classroo m items could be rearranged, he could establish “a boulevard going up and down here.” This idea illustrates a sh ift from merely coping with spatial limitations to actively exploring how physical configurations could bett er support instructional interactions. Additionally, Kasey adapted his physical movement and the u se of instructional tools to align more closely with students’ needs and the classroom's interactional atmosphere. He shared his strategy of using his own scratch paper rather than writing directly on students' papers or the whiteboard: My move, if I'm with the chair, especially this year, it's alw ays been to come over here, grab the scratch paper, because I don't want to write on the studen t's paper. And I'll roll up to you and write if I need to, on my paper... because I don’t want to touch the white board is the other thing. I used to just grab the whiteboard, but now I'm like, ‘N ow the whiteboard's for them,’ and so now I just use my pen (Interview 3, May 2024).

Here, Kasey reimagines the whiteboard as primarily a student-centered space for expression rather than a teacher-dominated resource. This reimagined approach to space, artifacts, and movement marks a significant progression in Kasey’s ongoing sensemaking within the embodiments of responsive teaching.

## Discussion

The current study explores how Kasey’s sensemaking of the embodiments of responsive teaching evolved over time by examining his identification of elements within the dilemmatic space, reconceptualization of the role of the body, and strategies for managing embodiment dilemmas. The findings first illustrate that embodiment is an integral component of responsive teaching. Kasey’s sensemaking around embodiment highlights how his physical presence shapes teacher-student interactions, classroom dynamics, and responses to shifting instructional needs. This emphasizes that the bodies and material tools are not peripheral instruments but central elements in fostering student meaningful participation (Vossoughi et al., 2020). The findings also show that responsive teaching is filled with embodiment dilemmas. Kasey’s embodiment dilemmas were embedded within the complex interplay of his personal identity, pedagogical values, and the local cultural context. O ver time, Kasey did not arrive at a resolution to his dilemmas; rather, he expanded his understanding of the dilemmatic space itself, which fostered her capacity for interpretation and ongoing professional learning through dilemmas. Traditional PDO structure creates a standardized “one-size fits all” approach that negates the highly situative and unique challenges that individual teachers face. These PDOs fail to address the highly situative nature of teachers’ dilemmas. This study employed IGS to visualize the embodiment of responsive teaching and utilized VFF to provide teachers with opportunities to discuss and reflect on their embodiment dilemmas using various types of representations of practice. By shifting the methodological focus toward visualizing and reflecting on dilemmas, we argue that teacher education addressing teacher dilemmas requires alternative approaches which emphasize sense-making and empowerment. Our study provides insights to inform further research on this goal.
