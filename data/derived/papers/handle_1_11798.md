# Building Capacity for Educational Innovation: Strategies to Scale Computational Thinking Pathways in K-12 School Districts

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
There are many examples of innovation within education, but oftentimes innovation happens in a single classroom or school without being able to scale to an entire district or beyond. When scaling innovations, it is difficult to adapt tonew contexts and student needs while promoting authenticity and sustainability. In this paper, we examine how three districts within a research-practice partnership scaled computational thinking (CT) pathways to provide CT learning opportunities across their district. We first detail case studies of how the three districts scaled their CT pathway implementation. Then, we present cross-district strategies promoting depth, spread, ownership, and sustainability: Clarity and Awareness, Alignment with Existing Priorities, Standards and Curricula Integration, School Based Leadership, Sustained Professional Learning, and Student Voice. These strategies support educational leaders to strategically design implementations that promote the scale of new initiatives.

## Introduction

Scaling educational innovations in ways that promote authenticity and sustainability is a challenge at the forefront of educational leadership. In recent years there have been calls for educational initiatives that better equip learners to be successful in our increasingly technological world and workforce,  such as in STEM and computer science (CS) (Mills et al., 2021; Code.org et al., 2024) while promoting durable skills such as creativity and collaboration (World Economic Forum, 2023). However, implementing educational programs at scale in response to these calls is complex and challenging (Wyss et al., 2023). In this study, we have facilitated a research-practice partnership (RPP) with six districts across the United States for over 5 years. These districts have designed and implemented K-12 computational thinking (CT) pathways so that all  students can engage in cumulative learning experie nces that enable them to solve problems with technology including collecting and analyzing data, programmin g and automating solutions, and creating and using computational models (Burke et al., 2019; Mills et al., 2024). In this paper, we seek to understand the choices that distr ict leaders made in how to design these learning op portunities for students. For example, some districts have adopted curric ula to articulate their pathway whereas others have promoted professional learning along with customized lesson planning. We explore how each of these approaches, along with supplemental strategies, affect ed the scaling of the initiative within each distri ct, attending to four different dimensions of scale: depth, ownership, spread and sustainability (Coburn et al., 2003).

## Background and theoretical framework

The challenge of scale in education Scaling educational initiatives presents complex ch allenges, including maintaining authenticity, adapt ing successful strategies to diverse contexts and alloc ating appropriate resources to ensure long-term sustainability and effectiveness. When innovative p ractices are adopted by many educators, maintaining  authentic implementation can be difficult. School systems are  often bureaucratic, and educators can prefer more familiar methods over what is new and unknown (Cobb et al., 2020). For example, Scratch, a block-based coding platform for youth, was designed based on constructivist theories of learning and peer-peer learning (Resnick et al., 2009). However, scholars have asserted that there is a ten sion between instructional direction and discovery or inquirybased learning, with some educators leveraging more  traditional teaching methods which are in conflict  with the constructivist and collaborative roots of the program (Brennan, 2015). Scaling initiatives is also difficult because educa tional settings have different contexts with varyin g student demographics, resources and community needs. There fore, there is no one-size-fits-all solution that can be equally effective across all educatio nal settings, and innovations  must be adapted to the unique needs of individual schools and districts (Barab et al., 200 3). Designing educational initiatives that are adaptabl e to these different contexts presents a significant challenge, particularly because there are often limited resources available to support innovations, such as additional personne l, tools, or training. As such, tensions arise arou nd the tradeoffs between the ambition of an initiative (e.g., s peed, scale, scope) and its specificity (e.g., curriculum, professional development, and educator choice) (Coe nraad et al., 2021). Olsen (2023) summarized relate d tradeoffs when discussing the reality of scaling ed ucational innovations, such as cost versus complexi ty of an initiative and speed versus depth of prog ram implementation. These tensions emphasize the cr itical role educational leaders have in designing systems to sc ale adoption of an initiative in alignment with the ir mission and vision.

In this study, we explore the implementation strate gies that leaders can leverage to promote scaling o f an initiative. We acknowledge the complexity of scale beyond simple measures of reach. As such, we are guided by Coburn et al. (2003)’s framework which includes four interrelated dimensions: depth, sustainability, spread and ownership. Depth is the extent to which participants enact the natur e of the change. For instance, teachers implementing a scripted curriculum (less depth) versus teachers modifying a pedagogical approach (more depth). Sustainability is the endurance of an initiative over time. Educational initiatives that are sustainable typically last over a decade (Olsen, 2023). Spread is the norms, culture and beliefs understood by par ticipants in the initiative. For example, pockets of teachers enacting an initia tive (less spread) versus many teachers doing so, w ith the support of building leaders and coaches (more sprea d). Ownership indicates that the participants see themselves as the leaders of the initiatives. Ownership involves each participan t understanding why an initiative is taking place and how it relates to their locus of control.  Together, these four dimensions provide an indicat ion of how successfully an initiative has scaled. We leverage Coburn et al.’s (2003) dimensions of scale to better understand implementation strategies that promote scale.

Scaling computational thinking pathways Educational leaders are currently seeking to scale the expansion of learning opportunities for CS and CT in primary and secondary education. These initiatives seek to prepare learners for successful lives in a world that is increasingly integrated with technology, as well as to diversify the CS workforce to include groups that experience exclusion from and marginalization in computing suc h as individuals that identify as Black, Native American, and Latinx; people with disabilities; women and non-binary individuals (Mills et al., 2021). In the past decade, the United States has made tremendous gains in expanding opportunities for computer science education. To date, all 50 states have adopted CS education policies th at articulate standards, identify required coursewo rk, provide professional learning and/or establish educator cer tification programs (Code.org et al., 2024). While these state level mandates provide guidance and resources for i nnovation, each state is composed of many school di stricts that independently determine how to implement programs, which curricula to adopt, and how to allocate staffing and resources. For instance, while CS and CT standa rds and progressions are the state’s responsibility, implementation of the computing learning opportunit ies are inherently a district’s responsibility to o wn and manage. The project in which this study is embedded has supported a network of districts from across the United States to design and implement learning pathways for CT within their unique district contexts. Learning pathways are an approach that educational leaders can use to scale educational innovations by explicitly connecting classroom learning to cross-cutting initiatives (Mills et al., 2024). CT Pathways identify and design opportunities for students to  integrate CT into subject area learning across K-12 learning experiences. These pathways are designed with the following tenets: cumulative (across K-12 grade bands), consistent (across classrooms and school), complementary (to subject area learning) and focused on fundamental computing competencies (e.g., “using algorithms”, “creating computational models”) that allow for integration across the school curricula rather than siloing CS and CT into  stand-alone classes and/or afterschool clubs. The pathway design process draws on the collective expertise of district leaders, school leaders, and teachers and is intended to be adapted, as each pathway is customized to the assets, needs, and contexts of specific districts and communities. Our Computational Thinking Pathways research team has have partnered with a nationwide cohort of distr icts to co-design an iterative process to design and implement  CT pathways that integrate CT practices across cor e subject areas (Burke et al., 2019). Within an RPP of district leaders across the United States, we ask the following research questions to understand the strategies the y leverage to expand CT pathways within their distr icts: (1) What are the strategies district leaders leverage t o scale equitable opportunities for CT? (2) To what extent does each strategy to build capacity for innovation impl ementation address each dimension of scale: depth, spread, shift of ownership and sustainability?

## Setting and participants

Through a large-scale RPP with seven district partners across the U nited States, we have explored building and scaling inclusive CT pathways and codesigning a net work of support across district teams. The RPP was formed with three districts in 2017, and later scaled up to an additional three districts in 2022 with one additional district brought onboard in 2023. In this research, we engag ed six district partners in interviews to understand the resources and strategies they have used to develop a meaningful and equitable CT pathway, and to what extent their pathway has met each dimension of  scale. District leaders from the districts have for med multi-year relationships with the research team in co-designing research questions and implementation pla ns related to scaling inclusive pathways. We conducted 30-40 minute interviews with district and school-based leaders from the six district teams, including Directors of Inno vation, Library Instructional Technology Specialist s, Instructional Technology Coordinators, and Curriculum Coordinators. The six districts were selected because all six had developed a CT pathway and have been implementing that pathway for multiple years. ## Findings

Our analysis revealed three distinct approaches lea ders used to specify CT pathways within their distr ict: Early Adopters, Targeted Integration and Curriculum Adopt ion. We found that each approach inherently promote s specific aspects of scale, and that wra paround strategies either promote or inhibit the de velopment of additional dimensions for scale. We present our findings below  as vignettes of three districts, each highlighting  one of the identified approaches, as well as the strategies which were related to scaling the initiative in that context. Early adopters Bayside County Schools is a large, urban school district with over 250,000 students and over 300 schools. In such a large district, they have leveraged a strong team  of district champions to provide professional lear ning workshops and resources to educator s, which has facilitated a group of early adopters  to integrate CT in their contexts. District leaders described these early ad opters as extremely enthusiastic, volunteering to a ttend professional learning sessions, posting on social media and reaching out to the district leaders to share their work. However, it has been challenging for district imple mentation to move beyond these early adopters due t o the district's large size, competing priorities and shifting leadership. Ownership: In Bayside County, ownership varies. While the early adopters and the district CT leader ship team have high ownership of the CT pathway, th ere is limited ownership on the part of district administrators and building principals. Outside of the early adopters, most classroom teachers are unsure what CT is and how it  relates to their instruction. Overall, administrators have not taken ownership of the initiative due to competing priorities. Currently leaders are focused on learning  gains in math and ELA, and they do not understand the potent ial of CT learning experiences to promote learning in these subject areas. The team has faced challenges commun icating with school leaders so th at they know what CT is and can understand how it connects to their broader goals. Additionally, changes in district leadership have made it difficult for school leaders to understand how i t aligns with ongoing initiatives. They described t he challenge of having to adapt and align to shifting initiatives as superintendent and other leaders change saying, “some of the key players keep changing. We get people in leaders hip positions that are advocates, bring it up at sc hool board meetings, and then they change positions or move on.” In the meantime, it seems most of the ownership lies with the district team itself. The team has been left to ask themselves how we can make it important to leadership while staying aligned with higher priority initiatives. One team member explained, “It has to go from just us doing it to them realizing they have some ownership too. Becaus e right now they think oh that’s important, but the y think oh we’re taking care of it.” While working with ear ly adopters has led to high ownership from this sma ll group, it has been challenging to bring administrators and  additional teachers on board. Spread: Bayside is a very large district, and awareness about their CT pathway is not very far reaching. District leaders projected that maybe 10% of people know about it. And of 252 teachers surveyed in 2023, only 20% indicated that they were aware of  CT pathways. The district team indicated teachers with  more awareness are likely educators that have a ba ckground in computer science or information technology. Because CT is related to problem solving across disciplines, there is a good possibility that teachers are integrating CT practices, such as debugging and pattern recognition, and are not aware of it simply because they do not know wha t CT is. However, district leaders believe it is important for teachers to be aware of CT in order to have common language to describe the practices. They explained, “It is not enough to know that you’re doing it. You have to model for students how you are doing it.”  They believe students need to see themselves as knowers and doers of comp uter science, so that they know they have the skill s and confidence to succeed in computer science classes a nd careers. Depth: In order to know if CT is being implemented in alignment with their vision for the initiative, the district leaders expressed a need for more access and insight to student voice. They explained that l earners should be able to use common language (e.g. “debugging,” “finding patterns”) to describe CT str ategies to solve problems. In PD with early adopter s of CT, teachers began using this common language related to CT but the district team has not seen the use of that common language translate to classroom practice more widel y. So while the small group of Early Adopters have depth in the CT implementation, that depth is only seen in a limited number of classrooms. Sustainability. District leaders are hopeful that new state CS standards will motiva te CT integration across content areas. In order to sustain CT integration, they believe that connecting CT to ins tructional calendars of core content seems critical. As a next step, they plan to meet with all of the curriculum areas and consider how they can include CT in their focus areas. Additionally, they have convened a core team of school and district leaders and external partners to conduct a root cause analysis around awareness of their CT pathway.

Targeted integration Trailblazer County School District is a small, rural district which has leveraged targeted integration to implement their CT initiative. Targeted integration involves designing lessons that intentionally integrate CT w here it is complementary to existing standards and curricula. This approach enables teachers to integrate CT with out additional demands on instructional time. In Trailb lazer County, teacher leaders from across the distr ict were invited to develop a “competency map” in which standards and learning opportunities were identified fo r each grade level across five CT competencies and provided resources and lesson plans for these CT practices and skills. The competency map has been iteratively improved over five years and CT continues to be a high priority initiative within the district. Ownership: The district and teacher leaders that envisioned, developed, and continue to shape the competency map have high ownership in the integration of CT practices and skills. The process of identifying synergistic points of integration of CT with content and then m atching activities and resources to these points results in CT lessons that teachers have designed a nd developed, resulting in high ownership for those  involved in the process. However, it is less clear h ow much ownership all teachers have in integrating CT. While the competency map is a robust, well-developed resource for teachers, those not directly involved in its creation have less ownership in the activities suggested. Spread: One of the key roles of the teacher leaders that contribute to the competency maps is to share the CT practices and skills identified in the competency map with other teachers in their building. As a district leader explained, “They meet with grade levels, and they share this i nformation with them.” The teacher leaders have also provided school-wide professional development for their buildings. This combination of teacher leaders and the compete ncy map itself has allowed knowledge of CT practice s and skills to reach many teachers across multiple schools. Furthermore, because every two years new teacher leaders are selected to revise and update the competency map, many teachers have deep knowledge about the competency map. Depth: While the competency map provides resources for teachers to richly integrate CT, Trailblazer County has found that ensuring the depth of CT integration  requires observing CT lessons in action. One tool that they have begun to develop to notice and recognize CT in practice is a competency “Look-for” document that focuses on student voice, which specifies what students sho uld be doing and saying during a CT lesson. These n onevaluative look-for documents have provided the district leaders with a way to assess the extent to which the CT lessons that have been written, ar e implemented in a rich and meaningful way that is impacting student learning of CT. The look-for documents can then assist the district leaders with getting a sense of where other teachers’ are in their implementation and whether or not the integration of CT is being done from a compliant stance or if the implementation reflects a robust understanding of how CT can elevate content area teaching and pro vide durable skills to students.  Sustainability. Sustainability is still a challenge in Trailblaze r. Post-COVID, a district leader shared “[Teacher turnover and lack of applicants] has just really been a really big challenge for us.” Having large numbers of new teachers every year means havi ng to train these teachers on district initiatives like CT in addition to the curriculum, standards, and systems of the county. Trailblazer also noticed that teachers were  not accessing the competency map document to implement the lessons. As a response, the district decided to take the lessons that were in the CT map and add them to the pacing guides that the teachers were already using on a daily basis in order to make the lessons and resources more readily accessible. Curriculum Adoption Imagination Park is a medium-sized suburban school district with 34 schools and over 25,000 students. They began their CT initiative in 2018 focusing on teach er professional learning and targeted integration. In recent years, they have shifted to an implementation strat egy centered around curriculum adoption  in order to promote spread and sustainability. As a district leader shared, The easiest way to make it stick was to have an exp erience kind of outlined through the curriculum. When it was more open ended and autonom[ous] it was hard to gain traction…it was too high level…it was so much time and effort and w ork…we weren’t moving th e needle fast enough…So having something concrete was really helpful at elementary school, and then tying it to required courses at middle school…was helpful.

Through this, the focus is less on teacher professi onal development and personalized lesson planning a nd more on curricular resources that can be provided in ali gnment with course-objectives to ensure that teachers have something concrete to do in the classroom. This has increased spread of the implementation and, although fidelity is still a work in progress, it has supported the jump from early adopters who were excited about CT to all teachers at the elementary level and access for all students at the middle school level through a required course. Ownership: Over the course of their CT Pathways work, district  level ownership in the initiative has expanded. In itially, one small team of district leaders owned the initiative  and led professional development efforts with teac hers based on their interest. Due to staffing changes and grow ing opportunities, the roll out of the new curricul um is being handled by a different team, expanding district-level ownership of the work. Simultaneously, owners hip of the individual learning experiences has shifted throughout Imagination Park’s work. Initially, teachers attend ed professional learning and developed CT integrated l esson plans based on their interest and excitement.  Now, to support spread and sustainability, the main push of the initiative is based on ready-made or district-wide resources. This shifts ownership of individual lessons from te achers to the district leaders. Spread: The CT initiative has certainly spread in Imagination Park since its conception, most recently due to the previously adopted elementary science curriculum which explicitly integrates CT practices and required middle school courses that integrate CT. Elementary Library Media Center directors also make connections to CT in their makerspaces and the “specials” classes that they teach. The team characterizes their current stage as “on a path towards full adoption.” To further promote spread, Imagination Park has explicitly con nected their CT Pathways work with their Portrait o f a Graduate. The district believes that CT “gives meat” to other initiatives such as Portrait of a Graduate, providing more concrete practices to operationalize the initi ative. Depth: In Imagination Park, the overall depth of teacher change was high for the early adopting teachers, bu t is lower for teachers who are implementing the ad opted curricula. To promote depth, Imagination Park has s upported teachers to implement the new elemen tary science curricula with wraparound supports such as coaches who work with teachers as they implement the new curriculum. The coaches first modeled teaching the lessons so that teachers “were able to see it direc tly in their classroom with their students.” This helped to tran sfer knowledge of the curriculum and lessons as the  teachers took over as the lead with the coaches supporting. Sustainability. Imagination Park has found success in making the CT initiative “stick” by having it outlined thr ough curriculum. When the initiative was more open ended and provided more autonomy for teachers, it was hard to  gain traf fic and get teachers, particularly teachers who weren’t early adopters, involved with the initiative. When there is a specific, clear action and resource connected to the initiative, such as the newly adopted curric ulum, teachers have concrete examples of what to implement and they are more likely to do it. Providing teache rs examples and PD and then having them create thei r own lessons was a large investment of time and professi onal learning and did not promote spread or sustain ability to the extent the di strict leaders wanted to see. Having a concrete res ource and concrete tasks for teachers also supported the district to sustain the initiative beyond early adopters and toward all teachers through modeling and support in their classrooms.

## Discussion

The previous section detailed three unique approaches our district partners have used to specify their CT initiatives over the past 3-6 years: Early Adopters, Targeted Integration, and Curriculum Adoption. In this section, we look across the vignettes to understand affordances and challenges of each approach, considering the dimens ions of scale (Coburn, 2003). First, we assess the degree t o which each approach fostered depth, ownership, sp read, and sustainability. Next, we share six strategies that one or more districts leveraged to promote scale wi thin their contexts. Finally, we identify implications of our findings for the research and practice communities. Districts’ approaches to specify computational thinking The approach each district used to specify CT inher ently promoted unique aspects of scale. The Early Adopters in Bayside have a great deal of depth and ownership. However, the district has not yet been able to sp read and sustain the initiative beyond the early adopters. Trailblazer County leveraged a Targeted Integration approach in which teachers identified synergistic learning oppo rtunities to integrate CT into existing curricula, promoting a medium level of depth and ownership. At the same time, the district has utilized a great deal of resources in order to provide ongoing professional development and sch ool based leadership to teachers in order to spread  and sustain the initiative. Finally, Imagination Park has leveraged Curriculum Adoption in order to provide resources to every teacher in order to scale and sustain CT w ithin the district. However, district leaders are u nsure about implementation fidelity and how much depth and owne rship teachers and students have over the initiativ e. Overall, each approach promotes varying degrees of depth and ownership  versus spread and sustainability; and occasionally these dimensions are at odds with one another. While we acknowledge the distinction between each of the four dimensions of scale, our analysis found  that depth and ownership and spread and sustainability a re mutually reinforcing in practice. These districts ele vate the need for methods to  elicit student voice as part of measuring the effectiveness of an initiative, which  is a key factor that is alternative to traditional  measures such as teacher surveys or test scores.

## Implications

Our study illustrated that the approach a district uses to specify an initiative, such as CT, along with supplemental strategies, can promote different aspects of scale.  In each case, specification alone (e.g. course cur riculum adoption) was insufficient t o effectively scale the initiative for all dimensio ns. Districts’ approaches initially prioritized depth, ownership, spread and sustainabi lity to different extents. While these are each dis tinct dimensions, we found depth/ownership and spread/sus tainability to be mutually reinforcing in practice.  Scaling strategies provide wraparound supports which are cr ucial to promote scale along each dimension. The mo st impactful scaling strategies for a district depend on contextual factors such as size, available resources, leadership, and existing initiatives. Therefore, educational leaders must strategically leverage scaling strategies that align with their district's mission and vision and effectively promote the adoption and sustainability of new initiatives.

## Conclusion

In this study, we explored how six districts in the United States approached the scaled implementation of districtwide K-12 CT Pathways. Over time, each partner has leveraged a different approach with unique affordances and disaffordances toward achieving  depth, ownership, spread, and sustainability. While  an early adopter approach promotes depth and ownership, it has not resulted i n spread and sustainability. Conversely, while a co urse and curriculum adoption approach promotes spread and su stainability, it hasn’t resulted in as much depth a nd ownership. Targeted integration promotes a mid-level of all four dimensions of scale which  promot e increasing depth, ownership, spread, and sustainability when s caling CT Pathways. These approaches, along with supplemental strategies inform how districts can strategi cally design implementation in alignment with their mission and vision to successfully scale educational innovations within their unique contexts.
