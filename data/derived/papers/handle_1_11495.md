# Cultivating Wellbeing in Learning Environments: Storywork, Kinship and Affect With Lands and Waters

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
There is a pressing need to reimagine education for social and ecological well-being. Persistent inequalities and underimagined forms of education not only prevent these developments, but continue to erode human well-being and creativity. Substantive changes require paradigm shifts in foundational conceptualizations of educational challenges and possibilities, including transformative understandings of students’ sense making and holistic cultural and intellectual lives. This symposium brings together scholarship focused on storywork, relationality or kinship and affect in the design of learning environments. Further the studies in this symposium are specifically focused on these dimensions of learning tied to field based or land and water based learning environments.

Paper 1: Designing for expansive affective landscapes in socio-ecological learning through beginning inquiry in noticings and wondering with places Jordan Sherry-Wagner, Megan Bang, Carrie Tzou

## Framing and study design

Our present moment is characterized by significant shifts across social and ecological systems that re quire us to develop more just and sustainable ways of learning with and relating to the rest of the natural world (Berkes, 2017; Whyte, 2018; Vossoughi et  al., 2024). In such a shifting landscape, socio-ecological stories of death, destruction, and decay have become commonplace, sig nificantly impacting mental health outcomes through narrowing possibilities to imagine and enact futures grounded in hope, health, and collective well-being (Cunsolo & Ellis, 2018; Ojala et al., 2021). In learning env ironments, a primary focus on  increasing disciplin ary content knowledge is often positioned way to ameliorate suc h feelings through greater epistemic know-how. While we place a high value on developing greater understand ing of complex socio-ecological systems, our work is grounded in the assumption that additional discipli nary content knowledge alone is not sufficient for supporting well-being, and in many cases the c ause of increased distress. In this space, we descr ibe a line of design and analysis aimed at beginning socio-ecological sensemaking in an affect of awe and wond ering through routine outdoor activities which position human beings as a part of the rest of the natural world (Bang & Marin, 2015). This paper analyzes data collected within the Learn ing in Places (LiP) project to better understand the impacts of pedagogical approaches designed to suppo rt a fuller range of affective and embodied states in socioecological learning environments. Through participa tory design-based research (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) we have intervened in knowledge-fixated paradigms of education through the iterative design and implementation of educative materials and learning activities in whic h pathways of inquiry emerge from noticings, wonder ing, and building relationships with local places and kinds. Within our broader ecosystem of materials, this paper elevates practices of “wondering walks” to demonstrate how b eginning inquiry in emplaced and embodied wonder, j oy, and awe can nurtures more healthful nature-culture relations to expand affective possibilities for life and learning (Sherry-Wagner, 2023) Data sources and analysis We present findings from analysis of video data gathered during “wondering walks” - a focal activity in Learning in Places designed to elicit noticings and wonderings from learners as they routinely walk and build relationships with local outdoor places. In total, this set includes just over 98 hours (98:0 8:43) of video data from a total of 38 wondering walk implementations across 10 classrooms and two schools. Video data was collected during i ndoor classroom activities (walk launches and syntheses) using a tripod-mounted camera, and during outdoor activities using chest-mounted Go-Pro cameras.

Data were segmented, normalized, and inductively coded for the presence or absence of key dimensions of socio-ecological sensemaking, nature-culture construals, and speculative practices in pl aces to produce internally comparable measure of representationa l density. These codes were developed and refined u sing methods in grounded analysis (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) to progressively refine conceptual categories (Engle et al., 2007; Derry et al. 2010). Beginning with a descriptive analysis, we report findings on both significant child code co-occurrence as well as shifts in code density across focal descriptors.

## Findings

We report on several significant findings. First, a nalysis of child code co-occurrence reveals a statistically significant triangulation between and among ‘place emergent’, ‘wondering at’, and ‘species kinds, behaviors’ (r=.716, r=.529, and r=.535 respectively). This finding reinforces how direct engagement with local places and morethan-humans’ kinds is conducive to sensemaking grounded in experiences of awe and wonder. Together, these relationships point to the special significance of speculative sensemaking emergent from noticing with in places which are mediated by an affect of wonder and awe. These findings support our design conjectures aroun d the importance of routine outdoor activity for facilitating both affectively rich learning experiences and sophisticated scientific inquiry into the form and function of natural kinds as mutually reinforcing domains of practice. Second, calculating shifts in the representational density of child codes across indoor and outdoor environment reveals noticeable shifts in socio-ecological sensemaking and wondering in places. Of particular note, there is a 810% increase in ‘wondering at’ ou tdoor compared to indoors. This finding empirically  supports our design conjecture that routine outdoor learning is central to nurturing affects of awe, wonder, and amazement within socio-ecological sensemaking.

Finally, density measurements of ‘place’ child code s across indoor and outdoor contexts document a 767% increase in ‘emergence’ outdoors, and an over 1200% increase in ‘abstraction’ during indoor activity. While clearly indicating the importance of outdoor  activity for such sensemaking, we understand these findings as highlighting the complementary nature of indoor and  outdoor activities. In elevating their respective strengths in cultivating learners’ abilities to toggle between s cales of concreteness and abstraction, these findings also reinforce the limitations of primarily indoor learning for generating affectively rich socio-ecological sensemaking.

## Implications and contributions

The findings of this analysis are of deep consequence for both design work in Learning in Places and for the field of education more broadly. To the former, these findings strongly suggests that the Learning in Places ecosystem of materials and activities is engaging youth in affectivel-rich and cognitively rigorous scientific practice. Aligning with how expert scientists talk about thei r affective motivations to pursue learning in their  field of practice (Jaber & Hammer, 2016), these relationship s also sug gest that our designs for socio-ecological sensemaking are shifting both the conditions and the content of learning through routine forms of outdoor activity that nurture both the cognitive and emotional dimen sions of learning. Indeed, these findings speak  to how close proximity within the natural world can generate affectively-engaged experiences of surprise, awe, and joy. Both practically and methodologically, this analysis contributes greatly to how we understand and design learning to support more ethical forms of wondering  with people, places, and more-than-humans as important to the well-being of youth, families, and communities. In evide ncing the occurrence of key ideas, forms of sensemaking, and design aims of the Learning in Pla ces project in unfolding interaction, we understand  the data and claims presented through this analysis as contr ibuting to the characterization of particularly pot ent possibilities for imagining and enacting new forms of socioecological engagement given the global chal lenges and opportunities of the 21st century.

We end with introduction and expansion on two emerg ent design principles evidenced by our primary findings to support teaching and learning in parallel context. ● Design Principle 1: Socio-ecological learning activities should be designed t o include routine outdoor experiences to cultivate expansive affective landscapes that enrich possibilities for life and well-being. ● Design Principle 2: Designs for socio-ecological sensemaking should elevate the strengths  of outdoor and indoor places to create local embodied experien ces alongside developing capacities for abstraction and generalization.

Paper 2: Storywork and well-being Cece Hoffman

## Framing and study design

This paper focuses on ways in which storywork creat es conditions for wellbeing. Specifically it will s hare how the mutually constituting and reciprocal practices of Indigenous storywork supports holistic and relat ional wellbeing. We will explicate how comm unity members design and engage in story as a form of felt pedagogical mediation for relational wellbeing. This process en tails storytelling / listening relations, interacti ons, and practices—all which are grounded in Indigenous ontologies of relational wellbeing. Taking form through Indigenous axiologies and epist emologies across space and time, storywork is rooted in place, land, and histories. Storywork, ev en if framed in different terms, is fundamental to the ways human beings live and learn (Burke, 1945; Herman, 2 013; Hymes, 1996). Grounded in Indigenous lifeways, stories are living (Archibald, 2008). They come from land and take shape across generations in which they travel. In other words, stories have been persisting across  generations and geographies since  time immemorial. It is because of these practices that storywork offers a pedagogical framework that supports the cultivation  of educators’ onto-epistemic and ethical attunement and deliberation amidst the complexities of moment-to-moment interaction. Through this paper we will uplift storywork’s abili ty to transform relational conditions amongst all those who take part in the process. As elaborated by Archibald’s (2008) work on Indigenous storywork, storywork engages complexity and holistically “educat es the heart, mind, body and spirit”, calls for engage d and felt listening, entails practicing values of respect, reverence, responsibility and reciprocity, offers teachings to “those who know how to ask and how to learn” (Cajete, 1994, p. 41). Data sources and analysis This paper takes place within ISTEAM (Indigenous sc ience Technology Engineering Arts and Mathematics) — an place-based designed learning environment for intergenerational, intertribal, multi-site Indigenous community members, also noted in other works (Bang,  et al., 2015). Through iterative design across deca des, storywork has become a key activity to the learning environment. This paper will share data from the most recent iteration, from the summer of 2024 in the great lakes area of Chicago. In the process of co-designing storywork with community members, the group intentionally decided to mix liv ed stories and culturally traditional stories with the goal of providing more opportunities for young people to he ar about the lives of people, and especially elder s, in their community. This decision to include more lived experiences created a large shift in the storywork makeup of the program. Utilizing interaction analysis of what we call opening and closing circles in our programming  which includes 11 sessions each approximately 45 minutes each for a total of approximately 495 minutes of implementation data, this paper analyzes how the design decisions, along with the pedagogical moves and nature of storywork, affected the story experience for people in the program.

## Findings

Our findings detail how intergenerational educators  of ISTEAM take up these practices for the purpose of collective wellbeing. Amongst early emerging findin gs, this study analyzes various types of stories and storytelling practices that community members engage in pedagogically throughout the week-long program. At a high level, the findings reside in three overarchin g areas 1) conceptual/relational ecologies within t he stories themselves, 2) felt pedagogical moves of storytellers, and 3) reception/reciprocal listening practices. For example, the stories that are told within ISTEAM touch on to pics relating to Indigeneity, just relations with l ands and waters, culture, histories, and many other ideas th at Indigenous youth are often deprived of in school s, and they are done in ways that move from Indigenous ontologi es and support relational wellbeing. This seems esp ecially imperative, given the unequal distribution of narrative rights—who is allowed to tell what stories under which conditions (Hymes, 1996) —and the enactment of cultural, epistemic, and lingu istic violence through settler colonialism (Battiste, 2000). Through this paper we will engage with these various forms of storywork, and attune to the ways that Indigenous relationality mediates across the whole space —atmospherically and within teller/listener practices.

## Implications and contributions

In understanding these complex practices we can better attune to the ways in which stories enact affective, ethical and conceptual sensemaking around living in recipro cal and caring relationships with one another and l ands and waters. Through the sharing o f traditional cultural stories, and lived experienc es, these stories share histories, philosophies and teachings around being a human amongst many relations within the world. They help provide a sense of belonging, connectedness and collective continuance through not only what is shared, but also how they are shared. In better understanding the beauty and power of storywork, we can more strongly attend to the ways in which our communities are always practicing story as a form of relational wellbeing. Paper 4: Storywork for Indigenous futures Forrest Bruce, Megan Bang, Anna Lees

## Framing and study design

This paper takes up the imagination of socio-ecological futures as a means of assessing and cultivating wellbeing for young people. While imagining thriving futures is vital to wellbeing (Oyserman & Fryberg, 2006), t he scale of socio-ecological change and uncertainty in this present moment means that young  people’s view of the future exists in an affective landscape saturated by clima te anxiety, worry and grief (Ojala et al., 2021). I ndigenous people are particularly affected by climate anxiety  as Indigenous lands are most heavily impacted by c limate change (Garnett et al., 2018), and resource extraction (Owen et al., 2023). Further, climate anxiety is compounded by settler colonialism which reinforces Indigenous erasure and can negatively affect Indigenous youths’ ability to project the collective continuance (Whyte, 2018) of  their community into the future (Elliot-Groves & Fryberg, 2018). Education plays a pivotal role in opening up  imaginative space for young people to envision fut ure possibilities and ena ct them in the here-and-now (Freire, 1970). However, visions and enactments  of thriving socio-ecological futures tend to be limited by the fact t hat dominant forms of education prioritize indoor a ctivity and foreclose interaction with the natural world (B ang & Marin, 2015). This study is focused on a learn ing environment deliberately designed to support youth in reasoning about the future of places and communities given a changing climate. This study was conducted across  two Indigenous communities, one rura l reservation community in the midwestern United States and one urban inter-tribal community whose current origins are often sourced to be a result of the 1950s and1960s termin ations and relocation act-despite their being a continuity of Indigenous peoples in this urban setting. This stud y asks: How do students reason about their futures?  And, how did our program narrate and support students in imagining futures for themselves, their communities and our lands and waters?

Data sources and analysis This study draws on two data sources across two years of the project. First we examine the ways in which presents and futures were framed in the context of 46 introd uctions of beginnings of program activities across the two years in one community and 11 introductions or begi nnings of activities in the second community in one  year of the program. The beginning of  activities were anal yzed for the ways in which past, present and future  were narrated to young people and how the focal activities were positioned in relation to these temporalities. A second data source and analysis is drawn from an embedded assessment of summer programs across the two Indigenous communities. These assessments asked youth to draw pictures of the places they were in now and in the future on the last day of programming. Then youth were asked to narrate their pictures to an interviewer. These data were coded for the conceptual ecologies reflected in the pictures and the narratives as well as the ways in which youth were conceptualizing futures. There were a total of 56 youth that participated in the study.

## Findings

There are several layers of findings we present in this work. First we provide findings about the narr atives and temporailites implemented by educators. These findi ngs are presented by year 1 and year 2. We coded fo r generalized affect around the future. F ive primary codes emerged from the data that includ e: thriving, negative, mixed or multiple, neutral, and specifically articu lated Indigenous futures. In year one we find that educators emphasize multiple temporalities in activity beginnings. However, the future temporalities are generalized and far less detailed than past and present. Findings from youth drawings and interviews from year one reflect  that 31% (urban) and 33 (rural) of youth imagined thriving futures, 38% (urban) and 44% (rural)  imagined negative futures, 13% (urban) and 0% of (rural) youth imagined multip le types of futures, and 19% (urban) and 22% (rural) were neutral or didn’t specifically assign an outlook. I mportantly, and perhaps most disturbingly are our y ear one findings about these youths' perceptions of whether  Indigenous peoples were present in the future. Onl y 19% or urbandwelling Native youth and 44% of rural reserv ation dwelling Native youth imagined Native people in the future. We engaged the community with these findings and redesigned the program. A key aspect of this redesign was to emphasize specific futures and well as lived stories of change and resilience in the program. Year 2 findings with student interviews demonstrated a significant shift in future imaginings wherein  61% of youth imagined thriving futures, 28% continued to imagine only neg ative futures, 12% were mixed or neutral. We also s aw a significant increase in (39% up from 19%) youth who  imagined Indigenous peoples in the future.  Finall y we share a deeper anal ysis of the features of students' pictures and narr atives that were associated with thriving Indigenous futures.

## Implications and contributions

There is an overall paucity of educational environments that teach about Indigenous peoples. Most educators and Americans do not have any accurate information abou t Indigenous peoples and regularly assume NAtive pe ople are gone (e.g. Fryberg et al, 2024). Indeed less than 50% of states in the US require schools to teach about Native people and of those that did the standards are almost exclusively of pre-1900 Indigenous people (Sher et al. 2019). This systemic narrative of NAtive people impacts Na tive youth  too. Native people experience extreme disproportionality with respect to rates of mental health and suicidation. The systemic message of our  absence contributes to these problems. Disrupting this mass  scale misinformation is crucial to Indigenous peop les wellbeing but also just democracies. While our prog ram findings  are not where we would like them to b e, this study helps to identify key designs and pedagogical  practices that help to disrupt the overwhelming pa tterns in learning environments. Further, our deeper analysis of students that did imagine thriving Indigenous futures helps to identify possible ways forward at a broader scale.

Paper 5: Indigenous storywork: Cultivating kinship with more-than-humans and supporting youth wellbeing Nikki Barry

## Framing and study design

This study takes place in the context of an Indigen ous summer program and explores the questions: 1) H ow can storytelling cultivate close plant-human relationships, and 2) How do these plant-human relationships relate to youth well-being? The work draws on Indigenous AOEs (described below) and Indigenous Storywork literature. We use Indigenous axiologies, ontologies, and epist emologies, or Indigenous AOEs to describe aspects of thinking, being, and valuing embedded within Ind igenous knowledge systems. Axiologies are what we v alue ethically or aesthetically, ontologies are what we believe and how we enact those beliefs, and epis temologies are what we know and how we come to know it. AOEs vary by culture and context, but central to many Indigen ous peoples’ AOEs, are respectful and reciprocal kin relations with more-than-human beings. Indigenous AOEs are also embedded in our stories. S torywork connects traditional and personal stories with cultural practices as they simultaneously shif t and are maintained through generations (Archibald, 2008). The process of storytelling and storylistening is a process of teaching and learning. Our stories are embedded with complex thought about social and ecological worlds and teach us to engage with the rest of nature resp ectfully and from a place of humility (Marin & Bang, 2015). And we posit that sto ries also help cultivate kin relations with more-than-humans.

The study occurs within the context of an Indigenou s STEAM program called ISTEAM, which was initially a summer program built from ongoing efforts that began in Chicago in 2002. It has since grown to occur in several different Indigenous communities and now  offers seasonal programming throughout the year ser ving youth and families. ISTEAM was co-designed & implemented with intergenerational Indig enous community members & organizations to teach STEAM in alignment  with our cultural practices & pedagogies. Cent ral to Indigenous AOEs, stories have been a major componen t of ISTEAM’s pedagogy. Storytelling starts program activities and learning goals throughout programs are connected to these stories. Data sources and analysis This paper is focused on three Indigenous siblings – Nuki (Noo-kee, he/him), Mea (Muh-ah, she/they), and A’nii (Ah-nee, they/them) – and their experiences of Storywork in relation to S tinging Nettle. Stinging Nettle is a culturally salient plant to many tr ibes and has territories across Turtle Island and b eyond. All three siblings had participated in ISTEAM between 2014 and 2023. These  youth were raised in the same home with learning experiences rooted in Indigenous AOEs within their family and through ISTEAM. Data for the study was primarily focused on 2023 in terview data from each of the focal participants. These semi-structured interviews were each about 30 minutes long. The data also included docum ents (such as daily schedules and curriculum), field notes, and a udiovisual data from 9 years of ISTEAM design meeti ngs and the program itself.

Analysis included organizing, logging, and then wri ting analytical memos of the data and then identifying where the Nettle story was referenced. From there I designed the interview protocols for t he focal participants, and conducted and transcribed the interviews. Next, I coded the interview data using a software called Dedoose. I then performed a construct analysis (Miles et al., 1994) to examine similarities and differences between the participants’ interview responses, a time order analysis (Miles et al., 1994) and a roles & relations analysis to understand patterns and themes that emerged.

## Findings

The study finds that listening to and telling How Nettle Saved the People encouraged kinship between Nettle and the participants through embodiment of teachings from the story. The study also finds that this kinship supported the youth’s wellbeing.

Listening to and telling How Nettle Saved the People  helped the youth to understand Nettle as their relative. For example, in their interview, A’nii said that they learned from the story that, “There's like a respect tonettle and to our plant families. Learning how they  can help us with the specific instructions, like drinking [tea], like whipping yourself with the Nettle, teaching us how we can use that all respectfully to help us. Kind of learning that... Nettle has power.” Further, the telling and subsequent embodiment of How Nettle Saved the People  created closeness, bringing the youth physically closer tonettle beca use they understood the nature of Nettles as healer s. This cultivated a socially close kin relationship between youth and Nettle while supporting youth wellbeing. Nuki said: “We were harvesting…so I was seeing {laughs} I was seeing how painful it would be if I got stung. It wasn’t that bad. But just, working alongside a bunch of other people harvesting Nettle is, I don’t know. Nettle taught me something there.  Not sure what it  was, but, I can’t put it in words. I feel like it made me closer to who I am. As Anishinaabe, as Diné. I mean closer to who I am, you know, practicing – even if it wasn’t my own lands and my own tradition, just knowing that people have been doing this for  more time than I can possible imagine. And there we re probably kids {laughs} doing the same stupid stuff like getting stung on purpose long time ago. It made me feel good. I remember looking up and jus t watching as everybody was harvesting the Nettle and just smiling and enjoying life. And just being grat eful for my family, for my siblings and for those who blessed me with this knowledge. I really enjoyed it.” Mea also described how teachings about Nettle’s sting helped them to feel strong. Mea said in their interview: “[Nettle] really helped me try to re-examine how I walk in the world. Normally, when peo ple hear 'Oh, if you touch this. It's gonna hurt.' They 're like, 'Why, why would that exist?'... I candefinitely translate a lot of the teachings that I' ve seen and heard about Nettle to myself and how to cope with different things. Because often at  school I'm labeled dramatic, extra loud, or I'm sarcastic. I have like a minuscule, a bite to how I joke around…And then [I] have to realize that moment where I was being mean, it was actually  a defense from someone not respecting me or my space. And then, similarly tonettle, there was that sting. It's not meant to be like a 'I hate you' moment …you need to respect the plant in the same way that people need to respect other people. …It easily could translate then into how to be a better person.” Mea saw the sting as a way of both learning to be r espectful and to demand respect through protecting one’s boundaries. These quotes illustrate how teachings about Nettle’s sting were significant for Nuki and Mea’s wellbeing and identity as Indigenous people. This is cr itically consequential. Research suggests that, for  Indigenous youth, feeling secure in their cultural identities may be a protective factor against suicide (Elliott, 2019) which is currently the leading cause of death for Indigenous  youth ages 10-14 and the second leading cause of death for Indigenous youth and young adults ages 15-34 in the United States (Center for Disease Control  & Prevention, n.d.).

## Implications and contributions

This paper shows how, when children are taught surr ounded by their stories, they begin to live the val ues that these stories teach. Stories and Storywork are alive in children in ISTEAM in ways they aren’t always – ISTEAM has regenerated the embodiment of culturally resurgent stories for children and youth. For A’nii, Mea, and Nuki, it supported their positive identity development as Indigenous youth. It taught them healthy ways of being, and it brought them closer to their relatives – human and more-than-human. The findings animate what it looks like for young Indigenous people who grow up in an education al program that does not question their stories or AOEs. Storywork and kinship with more-than-humans matters for them and perhaps for the world a s we shift  naturehuman relations and, ultimately, environmental beha viors and decision making toward sustainable and ju st futures.
