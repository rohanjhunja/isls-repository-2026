# “We Conquered the Blank Page”: Examining Researcher and Coach Partnerships’ Creation of Shared Objects

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explored how researcher–coach dyads collaborated to create research-based briefs for classroom use. Data from a two-day workshop and dyads’ final products were analyzed using interaction analysis. The dyad’s work illuminated how products and relationships evolved together, fostering ownership and collaboration. These findings inform future researcher–practitioner partnerships and guide facilitation of effective collaborations by highlighting relationship building, agency, and ownership to shape joint work.

## Introduction and theoretical framework

Despite advances in educational research, theory and practice remain separated, as do teacher and rese archer communities. Researchers provide theory and empiric al evidence to improve practice, while instructors provide classroom insights, making collaborat ion mutually beneficial (Crain-Dorough & Elder, 2021). Researchers and teacher coaches collaborated on a two-day professional learning event for this project. One dyad's interactions were examined to determine how they imagined a teac her-facing product and  how collaboration created shared ownership. The following research questions led to this work's analysis: (1) How do coach-researcher pairs create a teacher-facing product? (2) How did dyads' collaborative me thods lead to partner ownership of their shared object?

This study designed and analyzed coach-researcher interactions using third-generation Cultural Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) (Engeström, 1999, 2001). The second generation of activity theory introduces a collective activity system with a goal mediated by rules, community, and division of labor (Leont'e v, 1981; Engeström, 2001). Vygotsky (1978) prioritized media tors that guided individual goals. The third-generation studies multi-activity system interactions. We used CHAT to explo re how dyads learned collectively during a professional workshop using STEM coaches' and resea rchers' activity systems that moved towards a commo n system. This paper's CHAT analysis examines  the collective activity system and shared artifact,  a research-practice brief (RPB) that was created (Danish & Saleh, 2014; Engeström, 1999; Wertsch, 198 1). Shared objects may alter over time (Danish & Saleh, 2014; Cole & Engestrom, 1993; Engestrom, 1990).

## Methods

Three STEM coaches were recruited for this qualitative study. STEM coaches were chosen for their abilities to affect numerous teachers and bridge research and pr actice. Each coach identified a practice issue or o pportunity, and appropriate researchers were recruited. Based o n the coach's topic and the researcher's expertise, dyads were matched. In a two-day professional learning workshop, the dyads used planned and spontaneous activities and discussions to build trust, understand the coach's school environment, and solve the problem. In the training, dyads constructed a research-based teacher deliverable brief. The format was ope n-ended, although dyads received examples. We recorded researcher-coach communication during work sessions and final products. This report focuses on Emma (researcher) and Sarah (STEM coach), who developed a unique deliverable. We used interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to identify occ asions when the pairs established goals, expectatio ns, and intents. Cultural Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) guided our analysis of their  collaboration using Danish & Saleh's (2014) shared artifact creation framework.

## Findings

Our findings highlight one dyad, Emma and Sarah, wh ose final deliverable stood out the most —a double-sided flier with AI ethics scenarios, resources, and rationale for classroom discussions. Their work demonstrated strong agency and ownership, prompting fu rther analysis of the processes that led to their t eacher-friendly product. We define three phases to analyze this data from a CHAT approach. In the first two phases, which we call conceptualiz ing and designing, Emma and Sarah worked together to define their problem of practice and align their  professional development (PD) material. Initially,  Emma led the conversation, but Sarah became more engaged as they discussed dis trict policies on AI. Emma contributed when the discussion shifted to AI curriculum develo pment and her research. Their mutual affirmations a nd engaged dialogue led to a consensus to focus on AI ethics for their final brief, with Sarah suggesting a shift from district-specific policies to a student-centered approach. This led to both agreeing on the ir problem and collaboratively planning their solution. In the thi rd phase (creating), Sarah and Emma discussed how t o design a deliverable, ensuring it would be both teacher-guided and engaging for students. Through their con versations, Sarah's increased confidence and Emma's suggestions —such as using real-life "sticky problems" related to AI ethics, such as AI-generated art mimicking artists’ styles without permission, demonstrated their shared ownership and commitment to creating a useful, engaging resource. By the end, they celebrated their progress with laughter, with Sarah exclaiming, “We beat the blank page! We conquered it!” signaling increased comfort and collaboration between them and a sense of accomplishment with the ir shared object. These phases can be seen in the C HAT diagram in Figure 1 below.

## Discussion

At the start of the workshop, the dyad leveraged their individual backgrounds to define and narrow their problem of practice. This enabled focused discussions on ap plying their values to the brief, with Sarah expand ing the audience to include students, helping clarify goals  and regain momentum. By focusing on the audience, they envisioned how their brief could address the identi fied problem, and the dyad’s teamwork strengthened as they turned their ideas into a final brief. They resolved a logistical issue about section order by revisiting the audience’s needs identified earlier, aided by Emma’s clarifying questions. This alignment helped them quickly overcome the hurdle and resume a collaborative rhythm, finalizing their resource with humor and camaraderie. Throughout the three phases, as the shared object took shape, the relationship between the dyad became more trusting and easygoing, leading them to celebrate their accomplished goal together when their individual objects were brought into one shared product.  The dyads’ activities and  discussions offer insights for refining future ite rations of the project and workshops. Key recommendations for futu re iterations and similar research-practitioner partnerships include emphasizing relationship-building, guiding problem identification, fostering  productive disagreement, and encouraging focus on audience needs to create well-tailored final products. Future workshops should provide flexible models for deliverables to support ownersh ip and purpose while using a CH AT-based framework to analyze dyad dynamics and identify how context, interests, and experiences shape collaboration.
