# Constructing an “Us” in Teacher Work Groups: Affordances and Limitations for Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research on teacher collaboration suggests that shared values and a sense of solidarity are important resources for generative discourse and robust teacher learning. Drawing on data from two groups of elementary mathematics teachers, this paper examines how teachers construct identities as “us” and the effects of these processes on teachers’ opportunities to engage in equity-and justice-oriented sensemaking and learning. The analysis demonstrates that constructions of “them” sometimes accompany constructions of “us” and shows how this inhibits teachers’ opportunities to learn. Strategies for building solidarity without sparking us/them dynamics, grounded in data, are discussed.

## Background and purpose

Studies of teachers’ collaborative work groups often point to shared values and a sense of being “in i t together” as important for the kinds of critical, reflective discourse that support robust teacher learning, sug gesting that these groups benefit from developing a collective identity or sense of “us” (Baldinger, 2018; see also Lefstein et al., 2020; Bolam et al., 2005; Louis et al., 1996). A sense of “us” appears to be especially salient for groups with shared commitments to centering students and advancing equity and justice (e.g., Benitez et al., 2009; McCotter, 2001; Picower, 2011). Yet constructions of “us” may define what is bad, deficient, and wrong as well as what is normal, valued, and good. In this paper, we examine how teacher groups construct identities as “us,” what values anchor these group identities, who may be constructed as “them,” and the effects of these dynamics on teachers’ opportunities to engage in equity-and justice-oriented sense-making and learning. Prior research While many studies highlight the potential of teacher collaboration to support teacher learning (e.g., Bolam et al., 2005; Darling-Hammond et al., 2017), others indicat e that such collaboration may fall short of produci ng the intended learning or change (e.g., Author, 2016; St okes, 2001). Little (1990) has gone so far as to ob serve that teacher collaboration is often “contrived, inauthentic, grafted on, [and] perched precariously … on the margins of real work” (p. 510). One reason for this is that collaboration is inherently laden with the risk of others’ judgment, particularly when it involves opening one’s practice for the kind of examination and reflection that i s needed to foster learning. As one teacher explained to Le Fevre (2014), “It is hard to show what you do to the others, because … you just do what you think is best for your kids, what you feel in your heart but then it might not match up as good enough against someone else’s way of teaching”  (pp. 59–60). Vedder-Weiss and colleagues (2019) ha ve further found that teachers work to avoid “face threats” not only to their own public image, but also to colleagues’ images. For example, various studies have shown tha t teachers may focus their collaboration on adminis trative tasks, limit themselves to swapping classroom activities and practices in a superficial way, actively silence critical perspectives, or cancel meetings altogether, all of which can be understood at least partially in terms of managing the threat of unfavorable judgment from peers (Louie, 2016; Vedder-Weiss et al., 2019). Trust, shared values, and a sense of safety for intellectual risk-taking can support teachers to manage the risks of collaboration more productively (Bolam et al., 2005; Vescio, 2008; Lefstein et al., 2020). However, little is known about how groups of teachers develop these characteristics. Some studies suggest that convening groups based on core values that are already shared (such as a commitment to social justice) allows teachers to exist in a “sacred space” of support, honest dialogue, reflect ion, and critique (McCotter, 2001, pp. 690-691). Ye t this obscures the moment-to-moment work that goes into c o-constructing any learning environment, as well as  the ongoing negotiation of meaning that is required to make abstractions (e.g., social justice) concrete and actionable.

In this paper, we add to the small body of research  investigating the dynamics of teachers’ efforts to navigate the risks of professional collaboration, particularly equity-and justice-oriented collaboration, focusing on how teachers construct “us” and “them” in moment-to-moment interaction and the effects on their opportunities to learn.

Theoretical perspective A conceptual starting point for our inquiry is the awareness that all teacher learning is situated in what Horn and Garner (in press) term the “baggage of the binary,”  the culturally pervasive tendency to think of both  specific ICLS2022 Proceedings 337 © ISLS teaching practices and individual teachers as either “good” or “bad.” This good/bad binary imposes an evaluative frame that suggests that a good teacher should be s killed in applying “best practices” to deliver lessons that run smoothly and reliably produce measurable gains in student achievement. In this context, grappling with multiple perspectives and uncertainty exposes teachers to the risk of appearing bad at their jobs (the “face threat” described above; Vedder-Weiss et al., 2019). Moreover, when b eliefs about students are on the table, particularl y beliefs linked to racism and other systems of oppression, t here is the additional risk of appearing to be a ba d person as well as an incompetent professional (Stokes, 2001).

Teaching is much more complicated than a good/bad binary indicates. What counts as “good” practice depends on context. Furthermore, teachers must often juggle competing priorities, such as building on what their students know, cando, and are interested in learni ng and following externally mandated scope, sequenc e, and pacing (two priorities that are often in tension ev en when they are not directly opposed). For teacher s working toward a vision for mathematics classrooms that is not yet widely practiced (e.g., equity-and justice-oriented visions), tensions and contradictions between various goals and obligations can be constant. Research on teacher learning that focuses on the mastery of particular practices or the acquisition of specific types of knowledge along a linear trajectory miss these tensions and contradictions. Here, we emphasize an understudied aspect of teacher learning: the ongoing grappling that teachers engag e in as they attempt to re-solve “fundamentally uns olvable problems” (Philip, 2019, p. 5) in their everyday te aching. This grappling is necessarily social, in th at local communities of practice make available certain ways of understanding young people, mathematics, and learning. When these communities are formed around a shared vision for mathematics teaching, they can provide teachers with collective resources to engage in the ongoing grappling of how to live up to their commitments in  daily classroom teaching.

For teachers who are committed to anti-deficit math ematics teaching, living up to one’s commitments can present an even greater need for ongoing grappling. Deficit ideologies remain widespread and are embedded within systems and structures that overemphasize wh at students cannot or do not do. These ideologies f urther locate some students as intellectually deficient and inferior to others in ways that intersect with ex isting social hierarchies based on race, class, gender, cultural or linguistic background, and (dis)ability, thereby functioning to reproduce these same hierarchies (Adiredja & Louie,  2020; Martin, 2009). Because deficit ideologies ar e culturally dominant (at least in the United States), they influence teachers’ sense-making about their daily practice, no matter how committed teachers might be to working for justice (Louie, 2018). Philip (2019) has described the work involved in disrupting deficit ideologies and practices as “principled improvisation,” highlighting both the unpredictable and ever evolving nature of teaching and the necessity of constantly maintaining a princ ipled orientation toward justice when making moment-to-moment decisions. This conception of teaching acknowledges that “each moment of teaching and learning is conse quential as teachers and students continually and j ointly renegotiate power and possibility in every interaction” (Philip, 2019, p. 5). In this paper, we invest igate teacher learning from the perspective of seeking to learn m ore about how teacher workgroups can be co-construc ted to support the type of grappling that is necessary in equitable, student-centered mathematics teaching, e specially teaching that challenges deficit ideologies (i.e., anti-deficit teaching).

## Methods

The two of us, Nicole and Mallika, are united by our experiences as former teachers who have ourselves grappled with the challenges of realizing strengths-based, anti-deficit goals in our classrooms. We continued to engage in this grappling as graduate students at the University of California, Berkeley (where we first met) and still engage in it today, in our roles as teacher educators and researchers. We thus approach our research with a sense of the challenges many teachers face and an interest in un derstanding and supporting their ongoing learning, which contrasts with approaches that seem to blame teachers for their shortcomings. This relates to our shar ed goal of complexifying research on teacher learning by atten ding to the entanglement of this learning with poli tical and ideological contexts and its messy, non-linear character.

In this paper, our analysis draws from two research projects, which we initially conducted independent of one another. Mallika’s study was a social design-based research project of a teacher learning community that she co-designed with six first-year teachers from t he same K-8 teacher preparation cohort. These teach ers were all Mallika’s students in a math methods course and  were selected because they would be teaching eleme ntary school locally and had voiced a shared commitment w hich they articulated in the first community meetin g as “creating a math community where everyone meaningfully contributes to the mathematical work of the classroom.” This overarching commitment grew out of shared experiences in the math methods course of seeking to di srupt processes of marginalization within the mathematics classroom which function to uphold racialized hier archies of mathematical ability (Martin, 2009). The learning community (named Math Crew by participants) was intended to support teachers to work toward their shared commitments while navigating the tensions and contradictions of ICLS2022 Proceedings 338 © ISLS working within deficit-oriented systems of schoolin g. The support structure consisted of monthly commu nity meetings and periodic classroom visits from Mallika.

Nicole’s project involved groups of teachers at fou r schools, all participating in a professional development program called the Elementary Mathematics Collaborative (EMC; a pseudonym, like all school and teacher names in this paper). Situated in a large A merican city, EMC focused on “high-need” schools in economically and politically marginalized neighborhoods, with the goal of supporting “ALL students [to] engage in mathematical sense-making leading to deep mathem atical understanding” and “to develop a sense of mathematical agency and authority” (according to EMC materials). EMC’s mission was thus connected to equity concerns, but in ways that did not explicitly name or interrogate systems of oppression in mathematics education. “Cycles of collaborative practice” were an important component of EMC activities. Similar to lesson study (Lewis et al., 2006), these cycles involved teachers in collaboratively planning a lesson, observing one teacher enact the lesson, then sharing what they had observed and pla nning next steps together. Each cycle was facilitat ed by an EMC coach. Nicole was involved as a participant-observer (not as a coach or PD leader). To analyze our data, we examined the values around which solidarity was organized. What values did participants claim as their own? Relatedly, what did they characterize as qualities of “good” teaching/teachers or “bad” teaching/teachers? Because we were also concerned with whether teachers’ constructions of “us” set up a dynamic of “us versus them,” we asked: who was incl uded (implicitly or explicitly) in the solidarity b eing constructed? Connected to our interest in teachers’ opportunities to learn in these episodes, we also investigated how teachers framed grappling and struggle (e.g., a s an important and necessary part of learning, or a s an indication of failure) and what ideas they did or did not set themselves up to grapple with together. We selected five episodes for close analysis, aimin g for variety with respect to the answers to the questions outlined above. Two episodes were drawn from EMC’s School 53, involving the EMC coach and three teachers and the other three episodes are from community meetings in Math Crew.

## Findings

We use five episodes across the work groups from ou r two studies to elaborate how constructions of “us” and “them” are not stable characteristics of groups but instead are constructed from moment to moment in ways that shape opportunities for learning. In both Math Crew and School 53 there were moments when teachers and coaches/facilitators co-constructed “us” as a group in which teachers could struggle and grapple together toward their shared commitments, as illustrated with episodes 1 and 2 (Math Crew) and episode 4 (School 53). While in Math Crew these constructions of “us” were explicitly anti-deficit and supported teachers to grapple with deficit ideologies, the construction of “us” in School 53 focused more broadly on student-centered mathematics teaching, illustrating the ways that solidarity in teacher wo rk groups is co-constructed toward particular goals. In both teacher work groups, when constructions of “us” als o included constructions of “them,” this tended to lead to binaries of “right” or “wrong” answers and “good” o r “bad” teachers, which closed down opportunities f or grappling and deeper reasoning about teaching as illustrated in episode 3 (Math Crew) and episode 5 (School 53). Across these five episodes we emphasize the in the moment construction of “us” and “them” as affording  and constraining opportunities for teachers to grapple with teaching toward their commitments. Math Crew Math Crew was formed with an explicit commitment to  working toward anti-deficit mathematics teaching. The community was voluntary, and all six teachers who c hose to participate did so because they wanted to r eceive support in trying to live up to their commitment to  disrupting deficit ideologies in their everyday te aching. We selected three episodes to elaborate the ways const ructions of “us” and “them” afford and constrained teacher learning toward grappling with deficit ideologies. The first two episodes illustrate how constructions  of “us” fostered moments of solidarity in the struggle of m aintaining a strengths-based lens in everyday teach ing and created opportunities for teachers to think more deeply about deficit ideologies. The third episode illustrates how, even with an anti-deficit construction of “us”, moment to moment constructions of “them” can close opportunities for deeper reasoning and grappling about teaching.

Episode 1: Constructing an “us” that supports collective anti-deficit learning To illustrate constructing an “us” with anti-deficit goals, we use an episode from Math Crew during the learning community meeting in November. In this episode, one  teacher, Maritza, shared a story from her 4th grad e classroom. She told the community about how, during a classroom visit, [Author 2] had helped Maritza notice a mathematical strength of one of the students in her  classroom who had been labeled as “struggling” in math by school administrators. Maritza described how she had not initially noticed what the child was doing but that, once Mallika had pointed it out, she was able to highlight this strength both in the student’s small group and in a whole ICLS2022 Proceedings 339 © ISLS group discussion. She then described how this action shifted status dynamics and seemed to positively impact the student’s math participation during subsequent math lessons. Right after Maritza finished sharing this story of a “success” in her commitment to working toward anti-deficit math teaching, Kara jumped in to share, “I have a connection. It was so helpful to have [Author 2] in the classroom just cause I was so scattered when Mallika came.” After explaining more about why she was scattered, she went on to say, “[Author 2] was like ‘it would just be nice for you to be able to take notes on strengths.’ Literally the only thing I had on my notepad at the end of that session was (drawing a box in the air) a rectangle.” At this, the other teachers erupt in loud, exuberant laughter, with lots of head nodding and signals of agreement. Kara further explained, “One kid asked ‘what’s a rectangle?’ and it was a little blue rectangle. I was like ‘this is a rectangle.’ That's all I had taken (using air quotes) ‘note’ of.” At this, Maritza laughed loudly, Marie put her head in her hands laughing, and Selina commented, “Oh my god, that is amazing.” While Maritza’s story had focused on a positive mom ent in her teaching, Kara chose to draw out and connect with the struggle that Maritza mentioned about how hard it was to notice strengths on her own. With her rectangle story, Kara built on this acknowledgement of struggle to share a moment of total failure in being able to live up to her commitment to noting strengths in the day-to-day intensity of classroom teaching. The laughter in the responses from other teachers seemed to indicate a form of solidarity (Takeuchi, 2016) in the shared struggle to enact their commitments, and provides evidence o f the co-construction of an “us” committed to takin g an antideficit stance AND an “us” of human beings who sometimes fail in their attempts to embody that sta nce in their everyday teaching. In the intense reactions to Kara’s story, there was a palpable sense of relief in being able to hold that failure collectively, as a community.

Episode 2: Drawing on a co-constructed anti-deficit “us” to grapple with deficit ideologies The co-construction of an anti-deficit “us” that in cluded struggle helped to create a space in Math Cr ew for beginning teachers to grapple with deficit ideologies and the ways those ideologies are embedded within structures and systems. With this episode, we show the type of grappling that teachers were able to engage in, and how that grappling connected to an anti-deficit “us” that includes struggle and imperfection. Toward the beginning of the final meeting as a learning community, Kara shared a recent tension she had experienced at her school site where she was asked “to fill out these classification cards for next year and we have to rate each of our students like high, medium, or low in math.” Kara went on to describe the tension this caused for her: And I was like, ‘oh no, that’s terrible.’ It felt sad that that’s how the school wants to think about students and that they would think about distributing kids based on that, like ‘oh we don’t want to give this teachers too many “low” (air quotes with fingers) kids’ to ease the burden but it also made me think about how I see my students, and the kids who I feel anxious about in math are the kids who do have trouble persevering or kids who have low self-esteem. It’s not low based on any score, I’m looking more at their relationship to math and the way they see themselves as a math learner and that just makes me even more dri ven to set up my culture next year even more enforcing the expectation and the value that everyone is a math learner and has something to contribute. I also felt kind of like, moral, do I stand up to the system and say ‘I will not fill out these classification cards’ or is it like, what do I look at to fill these out, what are you asking for? In talking through her dilemma, Kara explicitly indexed an anti-deficit “us” by using the same language that had been co-constructed in Math Crew of everyone having something to contribute to a math community. In this story, deficit ideologies were located within systems and structures, which was evident when Kara raised the question “do I stand up to the system?” (emphasis added). By  bringing this “moral” dilemma to Math Crew, she wa s treating the constructed “us” as a resource to help her reflect on and grapple with how she was hoping to see the children in her classroom. Here, Kara interrogated the category of “low” by considering which students she felt “anxious about in math” instead of treating these c ategories as taken-for-granted and static, as the classification cards suggested.

Kara then talked out loud about what she might do, describing in detail her thinking about one of her students who “at the beginning of the year for addition and subtraction used his fingers and would do it under the table but felt super insecure about math.” She went on to describe how this student now “is just like an awesome partner to have in math and is seen as someone who has cool strategies and is seen as someone who participates in full group math thinking and is able to ask ques tions and get help.” She concluded by saying, “I ca n’t see putting him high, medium, low. Where would I? I don ’t know, none of the above. He’s just a cool kid.” Throughout this storytelling, the co-constructed “us” of Math Crew was a place where deficit ideologies could be ICLS2022 Proceedings 340 © ISLS questioned and grappled with, and where the point w as not to find the “right” answer to the dilemma of  the classification card, but instead to reflect and rea son about how to navigate it in a way that was cons istent with their shared commitment to disrupting deficit ideologies. Episode 3: Constructing a “them” that inhibits anti-deficit collective learning During the same conversation in Math Crew, another teacher, Selina, who taught at the same school site as Kara, told another story that also involved constructing an explicitly anti-deficit “us” (Kara and Selina ha d discussed these stories in the car on the way to the meeting). While Selina’s construction of “us” was similar t o Kara’s construction, when Selina spoke about deficit ideologies, she located these ideologies in individuals rather than in systems, thus constructing “them” in contrast to the anti-deficit “us” of Math Crew. The story Selina told involved an inquiry project a bout language and mathematics across different schools in her district. She described a moment dur ing the collaborative planning process when she sug gested randomly selecting a student to share and another teacher questioned that choice: One of the teachers was like ‘well what if you choose somebody that has it completely wrong and they’re totally lost and don’t know what’s going on?’ Aaand that just felt sonegative to me, like, ‘what do you do with this kid that doesn’t ge t it?’ So I said, ‘you know what, everybody has some kind of math thinking so we’re going to tease out whatever it is that they’re bringing and just talk about it. And, you know, at least in my classroom everyone has something to contribute.’ And I think in that moment is really w hen I saw that clear division between my thinking and other people’s thinking, which is craz y to realize that not everybody thinks like we do.

As in Kara’s story, Selina used the shared language  of Math Crew (“everyone has something to contribut e”) to refer to a co-constructed “us.” This “us” provided a resource for her to push on the deficit ideologie s she encountered during the project. However, in contras t to Kara’s story, Selina also constructed “them” w hen she emphasized what she tried to do in “my classroom” (in contrast to other teachers’ classrooms) and when  she described the “clear division between my thinking and other people’s thinking.” When Selina concluded by saying “not everybody thinks like we do,” the “us” that she constructed was in contrast to “them” as other educators who do not hold the same anti-deficit stance.

During her story-telling, Selina focused on “right” and “wrong” approaches to teaching associated with the “us” and “them” that she had constructed. We argue that this sense of “them” and of “right” and “wrong” is not supportive of anti-deficit teacher learning in that the quick offering of solutions interferes wit h the type of grappling that is essential for trying to engage in the ongoing task of living up to one’s commitments in everyday teaching. In this instance, for example, the group might have instead engaged with the problem of prac tice that her colleague had raised of how to respond to children when they have an incorrect answer—a genuine dilemma in anti-deficit instruction. In addition, when “them” is constructed as other educators, the potential exists for an undermining of trust, solidarity, and risk-taking because any teacher could at some point become “them” if they do not say or do the “right” thing. Selina’s and Kara’s stories, which were shared during the same conversation, highlight the dynamic, moment-to-moment nature of constructions of “us” and “them.” While Kara’s construction of “us” made space for deeper thinking about her ow n practice, Selina’s construction of an “us” that i ncluded “them” seemed to inhibit grappling with the everyday challenges of living up to her commitments. School 53 We now turn to two episodes at School 53. Episode 4 reinforces our finding from Math Crew that constructions of “us” can open up opportunities for teachers to l earn—i.e., to collaboratively grapple with the chal lenges of reconciling commitments to equitable, student-centered instruction with competing ways of understanding their own work. Episode 5 shows how even a subtle constru ction of “them” may close down opportunities to lea rn, similar to Episode 3 in Math Crew but with a “them”  that is present in the room. Together, the two Sch ool 53 episodes show that “us” and “them” are not stable; rather, they are constructed from moment to moment and in relation to particular goals and values. Both of th ese episodes are drawn from a single lesson debrief. Ms. Scott had just taught a lesson that did not go according to plan. She is joined by two of her colleagues at School 53 (Ms. Ortiz and Ms. Edmond) and the EMC coach (Ms. Koets).

Episode 4: Constructing an “us” that supports collective learning Coming out of the lesson, Ms. Scott was quite flustered. She was clearly aware that she had not followed the plan of allowing students’ ideas to drive the lesson, explaining that she had seen them making mistakes and “being a ICLS2022 Proceedings 341 © ISLS mother hen, I don’t want them to struggle or get frustrated.” Yet her efforts to guide students “in my direction,” to her solution strategy, had lost many students. Ms. Koets (the coach) immediately positioned herself in solidarity with Ms. Scott, saying, “It’s very hard. This is not how we were taught as children. This is not how we were taught when we went to teacher school, right? So it’s hard sometimes to, like let the class kind of go free.” She further noted that leading students to “the answer” is oftentimes “what [students] want, and what we kind of feel like we have to do,” framing what Ms. Scott did as fulfilli ng an obligation for which teachers are often made to feel responsible. Ms. Ortiz also hinted at ways that their shared project of engaging students with challenging, openended tasks ran counter to customary instruction, noting that open-ended tasks could be “scary”: “I can understand where I think you’re coming from, because I would t hink that. You have less control because there’s so  much freedom.” The group went on to examine the work students had done before Ms. Scott pulled them together. They focused their discussion on student strategies and insights that Ms. Scott could build on when she returned to the task the next day, as well as “good teacher move[s]” (in Ms. Koets’s words) through which Ms. Scott had actually supported students to generate those strategies and insights, despite her perception that she had made  a mess of the lesson.

Members of the group thus participated in construct ing an “us” united around centering students’ mathematical thinking, in explicit contrast to teacher-centered traditions in U.S. schooling. They made repeated use of the pronoun “we,” especially Ms. Koets, and positioned themselves as sharing in Ms. Scott’s struggles with giving students enough “freedom” to exercise mathem atical agency (similar to how teachers in Math Crew positioned themselves as sharing Kara’s and Maritza’s struggles to notice strengths in Episode 1). Notably, Ms. Koets also explicitly disrupted interpretations of herself as an expert, saying, “We, in this, safe environment right now, I’m not evaluating you in any way. I will never evaluate any of you.” She thus rejected the common framing of coaching as evaluative and hierarchical in favor of a collective “we.” This developing solidarity went beyond empathizing with struggles to open opportunities for group members to learn together in service of the ir shared project, specifically, to grapple with the feelings of discomfort and fear that the shift in “control”  from teachers to students brought out. The group framed this as a shared challenge that could and should be raised for discussion and collective sense-making in their meetings.

Episode 5: Constructing a “them” that hinders collective learning One might assume that the solidarity Ms. Scott, Ms.  Ortiz, Ms. Edmond, and Ms. Koets constructed aroun d supporting students to engage with open-ended challenges would extend to “ALL students,” in accordance with the PD program’s stated vision. Approximately 30 minutes into the conversation, however, Ms. Edmond remarked that she had been looking for ways to support “high er-order thinking” and give students opportunities to “take more charge,” musing that she could use an activity  similar to the one Ms. Scott had just taught—but “ maybe I would only do it for my higher-level groups, and not everyone.” Ms. Scott picked up this idea, starting to say, “I really believe, if I took some of my higher learners and, put them in a small group—” before being interrupted by Ms. Koets, who placed her hand in front of her chest, palm toward the group, and said, “I gotta, here’s, here’s my pushback. I want it for everybody.” A flood of overlapping talk ensued. Ms. Ortiz nodded and echoed, “Not only higher learners, yeah.” Ms. Scott repeated, “For everybody,” and shifted awkwardly in her seat. Three times, Ms. Edmond unsuccessfully attempted to break into the conversation to clarify her intent. Ms. Ortiz asserted, “There’s an entry point for everyone,” then qualified this statement, gesturing at the student work on the table and saying, “at least for this one. I’ve watched some other one s that have been very abstract.” On her fourth atte mpt, Ms. Edmond gained the floor and clarified, “So my small groups are more like centers. So each student gets a chance to visit a center.” Ms. Koets responded by briefly elaborating on her concern that “kids at this [low]  level may never get such fun wonderful tasks, and they cando  it,” then quickly moving on to “teacher moves that  have to happen” and cannot be done by students at centers if they work totally independently. Instead of building an “us” around the challenge of disrupting the deficit thinking that “we were taught as children … [and] taught when we went to teacher school” (as Ms. Koets did earlier in the conversati on), the group momentarily fractured. Ms. Koets interrupted Ms. Scott, limiting the group’s access to Ms. Scott ’s sensemaking (and resources it might have contained for collective sense-making) and shifting the conversation to Ms. Koets’s ideas. Furthermore, the coach framed her perspective as “pushback” and emphasized “I,” not “we,” putting Ms. Edmond and Ms. Scott on the defensive, physically on the other side of the small wall of h er hand. Ms. Ortiz aligned herself with Ms. Koets, contributing to the construction of an “us” who believe in making rich, challenging tasks accessible to “everybody” and “everyone,” in contrast to a “them” who thinks such tasks might be appropriate only for “higher-level” students. No tably, they worked to repair this rift in their sol idarity. Ms. Ortiz’s hedge, “at least for this one,” left open the possibility that there might be other tasks where they would all agree on restricting access to “higher learners,” a nd Ms. Koets responded with, “Yeah. Yeah.” And when  Ms. ICLS2022 Proceedings 342 © ISLS Edmond finally got to speak, she aligned herself with the “everyone” camp, as though she had never considered “higher-level groups, and not everyone.” In some ways, the group’s work in this lesson debrief might be considered successful. At a cross-school PD meeting a few days later, Ms. Scott shared her story of learning to give students more space to generate their own ideas, saying that she had returned to the task with her whole class and was “proud of my babies” for their breakthroughs. Collaborative coaching cycles continued at the school with all three teachers’ involvem ent, and Ms. Koets reported that she did not hear any of the m mention “higher learners” again. Yet the opportunities for teachers to engage in anti-deficit learning were quite limited and abstract. They were not able to grapple with the meaning of “high” and “low” as conceptual categorie s, or with the potential disconnect between specifi c experiences and observations that might seem to ind icate that these categories capture important disti nctions between students (in contrast to the Math Crew episode in which Kara opened a space to “think about how I see my students”). Indeed, the fact that these labels did not come up again in their conversations might indicate less that teachers learned something about the harm thos e labels could do than that the us/them dynamics in  this episode—with the intense risk they presented of being cast as “them”—discouraged participants from using this group as a space for such grappling.

## Discussion and implications

The episodes we have shared show that a sense of so lidarity as “us” can be an important resource for t eacher learning in collaborative work groups. In both Math  Crew and School 53 conversations, participants inv oked shared values and goals in ways that supported them to then grapple with the difficulty of enacting those values to achieve their goals in the context of systemic w ays of thinking that emphasized competing values and goals and which continued to influence them, making it hard for them to see students’ strengths (in Math Crew, Episode

1) and to give students more freedom to explore their own ideas and strategies (in the first episode at School 53).

We have also shown, however, that constructing an “us” that is defined by opposition to a concrete “them” can backfire. This opposition may momentarily unify (some) members of the group, but us/them dynamics can close opportunities for sense-making by emphasizing “right” and “wrong” answers, and create the potent ial for members of the group to slide from “us” to “them” at any time. This interferes with learning because it is difficult to reveal one’s struggles when faced with the risk of being judged a bad teacher or worse, a bad person. Further research and practice could productively explore how teacher groups can be supported to build solidarity without constructing someone else as “bad.” Our data sugges t several strategies. First, groups might be guided  to focus on the ways that deficit ideologies are embedded in institutions, structures, cultural traditions, and social practices rather than focusing on “bad” individuals. This appeared in our data when Kara referenced school as a system that operated by classifying students as “high, medium, or low,” and when Ms. Koets referenced “how we were taught as children” as an obstacle to student-centered instruction. A second strategy teacher groups might use to build an “us” without a “them” is to deliberately blur the line between “good” and “bad” teachers, allowing “g ood” teachers to make “bad” mistakes without becomi ng “bad” people. This was especially evident in the episode in which Maritza and Kara shared their failures to notice their students’ strengths. Instead of responding wi th judgment or censure, their colleagues laughed in  genuine solidarity. Together with the first strategy we described (focusing on deficit ideologies as embedded in institutions, structures, cultural traditions, and social practices instead of in “bad” individuals), blurring the g ood/bad binary recognizes that all of us are socialized into oppre ssive systems, so it is not a personal fault when t hose systems are reflected in our thoughts and actions—although we are responsible for repairing harm and for engag ing in ongoing learning to do better.

A third strategy that may be vital for building solidarity as “us” without constructing an opposing “them” is for teachers and teacher educators to deliberately invite one another to grapple with the messiness of realizing anti-deficit goals in everyday practice. This goes beyond the first two strategies, which may be employed in ways that teachers experience as supportive (because they may foster a sense of belonging, camaraderie, and empathy, for example) but which may fall short of engenderin g anti-deficit learning. This strategy also clarifi es that antideficit learning is not linear or something that can ever be accomplished once and for all. Such learning is not a matter of excising certain terms (like “high” and “low”) from our vocabulary but of continuing to wrestle with the ways that oppressive ideologies persistently shape how we think and act. Fulfilling the responsibility for ongoing anti-deficit learning in a world that remains strongly shaped by deficit ideologies is nontrivial. We have shown how  participants in our studies created opportunities to grapple with the challenges of realizing their ideals, without having to fear that their struggles made them “bad teachers.” We have also shown how constructions of “them” shra nk rather than opened space for such grappling. If participants had built up an “us” without creating a “them” in those instances, using the strategies we have outlined here and which we saw them use on other occasions, they might have been able to engage in more meaning ful ICLS2022 Proceedings 343 © ISLS learning in those episodes and perhaps in subsequen t interactions as well. More research is needed to further explore this possibility, but in the meantime, we hope that these strategies will be useful to other teacher groups, as they work to harness the power of collaboration to transform deficit ideologies and contribute to dismantling systems of domination, through daily teaching and (teacher) learning.
