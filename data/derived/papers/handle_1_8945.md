# “Learning Experiences, Technology, and No Grades” – What Students Expect From a Non-Formal Learning Setting Outside of School

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The present study investigates the expectations of 88 students towards their visit of an out-of-school lab (OSL) for social sciences and humanities and how these expectations relate to both their actual experiences of the visit and their situational interest in the project contents. OSLs are non-formal learning settings which are usually visited by whole classes and aim at authentically communicating scientific ways of thinking and working. In contrast to this goal, our findings suggest that the least students expect from an OSL visit is to get authentic insights into scientific research. They rather expect to have a nice time with their classmates and without pressure to perform. Our findings also point to the need to change these expectations, as especially the expectation to get authentic insights into scientific research positively correlated with students’ reported experience of having acquired knowledge during their visit and their situational interest in the project contents.

## Introduction

“Well, we’ll see whether I’ll do better or worse on  the next exam [pause/interruption] but the teacher  couldn’t care less about what we’re doing here anyway.1 These thoughts were stated by a secondary school student at the beginning of a collaborative problem-solving activi ty that took place in an out-of-school lab (OSL) fo r social sciences and humanities during one of our previous studies (Nachtigall et al., 2020). The student visited the lab together with his whole educational science course and attended a one-day project which focused on principles of experimental design in educational science research. Similar to other non-formal learning settings out side of school (such as museums, science centers, science o utreach programs, etc.), OSLs pursue the goal of fo stering students’ interest in and knowledge about scientifi c ways of thinking and working by providing them au thentic insights into scientific endeavors (e.g., Scharfenb erg & Bogner, 2014). These goals are partly in line  with the expectations that students hold when visiting OSLs for natural sciences. As the findings by Garner and Eilks

(2015) and Schwarzer and Parchmann (2015) demonstra te, students who visit those labs for natural scien ces

especially hope to conduct experiments (which is a typical scientific practice of natural scientists) and to have access to authentic scientific tools (Schwarzer & Parchmann, 2015) or at least to better laboratory equipment than in school (Garner & Eilks, 2015). In OSLs for social sciences and humanities, students usually cannot conduct hands-on experiments by applying, for instance, hig h-tech laboratory equipment. Instead, these labs im plement problem-or inquiry-based learning activities in order to emulate core features of scientific inquiry and to provide students with authentic experiences of scientific ways of thinking and working (Pauly, 2012). The effectiveness of OSLs for students’ knowledge about and interest in scientific ways of thinking and working could be affected by students’ expectations towards visiting an OSL. Findings from research on museum learning indeed demonstrate that the visitors’ expectations (especially the expectation to learn something) affect the impacts of a museum visit on student learning (e.g., Falk, 2006). According to the contextual model of learning developed by Falk and Dierking (2004), the visitors’ motivations and expectations represent one factor that affects the quality of a museum experience, and as Falk and  Dierking (2004) state “When expectations are fulfi lled, learning is facilitated. When expectations are unmet learning suffers.” (p. 141). In contrast to research on museum learning, researc h on OSLs has not yet focused on investigating students’ expectations and the role that these expectations may play for the effectiveness of OSLs. But knowledge about students’ expectations could be helpful in or der to enhance the intended effectiveness of OSLs. One outcome variable that has been particularly focused on by previous research on the effectiveness of OSLs relates to students’ situational interest. Situational inte rest is defined as “the psychological state of enga ging or the predisposition to reengage with particular […] content” (Hidi & Renninger, 2006, p. 112), and is assumed to be first triggered and then maintained by (usually but not exclusively) external features, such as learning materials and activities, of the learning setting (Hidi & Ren ninger, 2006). However, so far, empirical evidence barely supports the intended effects of OSLs on students’ development of (situational) interest or their acqu isition of knowledge. For instance, large-scale studies conduc ted by Itzek-Greulich and colleagues (e.g., 2017) ICLS2022 Proceedings 139 © ISLS demonstrated no (partly even negative) effects of OSLs on both students’ situational interest and achievement in chemistry. Thus, uncovering the expectations of stu dents visiting an OSL may be helpful or even necess ary in order to get a better understanding of the potentia ls and boundaries of OSLs for fostering students’ k nowledge about and interest in scientific ways of thinking and working. Investigating the expectations of students who visit an OSL for social sciences and humanities may be particularly interesting and necessary as students tend to associate the work of scientists within the social sciences and humanities – in contrast to the work of natural scientists – with activities, such as teaching and helping others, but not with scientific research (e.g., Nachtigall & Rummel, 2020). Thus, one could assume that studen ts’ expectations with regard to their visit of an OSL for social sciences and humanities are not in line with the goal of OSLs, namely to provide students with authentic insights into scientific ways of thinking and working. This assumption is further supported by the student’s statement above suggesting that students may hope to improve their achievement in school by visiting an OSL for social sciences and humanities or, at the same time, to just have a day off from school on which they do not have to meet any expectations from their teachers. Against this background, the present study first explores the expectations of students who visit an OSL for social sciences and humanities (Research Question 1). For this purpose, we developed and tested a respective questionnaire. We then examine how students’ expect ations at the beginning of their visit relate to th eir actual experiences of the OSL (Research Question 2). There by, we aim to investigate whether or not students’ initial expectations have been met during their OSL visit. Finally, we examine the relation of students’ initi al expectations to their (triggered and maintained) si tuational interest in the contents of the project (Research Question 3), whereby we investigate whether student s’ initial expectations are associated with an outc ome variable that has been focused on in previous OSL research, and that is assumed to be linked to student learning.

## Method

We developed a one-day project for an OSL for social sciences and humanities at a large German university. The project, entitled “Media and its impacts – critical interpretation of study results and reflection on one’s own media use”, primarily aimed at communicating social science research methods to students and consisted of two parts. While the first part of the project focused on the differences between correlative and causal evidence exemplified by studies investigating different impacts of media usage, the second part of the project tried to sensitize students for some actual risks while using media, or more sp ecifically, while sharing personal data in social m edia. It should be noted that we designed our OSL project primarily as a project for educational science courses. However, after our first data collection with an educational  science course and after the first COVID-19 regula tions, we opened our OSL project for all interested courses in order to increase our sample size.

## Measures

The pre-questionnaire assessed students’ expectations related to the OSL visit. The post-questionnaire measured stud ents’ experiences of their OSL visit and their situ ational interest in the contents of the OSL project.

Students responded to all items on a Likert scale r anging from 1 (not true at all) to 5 (absolutely true). As our OSL project was initially developed for educ ational science courses only, some of the items ref erred to the school subject/the discipline of educational sciences. However, as we opened our OSL project for all interested courses after the first COVID-19 regulations and our first data collection, we excluded four items that referred to expectations with regard to students’ educational science classes in school (e.g., “to understand the relevance of topics from my educational science classes for my everyday life”). We included the remaining 20 items in a principal component analysis with varimax rotation in order to examine whether the eight theoretically assumed fac tors for assessing students’ expectations could be found in our data. Using an eigenvalue of 1 or greater led to the extraction of five factors that explained 66% of variance. The first factor unified eight items from four different (initially assumed) dimensions (i.e., interest development: 3 items; knowledge acquisition: 1 item; career orientation: 2 items; insights into research methods: 2 items) that all somehow referred to the educational science discipline. Hence, we named this factor as KNOWLEDGE ABOUT & INTEREST IN EDUCATIONAL SCIENCES  (Cronbachs’s α =.88). The second factor combined all three items of the dimension scientific authenticity and one item of the dimension insights into research methods, which did not refer to educational science research methods in pa rticular but to research methods in general. We nam ed this factor as AUTHENTIC INSIGHTS INTO SCIENTIFIC RESEARCH (Cronbachs’s α =.78). The third factor unified all three items of the autonomy scale, one item from the “reference to everyday life” dimension (see example item above), and the following item of the social experience dimension: “to have a nice time”. We named this fa ctor as NICE AND FREE TIME (Cronbachs’s α =.74). The two remaining items of the social experience scale, which both referred to the expectation to spend (a nice) time with one’s classmates, loaded on the fourth factor, which is why we stuck with the original name SOCIAL EXPERIENCE (Cronbachs’s α =.77). One last item of the CAREER ORIENTATION scale (see example item above) loaded on the fifth factor. We will use these five dimensions for our p resent analysis of students’ OLS expectations.

We again excluded the four items that referred to educational science classes in school, and conducted an exploratory factor analysis. The results revealed a factor solution that differed from the solution for the da taset of the pre-questionnaire. Moreover, the extra cted factors could not be comprehensibly interpreted. As we, moreover, aimed to compare students’ initial expectations with their actual experiences, we decided to form the sa me five scales as for the dataset of the pre-questi onnaire, namely: KNOWLEDGE ABOUT & INTEREST IN EDUCATIONAL SCIENCES  (8 items: Cronbachs’s α =.89), AUTHENTIC INSIGHTS INTO SCIENTIFIC RESEARCH  (4 items: Cronbachs’s α =.67), NICE AND FREE TIME (5 items: Cronbachs’s α =.84), SOCIAL EXPERIENCE (2 items: Cronbachs’s α =.81), and CAREER ORIENTATION (1 item). For assessing students’ situational interest, we used the scale from Lewalter and Willems (2009). Building on the assumption that situational interest is first triggered and then maintained (e.g., Hidi & Renninger, 2006), the scale consists of six items (e.g., “To what extent was the engagement with the contents of this project exciting for you?”) that assess triggered situational interest (catch dimension) and of further six items (e.g., “To what extend would you like to learn more about certain topics of the project?”) that measure maintained situational interest (hold dimension). Students replied to all items on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The internal consistencies of the catch (Cronbachs’s α =.95) and hold items (Cronbachs’s α =.86) are satisfying.

## Research question 1: Expectations

With respect to our first research question (i.e., what do students expect from their visit of an OSL for social sciences and humanities?), the analysis of the stud ent answers to the two open-ended questions reveals  that ICLS2022 Proceedings 143 © ISLS students especially expect to get INFORMATION ABOUT THE TOPIC of the project (here: media) and/or to gain NEW (LEARNING) EXPERIENCES more generally. This may be exemplified by the following two student answers to the first open-ended question: “I expect that we will l earn something new, e.g., how to better deal with m edia.” or “Getting to know new things, maybe about the negative impacts of media.”. Students, moreover, think that their work in the OSL differs from their regular instruction in school in particular with regard to their access to materials and tools that are featured by MORE TECHNOLOGY. Students further think that learning in an OSL, i n contrast to learning in school, is featured by NO PRESSURE AND GRADES and/or MORE COLLABORATION AND AUTONOMY. One student, for instance, gave the following answer to the second open-ended question: “more relaxed atmosphere, no pressure to perform, better technology”. Other s tudents stated: “The work with technical devices and more work in groups could be a difference to school.” or  “more digital, more independent work”. ## Research question 2: Experiences

The descriptive statistics show that students had h igh SOCIAL EXPERIENCES  (M = 3.99; SD = 1.05) during their OSL visit. They, moreover, experienced their visit as a NICE AND FREE TIME (M = 3.84; SD = 0.89). Compared to these rather social and emotional experiences, students experienced their OSL visit as less beneficial for both their KNOWLEDGE ABOUT AND INTEREST IN EDUCATIONAL SCIENCE S (M = 3.13; SD = 0.86) as well as their CAREER ORIENTATION (M = 2.74; SD = 1.18). Their experiences with regard to gaining AUTHENTIC INSIGHTS INTO SCIENTIFIC RESEARCH (M = 3.46; SD = 0.88) ranges in the upper middle of the previous four types of experiences. To investigate our second research question (i.e., how are students’ expectations regarding their OSL visit related to their experiences of their OSL visit?), we first conducted a Wilcoxon signed-rank test (as most of the data deviated from normality) in order to examine the differences between students’ initial expectations at the beginning of their OSL visit and their actual exper iences at the end of their visit. We then calculate d the correlations between the two sets of variables. For  CAREER ORIENTATION (z = -3.63, p <.001) and KNOWLEDGE ABOUT AND INTEREST IN EDUCATIONAL SCIENCES (z = -3.96, p <.001), the results of the Wilcoxon test demonstrate that students’ expectations were significantly high er at the beginning of their OSL visit than their r espective experiences at the end of their visit. For SOCIAL EXPERIENCES (z = -3.41, p =.001) and AUTHENTIC INSIGHTS INTO SCIENTIFIC RESEARCH (z = -2.09, p =.037), the results show that students expectations were significantly lower at the beginning of the OSL project than their experie nces of the project at the end of their visit. We f ound no significant difference with regard to the variable NICE AND FREE TIME  (z = -1.23, p =.22). For the correlational analyses, we calculated spearman rho and used an alpha level of.05. ## Research question 3: Situational interest

To investigate our third research question (i.e., are students’ expectations towards their OSL visit associated with their situational interest in the contents of the O SL project?), we again conducted correlational anal yses and calculated spearman rho. The results reveal that both students’ triggered and maintained situational interest at the end of the OSL visit positively and significantly c orrelate with their initial expectations to (1) inc rease one’s knowledge about and interest in educational sciences (catch: rs =.38, p <.001; hold: rs =.31, p =.003), (2) get authentic insights into scientific research (catch: rs =.29, p =.007; hold: rs =.22, p =.04), and (3) get impressions helpful for one’s career orientation (catch: rs =.27, p =.01; hold: rs =.22, p =.04). The expectations to have a nice and free time as well as to make social experiences during the OSL visit doneither correlate with students’ triggered situational interest (nice and free time: rs =.20, p =.06; social experiences: rs =.12, p =.27) nor with their maintained situational interest (nice and free time: rs =.14, p =.19; social experiences: rs =.07, p =.52).

## Discussion

The findings of the present study on students’ expectations towards their visit of an OSL (out-of-school lab) for social sciences and humanities (Research question 1) demonstrate that secondary school students have r ather unspecific and broad expectations (e.g., ”making new learning experiences” or “more technology”) and especially hope to have a nice and relaxed time without pressure to perform and in which they can work independently and collaborate with their classmates. Students expect the least to get authentic insights into scientific research, which is, however, generally a goal of OSLs and was indeed the primary goal of the OSL project in which the present study took place. A possible explanation for this result may relate to the fact that the social sciences and humanities as well as scientists within these disciplines are barely associated with science and research (Nachtigall & Rummel, 2020). Thus, our findings point to the need to fost er students’ beliefs and conceptions about social sciences and humanities, and (maybe even thereby) their expectat ions towards visiting an OSL for social sciences and humanities. However, our findings also suggest that  OSLs may have the potential to change these belief s, as students experienced the OSL project at the end of their visit (Research Question 2) as one that provi ded them with insights into scientific research (i.e., this experience was significantly higher as the respecti ve initial expectation). Consequently, to enhance the effectiveness of OSLs, one could assume that students have to visit an OSL multiple times: first to adjust their expectations, and then to increase their knowledge about and interest in scientific research. To conclude, although more research is required to further inves tigate students’ expectations towards visiting non-formal learning settings, such as OSLs, and the role that these expectations play for different learning outc omes, the present study indicates that students’ expectations relate to both their actual experiences of the OSL visit and their situational interest in the contents of the OSL pro ject. With a better and deeper understanding of stu dents’ expectations towards visiting an OSL (for social sciences and humanities), future research could investigate ways for adjusting students’ expectations and, in turn, for improving their actual experiences and learning outcomes.
