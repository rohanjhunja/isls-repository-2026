# Bodies, Participation, and Power: Learning as a Site of Inclusion, Exclusion, and Resistance

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract

Youth bring diverse ways of knowing, communicating, and creating meaning to school, yet classrooms often privilege a narrow slice of these practices—treating difference as deficit and leaving many disabled, neurodivergent, queer, or trauma-impacted youth at the margins of participation. These exclusions affect belonging and access to civic, community, and disciplinary life. My research investigates how to design asset-oriented, participatory, and body-inclusive learning environments for young adolescents, where questions of identity and belonging are especially acute. Drawing on sociocultural and critical learning theories, participatory design-based research, and social models of ability and the body, I examine how multimodal repertoires can be recognized and leveraged as learning resources. This agenda advances three strands focused on designing multimodal learning environments that prioritize learner and educator dignity: (1) expanding conceptions of adolescent participation and literacy, (2) designing inclusive multimodal technologies, and (3) positioning youth as co-designers and co-theorists of their learning. Strand 1: Expanding conceptions of youth participation This strand is grounded in my experiences and those of current adolescents regarding secondary schools’ narrow conceptions of participation. Secondary schooling has privileged print and verbal literacies while devaluing embodied, affective, and multimodal avenues for meaning-making. These framings disproportionately exclude multiply-marginalized adolescents and reinforce deficit orientations and hegemonic norms. In my previous work, I employ a social model of disability to explore how schools ‘disable’ and ‘enable’ certain bodies, calling for researchers to shift responsibility for student performance away from the individual and onto the design of the system in which they operate (Vickery, 2023). In my dissertation, I introduce a five-dimensional inter-theoretical framework that conceptualizes the body as simultaneously thinking, feeling, observed, politicized, and autonomous. I also provide methodological guidance for conducting humanizing, dignity-affirming research with youth living in non-normative bodies (Vickery, 2025a) and employ critical resistance narrative analysis to examine how adolescents navigate and resist normative embodied expectations in schools (Vickery, 2025b). Moving forward, I expand normative notions of participation in youth civic education. I extend this work into civic and technical domains—motivated by the current sociopolitical climate. Such work demands multidisciplinary and multimodal competencies that cannot be siloed within individual subject areas. To these ends, I hope to support adolescents’ learning of media, data, digital, and scientific literacies through engagement with culturally relevant issues. Building from epistemic practices in science and engineering education (e.g., evidence evaluation, systems reasoning, modeling), I see opportunities to extend these into literacy/language education (rhetorical device usage, media & data literacy, interpretation of misinformation), social studies/history (complex sociopolitical systems, causal mechanisms), and computing education (engineering ethics, data privacy, transparency, ethical AI adoption). As political systems increasingly disenfranchise multiply-marginalized youth, these literacies become not abstract skills but conceptual and tangible tools for civic and social change. A society of care must acknowledge and design against these barriers. Strand 2: Designing inclusive multimodal technologies This strand is rooted in my experience as a UX designer and researcher, where access and inclusion were often addressed retroactively rather than prioritized from the start. In educational technologies, this reinforces deficit-oriented assumptions about learners whose practices do not align with normative expectations. This work centers on learners’ histories, needs, and desires in the edtech design process, ensuring technologies are proactive, responsive, and adaptive to learners’ needs. In my recent work (Vickery & Danish, 2025), I share my rapid design and iterative refinement of a mixed-reality embodied learning environment for a reverse-inclusion summer camp for youth with moderate-to-severe disabilities, aimed at identifying and addressing barriers to expression and participation. In my 2024 work o n the GEM-STEP project (Vickery et al., 2024; NSF Grants #1908632 & #1908791), I led my team in integrating trauma-informed pedagogies into the facilitation and evaluation of mixed-reality embodied activities (where students use motion-tracking technologies to collaboratively model complex scientific phenomena), demonstrating how centering comfort, choice, and agency can amplify students’ capacities for participation. In Zhou et al. (2025), we investigate how elementary learners' group-divergent embodied practices afford opportunities to learn about complex scientific phenomena. As a Visiting Research Scientist funded by the Engage AI Institute, I design and develop narrative-based, AI-scaffolded, gamified experiences for middle school students that are context-relevant and engage learners in critical thinking about food and environmental justice (Humburg et al., 2025). I build on my dual background in software engineering and UX design to critically engage with how ‘cutting-edge’ technologies such as AI, XR, and asynchronous learning tools are implemented at scale. My prior work has often relied on retrofitting inclusion into technologies – moving forward, I hope to (1) establish trusting partnerships with schools and communities, (2) conduct on-the-ground needs assessments, (3) co-design contextually grounded solutions with these needs prioritized at the onset, and (4) iteratively evaluate implementation. Access and inclusion are not endpoints but baselines; innovation must be defined by—and accountable to—those most impacted. Strand 3: Positioning youth as co-designers and co-theorists of their learning This strand addresses a tension across education and research alike – that too often, youth are positioned as passive users of and participants in designs they had no role in shaping. Thus, these designs (which here include pedagogies, activities, tools, communities, and theories) fail to provide the necessary scaffolding for youth to thrive and learn within them. My work takes an asset-oriented stance, treating adolescents’ perspectives and repertoires of practice as valuable resources for designing more sensitive, equitable, and emancipatory experiences for youth who would otherwise be forced to (at best) change themselves for the sake of compliance. In prior work, I’ve offered autoethnographic accounts of how my traditional notions of research-practice partnerships were challenged and remade in a collaboration with local paraprofessionals, special educators, and youth with disabilities (Vickery & Peabody, Under Review). In my dissertation Article 2 (Vickery, 2025c), I drew on Vygotskian notions of mediation and affect to understand what affective, sensory, and lived experiences are activated for youth with non-normative embodied experiences in the presence of various instructional design elements (i.e. various activity/task framings, technologies, proximity of embodiment, sensory demands, forms of movement, & participation structures). Further, Article 3 positions youth as co-theorists of a framework for dignity-affirming, body-inclusive embodied learning environments (Vickery, 2025d). This strand responds to a recurring theme across my projects—that “grown-ups just don’t get it.” This perceived ignorance strains opportunities for authentic educator–youth relationships and deepens youth distrust of educational systems. To address this, I aim to develop organized, protected “designer spaces”—digital and physical—for youth to co-design community-facing projects and professional development for educators and youth-service providers.
