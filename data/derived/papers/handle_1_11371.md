# An Exploration of Family Engagement in STEM Identity Work

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While learning sciences research has identified various processes involved in science, technology, engineering, and math (STEM) identity work, important gaps in the field remain. This conceptual paper reviews previous work on STEM identity and family engagement in STEM, arguing that the role of families within STEM identity work has not been deeply explored. Families may be uniquely positioned to provide support for STEM identity through facilitating connections across STEM experiences and navigating multiple identities. Future work should explore STEM identity work within family interactions using qualitative methods and within informal STEM learning programs. Through recognizing the funds of knowledge and identity that families contribute to STEM identity work, research candemonstrate more expansive forms of STEM identity.

## Introduction

Science, technology, engineering, and math (STEM) identity has become a popular focus within learning sciences and STEM education research (Danielsson et al., 202 3; Simpson & Bouhafa, 2020). While a great deal has been learned about processes involved STEM identity work  (e.g., Carlone & Johnson, 2007) and the relevant m acro and micro dynamics (e.g., Wade-Jaimes et al., 2021), STEM identity remains a narrow construct, tied to issues of power, and not inclusive of the many ways of being and becoming in STEM (e.g., Kayumova & Dou, 2022). One way to consider “the ethical and political dimensio ns of identity work” (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2023,  p. 362) as an important next-step in the field is to acknowledge the role of families, including familial funds of knowledge and identity (e.g., Esteban-Guitart and Moll, 2014; Moll et al., 1992). Despite being excluded from more fully participatin g and sharing knowledge in STEM (e.g., Ishimaru et a l., 2015), families from marginalized groups have p rovided various types of support for youth that hav e contributed to youth self-and future-beliefs in STEM (Starr et al., 2022). There are a variety of roles that parents have enacted to support youth engagement in STEM (e.g., Barron et al., 2009; Tolbert Smith et al., 2022; Varma, 2019), including in ways that have connected to and  leveraged cultural knowledge and values (e.g., Soto-Lara & Simpkins, 2022; Starr et al., 2022). However, while conceptual work has argued for the importance of connecting work on disciplinary identities (e.g., STEM identit y), learning tied to culture, and family engagement  (Ishimaru et al., 2 015, p. 9), only limited work has begun to explore how families are relevant to STEM identity work empirically (e.g., Beeton et al., 2012; Carlone & Johnson, 2007). This paper will review previous empirical and conce ptual work on STEM identity, describe family engagement practices in STEM, and highlight potential ways in which families are uniquely positioned to support STEM identity work. The goal of integrating these a reas of research is to open possibilities for a mor e inclusive and expansive sense of STEM identity and contribute to a more critical and justice-oriented field of STEM identity research more broadly (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2023; Carlone, 2023). Although this is not a systematic review, most of the articles focused on youth from marginal ized groups in STEM (primarily youth of color and girls/women), included families or youth in middle and/or high school, used qualitative methods, and d escribed STEM experiences generally and/or in informal STEM learning programs specifically.

## Theoretical framework

The connection between families and identity is rooted in the theoretical frameworks of funds of knowledge (Moll et al., 1992) and funds of identity (Saubich & Esteban, 2011). The goals of these approaches are to identify assets present within families fro m marginalized groups and promote the use of this k nowledge in schools (Esteban-Guitart and Moll, 2014). Funds of knowledge (FoK) c an be defined as “those historically-accumulated and culturally-developed bodies of knowledge and skills essential for household or individual functioning and well-being” (Moll et al., 1992, p. 133). Funds of identi ty (FoI), the subsequent iteration of funds of know ledge work, are the “historically accumulated, culturally devel oped, and socially distributed resources that are e ssential for people’s self-definition, self-expression, and self-understanding” (Esteban-Guitart and Moll, 2014, p. 37). FoK and FoI developed in everyday and family life can positively influence youth learning and identity work in a new domain (Moll et al., 1992; Saubich & Esteban, 2011), such as STEM. The current paper will leverage perspectives from FoK and FoI by considering how the knowledge and values families possess contribute to youth identities. STEM identity Engaging in STEM identity work is a dynamic process, not a static trait or final destination (Calabres e Barton et al., 2013), and a variety of factors have been found to be involved in this process. Internal mechanisms related to STEM identity work have included having knowledge about STEM (Brickhouse et al., 2000; Carlone & Johnson, 2007; Wade-Jaimes et al., 2021) and being interested in STEM (Brickhouse et al., 2000; Simpson & Bouhafa, 2020; Wade-Jaimes et al., 2021). STEM identity work has also i ncluded social and relational components, primarily receiving recognition from others as a STEM person (Calabrese Barton et al., 2013; Carlone & Johnson, 2007; Simpson & Bouhafa, 2020; Wade-Jaimes et al., 2021) and becoming a member of the s cience community (Beeton et al., 2012; Rahm & Moore, 2016). STEM identity work has occurred within individual contexts like school (Aschbacher et al., 2010; Beeton et al., 201 2; Brickhouse et al., 2000; Rahm & Moore, 2016; Sim pson & Bouhafa, 2020) and informal STEM learning prog rams (Aschbacher et al., 2010), as well as across m ultiple contexts (Aschbacher et al., 2010; Calabrese Barton  et al., 2013). Finally, STEM identity work is conn ected to existing identities, including general interests and experiences (Calabrese Barton et al., 2013), academic identities (Beeton et al., 2012), and social identities like racial, ethnic, and gender identities (Aschbacher et al., 2010; Beeton et al., 2012; Carlone & Johnson, 2007; Simpson & Bouhafa, 2020). While substantial progress has been made with researching STEM identity, inequities remain for students from marginalized groups. Dominant, Western norms i n science value objectivity, separation from cultur e, and white male identities (Avraamidou & Schwart z, 2021; Bang et al., 2012; Kayumova & Dou, 2022). Youth who fit the dominant practices and identities are seen as STEM people, while youth from marginalized group s, who have different values and identities, are excluded from STEM identity work (Avraamidou  & Schwartz, 2021; Carlone, 2023; Carlone & Johnson, 2007; Kayumova & Dou, 2022). Many have suggested that the various identities and cultural practices that youth from m arginalized groups have should be seen as resources  (not barriers) for STEM learning and identification (Bang et al., 2012; Kayumova & Dou, 2022). Since historical and present inequities can be re-created or disrupted in local contexts, there is po tential to transform STEM identity work within everyday spaces and interactions (Calab rese Barton et al., 2013; Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2 023; Carlone, 2023).

Some conceptual work has begun to argue for a form of STEM identity that is more expansive, inclusive of multiple identities, and representative of many enactments of a STEM person (Avraamidou & Schwartz, 2021; Kayumova & Dou, 2022). However, only limited work h as begun to demonstrate these possibilities empiric ally. For example, a group of women of color in Carlone and Johnson (2007)’s study identified themselves as altruistic scientists, which was characterized by the ways the y redefined science identity by valuing helping others and accepting recognition from people outside the science community, such as family members. While these examples highlight the agency youth have leveraged in constr ucting and navigating STEM identity work, they have  not specifically explored the ways in which families may be uniquely positioned to redefine STEM identity, especially through their funds of knowledge and funds of identity. Family engagement in STEM Families have contributed to youth STEM pathways in  a variety of ways (i.e., family engagement in STEM; Barron et al., 2009; Bell et al., 2009; Martin et a l., 2017; Starr et al., 2022; Tolbert Smith et al.,  2022; Varma, 2019). Early models of family engagement in STEM included types of involvement common with white families like helping with homework (Varma, 2019) or visitin g museums (Soto-Lara & Simpkins, 2022). Families from marginalized groups have encountered barriers such as accessing knowledge about STEM and careers (Archer et al., 2015; Aschbacher et al., 2010; Soto-Lara & Simpkins, 2022), engaging in school (Archer et al., 2015; SotoLara & Simpkins, 2022; Williams et al., 2020), and participating in informal STEM learning contexts (AllexsahtSnider et al., 2020; Archer et al., 2015; Martin et al., 2 017). More recent work has demonstrated that famili es from marginalized groups have supported youth in no vel ways (even if other forms of support have been inaccessible; Soto-Lara & Simpkins, 2022; Starr et al., 2022), provided encouragement verbally and emotionally (Archer et al., 2015; Allexsaht-Snider et al., 2020; Barron et al., 2009; Beeton et  al., 2012; Martin et al., 2017; Soto-Lara & Simpkins, 2022; Tolbert Smith et al., 2022),  held knowledge that a pplies to STEM (Barron et al., 2009; Tolbert Smith et al., 2022), aspired to be in volved in youth STEM pathways (Martin et al., 2017), and increased STEM engagement over time (Varma, 2019). Recognizing the many ways that families and parents have supported  youth in STEM is important to move beyond singular notions of STEM engagement (e.g., homework help), counter deficit-based thinking towards marginalized groups, and “ex pand our thinking about parent involvement so that parents and students are  continually exploring ways to connect academic experiences to meaningful, real-world contexts and students’ lived experiences” (Varma, 2019, pp. 4–5). Family engagement in STEM has supported important y outh outcomes like interest (Simmons et al.,

2022) and future aspirations in STEM (e.g., college  major, career; Aschbacher et al., 2010; Archer et al., 2015;

Starr et al., 2022). Although some work has simulta neously examined family engagement and STEM identit y (Aschbacher et al., 2010; Archer et al., 2015; Beet on et al., 2012; Brickhouse et al., 2000; Simmons e t al., 2022; Starr et al., 2022), family engagemen t has been explored as one factor among many that c ontribute to STEM identity work (Aschbacher et al., 2010; Beeton et a l., 2012; Brickhouse et al., 2000). Since family re lationships are especially important during adolescence, there is a need for integrat ed, multivariate research exploring the role of family engagement in STEM identity.

Potential connections between families and STEM identity Two specific ways that families may contribute STEM identity work are (1) bridging contexts and (2) supporting multiple identities. First, families are involved in numerous contexts where youth engage in STEM. Parents have engaged with youth academically, including helping with homework, finding tutoring, and volunteering at school (Archer et al., 2015; Martin et al., 2017; Soto-Lara & Simpkins, 2022; Starr et al., 2022; Williams  et al., 2020). Parents have also encouraged youth to participate i n STEM at home (Tolbert Smith et al., 2022; Williams et al.,

2020) and in informal STEM contexts (e.g., finding opportunities, providing transportation; Aschbacher  et al.,

2010; Barron et al., 2009; Martin et al., 2017; Tol bert Smith et al., 2022; Williams et al., 202 0). Not only have families encouraged youth to engage in STEM individually, family members have engaged in STEM with you th as well. STEM has been present in everyday family activities in a variety of ways (Barron et al., 2009; Brickhouse et al., 2000; Simm ons et al., 2022; Soto-Lara & Simpkins, 2022; Starr et al., 2022; Tolbert Smith et al., 2022; Williams et al., 2020), ranging from cooking (Soto-Lara & Simpkins, 2022; Williams et al., 2020) to ma king designs and constructions with family members like siblings (Tolbert Smith et al., 2022). More intenti onal ways of engaging in STEM as a family has included collaborating on projects (e.g., Barron et al., 2009) and participating in informal learning environments together (Simmons  et al., 2022; Starr et al., 2022; Tolbert Smith et  al., 2022). During these family interactions in STEM, parents, including those without STEM expertise, have shared knowledge (e.g., STEM-based, problem solving) relevant to youth STEM proj ects, collaborated with youth, and learned from them (Barron et al., 2009; Martin et al., 2017; Varma, 2019). Since youth have faced barriers making STEM connections across contexts (Wade-Jaimes et al., 2021), research could explore the ways that familial funds of knowledge and identity have been present across youth STEM experiences, how family conversations and interactions have helped to bridge gaps across contexts, and the impacts on  STEM identity work (e.g., Aschbacher et al., 2010; Calabrese Barton et al., 2013).

Second, families have supported youth navigation of multiple identities, including racial, ethnic, cultural, familial, and STEM identities. Parents have leveraged familism to support youth STEM engagement in culturallyrelevant ways (Soto-Lara & Simpkins, 2022; Starr et al., 2022). For exa mple, families have encouraged youth to focus on school instead of having a job, demonstrating the importance of academic and STEM-based identities at certain developmental stages (Soto-Lara & Simpkins, 2022). Families hav e also instilled family values through engaging in STEM together (Simmons et al., 2022; Wi lliams et al., 2020). For example, one study descri bed a family that taught their daughter about hard work t hrough their family farm (as an everyday STEM exper ience), which was a value that could then translate to other contexts (Simmons et al., 2022). However, previous work has also demonstrated that families have created roadbl ocks in youth STEM pathways. Youth from marginalize d groups, especially Latina girls, have faced challenges balancing familial responsibilitie s with STEM goals (Aschbacher et al., 2010; Starr et al., 2022) and have been discouraged from pursuing their desired STEM pathway (Archer et al., 2015; Aschbacher et al., 2010). Since STEM identity and other identities, especially social identities (e.g., race/ethnicity, gender) are intertwined (Asc hbacher et al., 2010; Beeton et al., 2012; Carlone & Johnson, 2007; Simpson & Bouhafa, 2020), it is essential to develop a greater understanding of the ways that families have utilized their funds of knowledge and identity to support or hinder youth STEM identity work alongside additional identities.

## Discussion

STEM identity is a multilevel phenomenon connected to people, settings, and systems (Aschbacher et al., 2010; Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2023; Carlone, 2023; Simpso n & Bouhafa, 2020). However, the relationship betwe en STEM identity and family engagement has remained pr imarily conceptual (e.g., Ishimaru et al., 2015) or peripheral (e.g., Aschbacher et al., 2010). Through  this review of the STEM identity and family engage ment literature, I have demonstrated how family engageme nt practices and funds of knowledge might be connec ted to STEM identity work through (1) presence across cont exts where STEM identity work occurs and (2) famili arity with familial values and social identities, both of which are intertwined with STEM identity work. Exp loring the role of families within STEM identity work is especially importa nt for youth from marginalized groups and may provide evidence of more inclusive and expansive wa ys of being a STEM person that is intertwined to fa milial and cultural identities (Avraamidou & Schwartz, 2021; Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2023; Carlone, 2023; Kayumova & Dou, 2022).

One context where exploring these dynamics would be  especially generative is within informal STEM learning programs. Research has investigated STEM i dentity work within informal STEM learning programs through methods like case studies and ethnographies  (Calabrese Barton et al., 2013; Rahm & Moore, 2016), reflecting the dominance of small-scale qualitative studies within STEM identity rese arch (Danielsson et al., 2023). Other research has included families as participants within informal STEM learning programs (AllexsahtSnider et al., 2020; Martin et al., 2017), facilita ted important family interactions in STEM in shared  activities (Martin et al., 2017) and conversations (Allexsaht-Snider et al., 2020), and contributed to positive o utcomes for families like developing shared interests and planning for future pathways (Allexsaht-Snider et al., 2020). Future work could leverage the methodological expertise of  qualitative, STEM identity research to gain a bett er understanding of how moment-to-moment family exper iences in informal contexts may contribute to STEM identity work. These fleeting family interactions might include verbal connections to STEM experiences in other contexts and enactments of familial or cultural values as previously described. At the same time, rather than only exploring (in)equities in STEM identity and family engagement from a research perspective, researchers need to be invo lved in the design and creation of more just learni ng environments (Carlone, 2023). Since one aspect of  successful informal STEM learning programs has been responding to youth experiences and knowledge outside of these programs, including their identities, knowledge, cultural practices, and curiosities (Calabrese Bart on & Tan, 2023), there is potential for valuing fun ds of knowledge and constructing a more equitable and inc lusive form of STEM identity within these contexts. Combining research and design efforts to support fa mily engagement in informal STEM learning programs,  redefine STEM identity in authen tic ways, and promote interactions within this new form of STEM identity are important future directions to the field.
