# A Case Study of the Barriers to Learning Social-Emotional Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This is a qualitative case study of a midwestern United States educator’s journey to learn and implement social-emotional learning (SEL) in an economically disadvantaged, public school district, the infrastructural challenges that she faced, and the design she was able to implement when these infrastructural challenges were removed. This case study unwraps a teacher learning story that highlights her perceptions of systemic pathways for SEL implementation as well as challenges, such as standardized testing, educational bureaucracy, and unmet child welfare needs.

## Introduction and background

Much of learning sciences research focuses on the design and implementation of learning innovations in specific subject areas such as math or science, but this cas e study illustrates infrastructural supports and challenges to social-emotional learning (SEL). By following one U.S. educator’s learning journey  from an economically disadvantaged public school through her design of a non-traditional, private PreK-6 school designed to specifically support SEL, this case study reveals issues that U.S.  educators (both public and private)  face to implement SEL with fidelity.

SEL is a layer in whole-child development used nation-wide as a framework for students to understand themselves and others, leading to stronger relation ships and improved PreK-12 learning environments. The Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL) is a leading research group provid ing evidence and supports for SEL.  Leading SEL researchers have called for implementat ion to be systematically integrated into daily interactions and practices at multiple levels using collaboration between school, family, and community (Jones & Bouffard, 2012) and for “synergy  among researchers, practitioners, and policy maker s” so that as many students as possible benefit from well-conceptualized and well-implemented SEL programs (Mahoney et al., 2018, p.  23). Educational reformers who are in tune with the line age of SEL reform recognize the importance of this call, as the systemization o f U.S. schools has repeatedly imposed barriers for addressing the role of affect in student learning (Hoffman, 20 09; Cuban, 2020; Rury, 2020; Rousmaniere, 1997; Ste ffes, 2012; Tyack, 1974). Though many barriers for SEL exist, researchers aim for improvement, as SEL is crucial for improving the global mental health crisis (Payton et al., 2000; Karnik & Winiarski, 2019; Lass-Hennemann et al.,

2023) and the teacher ‘burnout cascade’ (Jennings & Greenberg, 2009). However, SEL interventions lack a focus

on the systemic  and infrastructural issues that influence SEL implementation. There has been an increasing emphasis in the learning sciences on considering issues of implementation to ensure the sustainabili ty of innovations (Stevens et al., 2018; Ramey, Hilppö, &  Stevens, 2024; Penuel, 2019; Penuel et al., 2011; Penuel & Spillane, 2014). Penuel (2019) specifically suggest s learning scientists consider and design for components of educational infrastructure relevant to classroom learning, including: (1) teacher professional learning; (2) standards for student learning; (3) curriculum materials; (4) student assessments; (5) instructional techniques and routines; (6) building-and district-level policies; (7) school schedules that allocate instructional time for different subjects; (8) roles and positions focused on instructional support; (9) organizational routines, such as grade-level meetings, in which instruction is a focus; and (10)  personnel evaluation systems.  This case study contributes to implementation research as well as the literature o n teacher learning by focusing on the following res earch questions: (I) What infrastructural challenges, if any, did a midw estern U.S educator perceive in implementing SEL in economically disadvantaged public schools? (II) How did standardized testing, in particular, affect implementation of SEL in the se schools? (III) How did this educator use what she had learned to d esign SEL instruction at her new school that circumvented infrastructural barriers? Analytical framework Our study uses the term SEL to describe methods that incorporate the CASEL 5: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsi ble decision-making. SEL strategies can be embedded within many ecological contexts and can be taught through evidence-based programming as well as infused into existing curricula (Oberle et al., 2016).  CASEL offers the theory of action framework (ToA) t o serve as a blueprint for achieving school-wide SEL. Components of  ToA include: (i) establishing a shared vision for S EL among all school stakeholders; (ii) assess needs and availabl e resources for school-wide SEL implementation; (iii) provide ongoing and embedded professional learning in SEL i nstruction; (iv) adopt and incorporate evidence-based SEL programming into educational practices; (v) integra te SEL into everyday practices at school; and (vi) conduct cycles of inquiry to ensure continuous improvement (Oberle  et al., 2016; Durlak, 2011). The structure of this study adheres to the coupling of the SEL ToA framework with infrastructural research in the learning sciences to analyze how aspects of educational infrastructure impact teacher learning and implementation of SEL.

## Method

Upon coming across a private school in the midweste rn U.S. that aligned with SEL constructs, the first  author reached out to the director to learn more. This preliminary meeting led us to believe that the director’s viewpoint on SEL implementation and design of the school alig ned with the SEL ToA framework and research on infrastructure. Our subsequent data collection with  the director confirmed this initial impression and  provided further insight into teacher learning, infrastructural barriers, and SEL program design. Our research study adopted a qualitative case study design (Merriam & Tisdell,  2016) investigating the phenomena that affected on e educator’s implementation of SEL. The units of analysis were the reasons why this educator left the public-schools after 20 years and how she subsequently implemented SEL with fidelity in h er new school. This study used two sources of data: participant interview and field no tes. First, the first author interviewed the direct or, who we will refer to as Ms. Bridge, to gain a deeper understand ing of her journey to implement SEL. The interview was a retrospective, semi-structured, face-to-face interview with open-ended questions. The topics in the interview guide related to Ms. Bridge’s experiences implementing SEL in economically disadvantaged public schools and leaving to start her own private school. Interview questions paralleled the research  questions and sought to understand both how the school centralized SEL and how its design addressed infrastructural issues identified by the director. The first author gathered field notes from observat ions at the school during regular hours to fill gap s in understanding its design and SEL implementation methods. Observations of the school day totaled over 10 hours, spanned three school days, covering all aspects of the school day one or more times. The interview transcript was read and re-read to check for accuracy then coded using the fiv e phases of inductive thematic analysis to identify patterns and themes, including: (i) become familiar with the d ata; (ii) generate initial codes; (iii) search for  themes; (iv) review themes; and (v) define and name  themes (Merriam & Tisdell, 2016). Themes were guided by the research questions. For example, the first research question  centered around infrastructural challenges, so after generating the initial codes, codes that were related to political systems, educational systems, or societal issues that Ms. Br idge faced during her time in economically disadvan taged schools were gathered and grouped accordingly. As c odes were analyzed and understood, initial themes were identified. The subthemes that were identified were test scores, district optics, conflicting teacher responsibilities, district mandates, impractical curricula, unmet stu dent basic needs and mental health needs, and teach er distress/hopelessness. We grouped these subcategories into the three main inf rastructural issues: standardized testing, educational bureaucracy, and unmet child w elfare needs. Codes, themes and subthemes were revi ewed and member-checked to ensure accuracy of the participant’s perspective. We followed this process for codes that related to RQI and RQII. When data were triangulated, the approaches/methods identified in response to research RQIII were confirmed with the field notes. The firs t author also kept a reflexivity journal to r ecord reflections at the end of each day of research. These reflections allowed her to filter her thoughts so t hat she could see the findings separate from her own opinions and experiences in education, as a 5th grade teacher for seven years.

## Findings

During her 20 years in an economically disadvantaged public school district, according to parameters set by the U.S. Department of Education (U.S. Department of Education), Ms. Bridge served students in different capacities where she implemented SEL in each role. As a reading specialist, she supported all aspects of student literacy. As a Title 1 teacher, a role that only exists in qualifying (low income) sc hools, she provided small group or one-onone instructional reading support for students identified as “high risk” (a label we do not endorse) who were pulled out of regular instruction to receive support. As a multi-age, general educator, Ms. Bridge taught 4th and 5th grade students. As an instructional coach, she worked with teachers to improve effective teaching practices. Infrastructural issues The infrastructural issues Ms. Bridge learned fell into three categories: (i) standardized testing, (ii) educational bureaucracy, and (iii) unmet child welfare needs. Ms. Bridge concluded t hat standardized testing  was the dominant barrier that caused student learning issues, and most SEL barriers stemmed from related mandates. Standardized testing  required standardized curriculum, which hindered in dividualized supports for students.  It also steered age-graded systems,  tracking, and categorized student performance based upon socioeconomic background and race, which was consequential for self-esteem, identity development, growth mindsets, and relationship building. As an instructional coach, her job was to increase effective teaching practices, but the metric for measuring this was student test scores. Ms. Bridge disclosed that the district did not measure social-emotional competence; instead, attention was on learning that they could more easily measure and classify. Educational bureaucracy was another barrier to SEL in Ms. Brid ge’s experience. Ms. Bridge observed that the aspects of teaching that were measured, particularly by policies, were the aspects of teaching implemented. The district implement ed policies to provide evidence of teacher supports fo r students, without considering the impact of those policies. She concluded that "all o f those things that are mandated, it's about optics  rather than about kids." Ms. Bridge reported that the district’s desire to look like they were providing SEL supports for children was a barrier to actually supporting stude nts’ SEL. For example, Ms. Bridge recalled a policy  that required teachers to call home to each of their 30 students’ families six times a semester. This meant making 180 calls home per semester. Ms. Bridge explained that if each phone call took just 10 minutes, her planning time was gone, “and then I'm still going to be… my scores are still going to be flashed up on a screen in front of the whole district to look at. And so, it just feels really demoralizing.” She explained that every single moment in a teacher’s day already had a purpose, and there was no room for SEL. Over time, Ms. Bridge also witnessed a reduction of the agency of skilled educators as the district focused increasingly on metrics of academic performance. Lastly, Ms. Bridge recognized students’ welfare needs presented barriers to SEL. She noted that teachers had to address student survival needs before addressing student mental health needs, and both of those had to be addressed before implem enting SEL. The schools in which she worked had 80-90% of students who were low income (as measured by eligibility for free or redu ced-price lunch) and teachers were faced with providing  child survival needs and mental health supports beyond their areas  of expertise.  Families of students were unable to afford necessary mental health care supports, and i t would take a long time  to receive them through the school, due to extensive processing time and high demand. This meant that teachers had to spend much of the ir time addressing significant mental health needs  while also meeting other district demands. This  created impossible work environments and imposed trauma. She explained, “having the emotional capacity yourself to do that, when you're like, these kids need so much and I'm giving  them so much all day long …it's just a lot. And again, you know, I'm, I'm speaking from the past trauma of my old job." Ms. Bridge expressed hopelessness and despair that came from facing challenges that were outside of her control, no matter how hard she tried. A new design After 20 years and many attempts to make infrastruc tural changes within the schools in which she worked, Ms. Bridge felt her only way forward was to create a new private school. In this school, students work from an assetsbased approach to build up to and beyond the state grade level standards in proximity to their Zone of  Proximal Development (Vygotsky, 1978) through nurturing conn ections with each other, nature, and the community.  The first half of the school day is dedicated to  academic standards and th e second half of the day is dedicated to activities within the community and nature. The school conducts three major components of sustaining student learning processes that provide a systematic pathwa y for successful SEL implementation: multi-age grouping, centralizing instruction around a third entity, and supporting teacher and student agency. In the absence of standardized testing, students participate in multi-age grouping, and their growth is assessed accordingly during academic instruction. The grouping is her solution to embedding SEL into learning so that students can build both  self-awareness and academic knowledge. Students can go beyond their grade or have necessary time and tools to build up to academic goals from an assets-based approach. Ms. Bridge’s school also utilizes animals and other aspects of nature to teach academic content, which provides an organic avenue for students to engage in SEL through a third entity (Watt, 2022), not solely through themselves, the teacher, or other students. For example, students weighed ducklings o n a scale for a math lesson while observing the duc klings’ emotions. One student observed  that a duckling pooped while another student mentioned that he thought the duckling was “stressed”, which was why it may have had an accident so quic kly. Students noticed that the next duckling was “calm” when they measured its weight. Another duckling was a difficult one that ran away from the teacher. The teacher climbed into the pen and gently retrieved the duck,  then said, “you are the most anxious ducky.” Students’ eyes tracked the duckling’s chirping, observing what anxious behavior might look like as they weighed it. In this lesson, the ducklings served as a proxy to teach complex emotions, and students related each duckling’s emotions to their larger environment and other ducklings while learning math concepts. Other teachers at the school utilized similar strategies with a ha mster and bees. This way of center ing instruction around a third entity allowed students to learn SEL integrated with academic concepts, supported by tan gible interactions with living things. Teacher and student agency is another core component  of the school, which requires funds to be allocated to the teachers’ lessons instead of to on e large curriculum.  Teachers’ lessons are molded to fit the students in their group. Thus, Ms. Bridge works with teachers to purchase sp ecific materials they need (e.g., the baby ducks). Ms. Bridge also allocates funds to employ more staff as necessary.

## Discussion and conclusion

The results of this study indicate that standardized testing, educational bureaucracy, and unmet child welfare needs were key barriers to SEL from Ms. Bridge's viewpoint. Standardized testing conflicted with SEL by imposing standardized curriculum, age-graded systems, and tracking students, which conflicts with learning individual selfawareness, self-regulation, and social awareness. Though educators may not be able to eliminate stand ardized testing, they do have the ability to system atize pathways for SEL in their learning environment, learning from the barriers and strategies presented in this study. Furthermore, this case study revealed the trauma im posed on one teacher due to unmet child welfare needs, which impacted her learning and implementati on of SEL. These results unlock the possibility tha t unmet child welfare needs are an infrastructural barr ier to SEL, but may be specific to the U.S and less releva nt in countries with subsidized healthcare and childcare than the U.S. For example, a study completed by Blewitt et al.

(2021) studied barriers to SEL in Australian schools; however, healthcare and childcare are subsidized by the

government for all children and childcare providers are required to provide social-emotional supports. With differing systemic affordances and minimal literature involving unmet student welfare needs as an infrastructural barrier to SEL, it is imperative for researchers to consider U.S. schools as a vital source of support for the survival needs of U.S families. It is likely that the lack of support for U.S. families outside of schools adds to the pressure on the U.S. educator and is a barrier for teacher and student learning  of SEL. Paradoxically, U.S. children are in dire need of building social-emotional competence to endure a society with littl e social supports.  While challenging policy surrounding social supports and human rights in the U.S. is a continuous battle wor th contending, we also advise infrastructuring (Penuel, 2019) that considers the ways in which these systems impact U.S. teacher and student learning of SEL. It is our hope that this study connects learning sciences and SEL research into deeper comprehension of the infrastructural barriers that impact teacher  learning of SEL, with the intention of sustainable solutions for the global mental health crisis and for the benefit of students and teachers.
