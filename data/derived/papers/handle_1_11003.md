# Lifelong learning in Learning Sciences: Where are the Older People?

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We invite learning scientists to consider how we might include older people in our studies and designs. Our field is poised to study learning across the lifespan but has yet to take up the experiences of older people. We draw on our experiences with older people as starting points for connection, appreciation, and inquiry. Our stories highlight the material, relational, and ideational resources that shape older people’s lives and their learning, underscoring the value of viewing older people’s experiences ecologically, complexly, and dynamically.

Our title echoes Thorne’s (1987) paper “Re-visioning women and social change: Where are the children?” to draw parallel attention to how we, in the Learning Sciences (LS), might re-vision learning for social change by including the perspectives and experiences of older people (65+ years). As Thorne argued, children occupied a marginal position in sociology, often viewed as threats, victims, and objects rather than agents of learning. We notice a similar marginalization of older people in our LS field. We worry their negative positioning in wider medical and health discourses (in need of care), economic discourses (unproductive), and in relation to media (vulnerable) downplays their agency and capacity for continued learning (Centre for Ageing Better, 2020) and becoming (Hodkinson, 2008). LS, with its aim to study learning across the lifespan (Stevens et al., 2005), is poised to include the experiences of older people. Yet, few have taken up the learning of older people as a central concern (see Nummijoki et al., 2018 as an exception). Given this population is projected to double between 2015 and 2050, we ask: How might scholars in the LS engage with older adults to expand our understanding of processes of learning and their outcomes?

Building on Nasir and Cooks (2009), we highlight ideational, material, and relational resources that are available in the environment and peoples’ access to them to understand the interrelated processes of learning and identity development. Ideational resources  refer to ideas about oneself and one’s relationship to a target social practice. Material resources include how the physical environment, its organization, and its artifacts support one’s sense of connection to social practices. Relational resources  refer to the positive relationships with others in the context that can increase connection to a focal practice. This perspective attends to how older people have been represented in dominant discourses (ideational), the physical environments and tools that support older people’s learning (material), and the kinds of relationships that facilitate connections between older people and their families, caretakers, friends, and communities (relational). In this poster, we build on our experiences as the children, caretakers, and friends of older adults with different needs and experiences. As colleagues, we initially talked about our experiences informally, commiserating over healthcare challenges, sharin g curiosities and concerns about how our loved ones were changing and how we could support them (Represented as Theme 1 in the results). This led to discussions about our time with older people in educational institutions and social settings. We reflected that contradictory to some mainstream narratives, we witnessed agentic uptake of opportunities to build meaningful connections with others and to develop oneself in relation to changing circumstances (Themes 2 & 3). As we talked over meals, on walks, and over Zoom, we drew on our knowledge of learning and development to make sense of who our parents and friends were becoming. We started sharing our stories with other colleagues in the LS field and noticed that such experiences with older people were not well represented, despite deep concern and interest from colleagues. We 2335 developed this poster to spark more interest and scholarly inquiry into the lives of older adults, hoping to make the LS more relevant to our experiences and challenges, and to those of the growing population of older adults and their communities globally.

We name three themes that LS scholars could research with older adults, which come from our experiences. This is a starting point to generate ideas for how we could include older adults more centrally in research and practice. Learning and identity shifts for older people and their caretakers (Susan). As my mother-in-law's dementia made it so that she could no longer live alone, my family decided to move her closer to us. We organized a living situation (material) where she could maintain her sense of herself (ideational) as an independent adult, but her decline made this difficult. These changes were hard for her and my partner, whoneeded to assume greater responsibility for her physical, mental, and financial care. Designing a  learning environment was hard as we strived to enact these changes in power without shattering her sense of identity and capacity (relational). Creating complex representations of older people (Tom). In an inner-city suburb park in Beijing, I joined a multi-generational basketball community, consisting of men ranging from their mid-twenties to their late seventies. Using the Chinese social media app WeChat, the group discussed life on and off the court. One older gentleman captured and shared videos of our gameplay (material), prompting praise and critical analysis of players’ teamwork, skill, and character (ideational), as well as jovial interacti ons and in-group tensions (relational). This experience contradicts dominant Western narratives of older people as vulnerable or unproductive, raising questions about their complex roles, representations, and contributions in our community groups, and the types of learning that take place during such interactions. Learning to see anew (Liz). When my mom retired, her orientation to work obligations shifted (relational), allowing for more unstructured time to reflect and learn new ways of being. She said that what she learned was that the sky is beautiful-and that it always was-but now she was able to see it differently. The move away from work (relational) opened up time and capacity to see her surroundings and environment anew (material); as well as her role in gifting herself time to just be present (ideational). Th is observation provides a point of entry into the possibilities of learning when we see ourselves and other things anew beyond economic productivity.

## Significance

We aim to draw the field’s awareness to the diverse experiences of older people, recognizing that their contributions to our families, communities, and institutions are an integral part of our collective histories, our shared experiences in the present, and our visions for the future. Understanding their learning and becoming alongside the opportunities, challenges, and tensions they face is an important step toward inclusivity within our communities.
