# Physiological Indicators of Teacher Stress in CSCL Versus Non-Collaborative Learning Sessions

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This exploratory single-subject study examines teacher physiological stress across Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) versus non-CSCL sessions. One teacher was observed across 22 sessions using biosensors (heart rate variability, electrodermal activity) and questionnaires. Multivariate analysis revealed no overall physiological differences. However, exploratory analysis showed significantly elevated post-session electrodermal activity after CSCL. Given the single-subject design, findings provide methodological insights and hints for new knowledge regarding cumulative arousal, rather than broad generalizations.

Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) environments create complex orchestration demands for teachers, requiring real-time monitoring of multiple student groups, technological troubleshooting, and adaptive pedagogical decisions (Dillenbourg, 2013; Ortega-Arranz et al., 2024). While research has extensively examined student outcomes in CSCL (Jeong et al., 2019), less attention has been paid to the teacher experience and physiological load during collaborative technology-mediated instruction (Amarasinghe et al., 2022). A critical question for CSCL implementation concerns whether orchestration demands produce different physiological stress patterns compared to traditional teaching. Understanding these differences is necessary to enhance real-time, data-driven feedback and  support mechanisms for educators navigating complex digital environments (Haataja et al., 2018). If stress patterns are comparable, teachers are managing demands effectively; if elevated, specific interventions are needed.

This exploratory study addresses the research question: How do teacher physiological stress indicators such as heart rate variability (HRV) and electrodermal activity (EDA) and psychological indicators from the StateTrait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) (Spielberger et al., 1971) questionnaire differ between CSCL and non-CSCL real classroom settings? CSCL sessions refer to scripted collaborative learning using technology-mediated tools, while non-CSCL sessions comprise traditional instruction with individual work and whole-class activities.

## Theoretical background

Teacher orchestration in CSCL involves managing technological, pedagogical, and social dimensions simultaneously, requiring complex real-time decision-making processes (Dillenbourg et al., 2013; Ortega-Arranz et al., 2024). This multi-layered orchestration can create increased cognitive and emotional demands compared to traditional instruction (Prieto et al., 2011).

Recent studies explore teacher orchestration load using multimodal data, stressing the need for objective, continuous metrics of hidden instructional load to inform data-driven support (Hakami et al., 2024), but used only one biosignal (HRV) and could benefit from adding more data layers, such as EDA, in order to improve reliability in real classroom scenarios. Specifically, HRV reflects autonomic nervous system responses to stress and cognitive load (Bordi et al., 2018; Gedam & Paul, 2021), while  EDA indicates emotional arousal related to attention and engagement (Pijeira-Díaz et al., 2018).

Comparing these physiological responses across instructional modalities can reveal whether CSCL orchestration produces distinctive stress patterns. Such insights theoretically contribute to orchestration by informing teacher preparation and support systems for sustainable CSCL implementation (Gedam & Paul, 2021). This study employed a single-subject design, appropriate for exploratory investigations of individual differences and within-person comparisons. The research was conducted in Universitat Pompeu Fabra, Barcelona, Spain, and received ethical approval from the institutional ethics committee. One female teacher with over 20 years of teaching experience was observed across 22 sessions over one academic trimester. The teacher wore a biosignalsplux wearable device [PLUX Wireless Biosignals, Lisbon, Portugal] capturing electrocardiography (ECG) for HRV analysis and EDA for skin conductance measurement at 500Hz sampling rate. Sessions included CSCL sessions (n=7) using PyramidApp, a collaborative learning 692 application that structures student group work through scripted roles, scaffolded tasks, and real-time feedback mechanisms (Manathunga et al., 2016). These sessions required the teacher to orchestrate multiple simultaneous groups, monitor technology-mediated collaboration, and provide adaptive support across teams. Traditional nonCSCL sessions (n=15) involved conventional instruction including teacher-led explanations, individual student work, whole-class discussions, and direct instruction without collabo rative technology tools. Six sessions were excluded due to missing data. Leaving a final analytical sample of 16 sessions, 6 using CSCL and 10 non-CSCL. Pre-and post-session measurements included three-minute standing baselines for physiological data and State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) assessments.

ECG signals underwent R-peak detection and artifact correction to extract inter-beat intervals, from which Root Mean Square of Successive Differences (RMSSD) was calculated using 30-second windows (Castaldo et al., 2019). EDA signals were decomposed into tonic (Skin Conductance Level, SCL) and phasic (Skin Conductance Response, SCR) components. All physiological features were normalized as percentage change from individual baseline values (reactivity %).

Independent sample comparisons between CSCL and non-CSCL sessions were conducted using MannWhitney U tests for individual variables (non-parametric approach given small sample size). A one-way MANOVA examined whether the combined physiological profile (RMSSD, EDA-SCL, EDA-SCR difference between pre-and post-session measurements) differed between session types. Statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS v31. This study acknowledges that treating repeated measures from a single participant as independent groups violates standard test assumptions. Given the exploratory single-subject nature of this study, uncorrected p-values are reported to identify potential trends rather than confirm generalizable hypotheses. A mixed-design ANOVA was conducted to examine whether the psychological and physiological changes (post minus pre session values) differed between the collaborative (CSCL) and non-collaborative (non-CSCL) groups. The analysis revealed no significant between-subjects effect of the collaboration condition (F(1, 13)=0.044, p=0.838, η²=0.003). There was no significant interaction between the type of measure and the collaboration condition (F(3,39)=1.072, p=0.372, η²=0.076), indicating that the specific pattern of physiological and psychological changes did not significantly differ during CSCL versus non-CSCL sessions. Multivariate tests confirmed this lack of significant interaction (Pillai's Trace=0.257, F(3,11)=1.271, p=0.332, η²=0.257) Mann-Whitney U tests revealed significant differences in EDA between CSCL and non-CSCL sessions. CSCL sessions showed significantly higher skin conductance in post-session measurements (U=11.0, p=0.031, r=0.67), with a large effect size, indicating substantially elevated sympathetic activation after a CSCL session. The primary finding is that the multivariate physiological profile did not differ significantly between CSCL and non-CSCL sessions (Pillai's Trace=0.253, p=0.576). When HRV, EDA and STAI are considered together, CSCL orchestration does not produce a distinctly different physiological response pattern compared to traditional instruction (Prieto et al., 2011; Amarasinghe et al., 2022). As a single-subject design, these fin dings cannot be generalized to imply equivalence between settings or that experienced teachers universally manage demands without stress. Instead, this study provides methodological insights and hints for new knowledge regarding hidden instructional loads. This suggests teachers may effectively regulate CSCL demands without substantially elevated overall stress responses, supporting sustainable CSCL implementation while aligning with previous CSCL orchestration findings (Hakami et al., 2024), this must be interpreted cautiously given the non-significant multivariate test and requires replication.

Given the exploratory nature and small sample size (n=16), we examined individual variables. Post-session electrodermal activity was significantly elevated following CSCL sessions (U=11.0, p=0.031, r=0.67), with no pre-session differences (p=0.092). The absence of overall differences indicates CSCL does not generate substantially different demands than traditional instruction. While the pre-post design cannot assess moment-tomoment acute stress spikes, comparable pre-session baselines suggest teachers avoid sustained elevated arousal during teaching. However, elevated post-session EDA hits cumulative arousal effects showing after instruction. Limitations include single-teacher observation, small sample size, and lack of in-session continuous monitoring. Future research should replicate across multiple teachers, include continuous physiological monitoring during sessions.

This study provides initial evidence that CSCL may not be inherently more physiologically demanding for experienced teachers, while exploratory findings suggest potential cumulative arousal patterns warranting further investigation.
