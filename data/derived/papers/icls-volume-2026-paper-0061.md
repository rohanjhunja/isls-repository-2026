# Cultural Contexts as Catalysts for Learning: Integrating Students’ Lived Experiences into Physics Assessments

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how leveraging students’ cultural resources can support conceptual understanding in a university-level physics course. Drawing on culturally relevant pedagogy (Ladson-Billings, 1995), the resources framework (diSessa, 1993; Hammer e t al., 2005), and the cultural resource enactment framework (Mathis, 2025), the research examines how contextualizing motion-graph tasks within students’ lived campus experiences influences reasoning about kinematics. Forty-eight students completed four tasks, each situated in familiar campus settings, that required interpreting, comparing, and generating position-, velocity-, and acceleration-time graphs. Analysis using a four-dimensional rubric revealed that most students demonstrated scientific reasoning when tasks were culturally and spatially grounded, particularly in single-graph interpretation and comparative reasoning. Student reflections indicated that familiar contexts increased engagement and relevance. Findings provide empirical evidence that sit uating disciplinary ideas in culturally meaningful contexts can enhance both engagement and representational fluency, offering a practical model for culturally grounded assessment design in university physics instruction.

Students enter classrooms with a wide range of ideas shaped by their prior experiences, including formal instruction, interpersonal interactions, personal observations, overheard information, and instances where they have made mistakes and learned from them (Bransford et al., 2000). These accumulated experiences shape how students view the world and interpret new information. When ideas are presented within familiar contexts, students are often more receptive and able to make meaningful connections (Meyer &  Crawford, 2011). Conversely, when similar concepts are introduced through unfamiliar contexts, students may struggle to retain and apply new information (Lee & Luykx, 2006).

Beyond individual experiences, students also bring perspectives informed by their cultural and social interactions. Cultural background can influence religious beliefs, enthusiasm for particular sports, political views, and the kinds of examples that feel natural to use when reasoning about different topics (Moll et al., 2006). In this sense, students enter classrooms with cultural schemas, perspectives, and interpretive frameworks shaped by their cultural experiences (Nishida, 1999). These schemas influence how students engage with, interpret, and make sense of disciplinary ideas (Gutierrez & Rogoff, 2003). For example, during a classroom discussion about energy efficiency, students’ responses may reflect the dominant energy sources in their communities. In some regions, hydroelectric power is most common; in others, coal or wind energy may be the primary source. These contextual differences can shape how students think about heating homes, powering machines, or understanding energy use in everyday life. Consequently, cultural schemas play a significant role in shaping how students receive, interpret, and retain information about scientific concepts. These cultural schemas also shape how individuals make sense of new ideas, influencing their ways of reasoning, the epistemological stances they adopt, and the examples they draw upon during scientific inquiry (Nishida, 1999). Building on this understanding, the present study examines a university-level physics course to explore how students’ cultural schemas —situated within their institutional and regional contexts —influence their engagement with physics concepts. Specifically,  this study investigates the question: How does leveraging students’ cultural resources in the design and implementation of assessments influence their learning of physics concepts, particularly ideas related to the motion of objects?

## Theoretical framework

The theoretical grounding of this study draws on culture-based frameworks such as culturally responsive pedagogy (Gay, 2010) and culturally relevant pedagogy (Ladson-Billings, 1995). These frameworks emphasize 513 leveraging students’ cultural resources as meaningful entry points into canonical disciplinary ideas. In the context of this study, the canonical ideas focus on concepts of motion in physics, specifically, students’ understanding of position vs. time, velocity vs. time, and acceleration vs. time graphs. While culturally responsive and culturally relevant pedagogies encompass multiple dimensions, including the development of sociopolitical consciousness (Ladson-Billings, 1995), this study focuses more narrowly on how teachers candraw upon students’ cultural resources to support disciplinary sensemaking in physics. This emphasis aligns with the instructional goals and research questions of the study, which center on integrating students’ cultural knowledge into learning activities and assessments to deepen their understanding of motion concepts.

This study also draws on the resources framework (diSessa, 1993; Hammer et al., 2005), which conceptualizes students' ideas not as misconceptions to be replaced, but as productive starting points for disciplinary learning. By connecting cultural resources to students' existing conceptual resources, teachers can create more coherent and meaningful learning trajectories. In addition to these broader pedagogical frameworks, this study is informed by a newly developed cultural resource enactment framework (Mathis, 2025). This framework highlights how teachers can leverage students’ collective cultural and community interests as instructional resources. Through such enactment, teachers situate disciplinary ideas within culturally meaningful contexts, encouraging students to explore phenomena that resonate with their lived experiences. As students engage in these investigations, they participate in core scientific practices, such as modeling, argumentation, computational thinking, and experimentation, through both hands-on and minds-on activities (National Research Council [NRC], 2012). In this process, students use their investigative knowledge to construct deeper understandings of physics concepts. Following these culturally grounded learning experiences, teachers administer formative and summative assessments to examine students’ developing understandings. The central claim of this study is that engaging students in disciplinary exploration through culturally familiar contexts and practices will lead to improved learning outcomes.

## Literature review

A growing body of research has examined how teachers can leverage students’ cultural resources to support disciplinary learning (Ladson-Billings, 1995; Bang & Medin, 2010). Leveraging students' cultural resources involves identifying and drawing on the cultural experiences, practices, and epistemologies that they bring to the classroom to contextualize disciplinary ideas, support sensemaking, and validate diverse ways of knowing (Nasir et al., 2006). These studies highlight both the potential and the challenges of integrating culturally responsive or culturally relevant approaches into science and physics classrooms. While there is strong theoretical support for using students’ cultural knowledge as a foundation for learning, teachers often encounter significant barriers when attempting to enact these practices.

One major challenge identified in the literature is that many teachers lack a clear understanding of how cultural resources can be meaningfully connected to disciplinary content, particularly in physics (Mathis et al, 2023). Some teachers express uncertainty about how to identify relevant cultural resources or how to use them to deepen engagement with canonical ideas. Another challenge is the lack of curricular materials that integrate cultural context in meaningful ways. Teachers often report that available physics curricula are highly standardized and abstract, offering few entry points for connecting to students' lived experiences (Mathis et al., 2023). Developing such materials probably will require significant time, support, and professional learning, w hich are not always available. Others face institutional and sociopolitical constraints, as some schools, districts, and even students and families may resist culturally relevant approaches due to ideological, political, or curricular pressures (Mathis & Southerland, 2022).

Additionally, many teachers report that they were never exposed to culturally responsive or culturally relevant pedagogies in their professional development (Mathis et al., 2023). As a result, they may feel unprepared or unmotivated to implement such approaches (Gay, 2010). Some teachers express skepticism about their utility, in that it will compromise the rigor of what is taught, while others acknowledge their importance but struggle to apply them in practice (Young, 2010). These challenges highlight the gap between theoretical commitments to culturally responsive instruction and practical enactment in classrooms. The gap is particularly pronounced in the field of physics education, where few studies have systematically examined how leveraging students’ cultural resources impacts learning. In particular, there is a limited body of research that examines how teachers  identify and mobilize students' cultural resources during instruction, how students respond to these practices, and what mechanisms support disciplinary learning in these contexts. Moreover, few studies have developed design frameworks to guide the integration of cultural resources 514 into physics instruction, leaving teachers without concrete models to draw upon. While some emerging work (Mathis, 2020) explores how students’ cultural perspectives can inform physics instruction, much of this research has relied on interview studies that  document students’ ideas or teachers’ beliefs, rather than investigating curricular enactments in classroom settings. As a result, there is limited empirical evidence on what culturally grounded physics instruction looks like in practice and how it influences students’ disciplinary understanding.

To address this gap, the present study aims to contribute to the growing conversation around culturally grounded physics education by examining how leveraging students’ cultural resources can shape learning experiences and outcomes in a university-level physics course. Specifically, this study examines how instructors can recognize and leverage the cultural ideas that students bring into the classroom to facilitate sensemaking of motion concepts. This work represents an initial step toward building a more robust body of research on the impact of culturally responsive and culturally relevant approaches on disciplinary learning in physics. Context and Participants: This study was conducted in a studio-format introductory physics course at a large university in the Midwestern United States (hereafter referred to as Midwestern University). The course enrolled 48 undergraduate students, the majority of whom were biomedical science majors pursuing pre-medical pathways.

The study examines how leveraging students’ lived experiences and familiarity with their campus environment can support their understanding of motion concepts, including displacement, velocity, and acceleration. Instructional Design: The instructional activity was designed to situate canonical physics ideas within familiar and culturally meaningful contexts, specifically, locations and routes that students regularly encounter on their university campus. Students engaged in a sequence of four tasks that required them to construct and interpret position vs. time, velocity vs. time, and acceleration vs. time graphs, as well as to generate and analyze their own motion scenarios. These tasks were embedded within regular  in-class activities and homework assignments. The authors collaboratively developed the four tasks and the course instructor to align with core kinematics concepts and draw on familiar campus locations.

The four core tasks were as follows:

1. Biking to the Stadium

A student bikes from Residence Hall A to the Campus Stadium at a steady pace, stops to take a picture, and then accelerates to reach the stadium before a game begins. o Students were asked to sketch position–time and velocity–time graphs, indicating segments of constant velocity, stopping, and acceleration.

2. Vehicle Movement Along a Main Road

An ice cream truck starts at the Campus Center, moves south along Main Campus Road, slows down to stop near Academic Building B, remains there for a short time, and then reverses back toward the starting point. o Students sketched position–time and velocity–time graphs and explained how they represented the change in direction on the velocity graph.

3. Walking vs. Scootering to a Science Facility

Two students leave from Residence Hall B at the same time to travel to the Science and Technology Facility. One student walks the entire way; the other uses a scooter but takes a five-minute break midway. o Students created comparative position–time and velocity–time graphs for both travelers on the same axes and explained differences in motion, breaks, and arrival times.

4. Personal Route Mapping

Students chose a real route they had taken on campus that week (ex: from their residence hall to a classroom or dining hall). They measured approximate distance and time, then generated position– time and velocity–time graphs and a simple motion map with time stamps. o Students reflected on the challenges and assumptions involved in translating their real movement into graphical representations.

## Analytic approach

The analysis focused on descriptive patterns in students’ performance across tasks. Specifically, the team examined how students engaged with motion graphing tasks situated in familiar campus contexts, and how their graph interpretation, cross-representation reasoning, vocabulary use, and reasoning strategies varied by task type. These patterns informed our understanding of how leveraging students’ cultural and contextual experiences influences their engagement and conceptual understanding of motion. All procedures complied with ethical standards for research involving human participants. ## Overview of student performance

The Position –Velocity–Time (PVT) Graphing Activity consisted of four scaffolded tasks and one reflection question, designed to progressively elicit students’ understanding of kinematics representations. Each task targeted a different facet of students’ graphing abilities: interpreting given scenarios (Q1–Q2), comparing motion patterns (Q3), generating their own representations (Q4), and reflecting on the use of familiar contexts. Student responses were coded using a four-level rubric assessing Graph Interpretation, Connections Across Graphs, Vocabulary Use, and Reasoning Strategy, ranging from Level 0 (no evidence) to Level 3 (scientific reasoning). For this activity, 21 of the 48 students consented to have their problems analyzed. Across the 21 student submissions analyzed, most demonstrated some degree of scientific reasoning, though performance varied notably by task type. Question 1: Graph interpretation in a familiar scenario Question 1 required students to represent a scenario in which a student bikes from Residence Hall A to the Campus Stadium at a constant velocity, stops to take a picture, and then accelerates to the destination. Most students performed strongly on this task: 14 of 21 submissions (66.6%) were coded at Level 3, demonstrating correct use of slope and area reasoning and accurate matching between position– time and velocity –time graphs. Students effectively identified distinct motion segments (linear, flat, curve d) and translated them between representations. A smaller subset of responses at Levels 1–2 relied on descriptive statements (ex: “it goes up”) or partially applied slope/area concepts. These results indicate a solid foundation in single-graph interpretation when scenarios are structured and familiar.

Question 2: Representing direction change and cross-graph reasoning 517 Question 2 described a vehicle moving along Main Campus Road, stopping, and reversing direction. This task required students to interpret direction change as negative velocity and make explicit connections between position–time and velocity –time graphs. Performance was more varied: 10 students (47.6%) reached Level 3, while the modal response was Level 2 (42.8%), characterized by qualitative descriptions of direction change without explicit reference to slope or sign. For example, some students noted that “the graph goes the other way” without explaining that velocity crossed zero. Two students (9.5%) gave Level 1 responses that described shape changes without analytical reasoning. These results highlight directional reasoning and cross-graph connections as areas of conceptual difficulty, even among students who demonstrated proficiency on Q1. Question 3: Comparative reasoning between multiple motions Question 3 required students to compare walking versus scootering to the Science and Technology Facility, overlaying position–time and velocity–time graphs and analyzing differences in speed, breaks, and arrival times. Most students performed at Levels 2–3 (90.4%), indicating a moderate to high capacity to compare motion patterns and use appropriate vocabulary (ex, “constant velocity,” “break,” “steeper slope”). High-scoring responses used scientific language consistently and explicitly described how the sc ooter break was represented graphically. Mid-level responses noted differences but lacked deeper analytical explanations. Two students (9.5%) remained at Level 1, focusing on surface features. These findings suggest a developing capacity for comparative reasoning that builds on prior interpretive skills. Question 4: Generating graphs from real routes Question 4 involved graphing students’ own real campus routes, creating position–time and velocity–time graphs, and reflecting on modeling assumptions. This task revealed the widest variability. A little more than half of the students (57.1%) reached Level  3, producing detailed and accurate representations and articulating assumptions such as averaging speeds, smoothing curves, or marking stops. Seven students (33.3%) achieved Level 2, making reasonable attempts but oversimplifying features like turns or ac celeration. A smaller group (9.5%) remained at Level 1, offering descriptive rather than analytical accounts. These results indicate differences in students’ ability to translate real experiences into formal physics representations, reflecting variation in  reasoning strategies and modeling skills.

Reflection: Role of familiar contexts In the reflection prompt, most students indicated that using familiar campus contexts increased their engagement and visualization. Many noted that picturing real campus locations made the problems “easier to visualize” and “more relevant and interesting” than generic scenarios. A minority found no added benefit. Importantly, increased engagement did not always correlate with higher analytical reasoning levels, indicating that while culturally and spatially relevant contexts can enhance motivation and mental imagery, additional instructional scaffolds may be needed to support deeper conceptual reasoning.

Summary of patterns A clear progression emerged across the four tasks. Students performed strongest on single-graph interpretation (Q1), showed greater variability in cross-graph reasoning and direction changes (Q2), and demonstrated developing skills in comparative reasoning (Q3). Generative modeling tasks (Q4) revealed the most significant differences in reasoning strategies, suggesting the need for targeted support in helping students formalize real-world experiences into disciplinary representations. Integrating familiar c ontexts was largely successful in increasing engagement, but must be paired with explicit instructional support to promote more sophisticated scientific reasoning.

The findings from this study suggest that situating physics assessments within students’ cultural and contextual experiences can support deeper understanding of motion concepts, particularly in interpreting and connecting position–time, velocity–time, and acceleration–time graphs. Across tasks, a majority of students demonstrated Level 3 reasoning on the rubric, indicating strong representational fluency when problems were framed using familiar campus contexts. Students’ written reflections further revealed that these contexts increased engagement, helped them visualize physical scenarios more concretely, and made the tasks feel more relevant to their daily experiences.

These results provide initial empirical evidence that leveraging cultural and contextual resources can meaningfully shape how students make sense of disciplinary physics ideas. This aligns with prior theoretical work 518 emphasizing that students’ cultural schemas and lived experiences can serve as productive entry points for disciplinary sensemaking (Bang & Medin, 2010; Ladson-Billings, 1995; Nasir et al., 2006). However, while the performance patterns are promising, this study does not isolate the specific contribution of cultural context relative to more traditional problem settings. Future studies should consider incorporating control tasks that are culturally neutral or decontextualized to allow for direct comparisons. Such comparisons would make it possible to examine more precisely the extent to which cultural familiarity facilitates sensemaking and where its effects may be most pronounced (ex., in interpretation vs. cross-representation reasoning). Another key implication of this study concerns task and assessment design. While there is growing consensus that cultural resources are central to equitable science instruction, few concrete mechanisms exist to help physics instructors identify and incorporate these resources into their curricular and assessment practices. The campus-based motion tasks used in this study represent one potential model: instructors leveraged students’ shared familiarity with physical spaces to ground abstract concepts. Going forward, it will be important to systematically design and test task frameworks that guide instructors in identifying relevant cultural resources, whether these are physical spaces, community practices, linguistic repertoires, or everyday experiences, and in mapping them onto disciplinary ideas in ways that maintain conceptual rigor. Finally, the findings underscore the need for professional development structures that prepare and support instructors in this work. Many university physics instructors have not received explicit training in culturally relevant or culturally responsive pedagogies, and as a result, may not prioritize or feel equipped to attend to students’ cultural resources in their teaching. Targeted professional learning opportunities could focus on:

1. Raising awareness of students’ diverse cultural and experiential knowledge,

2. Modeling examples of culturally grounded task design and assessment, and

3. Developing practical tools for identifying and integrating cultural resources into instruction.

Such support systems may help bridge the gap between theoretical commitments to equity and the practical enactment of culturally grounded pedagogy in university physics classrooms.
