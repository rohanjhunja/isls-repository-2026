# Mental Simulations to Advance Adaptive Teaching Expertise in Reflection-Based Instructional Coaching

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Reflection on classroom artifacts is a core feature of teacher professional development. Despite its ubiquity, however, reflection as a learning mechanism is often illdefined and lacking clear connection to a larger theory of teacher learning and change. Here, we present Mental Simulations for Teacher Reflection (MSTR) as one kind of robust teacher learning routine. Our aim is to offer a well-specified routine that embodies core principles for developing adaptive teaching expertise in expert-guided reflection contexts.

## Introduction

Education researchers increasingly emphasize adaptive teaching expertise, i.e., the ability to read, i nterpret, and flexibly respond to students’ developing thinking d uring instruction, as critical for student-centered  instruction (van Es & Sherin, 2002). Despite wide consensus tha t adaptive expertise is an important teacher learni ng goal, research to understand the nature of its development and cultivating these processes in practice is still relatively thin (Carbonell et al., 2014). Reflection-based instructional coaching is recognized as one promising approach for developing the kind of pedagogical knowledge and sk ill needed for adaptive teaching expertise. However, significant variation exists in the effectiveness of such programs for transforming teaching practice, both within and across program designs and instantiations. More  work is needed to understand what specific mechani sms underlie robust teacher learning in these contexts, and how they can be concretely supported in coaching practices and routines. Here, we present one kind of teacher learning routine—Mental Simulations for Teacher Reflection (MSTR)—that culminated from the work of one interdi sciplinary research team focused on this issue. In what follows, we provide a summary of the conceptual backdrop, basic components, and quality dimensions of MSTR, followed by a concept map to illustrate how MSTR co nnects to larger mechanisms for developing adaptive teaching expertise. We conclude with implications for future work. Research Background MSTR primarily draws on adaptive expertise research to define the learning and change processes associated with developing a well-connected base of conceptual and procedural knowledge for student-centered pedagogies. We particularly focus on how to elicit and scaffold te achers’ pedagogical reasoning processes in ways tha t support two key components of adaptive teaching expertise: (1) Recognizing, interpreting, and resolving instru ctional ambiguities and discrepancies; and (2) Iterative li nking between teaching specifics (e.g., teacher mov es, lesson contexts) to more generalized representations (e.g., larger pedagogical dilemmas, goals, and principles) to support knowledge building and conceptual change (Walsh, 2021; Kavanagh et al., 2020; Bransford et al., 2005). Mental simulations for teacher reflection Mental simulations, as a process for systematically  engaging ‘what-if’ reasoning processes to generate  causal inferences in ambiguous and uncertain situations (M arkman & Dyczewski, 2013) provides an empirical framework for a coaching routine aligned with these  theoretical perspectives. Adapting mental simulations to a teacher learning context, MSTR includes three basic  components: (1) Establishing Ambiguities: coach and teacher iteratively discuss and refine shared under standing of the problem space, situating the contex t for the simulation; (2) Proposing Alternatives: coach and teacher iteratively propose alternative  teaching moves for addressing instructional ambiguities; (3) Weighing Alternatives: coach and teacher iteratively hypothesize and weigh the potential of selected alternatives for advancing pedagogical goals and principles. Table 1 (below) presents the quality dimensions of the interactions within each MSTR component that link to goals for adaptive teaching expertise. ## Discussion and implications

MSTR represents one artifact that emerged from our ongoing efforts to advance more robust, theory-base d empirical frameworks for studying teacher learning mechanisms. By explicitly describing the components  and quality dimensions within a coaching routine, MSTR can be leveraged to inform more effective coach tra ining and serve as a foundation for future hypothesis testing.
