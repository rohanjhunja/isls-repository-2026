# Teaching Machine Learning as Adaptive Design Work

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To broaden and improve machine learning (ML) education, researchers must partner with teachers and study the affordances and challenges of ML instruction. Yet, most teachers are not ML experts and may need curricula that align ML concepts with their disciplinary expertise. This study explores three cases of classroom enactments of Mathematics of Facial Recognition (FaceID): five co-designed ML lessons aligned to high school statistics standards. Each teacher was encouraged to adapt FaceID to meet their instructional goals. Drawing from Brown’s Design Capacity for Enactment framework, we examine the frequency and substance of teachers’ adaptations of FaceID, and the design resources that enabled their adaptations. We found that teachers’ prior knowledge of ML, their statistics pedagogical content knowledge, and their knowledge of students supported their adaptations. From these findings, we illustrate triumphs and challenges that teachers who are new to ML may face when enacting ML lessons.

## Introduction

As technologies that use machine learning (ML) continue to reshape the world writ large, integrating ML curricula into K-12 classrooms has persisted as a challenge. Althoug h many countries have developed educational frameworks to guide what ML concepts should be taught (e.g., AI4K12, Touretzky et al., 2023; Erasmus+, Bellas et al., 2023; Yang, 2019), and developers have designed K-12 facing ML curricula (Druga et al., 2022), classroom teachers face significant implementation barriers w hen considering when and how to enact ML curricula, including rigid s cheduling constraints, limited computational resour ces, pressure to teach to standardized assessments, and a general lack of ML subject-matter and pedagogical knowledge (Chiu & Chai, 2020). Many existing ML education efforts have focused on infor mal learning environments, with researchers rather than teachers serving as the primary implementers of ML curricula (e.g., Kaspersen et al., 2022; Vartiainen  et al., 2020). This points toward a need to understand how classroom teachers —particularly those in ML-adjacent disciplines like statistics—may adapt and enact ML curricula within the constra ints of traditional school settings while building from their pedagogical expertise and disciplinary subject-matter knowledge. Through a comparative case study, we examine how th ree experienced statistics teachers’ design resources influenced their enactments of Mathematic s of Facial Recognition (FaceID), an integrated ML lesson sequence co-designed between a researcher and a veteran high sc hool statistics teacher (Delaney, 2024; Kafai et al., 2024). Drawing from Brown's (2002) Design Capacity for Enactment  framework, we investigate how these educators’ enactments of FaceID were mediated by their design resources: their intersecting forms of knowledge and goals. We position teachers as agentic designer s who actively shaped and contextualized FaceID, which ultimately influenced what their students learned. By studying how statistics teachers adapt ML curric ula within the constraints of traditional classrooms, we aim t o inform the designs of future ML curricula that ca n better account for teachers' existing forms of expertise. We ask: (1) How and to what extent did teachers adapt FaceID? and (2) What design resources did teachers draw upon when making adaptations?

## Theoretical framework: Teachers as adaptive ML curricula designers

Curriculum resources – sets of materials provided to teachers that are enacted in classrooms and reflect district or state learning objectives (Sherin & Drake, 2009, pg. 468) - are inherently reshaped by teachers before and during classroom instruction. Building from Stein, Remillard, and Smith’s Temporal Curriculum Use framework (2007), we contend that teachers read, make sense of, and a dapt written curriculum materials (Sherin & Drake, 2009), first in ways that reflect their goals and aims (th e teacher-intended curriculum), then in ways that illustrate the interactions between students and the curriculum (t he enacted curriculum) (see Figure 1). In the digital age, teachers often make use of online curriculum resour ces to further build their knowledge of concepts wi thin the written curriculum before they adapt it (Remillard, 2016). While some work in curr iculum studies characterizes teachers’ translations of curriculum as low fidelity of implementation, or low adherence to the written curriculum (O’Donnell, 2008), a s undesirable, more recent studies in the learning sciences have shown that teachers can transform written curricula to provide students with ambitious, high-quality learning experiences. Figure 1 Temporal Curriculum Use (Stein et al., 2007) To adapt written curricula, teachers rely upon thei r design resources. Brown describes teachers’ curriculum adaption work as desirable, akin to the production of jazz music, where “although performers use prerendered scores as foundations to support their  practice, a great deal of the creative work takes p lace during the performance” (Brown, 20 09, p. 1). In the context of classroom teaching and o rchestration, Brown summarizes three teacher resources  in the Design Capacity for Enactment  framework (2002) tha t influence teachers’ adaptations: (1) their subject-matter knowledge, or knowledge about facts and conc epts as related to their discipline, (2) their pedagogical content knowledge, which describes teaching practices specific to th e content in their discipline (Shulman, 1986), and (3) their goals and belie fs, which inform what and how students learn  (see Figure 2). Our study focuses on the relationship be tween teachers’ curriculum adaptations and their de sign resources specific to teaching  (Figure 2, right circle), although Brown notes that physical  curricular resources (Figure 2, left circle) in the written curriculum can also influence teachers’ adaptations. Figure 2 Design Capacity for Enactment (Brown, 2002) As machine learning-the study of computer-based models that automatically improve  over time by viewing examples of desired input-output behavior, to visualize data, construct models with data that can improve over time, and make predictions with new  data (Jordan & Mitchell, 2015) - is a relatively novel discipline and certainly novel in K-12 classrooms, we believe it is essential to study how teachers’ design resources inform what aspects of ML curriculum they adapt and to what extent they adapt it. To study this, we recognize that teacher s’ adaptations likely come in varying forms and grain-sizes. We draw from Leufer and colleagues’ work (20 19) on levels of curriculum adaptation, which suggests tha t teachers’ adaptations may fall into one of five d escriptive categories. Follow implies that the teacher followed the written curriculum with high fidelity. Omit suggests that they removed portions of the curriculum, and create suggests they made new activities not specified in the provided materials. Sort means that the teacher re-arranged activ ities, and modify implies that one or more activities was kept but adapted by the teacher.  Us ing these categories, we will describe the extent to which statistics teachers with varying ML prior knowledge  adapted lesson segments (introduction tonew mater ial, ML investigation, and whole-group discussion) and the design resources they used while making adaptations.

## Methods

This study is one component of a larger artificial intelligence curriculum effort that aims to co-design lessons with practicing teachers and integrate them into middle and high schools by aligning them to core subject s tandards (Xie et al., 2024). Through teacher co-design partnerships, we have created an online repo sitory of open-source, multi-disciplinary lessons that contain necessary planning and activity components that practicing teachers need. Teacher selection Four high school statistics teachers participated in thi s study, recruited  and selected  from the first author’s professional teaching network. One teacher was the first author’s co-design partner and is not featured in this paper because his enactment of FaceID significantly diverged from the others’. His enactment will be the subject of future research. The remaining three teachers were selected based on the following three criteria (1) their prior experience teaching statistics, as first-year statistics t eachers often focus on mastering their own content knowledge (Delaney & Lee, 2022), (2) their prior experience teaching with technology, as FaceID’s investigations rely heavily on teachers’ skillful technology integ ration, and (3) their desire to teach ML with real-world applications. ## Data collection

Data collection for this project occurred in three phrases. Prior to teaching FaceID, the first author conducted interviews with each teacher about their ML prior k nowledge, teaching goals, teaching practice, and student composition.  The first author gave each teacher an orientation to FaceID and answered any questions. Then, the first author took field notes and video-recorded each teacher’s enactment of FaceID. Twelve lessons were recorded: five from Paxton, four from Westley, and three from Nolan, who c ondensed the five FaceID lessons due to time constraints. The first author took descriptive notes that focuse d on teachers’ adaptations at the level of lesson segments. Post-enactment, each teacher completed a reflection document about their experience teaching FaceID and descriptions of their adaptations. Each teacher  was interviewed by the first author again to discu ss their enactments and adaptations, with the goal of surfacing teacher rationales for each adaptation.

## Data analysis

Both authors analyzed each teacher enactment of FaceID with an iterative process. First, we identified all teacher adaptations using Leufer et al.’s adaptation framework (2019). We coded teachers’ adaptations by the three lesson portions: introduction tonew material, hands-on widget investigations, and whole-group discussion and closure, using the Leufer codes follow, omit, create, sort, and modify. In some instances, teachers modified FaceID more than once in a lesson portion. For instance, they a dapted the launch of widget investigations to inclu de livedemonstrations and they modified the activity by providing student-facing scaffolds. We noted instances where more than one modification occurred (see Delaney, 2 024); however, the number of individual teacher modifications is not the focus of this study.

Next, we identified teaching-oriented design resources from the Design Capacity for Enactment framework (Brown, 2002) that teachers used to adapt  the curriculum. We coded each adaptation with one of the following five design resources:  (1) teachers’ subject-matter knowledge of ML, (2) teachers’ subject-matter knowledge of statistics, (3) teachers’ pedagogical content knowledge of statistics  (PCK), including how to teach statistics with digital technologies (TPCK), (4) teachers’ knowledge of students, or (5) teachers’ goals, which we collected during their initial interview. Since codes 1-4 are based on teacher knowledge and code 5 is base d on teachers’ goals, we applied in some cases both a knowledge and a goal code, if evidence from our data suggested that an adaptation was motivated by knowledge and aligned with a goal stated by the teacher. We used teachers’ reflection logs, where they gave rationales for eac h adaptation, and teachers’ final interviews, where  we asked them to describe their unique adaptations in greater detail, to apply codes to the adaptations. Since teachers likely used multiple forms of knowledge to adapt FaceID, we coded the most pertinent knowledge type and te acher goal. Both authors coded the data during five research sessions and discussed codes until agreement was reached. Once the data were coded, the first author wrote a series of analytic memos  (Creswell, 2014) for each lesson enactment describing patterns in teachers’ adaptation codes and rationales. The analytic memos were then used as the basis for  three analytic cases, one for each teacher, describing patterns in their FaceID enactments. The analytic cases provide the basis for our compar ative case analysis, where we describe differences between teachers’ adaptations, design resources, and rationales.

Several patterns emerged from the data. First, teac hers’ ML prior knowledge seemed to influence how mu ch teachers were willing to adapt the introduction tonew material segments, which contained activities w here new ML concepts were introduced. Nolan, who was both un der time constrains to complete FaceID and whose prior knowledge afforded him a sense of which ideas were most important to ML, omitted portions of the curri culum liberally. Westley and Paxton, whose ML knowledge was much less developed, did not omit hardly anything from FaceID, but adapted it often.

Second, we observed that teachers adapted the investigative portions of the lessons in similar ways. These activities (as written) entailed students working with a partner to create statistical ML models of facial recognition, albeit with datasets of their  facial measurements that were small enough for stud ents to understand. All three teachers modified the investigations to include lau nches (Jackson et al., 2013), where they made the g oals of the investigation explicit and demonstrated how to use FaceID’s widgets to create statistical models before tasking students to build them on their own. Westley sugges ted that this practice helped his students focus th eir efforts without becoming overwhelmed by the open-ended nature of the investigations: Westley: “It was quite a long process…making sure, just poin ting out to them which pieces of information [in the widget] are important, how to use the pieces of information, I thought, would be clearer than any instruction. I mean, when I was  trying to put the les son together, I literally spent 35 minutes of just, okay, what about this sit uation? What about these variables? …and it was just, I don’t want to have 5 pages of covering every possible way to model which variables could be important in this. Wher eas it took…2-3 minutes of me talking about it and showing them for it to be clear.” (post-enactment interview, pg. 13) Furthermore, Nolan and Paxton routinely paused widg et investigations if they observed that multiple pa irs of students were confused, and addressed questions at the front of the room before releasing students to continue to refine their ML models on their own.

Finally, the data show that each teacher approached whole-group discussion differently, as evidenced by the range of their adaptations. Paxton preferred to  follow whole-group discussions as the written curriculum intended, and often stuck to the discussion  questions that we gave in the written materials. He  created time for students to get started on their homework at the end of class by ending whole-group discussions early. Yet, Paxton always gave time for students to express their curiosities that emerged from widget investigations (e.g., “Can face recognition be fooled by twins or doppelgangers?”), and was transparent with students when he did not know the answer to their questions. Westley modified most wh ole-group discussions by scaffolding them for stu dents’ participation. He told students to discuss and write down answers with a partner to his questions first, then called on students to read what they had written. His approach solicited a high volume of answers from students, which in turn, enabled more students to share their models and draw out features of their data that influenced their results. Nolan omitted some whole-group discussions for lack of time, but used his own ML knowledge to enhance other discussions. For instance, in the fourth lesson,  he introduced students to the ML concept of learning rate  (a hyperparameter in modeling that influences how rapi dly the system “learns” or improves), and asked the m to critically consider why attending to the learning r ate matters. By modifying discussion s and creating new opportunities to deepen discussion, Nolan supported  his students in concretely connecting their statis tical knowledge tonew ML concepts.

Again, several patterns emerged from the data. We n oticed that the less ML prior knowledge teachers ha d, the more they relied on their knowledge of students to enact FaceID. Westley and Paxton tended to modify the curriculum in ways that supported their students’ n eeds, whereas Nolan modified the curriculum to emph asize aspects of ML that he believed students would be interested in. Paxton’s approach helped to shift the focus away from the teacher as a source of expertise (he told students on the first day, “I’m not a machine learning expert, so we’re going to be learning this together,”) and toward students as experts of the technologies that currently impact their lives. He reflected: Paxton: “Facial recognition is accessible to all of them…they use it every day, every hour, every ten minutes. So just having that common thing that they know about and how to use. Because with stats, it is easy to do those real-world examples, right? It’s  like the word problems. But like, how often do they actually see that, rather than just to read it in a world proble m? (postenactment interview, pg. 3) Westley, likewise, explained that he wanted his students to become curious about technologies that use statistics. He drew from both his knowledge of students and kno wledge of orchestrating statistics discussions to a sk discussion questions that would increase students’ motivation, such as, what does it mean to look simi lar to someone else, in the eyes of a machine?: Westley: “Well you know, there were kids who were like, the computer thinks I look more like the kid that I look nothing like, than the one I do look like! Than the one I thought I looked like. And that’s the type of thing that I wanted, how do you make a kid ask “why?” Like, why be interested in why [a ML technology] works?” (post-enactment interview, pg. 14) Second, the influence of teachers’ goals as design resources seem to increase with teachers’ prior kno wledge of ML. Nolan, for instance, cited his goals significantly more than Paxton as a rationale for adapting the curriculum. Each teacher stated roughly the same number of goals for their students in the opening interview (Nolan gave six goals, Westley gave five, and Paxton gave four). It is possible that teachers’ prior knowledge of ML enabled them to adapt and connect lessons more flexibly back to their goals.

## Discussion and conclusion

Our results illustrate the relationship between thr ee teachers’ ML prior knowledge and their ability t o enact an interdisciplinary ML curriculum (Brown, 2009). Teac hers with low prior ML knowledge adapted the curric ulum differently, and in fewer instances,  but the frequency of their adaptations was still hi gher than we anticipated (Leufer, 2019). Westley, for instance, crafted launches for his widget investigations that enabled students to work productively, with curiosity, and without becoming distracted by the sheer number of modeling permutations that exist in machine learning. This finding suggests th at teachers with varying backgrounds and knowledges  may be able to use alternative forms of their expertise if they wish to introduce machine learning to students. Interestingly, no teacher adapted the hands-on investigation activities of FaceID to the extent that they changed their goals. This could be because FaceID was designed and prepared with written materials that teachers were accustomed to using in their own planning and instruction, such as educative curricula (Davis et al., 2017) and assessments (Druga et al., 2022). However, the notion that teachers should be supported to adapt ML curricula raises an important tension between teaching as age ntic design work and adapting the curriculum to the  extent that it no longer resembles its original form or goals. In the context of machine learning and statistics, for instance, a teacher could rely so heavily on statistics conce pts that they lose important elements of machine learning, such as enabling students to grapple with unstructured d ata sets or designing decision rules that are meant  to be processed by a computer. In this case, students wou ld not be doing machine learning. Although it did n ot occur in this study, teachers with low prior ML knowledge like Paxton could benefit from ML professional learning (in addition to high-quality curricular materials) in order to build teaching practices that are specific to ML. Collecting data during field observations permitted  us to view instances where enacting FaceID could have imposed design burdens on teachers. Whole-group discussions with stochastic, student-generated facial data were difficult to facilitate because they required students to reason about uncertainty (Watkins & Man z, 2022). Investigations in FaceID were fundamentally different than most statistics tasks because they were centered upon building correct ML modeling ideas and practices ra ther than methodological precision in determining a  correct statistical solution. This observation further supp orts the need for a combination of teacher professi onal learning and high-quality ML curricula if K-12 classrooms are to teach about machine learning i n the future. A key challenge to this recommendation is that teaching expertise for ML in K-12 has not yet been defined. Future work should seek to illustrate a pedagogical vision for ML teaching practice in K-12 in addition to the growing number of ML curricula that are being produced and deployed by the research community (e.g., Kaspersen, 2022).

In conclusion, this study offers insights into how teachers adapt a machine learning curriculum to sui t their expertise, goals, and students. This work enabled us to see challenges associated with teaching ML and how teachers used their design resources to overcome them. Future work should consider designing ML curricula with features that make them easy to integrate into K-12 classrooms while enabling teachers  with opportunities to flexibly and reasonably transform curricular materials in ways that support good teaching.
