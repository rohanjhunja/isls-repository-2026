# Implementing Knowledge Building in a Singapore Primary School: Multidisciplinary Practitioners’ Reflection

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines the implementation of Knowledge Building (KB) in a primary school in Singapore, focusing on practitioner reflections of their classroom practices and ways in which they shifted their practice to include higher level of student’s voice and agency by focusing on translation of KB principles into practice. The group of teachers formed a grounds-up interest-based community in the school to explore KB. They were cognizant of their school strategic thrusts of differentiated instruction and emerging 21st Century Competencies and saw the potential of KB in redesigning and transforming their pedagogical repertoire to achieve the strategic thrust. Through the reflections of these trailblazers in the school, we traced how they translate KB principles into practical knowledge and how they saw knowledge building practice brings about agentic teaching and learning across subjects, and with different learner profiles.

## Introduction

Knowledge Building propounds that  authentic, creative knowledge work can take place i n classrooms starting with the youngest students and is grounded by twelve p rinciples as its  foundation (Scardamalia, 20 02). Scardamalia and Bereiter (20 14) explained that Knowledge Building (KB) principles focused on encouraging student-centered inquiry promotes a collaborative and knowl edge-driven learning environment. Many practitioners are drawn to KB pedagogy for its  potential in expanding their pedagogical repertoire as well as to impact students' autonomous learning abilities and collect ive responsibility by continuously expanding the boundaries of collective knowledge (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014; Teo, Tan & Chan, 2021). KB pedagogy was introduced in Singapore since the beginning of the 21st century (e.g. Tan, Hung & So, 2005; Ow & Bielaczyc, 2008, Tan & Seah, 2011) and practitioners are beginning to provide valuable insights into how they have put KB principles into action through various teaching des igns (e.g. Chan & Lee, 2017; Xuan, Lee, Sedik & Ling, 2017; Teo, Arumugam, Loh & Ng, 2018).

## Methods

This practitioner paper compiles the reflection  of four senior teachers  in Science, English, Mathematic s and Mother Tongue Languages Department. Each reflection traced the teachers’ effort s in integrating knowledgebuilding principles into their daily teaching practices across diverse subject areas. The narrative explained how they make use of KB principles to guide lesson design and th eir pedagogical moves, and how they rationalized with the principles to create a more open inquiry and to give that little bit more agency to students each time. The reflections also painted the challenges in existing practice that impeded the adoption of innovative pedagogy such as KB, and how these challenges were considered as part of the design effort of curriculum and pedagogy. Each reflection also drew out the impact on students’ learning. Reflection on KB and science class KB science lessons started with Medium-Readiness (1) Grade 5 students (10-year-old). Right from the onset, students were engaged in whole-class discussion on the purpose of KB, its processes, and the desired learning outcomes. Ground rules for knowledge building were set, e.g. the need to respect each other’s ideas, acknowledge that everyone’s idea is promising and comment on th eir peer’s idea respectfully and this created a saf e platform to learn and reinforce the culture of building know ledge. KB provides the space and time for students to take charge of their inquiry and to build knowledge from their curiosity. Science teachers are always looking for ways to provide students with the oppor tunity to make sense of what they have learned and apply these concepts tonew contexts. In this case,  KB principles of (i) Providing students with real ideas and authentic problems, (i i) Idea Diversity, (iii) Improvable Ideas and (iv) Rise Above (see Table 1) were layered into the concept cartoon activities in science lessons. Concept cartoons is a visual tool that presents a scenario which typically related to  a scientific phenomenon with characters presenting  different point of views or ideas about a specific topic. They are commonly used in Singapore primary school scien ce. However, these lessons derived from concept cartoon tend to lack authentic exploration of the issue at hand as students only get to choose the options provided and argue their way through.  In addition, KB cloud  activities (Figure 1 and 2) were devised to refocus concept cartoon into an exploratory activity. SBC requires students to respond to a teacher examiner’s thematic questions based on a graphic stimulus. The conversation consists of three main questions asking students to share their opinions and experiences centered around a specific theme. Existing strategy: The Opinion – Reason – Explanation – Opinion framework (OREO) strategy for SBC tasks provided a sufficient platform for training c onversational skills in students. However, when ORE O was adopted, students’ responses seemed repetitive and curtailed due to the initial requirement of predict ing the possible main questions for the SBC. There was a ne ed to explore possible strategies that could expand  the repertoire of responses available to students witho ut the teacher spoon-feeding tons of b ackground information students could utilize. Furthermore, students usually felt anxious when predicting the main questions for the SBC and they became more anxious when their predicted q uestions were not asked by the teacher during the conversation. When that happened, students ended up  having difficulty remaining effectively engaged in  the conversation. Even when predicted questions were as ked, students began to feel limited by the OREO str ucture. They felt that responses were often a force-fit and variant perspectives were not always possible within OREO. ## Conclusion

Collaborating with university-school networks, this group of teachers have create d momentum for KB  in their school through their relentless sharing of new knowledge and experience gained from their own collective inquiry. Through the reflections, KB is seen to influence their teaching philosophy, focus their practice on their students and provided them space to design engaging lessons.  They admitted the challenges of KB for teachers such as scaffolding with questions and “adapting lessons on the fly”. They found the community-based approach in their school encourages experimentation and nurtures a KB culture among both teachers and students. In their words, “KB has been rewarding, boosting student confidence and transforming us into deep thinkers who thrive in collaborative settings”. KB has opened pathways to experiment and collabor ate, heightened student agency. The pedagogical shift promises to empower students and redefine approaches to language and broader education.
