# Design Patterns for Online Collaborative Learning in Higher Education: A Survey of ijCSCL Studies

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) focuses on designing tools and strategies to enhance collaborative learning. However, design knowledge within CSCL is often embedded in empirical studies without explicit emphasis, limiting its systematic documentation and dissemination. We analyzed literature from the International Journal of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (ijCSCL) and identified five design patterns for online collaborative learning in higher education, highlighting components such as learning outcomes, task structures, technology affordances, and collaborative processes. This work seeks to elevate design knowledge in CSCL, encouraging the community to refine and share effective designs.

## Introduction

CSCL is dedicated to advancing collaborative learning through sophisticated designs. To advance the field further, it is essential to have a comprehensive understandi ng of existing CSCL designs. Such an understanding enables researchers and practitioners to build on proven st rategies, identify gaps, and foster the development  of new designs to address emerging educational needs.  However, design knowledge within CSCL is often embe dded within empirical studies, and is rarely  explicitly recognized or disseminated. Such lack of emphasis  limits the systematic documentation and sharing of effective designs, which are crucial for the advancement of CSCL as a field. To fill this gap, we investigate design patterns in CSCL by surveying recent literature published in ijCSCL, the flagship journal of the field, focusing specifically on online collaborative learning in higher education due to its strong presence in ijCSCL. This survey aims to identify and distill design patterns that are reflected in recent CSCL projects to elevate the status of design knowledge within CS CL. Research questions are: (a) What design patterns have been developed in CSCL  field to support online collaborative learning in higher education? (b) Within these design patterns, what learning outcomes are targeted, what learning contexts are addressed, how are tasks organized, and how are technologies integrated to facilitate learners’ collaborative learning?

## Methods

We conducted a survey of ijCSCL literature from 2006 onward, screening 437 articles  and selecting 23 based on criteria including “related to task design,” “higher education,” and “fully online collaboration.” Using a coding scheme with four components —targeted learning outcomes, learning contexts, task  sequences, and technology affordances—we identified design patterns in CSCL.  Two researchers contributed to this analysis, resolving discrepancies through discussion until consensus wa s reached.  Tasks were classified as Directed (D), Collaborative Explorative (CE), Collaborative Productive (CP), or Collaborative Reflective (CR) (Li et al., 2022). We categorized design patterns based on the focal learning outcome and further defined each design pattern based on how tasks are sequenced and technologically supported.

## Findings

Five distinct design patterns across three categories were identified from the analysis. Below, we report each design pattern by describing its representative learning outcomes, context, task sequence, and technologies. CE-oriented design patterns CE-oriented design patterns focus on CE tasks, aiming for deep knowledge exploration and co-construction.

## Design pattern 1: Structured asynchronous discussion

Targeted learning outcomes: Active engagement; motivation to participate; enhanced discussion quality

## Learning context: Asynchronous settings, small groups (2-5 people)

Task sequence:  “CE-CP.” Learners use an artifact (e.g., an argumentation dia gram) as a scaffold to guide structured online discussions, facilitating idea ex ploration (CE). They then synthesize and integrate arguments into the artifact (CP).

Technology Affordance: Representational tools such as argumentation diagrams (for CE or CP). Example: In Nussbaum et al. (2007), students were tasked to  respond to peers' posts using argumentation vee diagrams (AVDs) to explore discussion question (CE), and then created a joint AVD and  a summary note (CP).

## Design pattern 2: Diverse perspectives for knowledge co-construction

Targeted learning outcomes: Conflict resolution; consensus building; argumentation; critical thinking Learning context: Asynchronous/synchronous settings, small groups (2-5 people)/large groups (6-10 people) Task sequence:  “D-CR-CE.” L earners buil d a common knowledge base (D), critically evaluate pe ers' contributions (CR),  and then discuss to co-construct knowledge (CE), responding to diverse and conflicting views.

Technology Affordance: Group awareness tools (for CR); collaborative scripts (for CR) Example: In Weinberger et al. (2013), learners took on roles as a case analyst and two critics, with critics providing feedback (CR) and learners addressing conflicting views, leading to further in-depth exploration (CE). CP-oriented design patterns Central to CP-oriented design patterns is the CP task, which emphasizes guiding students to co-create high-quality artifacts while fostering meaningful learning. One design pattern emerged from our coding: Design pattern 3: Collaborative artifact construction Targeted learning outcomes: Knowledge acquisition; ability to co-construct high-quality artifacts Learning context: Synchronous/asynchronous settings, small groups (2-5 people) Task sequence:  “D-CE-CP.” In this design pattern, D provides foundational kno wledge, CE enables deep exploration and collaboration, and CP requires group members to collaboratively create an artifact. Technology Affordance: Collaborative scripts (CE, CP); group awareness tools (CE, CP); simulation (CE, CP) Example: In Engelmann and Hesse  (2010), students read task-related information and group knowledge bases (D), had explorative discussions to integrate information (C E), and finally  negotiated their solutions (CP).

CR-oriented design patterns CR-oriented patterns center on CR tasks, emphasizing group-level metacognitive and regulative behaviors. Design pattern 4: Collaborative reflection for enhanced collaboration quality Targeted learning outcomes: Enhanced ability to engage in deep shared meaning-making; reflective skills

## Learning context: Synchronous settings, small groups (2-5 people)

Task sequence: “CE-CR.” Learners first engage in collaborative exploratory discussions (CE) and then reflect on the discussion process (CR), evaluating strengths, weaknesses, and potential improvements. Technology Affordance: Reflective scripts (CR); online discussion environments with transcript archiving (CR) Example: In Aldemir et al.  (2022), learners discussed challenging topics (CE), then collectively assessed their collaboration and developed a strategy to address weaknesses in future discussions (CR). Design pattern 5: Reflective refinement based on collaborative assessment Targeted learning outcomes: Production of higher quality work; ability to learn and revise based on feedback Learning context: Synchronous/asynchronous settings, small groups (2-5 people) Task sequence: Following a “CR-CR” task sequence,  this pattern includes peer or group assessment (CR) followed by learners critically reflecting and revising their work based on feedback (CR). Technology Affordance: Online work-sharing platforms for work sharing and peer review (CR) Example: In Zhang et al. (2021)’s design, learners were tasked with providing feedback on another group’s product (CR) and then revising their own group product (CR).

## Discussion and conclusions

To explore the landscape of design knowledge in the field of CSCL, we reviewed the flagship journal of the field, ijCSCL, with a focus on CSCL design  patterns that are less attended to. From this review, we identified and summarized three design categories  based on the focal learning outcome  and five design patterns  researchers generated to support these outcomes. These patterns provide valuable guidance for researc hers, designers, and educators interested in supporting collaborative learning. Building on this preliminary work, f uture research should further validate and refine these patterns in broader educational contexts.
