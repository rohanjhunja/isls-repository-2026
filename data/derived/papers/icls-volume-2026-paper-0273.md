# Facing Uncertainty and Ambiguity: Improvisation as a Mechanism for Problem- solving in Engineering

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Engineers and those learning to engineer engage with problem-solving as a disciplinary practice, including its uncertainties and ambiguities. However, engineering students rarely have opportunities to participate in open-ended and ill-structured, socially-oriented, problem solving. If students were afforded such opportunities, they could develop practical solutions where theoretical knowledge alone may fall short to meet the needs and wants of communities. Here, improvisation becomes a feature of the processes and practices for generating practical solutions. Although the notion of engineers improvising is relatively new, improvisation has been key for practicing open-ended problem solving in art disciplines. In the arts, improvisation is a competence that can be learned and improved through teamwork, collaboration, knowledge of the discipline and creativity, all of which are as important to engineering learning. Thus, this paper argues that improvisation in engineering learning affords for creative problem solving.

Problem solving is a broad umbrella term about the practices different disciplines engage in when developing solutions (Akinci-Ceylan et al., 2021). Although engineers participate in a variety of activities and practices as part of their work, the identity of engineering as a discipline has been closely tied to design and problem-solving, where engineers are positioned as those who develop solutions and technologies for people and communities (Downey, 2005; Fink, 2002; Sheppard et al., 2006). However, since problem-solving manifests in different forms across a variety of human activities, the concept becomes difficult to operationalize within specialized domains of expertise, particularly in technical disciplines.

Everyone engages in problem-solving but engineers engage in such practice following particular heuristics and with specific goals. Defining engineering solely as a discipline that solves problems risks oversimplifying the nature of the discipline while limiting the opportunities for students to develop a deeper understanding of what engineering entails and what engineers actually do in their work. In chemical engineering, problem solving may focus on identifying errors in a given chemical process design and making appropriate suggestions for design improvements. Whereas, in civil and environmental engineering,  problem-solving may focus on structural design and environmental factors of structural stability. We do not contend that engineers solve problems but that they engage in particular ways of doing, thinking and knowing in the process of developing solutions. Moreover, we argue that some of the epistemologies and practices around problem solving have been  historically unrecognized and understudied in engineering education and the discipline at large. For instance, engineering problem solving requires adaptations to unforeseeable situations, characterized by incomplete information and changing demands (Jona ssen & Hung, 2008), all features of improvising. These adaptations sometimes take the form of cognitive, linguistic and cultural variations to meet the needs of the activity at hand. Often, problem solving and improvising go hand in hand and may include elements such as sensibility check —a “conscious or subconscious review of potential solutions to assess whether the solution is reasonable in context” (Miskioğlu et al., 2023, p. 426) — and counterchecking, a critical thinking process to support manual calculations by checking expected outputs with manual outputs with the goal of having these outputs converge (Osman et al., 2020). In the particular case of sensibility check, engineers are tasked with making value-laden and situated judgements in the face of u ncertainty – a form of improvisation. While improvisations call for reactions in real time, in rapidly changing and demanding engineering contexts, from AI work to aeronautic engineering, engineers make educated improvisation moves to ensure that potential  solutions remain sensitive to the context and technically and scientifically sound. These elements of problem solving in engineering can provide a better picture about what real-world disciplinary learning encompasses and what role improvising plays.

As an integral practice and method to problem solving, improvising takes different forms in various areas of human activity. In fact, some scholars have described improvisation in expansive ways with direct applications to problem-solving across disciplines, from teaching and learning to liberal arts (Sandel, 2020). For example, in music and performing arts, improvisation is conceptualized as a form of spontaneous problem solving that trains 1852 the mind to think flexibly under constraints, tolerate ambiguity and uncertainty while making quick creative decisions and exploring multiple possible alternatives (Mackey, 2008). For instance, Vurkac (2017) uses the example of the clave direction in Latin American music to describe elements of improvisation, linking this rhythmic artistic principle with aspects of problem-solving and creativity. Similarly, engineers improvise when they must respond to unexpected constraints that cannot be foreseen and plan ned for in advance (Hamzeh et al., 2018). For instance, engineers improvise in design as they respond to changing conditions (e.g., demands for a new material to meet specifications of a product, expectations from community members who are directly impacted by proposed solutions and the emergence of new information). Art-making and developing solutions to problems in engineering design share similar cognitive processes, especially when considering dimensions of design associated with working under constraints, engaging in creative thinking, proposing innovative approaches and techniques, and collaboration with others. Gerber (2007) positions improvisation as crucial for helping designers build the competencies and perspective necessary for creative collaboration, fostering innovation, supporting spontaneity, learning through error, and presenting ideas which are vital for divergent thinking in designers. Similarly, Swenson et al. (2025) mentioned that situating intuition helps with sound engineering judgement to open-ended engineering problems, that involves a similar process to improvisation. Given that design is a key component of engineering, engagement in arts has the potential to enhance engineering problem solving competencies connected to improvising.

Despite the long history of improvisation in the development of engineering solutions and technologies, it is often overlooked as a core practice of engineers that ought to be taught in higher education. Drawing on Schön (1992)’s idea that professionals including engineers rely on improvisation learned in practice rather than the formulas learned in school, this paper investigates improvisation as a mechanism for problem-solving in engineering. In particular, we seek to answer the following research questio ns: (1) How can improvisation frameworks in performing arts help us conceptualize learning opportunities in engineering problem solving? (2) In what ways can improvisation be understood as an engineering learning practice in formal and informal settings? Improvisation across disciplines Improvisation is the use of one's spontaneous cognitive, cultural and linguistic resources for formulating a resolution for an unexpected situation with the resources at hand (Hamzeh et al., 2012). Beyond being a procedural decision making task, improvisation can be understood as a process of making sense of situations (Hamzeh et al., 2019). Hamzeh and colleagues (2012) use the word “bricolage” to refer to the act of improvising, which arises when people have to quickly think of ideas and solutions without time to optimize. Jazz theory influenced earlier definitions of improvisation where the term was used to explain how musicians played music on the spot (Hamzeh et al., 2018), meaning solving problems in real-time (Pulford & Falkenberg, 2016). Pulford and Falkenberg (2016) consider the improvisation practices of artists on the stage or “improv” - to solve problems or come up with new music on the spot – a necessary skill for engineers. Despite engineering disciplines having to deal with uncertainties all the time, Hamzeh et al. (2012) found that in construction engineering, improvisation is mostly being practiced in managerial positions, such as when engineers in leadership roles have to deal with a situation where there is a delay in the imported material. Hamzeh et al., (2018) found that experience and competencies like timely good communication in the team and risk taking ability played an important role in an improviser's chances of sound improvisation, thus a deep understanding of the engineering process i s required for improvisation. This is similar to Pulford and Falkenberg (2016)’s notion of improvisation as a method and framework used to scaffold a successful unscripted performance. Uncertainty and ambiguity are two important conditions for improvisation to take place—uncertainty in terms of knowing how a set decision will impact the result beforehand and ambiguity in terms of sifting through the various possibilities to make a decision. Offloading versus improvising There are major differences between offloading and improvising in the nature of what people do and its intent. Cognitive offloading is “the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task so as to reduce cognitive load” (Risko & Gilbert, 2016, p. 676). We engage in cognitive offloading when we tilt our head to see a rotated image on our computer or phone to remind us of an impending appointment (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). Similarly, an example of cognitive offloading is presented in the work by Ogata et al. (2020) on system engineers and their processes and behaviors when troubleshooting for network systems. The authors created a virtual network with multiple servers, routers, and terminals. In such a context, a half dozen of s ystem engineers performed everyday troubleshooting activities. The study suggested that system engineers deploy a well-known mechanism of connection confirmation between network devices. These participants appropriately hypothesized the troubled device based on information presented in the assessment tool. They reduced the problem space by logically separating the entirety of the system into sub-systems that fit a particular standard or not in order to 1853 identify the obstacle causes. Systems engineers used what they know of the network system context and their prior experiences with troubleshooting (Ogata et al., 2020). This process of systematically segmenting the problem space and verifying links allowed them to externalize cognitive demands, focusing on more manageable smaller subsystems rather than holding the entire network topology in working memory. How people in arts disciplines are using improvisation as a step for problem solving? Improvisation occurs in most, if not all disciplines and everyday life, ranging from comedy and sports to psychiatry and parenting. For instance, Marvis (1998) identifies synonymous terms for improvisation in other disciplines including biosociation in Bio logy and synthesis in Chemistry. While studying improvisation among jazz musicians, Sawyer (2000) identified two key components of improvisation: etiquette and collective emergence. Etiquette refers to the “collective social knowledge” one acquires through socialization in a community of practice, while collective emergence refers to “an ensemble art form [where] everyone involved in the improvisation is constantly offering new ideas” (Sawyer, 2000, p. 180). Contingent upon improvisation is the social actor undergoing some degree of uncertainty where “none of the actors…knows precisely which way to turn” and which decisions will result in certain outcomes (Mirvis, 1998, p. 587). However, there are mechanisms like rehearsals or practice meant to reduce the degree of uncertainty associated with certain outcomes. Therefore, significantly, improvisation in the performing arts rarely occurs outside the realm of some structure. Often, the improvisational artist has pre-existing structures to rely on and empirical knowledge from past experiences of what does and does not work, slightly narrowing the realm of their possibilities in what they cando. This helps simplify the decision-making process. For example, the jazz musician may rely on clichés— “short melodic lines that can be used at many different points during a solo and that are worked out and refined in the privacy of rehearsal” (Sawyer, 2000, p. 180). Thus, improvisation rarely occurs without the guidance of disciplinary knowledge and structure as well as the empirical knowledge of what components can be leveraged to produce the desired result(s).

How engineering is using improvisation for problem solving? In the context of engineering practice, improvisation likely functions in a similar manner to the arts —alleviating uncertainty—but occurs differently. The engineering practitioners may be working through novel processes like the synthesis of a chemical product using disciplinary knowledge like materials science characterizations, or reworking equations to fit to a specific context —a practice that often contributes to the development of disciplinary and organizational heuristics (Barner et al., 2021). Moreover, when considering the modes of improvisation outlined by Sawyer (2000), we may find engineering practice to contain clear examples of etiquette in terms of socialized norms (e.g., multimodal communication with stakeholders) and structures (e.g., scienti fic and mathematical theories as foundations to modeling a process or system). Yet, there are fewer clear examples of collective emergence outside of the context of engineering design where flexible collaborative methods (Fricke,

1996) like brainstorming are encouraged.

Vurkac (2017) argues that problem solving is different from solving a problem. Solving a problem is about s olving a close-ended textbook problem where it is known what to do when encountering a situation, whereas problem solving is about engaging in finding resolutions to real world situations that tend to be open-ended and ill-structured (See example Jonassen et al., 2006). Problem solving requires improvisation and creativity (Vurkac, 2017).  Drawing on ideas of improvisation from drama and theater, where  the improvised ensemble performance is known as “improv” or “impro” and treated it as a competency that can be learned and explicitly practiced by a group of performers to quickly self-organize and collaborate toward a shared product (Pulford & Falkenberg, 2016). In this way, improvisation can be formulaic (procedural) and/or novel (inventive/innovative).

In engineering, the understanding of improvisation is limited. But even if the scholarship is tangentially connected to improvisation or related to the actions that have characteristics of “improv” in the engineering scholarship, this research labels improvisation with different language, from “situating intuition” to “engineering judgement” or “principle improvisation.” In navigating engineering tasks, intuition emerged as an idea that is initially subconscious (e.g., a gut feeling or feeling of rightness)  and based on the experience Miskioglu et al. (2023). Thus, it is about leveraging one’s lived realities and expertise to access the present situation or predict a future outcome. In other words, situating intuition, or what we commonly known as improvising, is a crucial first step in problem solving (Miskioglu et al., 2023). Civil and environmental engineers rely on improvisation for making spontaneous and rational decisions in construction planning to keep projects moving (Hamzeh, Faek, & Alhussein, 2018). For instance, Alhussein et al. (2022) states that even after meticulously planning and control in construction to enhance the performance of construction projects and mitigate problems related to uncertainty, some unexpected uncertainties and shortcomings of planning are inevitable, in such case, planners and decision 1854 makers are advised to consider improvisational capabilities, which can record, continuously update, and provide corresponding classification to be used for future assignments. The authors further mentions that improvisational capabilities should be made by considering the requirements of trades in a construction project to improve the rising improvisational performance at the level of that project (Alhussein et al., 2022). This means improvisation in engineering is spontaneous yet rational decision-making under uncertainty, especially when plans or procedures fail or when no predefined method fits the situation, which is similar to the idea of intuition-acting while thinking when you adjust to the moment simultaneously (Schön, 1983). In a study about how undergraduate engineering students solve open-ended modeling problems (OEMPs), where the students were provided with some parameters and resources such that the OEMPs can be solved using engineering knowledge. The results found four engineering modeling judgement competencies which were “making assumptions; assessing the reasonableness of assumptions, outputs, and models; deciding when to override mathematically calculated answers; and deciding when and how to use technology tools.” (Swenson et al., 2025, P. 1 of 31). Swenson et al. (2025) mentions that to cultivate engineering judgement it is important to understand the genesis of that judgement. Improvisation to be work, sound engineering judgement is necessary.  Similarly, when sustainable activities like environmental protection, energy efficiency, and optimized mobility became the priorities in urban environment development, Zavadskas et al. (2018) argues that from the mathematical viewpoint, it became a multi-criteria group decision-making problem. And in order to solve these kind of problems, the authors highlighted Multiple-criteria decision-making (MCDM) techniques such as Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), fuzzy sets and Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Sol ution (TOPSIS) used by the civil engineers particularly in construction sector because of the technique’s capacity to address the problems with diverging goals, which allowed engineers to assess design and technological alternatives with regard to sustainability, vulnerability and other important aspects (Zavadskas et al., 2018). Drawing on these ideas, improvisation in engineering can be understood as adaptive application of situated intuition and engineering judgement in real-time. All these are interpreted as a skill that requires experience or practice, good knowledge of the work, time concerns when the proven theories fail to get the job done.

The genesis of thinking about improvisation in engineering learning Our team started thinking about improvising as a result of  a larger research project where we conducted an integrative literature review to understand how engineering practices are operationalized and defined in engineering  ((Pérez et al., under review).  After conducting this review and coding over 250+ engineering practices, such as problem-solving, design, and decision-making, we created a sorting activity for engineering education researchers and practitioners to participate in. The researchers were provided a list of approximately 50 engineering practices and asked to sort these practices into groups based on their own ideas of how the practices may relate to one another. From the sorted categories, a visual network analysis was conducted, which showed improvising to be the most salient and connected engineering practice among the participants. However, we noticed there were few references in the literature about improvising but it was the interpretations of engineering education researchers and practitioners that highlighted this important practice that engine ers engage in. Therefore, we decided to further explore this idea by drawing on Schon (1983)’s conception of thinking in action using intuition. We also looked at other terminologies used to describe improvisation activities in engineering to identify how the scholarship frames improvising (e.g., As situating intuition in decision making and decision making in open-ended ill-structured problems).

Engineering is rarely thought of as a discipline where people improvise. Through this work, we argue that improvising is a core feature of engineering learning and practice – e.g., improvising as a form of problem solving. As practice is mostly associated with musicians and artists, however we contend that those learning to  engineer can learn from improvising and engineers actually engage in improvising in their work. This paper highlights the advantages of engaging in improvisation in-and out-of enginee ring with direct implications for engineering pedagogy. The opportunities for using improvisation in the engineering classroom, outside of a theatrical context, in the form of improv in engineering problem-solving are situating intuition and judgement for open-ended engineering problems. We started this work by drawing on improvisation frameworks from musicology and performance studies, this work identifies and conceptualizes opportunities to learn through improvisation in engineering in formal and informal learning environments. Specifically, we provide examples in various learning environments – the classroom, community and in the workplace – where engineering students engage in the practice of improvisation.
