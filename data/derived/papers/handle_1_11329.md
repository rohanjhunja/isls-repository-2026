# Epistemic Agency Across Disciplines: Shared Commitments and Challenges in Science and History Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Student-driven inquiry has long represented a central goal in both history and science education, and the two subject areas despite substantive differences, conceptualize inquiry in similar ways. Still, classrooms abound with missed opportunities. We argue that missed opportunities for inquiry reflect a deeper challenge for teachers and teacher educators related to how they conceptualize students’ epistemic agency. In this unique conceptual paper, we look across our fields of history and science education research to better understand how each field has considered students’ epistemic agency. We look across fields to identify common features and points of convergence. We conclude with challenges and recommendations for teacher educators and teachers.

## Introduction

Student-driven inquiry has long represented a central goal in both history and science education, as reflected in empirical research (Bain, 2005; Cuevas et al., 2005), conceptual arguments (Duschl, 2008; Saye, 2017), and national standards (C3 Framework for Social Studies, 2013; Next Generation  Science Standards, Achieve, 2013). Despite substantive differences between the two sub ject areas, they conceptualize inquiry in similar ways. For example, in both fields, inquiry requires students to: (a) ask questions that have meaning for their lives; (b) d esign investigations into real life phenomena or is sues; (c) a nalyze and interpret data; (d) m ake claims from evidence; (e) e ngage in argumentation; (f) (sometimes) draw from community and cultural knowledges in addition to disciplinary concepts, to ols, and habits; (g) navigate inevitable uncertainty that arises during complex work and/or with open issues  (National Research Council, 2012). Despite these universal calls and shared features of inquiry, history and science classrooms often miss opportuni ties for students to engage in inquiry (Reisman, 2015; Thompson et al., 2019).

We argue that missed opportunities for inquiry reflect a deeper c hallenge for teachers and teacher educators related to how they conceptualize  students’ epistemic agency: How can teachers reframe classrooms as sites of inquiry in which students’ ideas and ex periences drive the ongoing work of the community? To help teachers, and our fields of history and science education, reimagine classrooms as sites of inquiry, w e propose that classrooms must become places where students take u p epistemic agency  or responsibility for shaping the knowledge and knowledge-building practices of a classroom community (Stroupe, 2014). As students assume epistemic agency, they engage in more authentic disciplinary inquiry (Bricker & Bell, 2016). In this unique conceptual paper, we look across our fields of history and science educa tion research to better understand how each field has considered students’ epistemic agency. We ask:

• How does the field of history education think about students’ epistemic agency in the context of

inquiry-driven student discourse?

• How does the field of science education think about students’ epistemic agency in the context of

inquiry-driven student discourse?

• When looking across fields, what are shared features of epistemic agency, teachers’ pedagogy, and

students’ discourse? Where might our fields diverge?

We first explain our reasons for focusing on how epistemic agency emerges in classroom discourse, noting that episodes of classroom discourse afford students unique opportunities to express epistemic agency. We then look across fields to see common features and where our disciplinary areas might further converge. We conclude with challenges and recommendations for teacher educators and teachers. Epistemic agency and classroom discourse In reviewing how the fields of history and science education consider students’ epistemic agency, we n arrow our focus to the literature on classroom discourse for three primary reasons. Classroom discourse affords students with opportunities to: (a) voice epistemic claims, justifications, and responsibilit ies in contingent and situated ways that teachers cannot fully plan or an ticipate; (b) e ngage in c ollective negotiations about which knowledge claims are valid; (c) negotiate how to contest and verify knowledge claims, including those related to personal experiences. Taken together, these reasons showcase how classroo m discourse affords students myriad opportunities to take up epistemic agency in classrooms. Epistemic agency and history discourse History education has often framed students’ epistemic agency in discourse in cognitive terms, through their ability to apply disciplinary habits of expert historians  to authentic epistemic issues, for example,  problems or arguments related to the nature of history and meaning-making with historical knowledge (e.g., Herrenkohl & Cornelius, 2013; Wineburg, 2001). While considerable research explores students’ epistemic beliefs and reasoning skills about the nature of historical kno wledge (e.g., Zanazanian et al., 2024), fewer examples illustrate how suc h beliefs or skills relate to student agency in shaping collective knowledge practices in  the classroom.

Disciplinary thinking and argumentation Historical discussions offer students opportunities  to develop the epistemic virtues (Chinn et al., 20 11) of tolerance and a capacity for complexity as they str ive to understand perspectives from the distant pas t and distinct worldviews (Reisman, 2015). Discussions can allow students to interrogate claims in their textbooks and challenge their misconceptions about historical  events (Bain, 2005). History educators generally agree that wrestling with implications of the past for the pre sent requires first exploring the past in sufficien t contextual depth. Such learning can include more affective, social, and cultural forms of epist emic agency and epistemic virtues and vices relate d to historical consciousness, an awareness of one’s historical subjectivity (Lévesque & Clark, 2018), and the ethical work of shaping or challenging  collective memory. Disciplinary discussions have shown the potential to build students’ historical e mpathy (Kohlmeier, 2006). Teachers, however, may experience tensions emphasizing cognitive historica l skills alongside historical empathy or  citizenship competencies (Marczyk et al., 2022).

Interrogating dynamics of culture and power within knowledge construction To support students’ engagement and agency, history  educators must recognize that disciplinary teaching practices, content, and curriculum all reflect political, cultural, and epistemological orientations, rathe r than objective truths (e.g., Salinas et al., 2012). Scholars in Canada (e.g., Lévesque & Clark, 2018), the United States (e.g., Girard & Harris, 2018), and the Netherlands (e.g., van Boxtel & vandrie, 2018) acknowledge that epistemic consensus  on what constitutes  historical inquiry  reflects white and/or Western perspectives, rather than cross-cultural ones. Current research also illustrates how emphasizing d isciplinary concepts and argumentation (e.g., contextualization and presenti sm) may be insufficient for promoting students’ epi stemic agency to challenge  systemic inequities related to  race and language (Santiago & Dozono, 2024), gender (Dozono, 2017),  and colonialism (Conrad, 20 20). Teachers’ positionality and  competing teaching goals may also complicate opportunities for student epistemic agency. For example, white teachers of Af rican American history all closed discussion with majority Black students when  their views differed, or when teachers perceived past-present connections as threatening (Marczyk et al., 2022). Epistemic agency and science discourse In science education, epistemic agency is often seen in student discourse  through two lenses: cognitive authority and “who knows”.

Cognitive authority While science is generally thought of as independen t of people’s biases, some scholars argue that who is theorizing matters for what is known and the discip linary practice that is undertaken in a context (Ha nkinson Nelson, 1990; Knorr-Cetina, 1999). As a society, certain people have, o r are granted, cognitive authority—their understanding of factual matters and the nature of the world becomes “expert” knowledge (Addelson, 198 3). Granting cognitive authority to some individuals im plies a division of cognitive labor exists in which  in which those with such authority get to "certify" and comm unicate knowledge, and those without authority are assigned the tasks and work that those with power deem neces sary. These divisions in cognitive labor result in a hierarchy in which those without cognitive authorit y are placed in less powerful positions. Addelson (1983) suggests that such divisions in power have a bearing on  how and what knowledge develops within science practice communities and how knowledge is communicated to and received by the society at large. Who knows Second, “who knows” asks whether an individual or community  is considered to be the unit of “knowing” and practice. Some researchers frame science as an indi vidual enterprise in which lone scientists discover  truths about the world (Hankinson-Nelson, 1990). This view of science positions indiv iduals and their actions as independent of a contextual influence (Grene, 1985;  Hankinson-Nelson, 1990). Other scholars, however, argue that individuals' ideas cannot, by themselves, have  value until they are worked on by and with others.  From this perspective, what individuals come to know and how they come to know it depends on their community’s standards for practice. This public negotiation is more than vetting ideas and peer-review to seek the truth; science knowledge and practice shape, and are shape d by, public interactions of actors, tools, resourc es, and historical norms in a context (Knorr-Cetina, 1999).

Looking across fields When looking across fields, we see two shared features of epistemic agency  in history and science classrooms. First, teachers are the interpreters and mediators of stud ents’ epistemic agency and authority vis a vis the content. Given that teachers have developed a deeper understanding of disciplinary content, they could be primed to recognize important ideas, to place value  on students’ experiences, and to consider directio ns for moving forward when students become conceptually and practically stuck. Importantly, teachers  can use talk moves during discursive episodes to simultaneously inject important ideas, and to position students wi th epistemic agency to continue to shape the knowledge practices of the community. Second, teachers who provide opportunities for epis temic agency have an overt goal of disrupting myths that disciplines are objective, apolitical, a nd exist outside of human construction. Rather than pretend that disciplines are fixed and objective, teachers purpo sefully elevate ideas that might trouble existing d isciplinary narratives, signal to students that questioning and  challenging disciplinary norms is appropriate and  promote students’ constructive disagreement about the meaning of complex phenomena. Convergence and challenges As teacher educators in different subfields (history and science) who support preservice and novice teac hers to create opportunities for students to take up episte mic agency, we rarely talk across disciplinary area s about how to support teachers and students.  Here, we name two points of possible convergence and two challenges for working with teachers across fields to promote epistemic agency. Points of convergence A first point of possible convergence is that in both history and science, te nsions exist between providing opportunities for students to become familiar with established facts and designing opportunities for students to construct meaning and knowledge that is relevant for their li ves/communities (Herrenkohl & Cornelius, 2013). As schools are increasingly scrutinized and censured, teachers might be hesitant to use classroom disco urse to provide opportunities for epistemic agency. However, we wonder if issues that stretch beyond disciplin ary boundaries, such as climate change, artificial inte lligence, and IVF treatments, might provide opportu nities for history and science teachers to collaborate around instruction that strengthens students’  epistemic agency across classrooms. Such opportunities for epistemic agency would neces sarily require students to leverage established facts in the service of future-oriented learning that transcends existing disciplinary bounds. A second point of possible convergence is that history and science teachers face challenges incorporating culturally specific knowledges  because they may not recognize the role that their identity/positionality plays in their facilitation of discourse and curricular choices. In history, for example, non-Indigenous teachers working with Indigenous history curriculum showed strong implementation and ability  to facilitate student discourse when they referenced p ersonal connections to cultural history and place (Conrad, 2020). Similarly in science, teachers might not recognize that their positionality inhibits students’ discurs ive resources when engaged in inquiry. For example, as Warren et al. (2001) note, students may be engage d in reasoning processes that are crucial for sensemakin g around puzzling phenomena, but teachers who are expecting science talk to sound like White scientis ts may be unable to hear students’ contributions. Thus, history and science teachers need support to recogn ize and use students’ discourse as crucial for clas sroom practices.

Challenges In addition to the points of possible convergence, we also share two further challenges around epistemic agency. First, teachers frame conversations around disciplines and  pedagogy in terms of what they think students are capable of successfully doing in their classrooms, cognitively, socially, and epistemically. While we hope that teachers frame students in positive ways, we recogn ize that many teachers are conditioned to frame stu dents from deficit perspectives, especially when listenin g to discourse. For example, teachers might label s tudents’ sensemaking talk as “misconceptions ” and could shut down students’ talk through public shami ng. How do we think across fields to prepare and support teachers  to use asset framing of students’ discourse and co mmunities to open opportunities for epistemic agency during inquiry? Second, teachers who provide opportunities for epis temic agency have an overt goal of disrupting myths that disciplines are objective, apolitical, a nd exist outside of human construction. However, t he rarity of these epistemic stances and awareness among practic ing teachers (mentor teachers, instructional coache s) and in teachers candidates’ schooling experiences makes teacher edu cators’ work supporting such goals even more difficult. For example, if a teacher learns that history and s cience are collections of objective facts, they mig ht be more likely to steer students’ discourse away fr om sensemaking talk and towards recitation of what the teacher deems as correct information. Therefore, ho w can teacher educators provide learning opportunities for preservice and inservice teachers to reimagine thei r disciplinary and pedagogical frames to better sup port epistemic agency?

Concluding thoughts for three overlapping communities We conclude the conceptual paper by offering though ts for three overlapping communities: classrooms, t eacher preparation, and research methodologies. For classrooms, we argue that b uilding and growing collaborative communities is a foundation for epistemic agency-students and teachers must trust and value each oth er, develop humanizing norms for dialogue, and support varied cultural/community knowledges as learning assets. Epistemic agency does not magically  or spontaneously materialize. Teachers and students actively create classroom communities over time that position all a s learners and knowledge-holders and  support epistemic agency. Finally, t eachers’ words and actions are crucial to show stud ents that they are valued members of a community.

For teacher preparation, we argue that supporting p reservice teachers  to frame teaching and students through a lens of epistemic agency  requires teacher educators to provide opportunities  for preservice teachers to navigate pedagogical and disciplinary uncertainty  (Manz & Suárez, 2018) and to link  their knowledge and interpretive power with students’ cultural and comm unity knowledges as it relates to their content area  (Brady, Fryberg & Shoda, 2015). Such opportunities could oc cur in methods courses during extended pedagogical rehearsals, as well as during field placements and with mentors. Such opportunities would allow preservice teachers to see and debrief classrooms in which students tak e up epistemic agency. We also encourage teacher educators to provide preservice teachers with oppor tunities to engage in cycles of pedagogical reasoning during learning opportunities so that they can consider pe dagogical dilemmas and multiple paths of instructio nal decision-making to support goals including developing epistemic agency. Finally, for colleagues’ methodological considerati on, we argue that longitudinal research is crucial for capturing how and why students take up epistemic ag ency over the course of evolving discursive episodes in classrooms. Rather than parachute into classrooms for a few les sons, we propose that researchers spend significant time in classrooms with teachers and students to see how opportunities for epistemic agenc y begin and grow over time. Such research should also inclu de debriefing with teachers and students to see how  their reasoning, actions, and participation reflect a shared understanding and commitment to ep istemic agency. Finally, we join calls for more field-based research of K-12 teachers’ epistemic commitments (e.g., Buehl & Fives, 2016; Chinn et al., 2014; Herrenkohl & Corne lius, 2013) and how these shape teacher action and decision-making in classrooms, specifically as related to fostering and centering students’ epistemic agency.
