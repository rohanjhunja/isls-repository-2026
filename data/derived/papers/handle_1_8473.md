# Tensions in Extending Visions for Social Change Through Educational Research in Indian Contexts

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Newly evolving methodological approaches in Learning Sciences that use critical and ecological lenses to design provide ways to identify and address emergent tensions regarding social change through educational research. Such tensions get uniquely manifested in different socioecological contexts. We analyse them in the three studies that we conducted in India. Based on intersubjective insights into the data, we identify ‘how do’ different tensions manifest in four categories (educators/researchers, practices, learners, and learning environments) and ‘how to’ engage in various ways to address these. Our work attempts to contribute to the discussion around educational approaches to social change making with the help of cases from contexts other than where the approaches were developed.

## Introduction

We, the authors of this paper, along with many othe r Learning Sciences researchers, care about transla ting our evolving personal visions into the domains outside the academic field of Learning Sciences. As differe nt methodological approaches in the Learning Sciences evolve with the intention of ‘social change making’; from the iterative approaches of design research to desi gn-based research (DBR) to community-based design experiments to participatory design research (PDR) to Social Design-Based Experiments (SDBE) (e.g., Gutiérrez, Jurow, & Vakil, 2020), it is also important to thin k about how the goal of ‘social change making’ and the methodological approaches to getting there extend in educational contexts outside the United States where almost all these approaches have been developed and studied.

The evolving methodological approaches in Learning Sciences such as Social Design-Based Experiments (SDBE) try to address a gap between theory and praxis by engaging with members of communities outside academia (Gutiérrez, Jurow, & Vakil, 2020).  SDBE approach seeks to co-design learning ecologie s in which learning is made equitable and consequential for youth from nondominant communities. SDBEs are different from DBR because they focus on joining equity and learning goals that centre people’s lived experiences as the driver of possibilities for learning such that participants become intentional designers of their own futures. Extending these approaches beyond the contexts in w hich these are developed requires deep consideratio n of some of the foundational ideas such as nondominant participants, experts, communities. For instance, s uch extension in Indian contexts would require treating these contexts as sites of knowledge production by recognizing ground realities, diversity of ideas, and lived experiences (Sarukkai and Guru, 2012). As learning sciences researchers, we attempt to ext end our personal visions for social change through education by learning about these methodologies and partnering with members of our communities. In this paper, we analyse our work to answer the research question: Which tensions surfaced when we used critical and ecological approaches to social design to analyse our visions for social change through educational research? Methodological approach Our methodological approach is inspired by an approach of Jackson and colleagues that builds on academic friendships and shared histories to generate analytical and theoretical insights into developing a Learning Sciences research agenda (Jackson et al., 2020). The academic friendships of the three authors of this paper led the authors to discuss the possibility of reflecting together on their Learning Sciences research visions and tensions. We used auto-ethnographic (Ngunjiri, Hernandez, & Chang, 2010) methodologies to identify and analyse tensions. Using the grounded theory approach (Charmaz, 2006) we dev eloped emergent themes based on our intersubjective insights into the empirical data from prior case st udies. We identified four common constituent catego ries in educational ecosystems. For each category, we developed questions that see the common emergent tensions in a broader light to enable further conversations and develop actionable directions. Our positionalities, positions, and shared histories influenced our collaborative auto-ethnographic work. Case 1-Epistemically Agentic Science Learning:  My (Sugat’s) evolving vision for my contributions t o the field of Learning Sciences and education is about designing learning environments to support the development of epistemic consciousness. The idea of epistemic cons ciousness is inspired by Morales-Doyle’s (2017) operationalization of Freire’s Conscientização, or critical consciousness (Freire & Macedo, 2005), in his justicecentered science pedagogy (Morales‐Doyle, 2017). Epistemic consciousness is about an in-depth understanding of one’s relationship with knowledge and knowledge-producing practices. My goal is to study if and how  such epistemic consciousness develops and translates into changed participation in different contexts. Research context: I conducted two related studies in the US and Indi a to design for participation in knowledge-producing practices in science classrooms. The first study investigated a learning environme nt designed for students to engage in investigating mo lecular mechanisms of gene regulation using computa tional microworlds (Dabholkar & Wilensky, 2020) in extra-school settings with middle school students (n=41). I was a teacher in these iterations and studied how the des ign features of the learning environment, both tech nological and pedagogical, supported students’ epistemic agency. The second study was a shift towards participatory design. It was about a program called CTSI (Computational T hinking and Scientific Inquiry) to co-design scienc e and mathematics curricular units with high school teachers. Tensions: My vision is aligned with the SDBE impetus of desig ning for potential opportunities for meaningful and consequential participation for the social futures of students and teachers. Such parti cipation is meant to transform their relationship with ‘knowled ge’, from seeing knowledge as something to be acqui red to something to be evaluated and generated by them. This matters more in the Indian educational context, in which textbooks are considered a prime source of knowledg e, and students are often put in the position of ac cepting even problematic content at the face value (Darak, 2018). However, the first tension arose when I positioned the values of the community of practice of ‘scientists’ as desirable values, which are mainly influenced by Western ways of doing science to design (Bang & Medin, 2010). The second tension was about co-designing learni ng experiences that use contexts that youth from nondo minant communities find meaningful to engage with contemporary ideas and practices in science. Meanin gful integration of contemporary disciplinary ideas  and practices into contexts that are relevant to studen ts of nondominant gender, caste, religion requires identifying ways that would support equitable participation. Si nce the discourse around supporting such participat ion is severely limited in India, I found it hard to press for these design goals while working with Indian teachers. Case 2-Urban farming as a transformative space: As an activist-researcher, my (Deborah’s) vision includes creating learning environments that can enable sust ainable transitions through developing critical ski lls and agency amongst youth; To empowering them with tools  and perspectives required for reimagining just soc ioecological systems. This effort, however, entails a radical rethinking of the role of environmental education as a crucial scaffold for creating the political, techno-scientific, and cultural mobilisation to bring about d esired systemic changes. There is an urgent need to pay at tention to the particular histories, contexts, and perspectives underrepresented in the environmental education discourse to create alternate imaginaries that are not dominated by superficial technocratic visions of ‘fixing’ a problem. Research context: My efforts to expose students to alternate forms of learning and critical inquiry took the shape of an urban terrace farm that was co-crea ted with them. I chose food-garden as a topic of ph ysical engagement because farming naturally provides a spa ce to raise questions, and develop an integrated understanding of weather, food, nutrition, the economics of food production, water, and local geography. I worked with 40 students from grade 8 who were studying in a private school in the suburbs of Mumbai. The scho ol I worked with is situated in the backyard of a landfill. The project was ungraded, and the school managed to allocate an hour every week in the morning for the activity.  I worked with the students as a co-facilitator for  an entire academic year along with two school teachers. The b road goal of the project was to understand and faci litate students’ evolving engagement with the space, in terms of knowledge, practice, and motivations. Tensions: The question of authenticity and legitimacy remained constant tensions that I had tonegotiate with, while facilitating the project. On one hand, students’ prolonged exposure and experience of creating a terrace farm from scratch led to a range of interesting responses and helped them connect ideas of waste, resource, pestpredator relationships, the role of biodiversity, r espect for labour, and so on (Dutta & Chandrasekhar an, 2019). However, the terrace farm failed to be seen as a legitimate space for inquiry within the curriculum. While it was positively received in the school by teachers, its definition as an ‘extra-curricular activity’ rendered it ineffective in expanding or subverting the conventional science/ social science topics in the school, even as stud ents raised critical questions such as the relation between far m labour, livelihood and food price, consumption pa tterns in cities, rural-urban migration and so on. The possibilities were thus curtailed because teachers found it difficult to ICLS2022 Proceedings 1310 © ISLS transgress their own disciplinary boundaries and em brace the ‘possibilities of radical openness’ offer ed at the farm site. In fact, the space to a certain extent got appropriated as a tool to teach ‘better science’. The question I wrestled with throughout was, ‘What voices can radical, transformative experiences assume if they don’t find a legitimate space in mainstream educational practices?’ Additionally, though the experience of growing food was transformational for the students in many ways, it was still an isolated activity that did not engage with other aspects of their life or aspirations, unlike a rural population who are engaged in farming as means of livelihood, and thus would have a lot more at stake in terms of practice. Case 3-Contours of just and sustainable technology design: Designing solutions, including technologies, to address the unmet needs of the marginalised while mitigating the threats of climate change, is an imminent messy challenge that educational systems want to build agency and competency for. Towards this vision, as a Learning Sciences researcher, one of my (Geetanjali) efforts  has been to characterise the examples of alternate  practice, such as that of grassroots technology design, and d raw takeaways to supplement, augment, or redesign learning systems or environments.

Research Context:  In the Indian context, grassroots technology desig ners have developed micro hydropower systems as sustainable and renewable energy solutions for communities that the mainstream systems have failed to serve. I characterised the design practice of two such innovators: 1) a grassroots innovator1 and 2) an engineering professional 2 (Date, 2019). An insight, from analysis of these a nd other documented cases, supplemented by a simulation-based probing of the design thinking of these innovators, suggests that the societytechnology connection can be just and equitable whe n the design process starts with need identificatio n and problem formulation, and not context-sanitised technical specifications (Date, 2019). Tensions: Since one of the designers had studied only up to grade ten, a major underlying tension that surfaced from this study was the legitimacy of other forms of knowledge (procedural, experiential, place-based, traditional) and thinking (situated, distributed, embodied) vis-a-vis the formal, declarative, disciplinary bodies of engineering sciences and mathematics knowledge. This reflects in the mainstream industry’s success criteria for a technology-efficiency, replicability, mass-manufacturability through standardisation leading to profit-and the values of the innovators that prioritise addressing  the problems of the underserved, creating liveliho ods for the local community, and supporting entrepreneurs, while causing minimal disruption to the natural ecosystems. The tension translates into the question of whether the  alternate practice gets a legitimate space in the education, practice, and identity of future engineers, alongside the dominant formal knowledge. Another tangible aspect of the tension was how the other designer-a B. Tech from a top Indian Institute of Technology-negotia ted the hierarchy between him and the uneducated villagers,  to arrive at their desired solution. Expertise, when understood as formal training, and knowledge of formal structures, concentrates the power to define the ‘desirable’ into the hands of the trained engineers, even if th ey aspire to design for the nondominant. This generates the question of how students can be trained to recognize other forms of ‘expertise’ so as to develop the sensibilities to design with the nondominant, including the non-human species whose voice it is hard to capture?

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1311 © ISLS Our analysis revealed that the tensions were spread  across the range of stakeholders in the education ecosystem. The present and historical considerations of dominant and nondominant communities manifeste d in various ways for researchers, practices, learners, and designs of learning environments. In the further analysis of the tensions, we focused on these four categories (Figure 1). Based on the crosscutting tensions across our studies, we developed ‘how to’ and ‘how do’ questions. The first - 'how do' – question in a category captures tensions that we see as generative outcomes of our work. Building on these, we further developed 'how to' questions, which are intended to generate ongoing conversations and enable evolving ideas for future work. The main commonality across these studies in differ ent Indian contexts is that they aim towards reimagining learner participation in different communities of practice-scientific knowledge production, organic farming, and technology design. Such reimaginations requires questioning rigidities in educational systems-the ontological (who are considered experts in a particular field?), the epistemological (what practices of knowledge construction and evaluation are privileged?), and t he axiological (what value considerations govern de cision making in these systems?), which manifest in design s of the learning environments and educational prac tices. Given the existing legitimization of dominant educational practices, such as, focusing on student performance in standardised exams, our visions entailed a certain normative imagination regarding the desired directi on of envisioned social futures and educational design. As we hold our axiological positions as researchers, regarding designing educational experiences (epistemic autonomy, justice, care, equity), and had other curriculum-specific goals such as computational thinking or organic farming, we need to be open for continuous re-evaluation as we seek to align those with our design partners who work in the existing rigid education systems with severely limited epistemic autonomy.

Our research engagements with the nondominant individuals and communities in diverse Indian contexts highlight some tensions that need to be explored, understood, and clarified as we engage with SDBE and similar methods. Rather than seeing these tensions as limitations, we treat them as generative outcomes that could be used to identify existing lacuna and build newer imaginaries. These may help the research community better understand what elements of the design and processes of social experiments need to be foregrounded in such work.
