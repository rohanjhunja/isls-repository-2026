# Fostering Relevance in Life Science Education for High School Students: A Qualitative Content Analysis of an International Synthetic Biology Competition

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
One of the key challenges in science education is that students often perceive science lessons as irrelevant to their everyday lives. Biodesign, which leverages the properties of living organisms to design functional or creative artifacts, presents opportunities to bridge this gap by connecting life science education with real-life contexts. Consequently, there is growing interest in integrating biodesign into K-12 settings, especially as an engaging, hands-on approach to explore synthetic biology. Our study investigates how the iGEM competition fosters science learning experiences that are relevant to high school students. iGEM is an international synthetic biology competition where students use biodesign to develop artifacts to address real-world issues. Our findings reveal how the competition facilitates life science learning that is personally, societally, and vocationally relevant to students. We offer insights for designing learning environments that support engagement with biodesign and synthetic biology in ways that better align with students’ needs and interests.

## Introduction

One of the major problems in science education is t hat students often perceive science lessons as irre levant to their everyday lives and the society in which they live (Çimer, 2012; Gilbert, 2006; Potvin & Hasni, 2 014). This perception of irrelevance primarily stems from the lack of learning context in science teaching that r eflects realworld situations (Childs, Hayes, & O’dwyer, 2015; Gebre & Polman, 2020). Biodesign, which involves leveraging the properties of living organisms to design functi onal or creative artifacts, presents opportunities to bridge t his gap. Some examples of biodesign activities include genetically modifying yeast to bake vitamin-enriched cakes and using kombucha cultures to produce biofilms for  craft projects (Kafai & Walker, 2020c). In their analysis of 23 projects submitted to the International Biodesign Challenge, Walker et al. (2023) demonstrated how biodesign can be leveraged by high school students in inquiry  projects to connect life sciences with real-life contexts. For instance, one of the teams from Flint, Michigan, construct ed a biodegradable water filtration prototype using mycelium to tackle the city’s water contamination c risis, exemplifying the students’ application of bi odesign to address problems rooted in their lived experience.

Synthetic biology is a rapidly evolving interdiscip linary field that focuses on genetically engineerin g organisms to carry out new functions (e.g., bacteri a engineered to detect environmental pollutants). D espite its growing presence in everyday life, synthetic biolog y remains largely absent from K-12 education (Kafai & Walker, 2020a). However, the increasing availabilit y of low-cost, portable wet lab devices and ready-to-use kits is paving the way for integrating biodesign activit ies into K-12 settings as an engaging, hands-on approach to explore synthetic biology (Kafai & Walker, 2020b). Our study investigates how existing biodesign oppor tunities foster science learning experiences in synthetic bi ology that are relevant to students. We aim to gain  insights for designing learning environments that support engagement with biodesign and synthetic biology in ways that better align with students’ needs and interests. Building on Walker et al.’s (2023) findings, we operationalized Stuckey et al.’s (2013) theoreti cal framework for relevance in science education to  analyze projects submitted by high school students and their perspectives on participa ting in the international Genetically Engineered Ma chines (iGEM) competition. In this competition, students use biodesign to develop artifacts to address real-world issues. Our analysis focuses on two main aspects: the relevance of the problems students chose to address using synthetic biology and the relevance of participating in the c ompetition for students. To guide our study, we for mulate the following research questions:

1. What do iGEM projects reveal about the relevance of synthetic biology for high school students?

2. In what ways is participating in the iGEM competition relevant to high school students?

## Theoretical framework

We use Stuckey et al.’s (2013) theoretical framewor k for relevance in science education to examine hig h school students’ iGEM projects and their perspectives on p articipating in the competition. According to Stuck ey et al. (2013), relevance in science education encompasses three dimensions: individual, societal, and vocatio nal. Science education is relevant for the individual wh en it aligns with students’ curiosity and interests, equips students with practical skills for navigating their  current and future lives, and promotes intellectua l skill development. From the societal viewpoint, science e ducation is relevant when it enables students to un derstand the implications of science on society and fosters the development of skills and competencies for citi zen participation and sustainable development. Lastly, vocational relevance is established when science ed ucation introduces students to potential professions and careers, prepares them for further academic or vocational pursuits, and creates opportunities for employment.

## Methods

To address our research questions, we conducted a qualitative content analysis to examine open wikis created by high school teams about their iGEM projects (see iGEM Competition Team List, n.d.). For this preliminary study, we focused on projects submitted in 2024 by high sc hool teams based in North America. These selection criteria yielded sixteen teams, of which fourteen teams were  based in the United States and two teams were base d in Canada. We used the project description and team pag es of each wiki as  our primary data sources. While the former contains information on the project backgrou nd, inspiration, and methods, the latter provides u s with insights into students’ perspectives on participating in the iGEM competition. We employed a deductive approach to qualitatively a ssess these data sources using codes from Stuckey et al.’s (2013) framework for relevance in science education, as summarized in Table 1. The first and second authors started by selecting three team wikis and reviewing them independently based on the codes  in Table 1. Both authors then discussed and refined how these c odes were applied until they reached a mutual agree ment. The first author proceeded to code the project description and team pages of all sixteen wikis independently. This analysis was subsequently reviewed by the second author. When there was disagreement in the analysis, the codes applied were again discussed and refined until both authors agreed on the final results. Table 1 Coding Scheme Based on Stuckey et al.’s (2013) Framework for Relevance in Science Education. Code Description Individual Science education is relevant for the individual when it (1) matches the learners’ curiosity and interests, (2) provides students with necessary and useful skills for coping with their everyday lives today and in the future, and (3) contributes to the development of intellectual skills.

Societal Science education holds societal relevance when it (1) prepares pupils for self-determination and a responsibly led life in society by understanding the interdependence and interaction of science and society and (2) fosters skills for societal participation and competencies for contributing to society’s sustainable development.

Vocational Science education holds vocational relevance when it (1) offers orientation for future professions and careers, (2) prepares students for further academic or vocational training, and (3) opens up formal career chances.

## Findings

RQ1: What do iGEM projects reveal about the relevanc e of synthetic biology for high school students?

Given that the iGEM  competition aims to “[give] students the opportunit y to push the boundaries of synthetic biology by tackling everyday issues facing the worl d” (About the Competition, n.d., para. 1), it was unsurprising that all sixteen projects demonstrated societal rel evance. For example, Team CSW stated in their proje ct background that “the world population is…expected t o grow to over 9.7 billion in 2050” and that “globa l food demand will need to increase by over 50 percent in 2050, but major crop yields might decrease due to climate change” (Background, n.d., para. 1). This claim shows that the team recognizes the societal impact of their project, which involves genetically engineering crops to address food insecurity caused by climate change. Although societal relevance was emphasized in all sixteen projects, only six projects specifically outlined the project’s relevance to the team. We considered these projects to fulfill the individual dimension of Stuckey et al.’s (2013) framework because they illustrated how synthetic biology could be applied to address issues that were personally relevant to the students. For instance, Team DelNorte-SD explained that their motivation to improve acne treatment by genetically engineering bacteria to target a nd degrade biofilm stemmed from the personal impact acne had on their lives as high school stude nts (Inspiration, n.d.). Likewise, Team Westview-SanDiego recounted that their motivation to genetically engineer bacteria to break down plastic arose from witnessing plastic waste on the beaches of their local community (Project Description, n.d.). RQ2: In what ways is participating in the iGEM competition relevant to high school students?

Our analysis of each team page revealed how partici pation in the iGEM competition was relevant to stud ents across all three dimensions of Stuckey et al.’s (20 13) framework. With regard to the individual dimens ion, we found that students participated in the iGEM competition because it aligned with their interest in a wide range of science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) disciplines. A few students also mentioned no nSTEM disciplines that drew them to participate in t he iGEM competition, such  as finance, fundraising, and entrepreneurship. Additionally, several students hi ghlighted how participating in the iGEM competition  enabled them to combine their interests in multiple disciplines, as illustrated in the excerpt below: This is my first year in iGEM and I have already le arned so much about biology entrepreneurship! I love spending time in lab and researching all of the synthetic biology behind our project. iGEM has encouraged me to expand my in terests within science and pursue my interests in health policy and entrepreneurship (Alexa Seltzer, n.d.). We also identified multiple students who described how participating in the iGEM competition enabled them to develop essential life skills and learning dispositions such as collaboration, problem-solving, leadership, perseverance, a failure-positive mindset, and effective time management. These perspectives illustrate how iGEM participation aligns with the individual dimension of relevance as it equips “students with necessary and useful skills for coping with their everyday lives today a nd in the future” (Stuckey et al., 2013, p. 18). An  excerpt that reflects this perspective is included below: From beginning at “zero”, I’ve learned not just the  technicalities of synthetic biology, but the profound importance of teamwork, resilience, and embracing failure as part of the creative and scientific process. Overcoming every formidable obstacle that has unexpectedly challenged the persistence and mental fortitude of our team has reaffirmed within me the proverb: “if there’s a will there’s a way” (Matthew To, n.d.).

In our evaluation of each team page, we found students’ perspectives that reflected the societal dimension of Stuckey et al.’s (2013) framework. We considered these views to demonstrate societal relevance when students acknowledge that participating in the  iGEM competition offers them opportunities to addre ss critical societal issues. An excerpt that expresses this insight is included below: This is my second year participating in iGEM. iGEM has allowed me to take a deep dive not only about biology, but more so about how science h as the power to affect the world and the people in it as part of the human practices team (Amelia Sun, n.d.). We were also able to discern vocational relevance b y assessing students’ perspectives found on several team pages. We considered these perspectives to ful fill the vocational dimension of Stuckey et al.’s (2013) framework when students described how participating in the iGEM competition helped them gain experience and skills that were essential for further academic or career pursuits. For instance, the majority of stud ents conveyed that the iGEM competition gave them opportunities t o engage in authentic pra ctices of scientists, allowing them to learn skills beyond the classroom. Furthermore, a few students revealed that participating in the i GEM competition allowed them to connect with experts in  the field. We considered these perspectives to be of vocational relevance because the experience introduces stude nts to potential professions and careers by enablin g them to build a professional network early in their journey. The following excerpt illustrates this insight: I am continuing to learn lifelong skills of time ma nagement, the art of prioritizing, and have gained a foundational network of communication with  professors, mentors, and empowered peers (Angela Wang, n.d.).

Finally, our analysis also uncovered that peer cult ure played a significant role in motivating student s to participate in the iGEM competition —a theme not addressed by Stuckey et al.’s (2013) fr amework. While some students expressed enjoyment in connecting  with like-minded peers, others highlighted the opportunity to  form friendships as a key aspect of their experience in the competition. An excerpt that showcases this emerging theme is included below: Since joining iGEM, I have gained so much knowledge  about synthetic biology. iGEM has given me the opportunity to learn how to engineer devices, get hands-on experience of working in a lab, and make lots of new friends (Khushi Patel, n.d.).

## Implications and conclusions

Guided by Stuckey et al.’s (2013) framework, we ide ntified several ways the iGEM competition facilitat ed relevant science learning for students at individua l, societal, and vocational levels. At the individu al level, the competition offered diverse pathways for participation, as completing an iGEM project involves a variety of tasks, including fundraising, conducting experiments, and leading outreach initiatives (see Navigating iGEM, n.d.). This broad scope of tasks appealed to students with dive rse skills and interests in STEM and non-STEM fields. The competition also contributed to individual relevance through its student-centered approach, which grants students ownership of their projects and the responsibility of managing them. As a result, the competition equi pped students with essential life skills and learning dispositions (e.g., effective time management and critical thinking) that apply to academic and professional settings in  and beyond life sciences. Moreover, the competitio n created opportunities for personally and societally relevant science lear ning by allowing students to define their driving questions, encouraging them to integrate their inte rests, personal experiences, and societal concerns into their biodesign projects. Additionally, the competition’s  emphasis on artifact production also supported sci ence learning that held individual and societal relevanc e. By using biodesign to create functional artifact s aimed at addressing real-world problems, students gained a deeper understand ing of how scientific concepts, particularly in synthetic biology, can be applied to issues that  impact themselves, their communities, and society at large. Lastly, the competition provided vocational relevan ce by engaging students in authentic scientific pra ctices and connecting them with experts to access mentorship a nd professional tools and environments (e.g., acade mic laboratories). In addition to the three dimensions outlined by Stuckey et al. (2013), our study reveal ed a fourth dimension, which we refer to as the relational dime nsion. As a team-based initiative, the competition fostered collaboration among students to achieve shared goal s, enabling them to build meaningful connections wi th likeminded peers and develop a sense of belonging withi n the scientific community. Further research is nee ded to explore the role and significance of this dimension  in science learning and biodesign. Collectively, o ur findings also indicate that the competition fosters relevant  science learning by employing a project-based learning (PBL) approach, in which learning is student-centered and occurs through working on projects (Th omas, 2000). Of the six key features of PBL environments in science education described by Krajcik and Shin (2014), four w ere illustrated in our results: defining a driving question, creating artifacts tha t respond to the driving question, participating in scientific practices, and engaging in collaboration.

## Recommendations, limitations, and future research

Based on our findings, we recommend several strateg ies for incorporating biodesign and synthetic biolo gy into K-12 settings. These strategies include creating diverse pathways for participation, emphasizing student-centered learning, empowering students to define their own learning problems, focusing on fun ctional artifact production, and fostering collaboration among peers and with mo re knowledgeable others. However, this study has se veral limitations. First, our analysis is restricted to projects submitted in 2024 by teams from North America. Examining projects from other regions and competition cycles could provide a more comprehensive understanding of  the competition’s role in fostering relevant science le arning. Second, the use of content analysis means  that our findings are based on inferred insights. Future research could capture richer descriptions of students’ experiences by conducting interviews and/or ethnographic studies. Finally, the iGEM competition offers a narrow perspective of how biodesign-centered learning environments could facilitate rele vant science learning as it demands significant financial, logistical, and mentoring re sources. Exploring biodesign’s application in every day life contexts, particularly among students from minoriti zed com munities, could reveal additional insights into its potential to make life science learning more relevant and equitable.
