# Exploring the Synthesis of Geologic Concepts in a Field Geology Setting

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Scientific observation is a complex process involving synthesizing varied information. While research has documented learners’ struggles with scientific observation, less attention has been given to how they successfully navigate multiple concepts when making and interpreting observations in the field. This study uses Coordination Class Theory to examine the concepts learners employ during scientific observation in a field geology setting. Data comes from video recordings of teachers’ participation in a field geology professional development experience. The analysis identified several key concepts teachers used when interpreting observations, composition, weathering, and sedimentary structures, which were crucial for making broader interpretations about sedimentary rock depositional environments. The findings suggest that depositional environment is a coordination cluster involving multiple geologic concepts that learners navigate to make interpretations. Future work can examine how learners form connections between concepts in both formal and informal learning settings.

## Introduction and background literature

Scientific observation is a complex practice but is relatively under-studied. Some research has assumed learners’ struggles with scientific observation are due to a lack of content knowledge (Eberbach & Crowley, 2009). However, scientific observation is a complex, iterative process of gathering information from the environment (or a specific object) and interpreting that information to explain how the environment or object came to be  as it is today, with the potential to use that explanation a nd further observations in a prediction (Brierley et al., 2021). This process is foundational to learning in field geology settings (Brierley et al., 2021; Mogk & Goodwin, 2012). Scientific observation relies on domain knowledge, skills, and discipline-specific social practices to interpret what is noticed  (Goodwin, 1994). This can be a place where learners “get stuck” when engaged with  scientific observation; they may be able to identify a sedimen tary structure as important for determining the depositional environment, but not know why it is important (Barth-Cohen & Braden, 2021). Learners’ struggles may also be due to students’ tendency to consider only one, rather than multiple  hypotheses (Balliet et al., 2015). There are benefits to students learning  to identify rocks by patterns rather than relying on geologic terms  (Frøyland et al., 2016). Placing greater emphasis on scientific reasoning ove r terminology in geology education can improve learners' ability to use observations to support their arg uments (Remmen & Frøyland, 2020). However, students rarely learn concepts in isolation, and often need multiple concepts when forming complex explanations. Further, experts integrate multiple concepts when working in their area of expertise (Reimann & Markauskaite, 2018). Missing from this literature is an understanding of how learners synthesize several observations to form complex explanations, particularly in real-world, non-classroom settings like field courses. This study explores how in-service secondary science  teachers synthesize geologic concepts during scientific observation in a field geology setting. We use Coordination Class Theory (CCT; diSessa, Sherin, & Levin, 2015) to guide analysis with the conceptuali zation of a geologic concept as a network of extractions and inferences. This project seeks to examine multiple concepts involved in making and interpreting observ ations to explain the depositional environment in which a sedimentary rock formed.

## Theoretical framework

To explore teacher’s synthesis of geologic concepts during scientific observation, we used the lens of coordination class theory (CCT; diSessa et al., 2015). CCT provides a framework to explore learners’ reasoning about concepts across contexts. Coordination classes are collections of knowledge pieces that allow a person to ‘see’ scientific concepts in the world.  To “see” a concept, a person makes extractions as they gather information related to that concept from the environment and about particular objects in that environment (e.g., color, texture, size, patterns, orientation, measurements, etc.). A person uses readout strategies to focus their attention  on specific features when gathering information. After generating extractions, a person can make inferences, which are interpretations of the extractions using their prior knowledge. Developing expertise in a concept is a complex process of gaining useful readout strategies  to focus on relevant  extractions, acquiring knowledge elements, and associating knowledge elements with extractions to support maki ng inferences. Field geology provides a rich environment for exploring  how learners use geology  concepts, particularly when interpreting how a sedimentary rock  was formed. Determining a sedimentary rock’s depositional environment invol ves coordinating several extractions and inferences for different but related geology co ncepts. Thus, we hypothesize that depositional environment is not a singular coordination class but a coordination cluster that uses several geology concepts to make a detailed interpretation. In this study, we explore how learners coordinate multiple extractions and inferences  involving several concepts to generate an explanation of the depositional environment. CCT has been used to explore students’ understandin g of concepts such as force (diSessa & Sherin, 1998), proper time (Levrini & diSessa, 2008), and e quilibrium (Barth-Cohen & Whittman, 2017). CCT has also recently been applied to field geology, to explore learners’ successes and challenges in navigating extractions and inferences (Barth-Cohen & Braden, 2021). This project extends that work by focusing o n how multiple concepts contribute to the explanation  a depositional environment associated with sedimentary rock. Thus, the research questions are: 1) What extractions and inferences are associated with the concepts identified? 2) How are concepts synthesized to explain the depositional environment of a rock formation?

## Methods

Data analyzed in this study comes from a larg e project providing a geology-focused professional development learning experience for secondary science teachers in the Western US. Most of the 20 teachers in the course were middle-school level science teachers (76%), just over half were women (55%), and varied in teaching experience (Range: 1-15+ years). Teachers attended a semester-long introductory geology course via Zoom in the sp ring semester and then a week-long intensive summer field course at Capitol Reef National Park. This paper focuses on observational data from the field course.  Data comes from teacher’s GoPro cameras and audio recorders. We collected approximately four to six hours of observational data per teacher per day. The research team also made field notes to support the analysis. We initially focused on two teachers, Kelly and Mark, to guide data selection, as they were previously noted for having rich discussions while making observations. Analysis of the data involved a two-phase process. First, field notes were used to select an initial episode where Kelly and Marc were making observations to begin the analysis of the ir extractions and inferences. Transcripts were generated using ai transcription s oftware (Assembly AI, 2024)  and then further refined by the first author. Using both video and transcripts, extractions were identified when teachers made statements that were descriptive (e.g., “this section is has a lot of small layers”). Gestures were also included as they were occasionally used to convey shape and orientation information du ring the conversations (e.g., pinching fingers whil e moving hand horizontally to describe a rock layer that thi ns out). Inferences were then identified when an interpretation was made about one or more extraction s (e.g., “there are a lot of depositional environment s lumped into one formation”). Not all observations had accompanying inferences, whether the teacher did not make an inference, or did not voice the  inference aloud. Each extraction and inference were  given a code and when possible, extractions were linked to related inferences. We then organized the linked extractions and inferences from that episode into geologic concepts, often by noting wha t extractions and inferences were used to make larg er interpretations about the rock. We then conducted a second pass of the data  focusing on Kelly and Mark (and nearby teachers) at different stops on the field co urse to provide additional examples of the concept s in use, a form of triangulation within the data corpus (Miles et al., 2014). Quotations are used to highlight how the teachers were interacting with the concepts, along with relevant images to accompany the quotations for context. We opted for a narrative approach to describe how Kelly and Mark’s group synthesized an explanation of the Moen kopi Formation’s depositional environment because synthesis of ideas took place over the span of 20 minutes.

## Results

Here we present three geologic concepts that teachers often discussed, composition, weathering, and sedimentary structures, all of which support interpretations for the coordination cluster depositional environment. Composition The materials that compose a sedimentary rock provide key information about the depositional environment. Key extractions include the size of particles or grains  present, minerals present in the rock,  as well as patterns (e.g., banding in color or particles of different sizes, mixtures of differently-sized particles, etc.). Considering the size and mix of grains present supports inferences about energy flow. Fine particles like silt or mud indicate low energy environments like a slow stream or pond, where those small particles can settle  out of suspension. Larger grains like sand and even pebbles require much higher energy to move them to their final location. Further, certain minerals require particular conditions for deposition. In the context of Capitol Reef, the presence of gypsum is an important observation because it is deposited as marine or brackish water  evaporates completely in very shallow seas or tidal flats, leaving behind its solutes. In the rock, gypsum a ppears as a thin layer of white material that does not taste salty and flakes in sheets. Thus, an important readout strategy is to focus on the size and mixture of particles and grains in a rock to be able to infer conditions necessary to deposit the materials present in the rock. For example, Kelly had identified gypsum layers in a portion of the Moenkopi formation that her group was standing next to. Turning to the cliff behind the group (Figure 1), Kelly used patterns of the layers to match their location to the similar part of the Moenkopi on the cliff. S he further noticed “if we look at that cliff face, you don't have a gypsum layer, like every other layer kind of thing. They are spread apart,” and that towards the top of the cliff under younger sandstone layer, “there are fewer gypsum la yers, which could be one of our evidences of things changing.” As the group needed to explain what changed environmentally from the Moenkopi to the younger sandstone formation  on the top, Mark and Tom used that evidence to form an explanation (Mark: “the fewer gypsum layers, too, could be the water ju st receded and never…” Tom: “Didn't have time to evaporate because it receded too quickly.”). These extractions can be made from a distance, rather than up close. As seen in Figure 2, some rocks form cliffs, while others erode easily. The grain size of a rock impacts how resistant to weathering it is, so one can infer relative composition by the way a rock weathers. Larger grained rocks like sandstone have more space between grains, which allows mineral cement to form. Shale is composed of fine-grain particles generally not visible to the eye, which allows the particles to fit more tightly, decreasing room for cement. More cement between grains typically creates a more weather-resistant rock. Sandstone “caps” can also protect softer layers underne ath from fully eroding, as seen in figure 1.  Thus, a “large-scale” focus readout strategy  (compared to the small-scale focus discussed for composition), allows for extractions of the weathering patterns present, which can provide some inferences of the rock’s composition from a distance. For example, Jennifer compared a large cliff face that had silty slopes underneath it (Figure 2). One such structure is crossbedding, which appear as  d iagonal or swooping lines  in the rock, such as when Mark mimicked the crossbeds he noticed with gesture in image A and B of Figure 3. (Mark: “you candefinitely see [swings hand at an angle back and forth in the same angle as the cr ossbeds].”). These lines are often visually prominent and  can slightly protrude  from the rock due to subtle changes in composition as the depositional environment shifts, resulting in slightly different weathering patterns on the rock, Crossbeds form as sediment is pushed by a current (air or water), creating potentially large sand dun es or small ripples in a riverbed or a tidal flat. Extracting the crossbeds’ size and angle supports inferences about  the fluid that transported and deposited the sedim ent; large crossbeds with sometimes steep angles are indicative of wind-blown sand (image C in Figure 3) and thin crossbeds with shallow angles indicate water deposition. As the sediment is pushed over the dune or ripple, the material that settles on the leeward side is eventually buried. The direction of flow is preserved as the buried sediment becomes rock, and when the flow direction  shifts, the more recently deposited sediment’s orientation changes. Thus, extracting the orientation of crossbeds provides an  inference about the current flow (Alice: “It [the wind] was blowing this way, then it blew that way.”). In the case of tidal flats, the regular bi-directional flow of water can create symmetrical ripples compared to the unidirectional flow from wind or a river. Further, as a tidal flat would occasionally dry out, it could form layers of gypsum.

## Discussion and conclusion

The results suggest that depositional environment could be a coordination cluster consisting of many different geologic concepts. In this project, we examined how  the concepts of composition, weathering, and sedimentary structures contribute to an explanation of a rock’s deposition al environment. Each concept provides a set of extractions and associated inferences that contribu te to a holistic explanation. Further, these concep ts are also interrelated. For example, considering how a rock i s weathering provides information about its relative composition, and how a rock weathers can be impacted by sedimentary structures. These results suggest that a well-developed coordination cluster for depositional env ironment may be beneficial for navigating the many related concepts, along with extractions and inferences that come into play when observing in the field. This work builds on th e CCT literature in multiple ways. First, coordinatio n clusters are relatively understudied compared to singular coordination classes. Researchers have explored coordination clusters in the concept of harmonic motion (such as a pendulum or spring), which unites the concepts of velocity and frequency to understand an object’s movement (Parnafes & diSessa, 2013). Considering that studen ts rarely learn concepts in isolation, coordination clusters are more representative of concept navigation that learners do when developing an explanation  in a real-world learning environment. Second, this project used CCT in the context of a f ield course, a learning environment quite different from  traditional classroom settings where CCT has been applied. Field experiences are generally understudied despite their importance to geology education (Petcovic et al., 2020), but these spaces can be rich learning environments as learners interact with the world outside of the classroom (e.g., Barton & Tan, 2010). Yet, learning can be difficult in the field as students navigate abstract representations like geologic maps and the physical environment (Kastens and Ishikawa, 2006). Thus, this work takes a learning sciences approach to shed light on the complex learning occurring in this setting. This work raises questions about learning in a field setting. Due to the nature of the field cours e, where learning often happens in small groups, our analysis is limited in making claims about specific individuals’ learning. However, this raises questions about how social context impacts students’ reasoning as individuals take up or ignore information from their peers.  Finally, this analysis focused on the identificatio n of concepts  that contribute to the coordination cluster of depositional environment. Future work may focus concept evolution over time, such as how learners form connections between concepts.
