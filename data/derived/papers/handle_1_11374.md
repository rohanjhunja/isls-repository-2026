# Comparing Human and Machine Responses to Foster In-service Teachers’ AI Literacies

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explored in-service teachers’ perceptions of large language models, focusing on their ability to generate human-like responses. The data were collected from 47 in-service teachers who enrolled in a graduate-level computer science education and technology course. In a weekly module, teachers were asked to compare data-driven machine intelligence to humans based on the characteristic features of responses. Qualitative content analysis revealed that teachers perceived machine intelligence as limited in replicating human qualities. However, they saw utility value, suggesting meaningful interactions could still be established. Implications for the field of teacher education and professional development in AI literacies are discussed.

## Introduction

As generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) tools  become more publicly available, scholars are incre asingly exploring both their potential to enhance teaching and learning and the possible harms they could intr oduce. To date, such tools have shown some promise in enhanci ng instructional practices (e.g., automation of gra ding and tracking attendance) and supporting student learning (Akgun & Greenhow, 2022; Hrastinski et al., 2019). Despite promising evidence, one major concern is that limit ed understanding  of how GenAI tools work might hinder teachers’ ability to optimize these resources for teaching and learning. It could also adversely impact their critical reflection on automated responses, which might lead  to an overreliance on the outcomes of such interac tions. Thus, this study aims to enhance in-service teachers’ understanding of how large language models operate and generate human-like responses to prompts. For this purpose, OpenAI’s ChatGPT was selected because of its fastgrowing user base, with over 400 million users by February 2025, and wide accessibility (Reuters, 2025). Utilizing ChatGPT as a use case, we asked teachers to compare and contrast human and machine responses. Through these comparisons, we anticipate in-service teachers engaging in a knowledge-building process and becoming aware of the affordances and constraints of GenAI tools. As a result, they can then use this knowledge to model its use for their students. Guided by this purpose, the study addresses the following research question:

• RQ1. In what ways do teachers perceive machine responses as different from human responses?

Review of the literature To date, numerous researchers and organizations have proposed competencies for teachers to effectively integrate AI into education. For example, Kim and Kwon (2023)  outlined 22 teacher-centered competencies based on the TPACK framework. These competencies focus on content knowledge (e.g., basic concepts of  AI and ethics), pedagogical knowledge (e.g., managing online and in-person classrooms), technology knowledge (e.g., fin ding relevant tools and resources related to AI), pedagogical content (e.g., designing AI-integrated curriculum for other subject areas), and technology content knowledge (e.g., assess student outcomes using AI tools). Similarly, Ng et al. (2021) also used the TPACK framework to categor ize AI literacy for educators: technological knowle dge, focusing on learning artifacts (e.g., AI-related agents, unplugged artifacts); pedagogical knowledge, emphasizing pedagogical approaches (e.g., collaborative learnin g); and content knowledge, addressing teaching and learning content (e.g., AI awareness, ethics). Broadly, thes e attempts emphasize AI literacy for teachers not o nly as a combination of technical and pedagogical content kn owledge and skills related to integrating AI, but a lso as an understanding of AI’s social, cultural, and ethical  implications. Central to these competencies is a f oundational understanding of core concepts in AI, which is cruc ial for responsible and ethical AI integration in e ducational contexts. One way to develop such foundational understanding is through users’ constant comparison of machines to human capabilities, such as the ability to generate responses. This process allows users to recognize differences between human-and machine-generated texts. For example, Sardinha (2024) found  that machine responses lack persuasive language and show a greater tendency toward abstraction compared to humandialogue.

## Context and participants

This study was conducted in the summer of 2024 at a mid-sized university in the Southeastern United States. The participants were 47 in-service teachers from various disciplines, the majo rity of whom taught at the K-5 level. They were enrolled in a 7000-level graduate course focused on the foundations of  technology and computer science education for teachers.

## Data collection and analysis

The data was collected from an online asynchronous discussion within a weekly module where teachers le arned about data-driven machine learning intelligence. As part of th e module, teachers completed and reflected on an adapted Turing Test activity  (Lindner, &Seegerer, 2020), “Exploring Human and Machine Intelligence with ChatGPT,” designed to assess whether a computer cou ld mimic human intelligence. They created a mini te st to explore this question, completing the activity through several steps. In step 1 (designing test questions), teachers wrote approximately five questions they believed co uld distinguish between a human and an AI and provi ded a brief rationale. In step 2 (interacting with ChatGPT), teachers posed their questions to ChatGPT and recorded its responses. In step 3  (answering their own questions), teachers generated their own responses to the sa me questions. While prior access to ChatGPT’s responses could hav e influenced them, this was mitigated by the nature of the task: to ‘trick’ the GenAI, meaning participants drew on their own understanding rather than relying on its answers.  In step 4 (comparing machine and human intelligence), teachers compared their answers with ChatGPT’s responses, analyzing similarities and dif ferences. Finally, in step 5 (reflection), teachers reflected on the process, discussing the challenges of distinguishing between human and machine answers and how the activity influenced their understanding of AI. To analyze data, we conducted a qualitative content analysis using open and axial coding (Saldaña, 2016). After familiarizing ourselves with the data, we conducted an open coding to develop an initial coding schema. The second author then in dividually coded all the discussions, adding new co des as necessary. Afterward, w e held group meetings to review and refine the code s and finalized the themes and categories. We listed the categories and their associated codes for each theme, incorporating teachers’ own words to preserve the essence of their meaning (Mills & Birks, 2014).

“While ChatGPT would not state its opinion, it gave information for me to make my own opinion.” The first category that marks the difference betwee n human and machine responses was response characteristics, where teachers judged differences based on the ch aracteristics of the response received from ChatGPT. For example, some teachers observed that ChatGPT generated an excessive amount of information and lacked conciseness compared to human responses. Another response characteristic that marked the difference was reported as impersonal responses. Most teachers rep orted that when the prompts required subjective opi nions, such as whether one thing is better than the other (e.g., whether i t is better to go on vacation to the mountains or the beach), ChatGPT neither provided or favored an opinion on controversial topics, nor did it respond with prior experiences. While some viewed this as helpful, for  others, lack of emotional depth in responses was v iewed negatively. For example, one participant highlighte d the emotional depth as part of usual human intera ction and noted that humans rely on such cues to quickly understand the context of a situation. The second category is utility and efficiency. The majority of teachers recognized and appreciat ed the amount of information that is accessible to them in a short amount of time. This has been recognized as one major strength of the tool, where the users saw themselve s as collaborators of ChatGPT in generating new ideas and conducting comprehensive search. Although the teach ers highlighted the role of ChatGPT as a collaborat or, one reported drawback was that it lacked the creative aspect in the sense that a human has. For example, one participant mentioned that when they experimented with vocabula ry, such as by creating a new word, the tool strugg led to move beyond the rule-based governance of the language. They also highlighted that, to optimize their interactions with ChatGPT and receive desired responses, they need to be able to come up with appropriate prompts. They saw this one potential drawback because it required  mastering an additional skill, which was not typic al to a human-humandialogue.

## Discussion and conclusion

Our findings provided insights into teachers’ thoug hts and perceptions of human versus machine respons es. Teachers discussed the LLM’s strengths and limitations based on their unique int eractions with ChatGPT, and our analysis suggested that teachers predominantly proposed that human and machine responses have dist inct qualities, as Sardinha (2024) also found, and each could complement the other’s s trengths while compensating for weaknesses. Jeon and Lee’s (2024) study support ed our findings, suggesting tha t teacher-AI collaboration might lead to better instructional decision-making for teachers. Another finding indicated that teachers’ interactions with ChatGPT led to a perception of its utility value. For example, several teachers reported that it coul d extend their capabilities by allowing them to con duct comprehensive searches quickly. This finding holds implications for teacher education, as several stud ies have suggested that utility value can play a role in tea chers’ adoption of technology in the future (Cheng et al., 2020). Introducing LLM’s pedagogical uses in teacher education and/or professional development may further strengthen teachers’ perception of its relevance and utility a nd, thus, its future implementation in classrooms.  Although teachers recognized the value of ChatGPT’s ability to provide quick and efficient responses, our findi ngs also revealed that teachers had mixed feelings about its  response quality and characteristics. They highlig hted a lack of creativity and emotional depth, which stood in c ontrast to their expectations of a conversational p artner. In addition, teachers at times felt discouraged when responses did not dir ectly address their questions. Collectively, these findings suggest a need for additional suppor t and training to help teachers build meaningful co llaboration with AI in educational settings. Another noteworthy finding is that only a few teachers were concerned about the accuracy and credibility of the responses. This raises an important question as to whether teachers are prepared to anticipate both the intended and unintended consequ ences of using such tools in classrooms —which was suggested as a core instructional technology practi ce recommended by Heath and Mishra (2023) and Trust et al. (2023), as well as a knowledge component of the AI literacy frameworks referenced above (Kim & Kwon, 2023; Ng et al., 2021). This finding is critical, suggesting that emergent frameworks ne ed to place greater emphasis on this aspect. Otherwise, teachers’ lack of a critical lens or healthy skepticism about the accuracy of responses might cause them to struggle with establishing boundaries  when adopting this technology in the future.  In sum, our findings suggest that future studies should focus on fostering teachers’ AI literacy by identifying acceptable uses of AI, understanding its limitations, and promoting its ethical and responsible use in teaching and learning.
