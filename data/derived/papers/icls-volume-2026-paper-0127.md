# Embracing the Complexity of a Learning Ecology Design for an Online Asynchronous Undergraduate Science Course

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Online asynchronous courses present a fundamental challenge in that uniform designs encounter wonderfully diverse learners, creating heterogeneous experiences that traditional analytics fail to capture. This study adopts a complex systems perspective drawi ng on principles from cognitive constructivist, social constructivist, constructionist, and transformative learning theories to understand learning ecologies in an undergraduate marine science course. Using learning experience network analysis (LENA), we analyzed 38 student reflection papers, identifying patterns among struggles, aspects that worked well, and theoretical alignments. Three distinct learning ecologies emerged. Foundational Learner experiences engaged primarily with cognitive constructivist pr ocesses. Collaborator experiences thrived in social meaning-making frameworks. Transformer experiences navigated transformative learning through critical reflection. Rather than universalized designs, we developed ecology-specific design moves that embraced the complexity and messiness of learning. This approach suggested that powerful online learning design requires environments capable of supporting multiple simultaneous learning ecologies rather than flattening student diversity into averaged experiences.

A central challenge in online asynchronous courses in undergraduate higher education is the heterogeneity of student experiences. Courses in the learning management system are pre-designed and uniform, yet learners bring a wonderful diversity of perspectives, interests, assumptions, ontological stances, epistemic stances, and so on, leading to unique experiences of the learning (Fischer et al., 2018). Common analytics approaches have flattened this complexity by investigating a small number of variables or treating factors in isolation rather than seeking to understand patterns of interdependencies among as many aspects as possible (Jacobson, 2020). Designing for an average student is not enough, and small fixes aimed only at the most struggling students may lead to a persistent mismatch between our design intentions and the varied way students actually experience learning. The true challenge (and opportunity) is understanding and responding to the diverse learning ecologies that emerge when students with different assets, needs, and approaches encounter the same learning environment (Vossoughi et al., 2023). This study attempts to understand these ecologies and make design changes that embrace the complexity of learning.

We adopt a complex systems perspective that frames learning not as a collection of isolated events but as an emergent property of an interconnected system (Jacobson, 2020). Designing for complex learning ecologies requires moving beyond any single theoretical framework to draw on principles from multiple complementary theories (Barab & Squire, 2004; Sandoval, 2014), as well as attentiveness to the interdependence among them. In the current study we leveraged principles from cognitive constructivist theory (Piaget, 1952), social constructivist theory (Vygotsky, 1978), constructionist learning theory (Papert & Harel, 1991), and transformative learning theory (Mezirow, 2000), supported by principles from self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2017) and the dynamic systems model of role identity (DSMRI; Kaplan & Garner, 2017). Iterative design that embraces complexity must account not only for learner experiences that align with principles from theories, but must also be attuned to the ways in which aspects of the experience were beneficial in different ways for different students as well as struggles (Donaldson et al., 2024). We treat these three different categories of learner experiences as signals of mechanisms in the complex ecology rather than wins to keep or problems to remove (Sandoval, 2014). We treat codes, co-occurrences, and clusters as traces of mediating processes that link concrete design features to patterns in student experience, which serves as our conjectural chain. This approach embraces and ho nors the complexity of learning by examining not only theoretical alignments but also the interplay between them and the successes and challenges that emerge in authentic asynchronous online learning contexts. 1119 BY103, “Oceans and You,” is an online, asynchronous first-year science course that is part of the Blazer Core (the general education program) curriculum at the University of Alabama at Birmingham. Students participate in an online lecture section and a separate online lab section. They are placed in teams of approximately 4 students for various activities, and the teams are the same in both the lecture and lab. The overarching theme of the course is the interconnected nature between the ocean and humans: What does the oceando for you and what impact do you have on the ocean? This course used inquiry-based learning, where students engaged with material through lectures, online activities, and research projects. Learning extended beyond the digital classroom through the use of hands-on lab kits for experiments on bioremediation and organism adaptation, as well as outside assignments that had students documenting local plastic pollution or investigating seafood sustainability in their communities. The course aims to develop students' skills in scientific inquiry and empower them to apply their knowledge of marine systems to complex societal issues.

This study used learning experience network analysis (LENA; Donaldson et al., 2024) to analyze student experiences and struggles within the Spring 2025 course. The primary data source was qualitative, consisting of end-of-term reflection papers (n=38) responding to the same prompt from both lecture and lab components. These assignments prompted students to critically evaluate the aspects of the course that worked particularly well for them, struggles, and what the experience meant to them. To prepare the data for network analysis, a systematic coding process was applied to the nineteen student reflection papers for the lecture and lab. This iterative coding identified recurring patterns of student experience categorized into three primary themes: "what worked well" (WORKED), "what students struggled with" (STRGL), and "alignment with principles from learning theories" (THRY). The research team met frequently to resolve coding issues. This initial coding yielded a total of 744 distinct coded s egments. To explore relationships between these codes, a one-tailed Pearson's co-occurrence correlation analysis was performed on all code pairs. A correlation matrix including only significant correlations at p <.001 was then imported into UCINET network analysis software. The Girvan-Newman clustering algorithm (Girvan & Newman, 2002) was subsequently applied, to identify distinct clusters of codes. The resulting clustering structure demonstrated robust differentiation, evidenced by a modularity score of Q = 0.538. This methodological integration of qualitative thematic analysis with quantitative network analysis provided both rich descriptive insights into student experiences and a data-driven framework for identifying distinct learner profiles. Each identified clus ter is conceptualized as a "learning ecology," representing a unique interplay of cognitive, social, and emotional learning experiences within the course. The analysis revealed three distinct clusters of student experiences (see Figure 1), each characterized by unique patterns of struggles and successes, and aligning with specific theoretical principles. These clusters, hereafter referred to as the Foundational Learner (green triangles) cluster, the Collaborator (blue squares) cluster, and the Transformer (yellow circles) cluster, highlight varied engagements with cognitive, social, and transformative learning processes. Clusters represent groups of codes rather than groups of students. The Foundational Learner cluster predominantly represented students grappling with core cognitive and academic tasks, with the number of struggles outweighing aspects that worked. Their struggles were centered on comprehension, problem framing, and interpreting complex visual information. These challenges suggested a need for foundational support in knowledge construction and sense-making within the discipline. Aspects of the learning experience that worked particularly well for the Foundational Learners we re general skill development and a positive overall experience. Particularly beneficial were visual representations, indicating a capacity to engage with visual artifacts when appropriately supported. This suggested that while abstract conceptual understanding was challenging, these students could thrive in hands-on, visually-rich learning environments that facilitated skill development. This cluster contained no codes for experiences aligned with principles from theory, but resonated with cognitive constructivist theory where students actively build their mental frameworks (schemas) of a topic because their struggles with initial comprehension and problem framing pointed to a need for more scaffolding of these processes.

Based on the analysis of the Foundational Learner cluster, a design move was crafted to address their specific challenges with comprehension and knowledge construction. The existing end-of-module checklist, intended as a self-check, will be transformed int o a structured metacognitive reflection. In this new activity, students will be prompted to identify five concepts from the module they understand well and five they still find challenging. This initial step of recognizing their own learning successes is intended to build confidence. To address the concepts they struggle with, students will be asked to create a concept map. This part of the design change leverages an identified strength of this cluster which was the helpfulness of visual representations. By 1120 using concept mapping, students can apply this strength directly to the task of organizing their thoughts and making connections between what they know and what they are still learning, providing necessary scaffolding for their knowledge construction process.

Their struggles were minimal (only 2 out of 19 codes) and focused on personal time management and self-regulation, as well as initial difficulties with interpersonal communication and networking. The dominant theme for this cluster was positive experiences across numerous dimensions of teamwork. Students with experiences represented in this clus ter reported exceptional experiences in peer interactions, collaboration, fair contribution, and building trust. Their successes were bolstered by the 1121 hands-on nature of activities and positive feedback from the instructor. This cluster aligned with the social constructivist principles of co-constructing knowledge through interaction and collaboration and engaging with the perspectives of others. The self-determination theory principle of learner autonomy and agency was reinforced through the constructionist principle of tinkering and exploration, along with the cognitive constructivist principle of multiple pathways.

For the Collaborator cluster, the proposed design move targets their primary reported struggle with personal time management and self-regulation. The design change intentionally converts this individual challenge into a group-based activity to take advantage of their demonstrated positive experiences with collaborative work. Instead of an individual reflection on time management, students will be prompted to engage in a team discussion about their strategies for the upcoming module. This peer-to-peer sharing is informed by the social constructivist principles of co-constructing knowledge and engaging with multiple perspectives which were already prominent in this cluster’s experience. This change reframes time management as a shared, solvable problem rather than an individual failing. It supports the principle of learner agency from self-determination theory by empowering the group to take collective ownership of their learning process and workflow. The Transformer cluster represented a complex interplay between a large number of struggles (10 struggles, compared to only 2 aspects that worked) and an even larger number of aspects of their experience that aligned with specific principles from theory (14 codes). The struggles in this cluster were diverse, spanning logistical issues like project management, timing, and a desire for more flexibility in assignments. On a more personal level, struggles included dealing with anxiety, fear, and discomfort and resistance tonew perspectives. Despite these varied challenges, their key successes centered on finding personal relevance in the work and developing deep personal insights and motivation. We interpret the high number of theory alignment codes as evidence of active meaning making despite reported struggles. Students whose experiences were represented in this cluster suggested engagement with processes of reflection and metacognition that are important for both cognitive constructivism and transformative learning theory. Other principles from cognitive constructivist theory included individual knowledge construction and schema building, supported by the social constructivist principle of scaffolding by the teacher. Principles from transformative learning theory included developing systems thinking abilities, developing new perspectives, and changing assumptions or beliefs. The dynamic systems model of role identity appeared only in this cluster, represented by principles including exploring one’s own reactions, broadening perspectives, and exploring beliefs about knowledge and reality. Constructionist learning theory principles included making things, relevance exploration, and productive failure. To support the students who had experiences aligned with the Transformer cluster, the design move focuses on alleviating the logistical and structural struggles they reported. These students demonstrated a deep engagement with the course material, finding personal relevance and insight despite challenges with project management and assignment timing. The proposed design involves removing a module that was less effective in the online environment and redistributing that time across the remaining modules. Thi s adjustment will create a more spacious schedule and reduce the logistical pressure on students. The additional time is intended to provide the necessary room for the deep, reflective processes that are central to the transformative learning experiences characteristic of this cluster. By decreasing the structural burdens, the design gives students more autonomy to manage their work in a way that supports the development of personal insights and the construction of meaningful knowledge.

## Findings & Analysis

Our analysis revealed three distinct but interconnected learning ecologies that emerged from students’ experiences with the same online science course. Rather than representing different types of learners, these clusters demonstrated that the same learning environment affords different patterns of theoretical alignments for different students. These ecologies were not characterized only by their theoretical alignments, but by the interdependent relationships among struggles, successes, and theoretical princ iples that emerged within each cluster. The evidence-driven design moves we developed represent an attempt to honor this complexity rather than flatten it. We crafted ecology-specific supports grounded in the stance that powerful online learning design requires not choosing among theoretical frameworks, but creating environments capable of supporting multiple simultaneous complex ecologies. Future work will investigate how our design changes play out in the experiences of students in future semesters with the understanding that each group of students is fundamentally unique and learning is inherently contextual. In addition, we want to explore any interactions between the clusters such as tensions or feedback loops. The contribution of this work is a methodological and theoretical argument for designing with complexity rather than against it to create online learning environments that are more adaptive, inclusive, and effective for the wonderfully diverse students they serve. 1122 Fischer, F., Hmelo-Silver, C.
