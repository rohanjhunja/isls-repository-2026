# Belonging in Tech: Effects of Gender and Race on Sense of Belonging in Undergraduate Computer Science Group Projects

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The field of computer science faces challenges with representation both in the workplace and in the classroom. Our study examines demographic group differences in sense of belonging and related experience factors in undergraduate computer science group projects. 362 undergraduate students answered questions about sense of belonging, psychological safety, team conflict, and team satisfaction for a recently completed group project in a computer science class. Surprisingly, we found no gender differences in any category. However, there were significant racial differences in sense of belonging and psychological safety. We believe that our work not only adds to the research on the relationship between gender, race, and belonging in computer science education, but also starts a new conversation around assessing differential impact of group projects in computer science courses. This may not only improve the group project experiences of underrepresented students but also contribute to increased representation in computer science.

## Introduction and background

It is no secret that the field of computer science faces challenges with representation. The tech indu stry is composed of only 25% women compared to 50% for STEM  fields and the workforce more broadly (Fry et al., 2021). Likewise, the tech industry is only 7% Black and 8% Hispanic compared to 11% and 17% for the workforce overall (Fry et al., 2021). This pattern is reflect ed in higher education. In 2022, only 21% of underg raduate computer science degrees were conferred to women even though women made up 58% of undergraduate degrees conferred (National Center for Education Statistics, 2022a, 2022b, 2022c). Additionally, Black and His panic students made up only 9% and 12% of computer scienc e degrees conferred (National Center for Education Statistics, 2022a, 2022b, 2022c).

In higher education, computer science departments h ave struggled with retention of students from underrepresented groups (Stephenson et al., 2018), and previous research suggests that low sense of be longing may be a contributing factor (e.g., Mooney & Beck er, 2020; O’Keeffe, 2013). While there has been res earch on ways to improve sense of belonging, such as recogni zing and mitigating bias in the classroom or implem enting affinity groups outside of class (Stephenson et al., 2018), most of this work has focused on classroom  dynamics and extracurricular programs, with few studies investigating specific elements of classwork. In particular, there is no past research to our knowledge examining demographic differences in belonging for class group projects. This missing area in the literature is especially problema tic for computer science education, as many undergr aduate computer science courses include a group project (Lingard & Barkataki, 2011). Our research adopts a quantitative approach to inve stigating the effects of gender and race on sense o f belonging in undergraduate computer science group p rojects. As there are no previous studies on demogr aphic differences in belonging for class group pro jects, our research contributes by 1) extending res earchers’ current understanding of the relationship between gender, r ace, and belonging in computer science education, a nd 2) by initiating the important conversation around the di fferential impact of havi ng group projects in undergraduate computer science courses. Additionally, our researc h investigates other experience factors related to sense of belonging (psychological safety, team conflict, and team satisfaction) at the same time. To this end, our primary research question is wheth er different demographic groups experience differences in sense of belonging in undergraduate computer science group projects. We proposed the fo llowing hypotheses: H1: Men will report higher sense of belonging, psychological safety, and team satisfaction, and lower team conflict, compared to women; H2: White student s will report higher sense of belonging, psychologi cal safety, and team satisfaction, and lower team confl ict, compared to non-White students. To investigate this research question, we conducted a quantitative study measuring these factors at the completion of a group project for an undergraduate computer science course.

## Method

Current students (n = 362) at a small, undergraduate-only, STEM-focused institution were invited to participate in the study. Students were members of the Computer Science and Software Engineering (CSSE) department and were from all class years. There were 297 (82.04%) men, 58 (16.02%) women, and 7 (1.93%) nonbinary participants. There were 232 (64.09%) White, 102 (2 8.18%) Asian, 18 (4.97%) Black, and 10 (2.67%) Hisp anic participants. The study consisted of two parts: a s ense of belonging survey and a CATME survey. Not ev ery participant completed the sense of belonging survey (n = 99) as participation was voluntary for that survey while completion of the CATME survey was a routine, requi red part of the course (details in the sections bel ow). This study received approval from the Institutional Revi ew Board of our institution, and all methods were p erformed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulati ons. We used R to conduct independent samples t-tests to analyze differences in group means and generalized linear modeling to analyze interaction effects. Due  to small sample size, we removed nonbinary participants from our analytic sample. Sense of belonging survey All CSSE students received an email at the end of the Winter 2022-2023 term inviting them to complete a survey about their recent group project experience. The inclusion criteria was students who had completed a CSSE course with a group (two or more students) project. Students did not receive an incentive for participation. The survey consisted of six Likert-scale questions about sense of belonging (SB) and d emographic (gender, race) information. SB1 and SB2 were design ed to measure belonging of contributions; SB3, SB4, and SB5 to measure belonging of presence; and SB6 to measure belonging in the field. The questions also intended to capture both self-and others-focused value. SB2, SB5, and SB6 focus on the stude nt’s beliefs about their own value to the group, while SB1, SB3, and SB4 focus o n the student’s beliefs about how others in the gro up value them. Together, these questions aimed to capture a comprehensive definition of SB used in psychology a nd education research: “feeling of acceptance by an in dividual into a community where their contributions and presence are valued” (Mooney & Becker, 2020).

The questions were as follows: SB1: My team members were willing to listen to and consider my ideas; SB2: My contributions were important to the group’s success; SB3: I felt included by others in the group; SB4: I felt accepted by others in the group; SB5: I felt like an important member of the group; SB6: I felt like I belonged in computer science and software engineering.

CATME survey CATME (The Comprehensive Assessment of Team Member Effectiveness) is a web-based student group project assessment tool used by 2,200 institutions globally  (Ohland et al., 2012). Instructors can select ques tions from a variety of categories and students pro vide self-reported scores. At the participating institution, CATME is used by the majority of instructors for project grade ad justments. For this study, we chose to use secondar y data collected for CSSE 232 (Computer Architecture I). W e chose this course because it is required for CSSE majors at the institution and is a standard CSSE course. W e were specifically interested in Psychological Saf ety, Team Conflict, and Team Satisfaction and focused our analysis on these categories.

## Results

Sense of belonging Our analysis revealed significant racial difference s in sense of belonging (SB) scores (see Table 1). There was a significant difference in average SB (i.e., the mean of the six SB questions) between White and Black students (p < 0.05) and White and Hispanic students (p < 0.1); average SB was not significant between Whit e and Asian students. Additionally, there were significant diff erences for specific SB questions, with Black and H ispanic students scoring lower than White students. For Bla ck students, there were significant differences for SB1, SB2, SB3, SB4, and SB5 when compared with White students (p < 0.05 for SB1, SB2, SB3, SB4; p < 0.1 for SB5). For Hispanic students, there were significant differenc es for SB1, SB3, and SB4 (p < 0.05 for SB1, SB3; p < 0.1 for SB4). There were no significant gender differences for average SB or for any specific SB question. The interaction effect between race and gender was significant (p < 0.05) for average SB and for all SB questions. Psychological safety, team conflict, and team satisfaction Our analysis revealed significant racial difference s in CATME scores (see Table 1). There was at least  one category that differed between White and each non-White racial group. For Asian students, there was a significant (p < 0.05) difference in Psychological Safety (PS) whe n compared with White students; for Hispanic studen ts, there was a significant difference in PS (p < 0.01) and Team Conflict (p < 0.1); and for Black students, there was a significant difference in all categories (p < 0.05 for PS, Team Conflict; p < 0.01 for Team Satisfaction). There were no significant gender differences and no signi ficant interaction effects between race and gender for any category.

## Discussion and future directions

Our findings did not support H1 and partially supported H2. For H2, we found the most significant differences in sense of belonging and related experience factors b etween Black and White students followed by Hispani c and White students. For specific questions, Black stude nts scored significantly lower for SB1, SB2, SB3, S B4, and SB5, which measured belonging of contributions and belonging of presence with both self-and others-value focus. Hispanic students scored significantly lower for SB 1, SB3, and SB4; notably, all of these were others-value focused.

The lack of significant differences for SB6 is inte resting as it suggests that despite experiencing lo wer sense of belonging in the group project setting, this did not generalize to lower sense of belonging in the field for Black and Hispanic students. Future work could expl ore reasons for this phenomenon. For example, one explanation is that Black and Hispanic students in the participating institution have dedicated comput er science communities for their respective demographic groups  outside of classes that help establish a sense of belonging in the field. Another possibility is that Black and  Hispanic students expect group project experiences  to be negative, and therefore do not extrapolate those experiences to other settings. Aside from Psychological Safety, there were no sign ificant differences between Asian and White students. Future work could examine whether this is  a result of the larger Asian student population (1 3.8%) in computer science compared to the Black (3.0%) and Hispanic (3.4%) populations at the participating institute, the larger Asian population in the tech industry, or ot her factors. This could be investigated by comparin g results between schools or subareas of computer science with different demographic compositions. For H1, we did not find significant gender differen ces for any category. This was surprising as women are notably underrepresented (14.6%) in the partici pating institution’s computer science department (l ower than the 21% average of computer science departments in the United States). Future research could test diff erent theories for this finding. For example, one theory is that woman computer science undergraduate studen ts are accustomed to being “the only one” from previous computer science group projects and activities in high school. Because of our use of secondary data (for CATME) and our choice to keep the SB survey short as participants were not compensated, we did not collect data on socioeconomic background, age, personality traits, and previous group project experience. Future r esearch could examine the impact of these variables  on sense of belonging and how these variables interact with race and gender. Future work could also examine which specific elements of group projects (e.g., team meetings, di viding tasks) affect sense of belonging. Incorporat ing these perspectives could provide a more holistic understa nding of the factors contributing to sense of belon ging in computer science education.

Computer science educators generally agree that hav ing group projects in computer science courses is beneficial (e.g., increased understanding of materi al, employer interest; Lingard & Barkataki, 2011). However, our findings suggest that class group projects may affect underrepresented students more negatively th an their majority counterparts. Undergraduate classes and gr oup work more broadly should contribute to communit y building and equitable outcomes, and introducing el ements that increase disparity in sense of belongin g and psychological safety are a hindrance to achieving this goal. Understanding demographic differences in class group project experiences is a fundamental step toward im proving sense of belonging and increasing represent ation of underrepresented groups in computer science.
