# Exploring Tensions in University Policies and Students Practices on Generative AI

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The integration of Generative AI (GAI) in higher education creates tensions in policy, pedagogy, and ethics. This mixed-methods study analyzes university policies and interviews with master’s students, revealing positive sentiment toward teaching and students but negativity toward GAI, assessment, and ethics. Students report confusion over ethical guidelines and divergent views on GAI’s impact on learning. Findings highlight the need for clearer policies and pedagogical support, addressing contradictions in how GAI affects skill development and education.

## Introduction

The introduction of tools like ChatGPT has sparked conversations in higher education about their benef its and challenges. At first, universities focused on preve nting cheating, creating strict policies to limit t he misuse of Generative AI (GAI) in assessments. Over time, GAI's potential to enhance teaching and learning became clearer, but the policies surrounding its use remain inconsi stent. For instance, while encouraging critical use, many policies also present GAI as problematic in ethical  and academic conte xts. This paper explores these mixed messages by analyzing university policies and interviewing students about their experiences. By combining these perspectives, we uncover how unclear guidelines leave students uncertain and emphasize t he need for clearer, more supportive policies.

## Methods

Quantitative Analysis: We analyzed 36 university AI policies from Denmark,  the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and the United States, sourced from their respective provost websites. Using aspect-based sentiment analysis, we categorized the policies by thematic a spects (GAI, students, teaching, ethics, assessment) and sentiment (positive, neutral, negative). The analys is utilized SetFit (Tunstall et al., 2022), a few-shot learning algorithm that efficiently fine-tunes models with minimal labeled data. This method  achieved accuracy scores of 0.61 for aspect classification and 0.68 for sentiment classification. Qualitative Analysis: Semi-structured interviews with eight master’s students at the Danish University. Students reflected on their use of GAI and percepti ons of university policies. Data was coded to align  with thematic aspects used in quantitative analysis, sup plemented by a "policy" code to capture specific re ferences to guidelines.

## Quantitative findings

The quantitative analysis shows that, overall, sent iments across the five aspects —GAI, assessment, ethics, students, and teaching —tend to be negative, although sentiments toward stu dents and teaching are closer toneutral. In contrast, GAI and ethics lean more towards the negative. A comparison of sentiment sco res across Denmark, the USA, the UK, and the Netherlands reveals clear cross-country differences. For GAI, sentiments are predominantly negative acro ss all countries, indicating a broadly critical perspective on this technology. In the context of a ssessment, sentiments are more balanced. The USA, U K, and the Netherlands display neutral to positive median scores, while Denmark’s sentiment remains negative. Additionally, the UK and the Netherlands show higher variability, reflecting diverse views within these countries. Ethical issues produce the greatest variation across countries, with the UK showing a positive median sentiment, Denmark remaining largely negative, and the USA and  the Netherlands exhibiting mixed, neutral distributions. Sentiments regarding students and teaching are gene rally neutral to positive, with the Netherlands sho wing the most positive sentiment toward students. Teaching i s viewed relatively neutrally in the USA, UK, and t he Netherlands. However, Denmark stands out  with negative median sentiments in both areas, sign aling concerns about the integration of GAI in higher education.

In summary, Denmark consistently exhibits negative median sentiments across most aspects, highlighting challenges or criticisms in the context of GAI, ethics, student engagement, and teaching. In contrast, the UK and the Netherlands display more positive sentiments in specific areas, suggesting regional differences in attitudes and varying levels of acceptance and satisfaction with GAI. Qualitative Findings: A general finding across the interviews was students' fear of cheating when using AI for their assignment s. Students emphasized that this fear motivated them to  read the university’s AI policy (rather than a desire to understand its guidance). They expressed frustration with the vagueness of the policy, which encouraged weighing ethical considerations but failed to provide clear guidelines on when and how AI could be used appropr iately. One student explained that consulting a teacher or advisor about AI use felt risky, as it might raise doubts about their work's originality, even if they ultimately d ecided not to use AI. This uncertainty left student s to interpret the policy on their own, relying on gut feelings to decide whether their AI use crossed ethical boundaries. The confusion about the policy was closely linked t o a lack of education about AI in their curriculum. Students expressed a strong need for someone to hel p them interpret and clarify the policy, as one stu dent explained: Because you haven't received any... teaching, you haven't gotten to know it through the teaching, and you actually don't know what the rules are. (…) Then you should also have some guidelines that you can follow, that people are aware of, so as not to fall off the wagon. And it just makes people careful to use it, because you don't know if  it's actually something you can use or not. They [the teachers] think we use it wrong." (C, p.14) Students proposed that introductory courses could ad dress this gap by teaching the fundamentals of AI alongside other essential academic skills. Offering consistent guidance on how to use AI ethically and effectively could help students feel more informed and confiden t about their choices. Such efforts would not only reduce confusion but also ensure more equitable and policy-compliant use of AI across the student body.

## Discussion

We expect a productive discussion at the conference  around the significance of the mixed messages in t he university policy texts and the Danish students’ confusion about policy requirements. We propose tha t their fear of being accused of cheating is a natural consequence of the general negative senti ment across aspects in the Danish policy texts: If universities are in general skeptical of GAI, then teachers can be expected to be wary of GAI use in assignments. We consider it probable that students in consequence will engage less with GAI in their studies, and therefore not develop the GAI skills necessary for their future professional life. For the other countries in our study, the consistently negative sentiment towards GAI in asse ssment may similarly result in student fear of cheating, with potentially the same effect  of refraining from using GAI in assignments, and thus lack of development of GAI  skills, despite the positive sentiment towards learner use of GAI. In any case,  we suggest that the mixed messages of university policies will leave students unsure how to navigate  teachers’ implicit and explicit expectations and requirements towards their learning.

## Conclusion

This research highlights critical tensions in how u niversities manage GAI integration. Positive attitu des toward students and teaching coexist with restrictive and negative views on GAI itself, creating a challengin g environment for students. To harness GA I’s potential responsibly, universities must provid e clearer guidelines and pedagogical support while addressing contradictions in policy design. This paper contributes to understanding the intersection of technology, ethics, and education, offering actionable insights for policymakers and educators.
