# Teachers’ and Principals’ Views of Interdisciplinary Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Little is known about how teachers and principals view interdisciplinarity at the K12 level. In recent years, we have been working with Israel’s Ministry of Education and ten secondary schools to develop and enact interdisciplinary curricula in the humanities. During this partnership, we conducted a series of twenty-four interviews with teachers and principals, to glean their views of interdisciplinarity. We found that they understood interdisciplinarity as a broad reform with the potential to impact school life in myriad ways. Moreover, their views on the definitions, potential benefits, and ways to enact interdisciplinarity at the K-12 level extended beyond a cognitive lens, to include sociocultural and critical interpretations as well. Ultimately, we believe that teachers and principals are uniquely situated, and that their points of view could contribute to a grounded theory of interdisciplinarity at the K-12 level, which is currently lacking.

Over the past century, interdisciplinary learning at the K-12 level has often been seen as a promising innovation. Yet, notwithstanding many efforts at interdisciplin ary reform, there is scant unbiased theoretical and  empirical research explicating what it is, why it may be worthwhile, or how best to implement it (for a brief historical review see Grossman et al., 2000). Indeed, not much has ch anged since Applebee et al. (2007, p. 1004) noted t hat “scholarship in the field is remarkably underconceptualized”. With an eye towards understanding the meaning of in terdisciplinary learning at the K-12 level, its potential value, and how it is best enacted, this paper presents teachers’ and principals’ views on these questions. The data we analyzed was drawn from an ongoing research-practice partnership (RPP) between our group from the University of [blinded] and Israel’s Ministry o f Education, which may pave the path towards a larg e-scale shift to interdisciplinarity in the humanities across Israeli middle schools. During the first phase of our study, we conducted t wenty-four interviews with twenty teachers and principals representing ten schools, to glean their  views on what interdisciplinarity means in a schoo l context. Most of the existing research on interdisciplinarit y at the K-12 level is rooted in three approaches –  cognitive, sociocultural and critical – which we describe briefly in the Background section. In the subsequent sections, we show how our interviewees simultaneously adopted all three approaches, leading us to conclude that they ought to be viewed as coalescing; and setting the stage f or future efforts to generate a grounded theory of interdisciplinarity at school that takes all three approaches into account.

## Background

Underlying any definition of interdisciplinarity is a particular view of what constitutes a discipline. Three central ways of conceptualizing disciplines can be found in the literature and each bear on interdisciplinarity differently. The cognitive approach, which is focused primarily on knowledge, understanding, and ways of thinking (e.g., Hirst, 2010), views disciplinarity in terms of content and expertise. Bruner, for instance, argued that at the core of each discipline are some fundamental concepts, such as tragedy in literature or number, measure, and probability in science (Bruner, 1960/1999; cf. Schwab, 1964). In another notable example, Wineburg (2001) identified and articulated professional historians’ disciplinary heuristics and unique disposition towards far-removed objects of knowing (cf. Herrenkohl & Polman, 2018). From a cog nitive perspective, an interdisciplinary curriculum could entail investigating complex phenomena that are better understood when viewed simultaneously through multiple disciplinary lenses (e.g. Boix Mansilla et al., 2000).

While the sociocultural approach recognizes that di sciplines are marked by identifiable bodies of knowledge and ways of thinking, it views them as part of a complex system: “mind, culture, history, and the social world [are] interrelated processes that constitute each other.” (Lave, 1991, p. 63) This view considers disciplinary knowledge in light of the identities of the people who create and use it and the human contexts within which it is applied. Viewed in this way, interdisciplinarity would manifest itself first and foremost in the creation of a new community whose members hail from diverse disciplinary ones, which in a K-12 context could comprise teachers of different subjects. Forming the community would require new routines, a shared history and a collective sense of purpose, in addition to intellectual innovation (Grossman et al., 2001). Finally, the critical approach views the discipline s primarily as hierarchical structures that regulat e discourse by determining how people are allowed to use language. Most notably, it was Foucault who argued that ICLS2022 Proceedings 123 © ISLS to partake in a disciplinary discourse, one must “f ulfil certain conditions, in a sense stricter and m ore complex than pure and simple truth… in order to part of a d iscipline, a proposition has to be able to be inscr ibed on a certain type of theoretical horizon” (Foucault, 1981, p. 60; cf. Apple, 2004). By extension, disciplinarity can also determine how financial resources are allocated and  which activities are valued by the institutions th at allocate them (Weingart, 2010). Thus, interdisciplinarity would mark an opportunity to unveil and undermine institutional power structures and empower individuals to engage in activities previously considered illegitimate (Fish, 1989) (1).

Several empirical studies have fleshed out these theoretical approaches. Applebee et al. (2007) adopted a cognitive lens, distinguishing between different levels of conceptual integration that form an “interdisciplinary continuum.” They found a correlation between the de gree of integration and the likelihood that teacher s incorporate discussions and “envisionment-building” into their instruction; but they also warned that there are additional factors at play (p. 1035). Grossman et al. (2001) established an interdisciplinary community of English and History teachers and studied it through a socio cultural lens, to show how the evolution of the com munity’s integrative epistemic discourse was intertwined with interpersonal dynamics and additional cultural dimensions. Brandes and Seixas (2000) used a critical lens to e xplore how a group of teachers navigated the instit utional tensions and inconsistencies between school and uni versity contexts, when studying at an interdiscipli nary program in the humanities. Yet lacking from the exi sting scholarship is an in-depth view of teachers’ and principals’ own perspectives on interdisciplinarity  at school – what they understand it to mean, why t hey are drawn to it, or how they think it is best to implement it. To address this gap in the literature, we asked:

1. How do teachers and principals understand interdisciplinarity?

2. What motivates them to implement interdisciplinary curricula?

3. How do they believe interdisciplinarity should be enacted in school contexts?

## Context

In 2019, we partnered with Israel’s Ministry of Education for a four-year Research-Practice Partnership (Coburn & Penuel, 2016) with two goals in mind. First, we s et out to develop, implement and test new interdisc iplinary curricula in the humanities at Israeli middle schoo ls. Second, we were interested in further conceptua lizing interdisciplinary learning, understanding its uniqu e features, and perhaps most importantly, articulat ing its affordances and constraints in educational contexts. Because we hope to make contributions to practice and theory in an iterative process, a Design-Based Implementation Research (DBIR) approach was selected (Fishman et al., 2013). We commenced by recruiting ten middle schools, which had already begun implementing interdisciplinary programs or were intent on doing so soon. For the f irst iteration of our study, each school sent sever al representatives to a professional development (PD) course in which we discussed some theoretical background; provided scaffolds for each team to develop a concr ete plan of action to implement in 2021/2; and enco uraged each team to experiment with a small-scale pilot implementation (2).

## Participants

In this paper, we present findings from twenty-four interviews conducted in two rounds, with representatives of each of the ten schools in our project. The schools  represented a variety of sizes, socioeconomic leve ls, and academic achievement levels. The practitioners we i nterviewed ranged from second-year teachers to seas oned principals.

## Data collection and analysis

Our data sources included recordings and fieldnotes  from all PD sessions; artifacts produced by participants, including a set of lesson plans and a summative poster by each school team; and interviews, which provided the bulk of the data for this paper. For each of the ten schools, we interviewed either the principal or vice-principal, and one teacher. The first interview focused on interdisciplinarity in K-12 in general, whereas the second round, which only included the teachers, focused on their experiences at the PD course, and how their views and plans had developed throughout the year (3). Interviews were semi-structured and were conducted via ZOOM, recorded, and transcribed. We used a thematic approach (Braun  & Clarke, 2006) to analyze the transcripts, genera ting a detailed portrait of their views of interdisciplina rity, their motivations for implementing it, and th e changes to school life that they think it might entail. Where possible, the interview data was triangulated with other data sources to increase validity and broaden our interpretive horizons. ICLS2022 Proceedings 124 © ISLS

## Findings

For our interviewees, interdisciplinarity extends beyond the walls of the classroom and can impact life at school in myriad ways. Our analysis culminated in a distin ction between three spaces that serve as metaphors for the kinds of dynamics that were described: the classroom, the teachers’ room, and the principal’s office. Inside the classroom How do teachers and practitioners perceive what goe s on in class during an interdisciplinary lesson? I n a traditional disciplinary setting, the content of a curriculum is often organized around the big ideas that shape a specific discipline’s discourse. But in lieu of the typical disciplinary structure, how are interdisciplinary curricula organized? Our interviewees described three interdi sciplinary foci: historical, topical, and identity-centered. Describing the historical focus, one teacher explained that “for every grade we choose three disciplines, and the central discipline is always history… [the others] could be literature and civics, [or] geography and literature” (Esti) (4). The topical focus invites learners to investigate a single topic from multiple disciplinary perspectives; for instance: “We discussed nature, the connection between humankind and nature. Really nice. We learn ed a poem […] and the Jewish attitude towards nature and the environment. We also learned about social activism in Jerusalem” (Adelle). Another teacher described a un it that was organized around an investigation of students’ identities: “each day we ask a specific question about identity, and then all of the disciplines are learned through the prism of that identity-focused question” (Neta).

Integrating the disciplines in these three ways – w hich are not mutually exclusive – is intended to highlight the connections across knowledge that wou ld otherwise remain fragmented, rendering it more meaningful. One interviewee explained: “[interdisciplinarity is] when the students get the coherence of the whole story, [when] they understand that now we’re talkin g about racism and they find it in the story we’re reading, [and] in human history, and understand what Judaism’s attitude towards it is.” (Adelle) For another interviewee

– an experienced principal – the difference between fragmented bits of information and interconnected knowledge

is equivalent to the difference between rote learning and learning for understanding: “shifting from a student who knows s/he needs to memorize the material to one who understands, who really understands the topic and sees it from multiple perspectives. That, from my point of view, is the [interdisciplinary] approach.” (Sivan) In addition to content, the practitioners we interv iewed also described other ways in which a shift to interdisciplinarity could impact classroom dynamics. By default, abandoning disciplinarity in Israel m eans departing from the mandatory curricula, which are o verseen by the Ministry of Education’s Pedagogical Secretariat. This comes with a renewed sense of freedom, which Ari, an experienced principal, called “complete autonomy”, allowing teachers to do “whatever they want.” While this principal wasn’t happy with all his teachers’ choices, he still called this aspect of interdisciplinary learning “a huge success.” Another principal explained how this sense of freedom that derives from the lack of oversight inherent to interdisciplinarity affords a practice that he called “to flow,” allowing the lesson to develop in unpredictable ways that aren’t constrained by disciplinary boundaries: “all of my pedagogical sensors light up when a discussion in class flows and flows… looking back, […] these [interdisciplinary] lessons were much mor e interesting and instructive.” (Amir) Another teac her reiterated this: We start talking about refugees in Israel [but] acc ording to the curriculum we designed, next lesson is meant to be about the French Revolution… dammit, there is no matriculation, we just had an amazing discussion about immigrants’ childre n, which is important from an ethical perspective, I go with it, happily! So, it will take some time, OK… fourteen-year-old’s finally express their opinion about something… you want me to interrupt that? It’s anti-democratic (Alex).

Some interviewees also tied interdisciplinarity to other pedagogical reforms that they hoped to implement. For example, one teacher explained how the interdisciplinary curriculum they designed was primarily intended to grant students more agency: “For us, interdisciplinarity came after we decided to focus on student proactivity as a pedagogical goal.” (Neta) Another principal expla ined that “interdisciplinary learning at its best i s something that is very, very independent for students. They know how to do it on their own and in groups. The teacher is a mediator not a lecturer…” (Sivan). One teacher expl ained that while inquiry learning was not necessari ly contingent upon interdisciplinarity, the latter is “much more inviting, because it opens things up.” (Jen) Some examples of project-based alternatives to traditional forms of teaching and learning that interviewees associated with interdisciplinarity included field trips, creative art projects, recording a podcast, and producing books. A final feature of the classroom dynamics that our interviewees tied to interdisciplinary curricula is that it affords more meaningful learning for students. deeper relationship between the teacher and the cla ss. Several interviewees perceived interdisciplinary learning as an opportunity to focus on age-appropriate transferable skills, at the expense of discipline-specific content. One principal explained that there is not enough correspondence between the official disciplinary curricula overseen by the Ministry and students’ stage of cognitive de velopment; whereas “when we’re talking about interdisciplinary pedagogy it means we aren’t looki ng at each subject, we are looking at what skills t he learner needs in the middle school.” (Dvir) Ron explained t hat while interdisciplinary settings afford an oppo rtunity to focus on skills, the nature of these skills is similar to those taught in traditional disciplinary lessons: “it’s the same skills: reading, writing, comparing texts.” In cont rast, Neta described a unique skill that is inheren tly interdisciplinary: Once you connect the [multiple] disciplines, you understand complexity. If you bring a literary work that illuminates the aspects of dealing with a particular issue, then history will gain depth […] the connectedness allows us to deepen students’ perspectives. A lot of times at the year’s end [students] themselves tell us that they acquire d diverse perspectives on events or phenomena, they tell us that they learned to look at the world in a more complex way. For some educators, the main reason for adopting an interdisciplinary approach is because students are bored with disciplinary content or unable to relate to it: “I [also] teach history, and the student can’t connect with that.” (Esti) Along the same lines, one principal e xplained that the primary motivator for an interdis ciplinary curriculum is to make learning more relevant to stu dents’ lives: “for it to annoy them, make them happ y, do something to them… they may not remember the details, but they will remember the values that derive fr om it, the things that are relevant to their lives.” (Siva n) Another principal explained that interdisciplina ry learning is more relevant and more interesting, because “reality is interdiscipl inary. You look at the Supreme Court [for example]; understanding what happens there at any g iven moment requires an understanding of civics, hi story, and sociology.” (Amir) Some teachers and principals identified interdisciplinarity as a setting that can also contribute to students’ emotional wellbeing. According to one principal, “t he main benefit is that it will be good for kids to  come to school. It’s considered almost taboo or rude to tal k about the enjoyment of learning, the pleasure tha t Socrates talks about.” From his perspective, “connecting lea rning to kids’ lives will make it more interesting and by extension more pleasurable.” (Amir) This idea related to another theme that emerged from our analysis. Some of the interviewees explained that interdisciplinarity enables teachers to forge deeper, more meaningful, relationships with their students, due to the greater number of t eaching hours dedicated to interdisciplinary curric ula, which essentially pools resources from multiple subjects:  “…I get to see these students for all their diffic ulties, complexities, successes… these connections forged over time transform me into a meaningful figure for them.” (Esti) Teachers’ room Being a teacher extends beyond the classroom. The practitioners that we interviewed described various ways in which enacting a new interdisciplinary curriculum might relate more broadly to a teacher’s professional identity and daily routines. For instance, one interviewee discussed how teacher training programs cultivate a disciplinary identity among trainees, which can be difficult to apply in an interdisciplinary setting. Others also identified a similar tension – when they assume a disciplinary identity, teachers have a sense of confidence grounded in their expertise; but at the same time, this limits opportunities to learn new content: This year I began teaching literature, which is something I’ve never done [because I am a history teacher], and I really enjoyed it. On the other hand, this raises the question of professionalism… I am the only person at school with an MA in history and I think I’m a good history teacher [so why should others teach history?]. There is a tension here. (Jason) What characterizes the professional identity of interdisciplinary teachers? Given the requirement to teach content outside their realm of expertise, a teacher must be “open to learn, an autodidact, creative, think outside the box… there needs to be openness and desire, it won’t wor k with everyone. For a teacher who’s been in the sy stem for thirty years it could cause an identity crisis.” (E sti) Others mentioned a sense of professional lonel iness that accompanies interdisciplinarity, which isn’t backed by any formal body in the Ministry of Education: “I am doing something so lonely… When I talk about it with the head of History or Literature [at the Ministry] the y are ICLS2022 Proceedings 126 © ISLS shocked, so who’s backing me?” (Adelle) Another teacher explained that the interdisciplinary approach requires a different disposition towards students: For my professional identity the success of the interdisciplinary program is a dream… if there are teachers that think that children, teens […] don’t have a deep inner world or well-formed or interesting views, a teacher who believes that is unsuited for interdisciplinary teaching. Really. Interdisciplinary teaching is for teachers who believe that each student has depth, curiosity, an opinion […] whoever believes that is suited for interdisciplinarity. (Alex) Almost all the practitioners we interviewed discussed the process of developing the interdisciplinary curriculum at length, identifying it as a unique experience that had an impact on their day-to-day lives at school. One teacher with three years’ experience teaching interdisciplinary curricula described the norm at her school: We sit and build the curriculum; each teacher in th e community comes from a different discipline, and then according to the topic we choose, everyone brings content from their own world… no year is like the previous one, because the curriculum is always being updated, and if there are new teachers I believe everyone needs to leave their mark, because otherwise it’s like “let’s find a lesson plan online and teach it”, which is worthless… (Esti) Designing the new curricula requires effort on beha lf of the teachers, who don’t always feel they’ve r eceived adequate training: “no one taught me how to design an integrated curriculum.” (Adelle) Indeed, one pri ncipal identified curriculum design as a major hurdle for enacting a new interdisciplinary curriculum: “[it] requires intense collaboration between teachers… how do you indicate that they’re doing something amazing that not everyone cando?” (Amir) Given that the content in the humanities can be controversial, designing the curricula also generates an ethical discourse among teachers,  whoneed to decide what’s worth teaching and what is offlimits: “[the conflict with the] Palestinians is an explosive topic… we’re not going to agree, as colleagues, we’re not going to agree; we won’t form a clear message on this issue […], which is fine, I love it!” (Alex) With regards to this tension, another interviewee suggested that teachers be allowed to choose their own path: “not everything will be dictated [to teachers]; there will be suggestions, but they won’t be dictated.” (Ron) Designing the curriculum is typically done collabor atively, meaning that enacting an interdisciplinary curriculum requires intense teamwork, which tends to revolve around a weekly meeting that is seen as essential: “there is no such thing as cancelling the [interdis ciplinary] staff meeting, it’s our ‘holy of holies. ’” (Noam) Sometimes, collaboration can lead to power struggles between teachers of different subjects, which one principal called “the discipline wars.” (Ari) Some of the teachers also drew a parallel between the collaboration among the teachers on the team and the ways in which students interact in class: “I do the same with the teachers as I demand from students. Meaning I form a group, make sure there is all kinds of enrichment and camaraderie, and that it is experiential… meetings are significant, not just coming together to chat.” (Noam) What purpose do interdisciplinary staff meetings serve? One of the teachers discussed emotional support, explaining that at a certain point “it really feels like family, you can share, everything said there stays there, I feel safe to vent… it’s totally open.” (Alex) Other teac hers pointed out that they sometimes need their col leagues’ professional guidance: “if I am a language arts tea cher I will teach history if need be, but I will fi rst sit with someone who is more professional.” (Sarah) Others pointed out that collaboration affords an equal distribution of the workload: “in the end it pays off, because I wrote a [lesson] plan, and six others each wrote one, so we already have seven lesson plans.” (Esti) Finally, one principal described how collaboration offers “a new group to belong to. I no longer belong to the literature staff, or bible, or history, I am part of a development staff … everyone contributes something that the others don’t have” (Dvir). Organizationally, Israeli schools face several acute challenges that can be tied to interdisciplinarity. The interviewees identified three specific organization al problems that might be overcome by enacting an interdisciplinary curriculum: a surplus of subjects, a lack of teachers, and a lack of facetime for each teacher per class. One principal in a school with a well-establ ished interdisciplinary curriculum explained: “if I  teach disciplinarily, I am talking about twelve subjects for each Israeli student… so I ask myself, how is m eaningful learning possible [with so many subjects]?” (Ari) A nother teacher suggested that her principal chose interdisciplinarity because he thought: “I don’t have enough literature teachers, so I cando humanities [instead] and the history teacher can teach literature too.” (Adelle) Another principal also noted that “initially our goal was organizational, to give humanities teachers six hou rs [in each class] instead of the normal two.” (Siv an) She explained: “Two hours a week isn’t enough; a cancellation here, a field trip there, another event, and there is no connection [between the teacher and the class] … That was the need at the beginning.” Leveraging the interdisciplinary curriculum to over come these challenges can require changes to the schedule, such as shifting from single-period to do uble-period lessons, yet along with the excitement about the organizational affordances of implementing a new interdisciplinary curriculum, interviewees noted challenges to planning a new schedule: I, right now, have no idea  how I’m going to do it, really… I have teachers wh o supposedly have blank hours in their timetables, or I give some classes too many hours. This is where I am stuck.” (Natalie) Another problem that one teacher identified was that interdisciplinarity might serve as an instrument to accelerate the decline of the humanities in Israel’s education system: “the school has to be very honest not to take teaching hours intended for Literature and History and allocate them to Math and English.” (Adelle) Yet another challenge was the increase in workload that worried some of the interviewees. In response, most of them suggested freeing interdisciplinary teachers from some of the ir normal responsibilities and compensating them fo r developing the new curricula. In two cases, we were told about teachers who are paid the equivalent of a weekly teaching hour, which they dedicate to staff meetings and curriculum design. One teacher had another idea: “in my opinion… [they should] give us two days off [every year] so we can write lesson plans and develop new ideas, because there is no textbook, and usually it’s on our own time. ## Discussion and conclusion

The teachers and principals we interviewed understo od interdisciplinarity broadly, as an innovation th at could have a major impact on life at school. And while we  distinguished between the classroom, the teachers’  room, and the principal’s office for analytic purposes, our interviewees described intricate ways in which they overlap with one another. For instance, the lack of institutional supervision was viewed from the principal’s office as an advantage (less restrictions) and a drawback (less backing). In the classroom, it crucially allows teachers to “flow”; whereas in the teachers’ room, it was tied to feelings of loneliness and disempowerment. Based on this example and others like it, one key takeaway was that teach ers and principals view interdisciplinarity as a br oad and complex reform that cannot be contained within a single part of school life. We also found that teachers and principals view int erdisciplinarity simultaneously through all three theoretical lenses introduced in the background section. For instance, consider how teachers pool their hours when teaching interdisciplinarily. This shift in teacher s’ timetables has profound implications that extend  across all three approaches. Cognitively, interdisciplinarity in this context affords drawing connections between  subjects typically learned separately (Stevens et al., 2005), which interviewees saw as an opportunity to cultivate students’ understanding. Socioculturally, it lays the foundation for new types of interactions between teachers and students that afford deeper, more meaningful relationships. Finally, viewed through a critical lens, changing t eachers’ timetables in this way is nearly impossible, due to  pressure from parents, teachers’ unions or governm ent supervision. This example, and several others that emerged in our analysis, indicated that the three t heoretical lenses of interdisciplinarity actually overlapped and interacted in educators' views. In future, this could form the basis of a grounded theory of interdisciplinarity at school, which is currently lacking. Almost three decades ago, Lytle and Cochran-Smith (1994, p. 24) argued that “Missing from the field of research on teaching, then, are the voices of teachers themselves, the questions teachers ask, and the interpretive frames that teachers use to understand and improve their own classroom practices.” This paper shows that teachers and principals understand interdisciplinarity in mu ltiple ways, which overlap, complement, and contrad ict one another. It follows that the benefits they associat e with interdisciplinarity extend across school, to uching on content, students’ wellbeing, teachers’ professiona l identity, and even their terms of employment. Fin ally, enactment requires meticulous planning, and must overcome many hurdles.
