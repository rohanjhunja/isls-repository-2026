# A Computational Thinking and Equity-Centered Urban Preservice Residency Program’s Impact on STEM Teachers

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Situated in an urban teacher residency program at the University of California, Los Angeles, that aims to prepare high school teachers for integrating computational thinking (CT) into equity-focused STEM teaching, this paper investigates the impact of the STEM+C3 program on preservice teachers' conceptualizations and integration of CT in classrooms. Our analysis of 10 interviews suggests that the STEM+C3 program had an overall positive impact on teaching residents’ (TRs) understandings of equity-centered practices in CT pedagogy with a few teachers demonstrating shallower understandings. Most TRs were able to characterize identity and positionality while only a few were able to characterize diversity, inclusion, and social justice in their lesson plans. The findings also contribute to a growing body of literature on how programs may support CT integration in STEM equity pedagogy.

## Introduction

Over the last two decades, the integration of compu tational thinking (CT) in science, technology, engi neering, and mathematics (STEM) education has been gaining m omentum (Tang et al., 2020; Weintrop et al., 2016; Weintrop et al., 2021; Wing, 2006). Moreover, ever since Barr and Stephenson’s (2011) call for more CT integration within different subject areas within K-12 education, there has been an increasing prolifer ation of studies on integrating CT within STEM education. Mo reover, we have seen, as a field,  a burgeoning of such programs that integrate CT into STEM in teacher preservice education (Tang et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022). The current literature is replete with empirical studie s that suggest that pre-service teachers can and do respond favorably to CT training within STEM preservice programs. Thi s bodes well for K-12 students who will be expected to be knowledgeable of, and to have some d emonstrable skills in, CT thinking and application.  While the literature is relatively rich with information about the impact of participation in such preservice programs, it is largely silent on programmatic design. Even less  has been discussed concerning how such programmati c integration is perceived and experienced by preserv ice teachers. What is needed is a clo se examination of the relationship of these programs to the experiences o f preservice teachers. In this paper, we aim to ope n that conversation by offering the preliminary findings of our inquiry for consideration.

## Conceptual framework

The STEM+C3 program focuses on equity-centered CT-integrated STEM by drawing on culturally relevant pedagogy (Ladson-Billings, 1995) and its integration with CT (Kafai et al., 2020; Scott et al., 2015). STEM+C3 emphasizes the importance of incorporating students' cultural references in all aspects of learning, which involves cultural knowledge, prior experiences, frames of re ference, and diverse performance styles. In additio n, the program conceptualizes the integration of CT by uti lizing coding and computational tools as a means to explore and address issues relevant to students’ communitie s or cultural backgrounds while bridging cultural k nowledge with computational and technological skills.

This paper aims to explore STEM+C3’s impact on supp orting teaching residents’ (TRs) becoming equity-centered CT-integrated STEM educators by examining the followin g questions: a) In what ways did the program support TRs’ becoming equity-centered CT educat ors? b) How did the program contribute to TRs’ developing conceptions of equity-centered CT pedagogy? c) In what ways, if any, did the program support TRs’ development of their pedagogical practices for equi ty-centered CT pedagogy? d) In what ways did the program support TRs’ integrating and examining equity, dive rsity, inclusion, social justice issues, identity, and positionality in equity-centered CT pedagogy?

## Methods

STEM+C3 is an urban teacher residency program withi n the teacher education program at the University o f California, Los Angeles (UCLA) that seeks to prepare equity-centered STEM teachers to work with underserved high school students in the public school districts in Los Angeles. The conceptual structure of STEM+C3 involves the integration of three critical C’s: 1) computati onal thinking practices; b) computer science princi ples; and c) community of practice to foster a sustainable and s ystemic approach for developing equity-centered STEM teachers who understand and leverage CT in their te aching practices. The program has a two-year cohort model approach for future teachers. In year 1, TRs comple te master’s coursework on teaching methods, coding skills, and computational thinking as well as a series of p rofessional development workshops on integrating CT  and equity into their instruction and pedagogy. Concurr ently, TRs begin student teaching under the mentors hip of an experienced partner teacher, staffed at a school partnered with the residency program. The partner teachers mentor the TRs following a gradual release of responsibility model (Fisher & Frey, 2013). In year 2, TRs begin teaching full-time while completing their master’s capstone project. In an effort to explore TR's perceptions of, and experiences in, the program, we invited all 16 TRs in the second cohort of this program to participate in interviews. These TRs started STEM+C3 in the 2021-2022 school year and have already finished their master’s cours ework, now full-time teachers at their respective schools. In total, 10 TRs, six math and four science teachers, participated in this study. The TRs who are include d in this study, are representative demographically (e.g., education, ethnicity) of the larger cohort of 16. Their names have been replaced for anonymity.

Data sources Our data included 30-minute individual interviews in the winter of 2023. The interviews involved teachers talking through, and reflecting on CT-integrated and equity-centered lesson plans. TRs were asked to submit an equity and CT integrated lesson plan that they had recently taught as part of the interview process. ## Results

In what follows, we offer TR perceptions of their e xperiences with STEM+C3. We begin by examining the program's impact on how the TRs view equity followe d by a description of their views of the program's impact on how they think of CT. We close with a few comments about how the findings from this effort contribute to the field.

Varied levels of teachers’ articulation of CT and equity integration Most teachers articulated equity conceptions and integrations regarding identity and positionality but only a few were able to extend this articulation to inclusivity, diversity, and justice. Most teachers were able to express how they conceptualize and integrate equity and how it can be integrated into their teaching practice to some degree. When asked about how the STEM+C3 has impacted her a round identity and positionality, Grace responded that “STEM+C3 showed me, personal stuff are assets to bring into the space and it humanizes you.” She also talked about how equity was integrated into her lesson saying that “a lot of [students] put their ethnicity, or, I’m an older sibling, or I work this kind of jo b, or I’m an immigrant, or I am LGBTQ… so I feel it  invited them to be their whole selves.” In this excerpt, Grace demonstrated the integration of identity into her pedagogy. When asked about her understanding of identity and positionality, Catherine said “Just a couple of weeks ago I was telling Hannah how, I know this sounds silly, but before the program, I didn’t really know what identity was.” Sam said “I think STEM+C3 really helped me to think about who I am as a teacher, but also as a person. It really helped me to think about the positions of power that I’m in.” These excerpts point to how, for the most part, the program successfully impacted teachers’ conceptions and integration of identity and positionality withi n their classrooms. However, only a few TRs were ab le to talk specifically about how their classrooms integrate inclusivity, diversity, and social justice. Some teachers were able to articulate CT understandings and integration Anna, who demonstrated a developing understanding o f CT and equity and how to integrate them into her math classes, said, “I never really thought about CT nor  equity, I think because maybe I didn’t see it in m y own education so it wasn’t something that was  at the forefront of my mind. So I learned mostly ev erything from the program.” When asked about CT integration, Anna described her interaction with her students: “Okay, so what is decomposing? And [the students are] like, breaking it down… I said all right, let’s break down this expression… the numbers, variables, operations.” Another teacher-trainee, Alice, also iterated that STEM+C3 was her “first time hearing about CT.” When asked about which parts of the program helped her u nderstand CT, Alice shared, “it’s very helpful to s ee people run through an example lesson and have you engage with it as if you were a student.” These excerpts highlight how most teachers experien ced a positive impact on their CT and equity conceptions; concepts previously unfamiliar to many of the teachers prior to joining the program. The program’s focus on equity exposed TRs to equity issues such as identity, positionality, diversity, inclusion, and social justice. We found that for most of the TRs, thinking about e quity issues was generally limited to their persona l experiences.

A few teachers expressed limited articulation CT conceptions and integration Three teachers' comments reflected a limited program impact on their understanding and integration of CT. Camilla emphasized, “I just still don’t feel comfor table with CT.” She talked more about her qualms saying, “I don’t really feel I retained any of the information that I learned in those [CS] classes… I  would feel extremely not confident if I had to teach someone h ow to use R.” When asked about how she would define  CT, Camilla responded, “I still don’t feel like I have a full picture of what [CT] is.” Jessica explained, “I have no idea if this is a CT lesson… I feel like I haven’t really thought about CT in a while, and the different components that it takes to make a CT lesson, just because I feel s till, even right now, I feel like I’m always just t rying to get my lessons done.” These excerpts suggest that with respect to understanding and feeling efficacious about CT integration, the program did not have a strong programmatic impa ct. Some TRs struggled to learn a CS language, such  as R, and found the workload heavy during the first year of teaching. The TRs, however, did point out, withi n their interviews, programmatic features that they suggest ed had an impact on them such as the cohort model a nd community of practice.

## Scholarly significance

This study explored the impact of STEM+C3 on preser vice teacher candidates’ conceptions and integratio n of equity and CT in STEM. In terms of equity practices, our study found that the TRs believe that the pro gram had an overall positive impact on their conceptions and integration of equity in their pedagogy. A majority of teachers reported developing understanding of equity-centered practices in CT pedagogy with a few of the  TRs demonstrating shallower understandings. Most were a ble to generally characteri ze how they integrate their conceptions of identity and positionality in the cl assroom and  few were able to characterize diversit y, inclusion, and social justice.

Our inquiry revealed that three teachers perceived that STEM+C3 had a limited impact on CT conceptions and integration. In addition, our inquiry revealed areas of improvement that may help us to iterate the design of the program to have more impact, includin g the difficulty of learning a computer language. A  possible programmatic improvement might be the addition of b lock-based programming languages as a stepping stone to text-based programming languages. Additionally, many TRs  expressed how they were overwhelmed not just by the program, but also by other responsibilities that come with certification and a higher first-year workload. Some teachers directly attributed their overwhelming workload to their lack of explicit CT instruction while at the same time expressing how they would like to integrate CT more in-depth. A potential improvement for our program is to create lesson resource banks for teachers to more easily and efficiently adapt and design CT-integrated lessons.
