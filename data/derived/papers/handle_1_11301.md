# Inclusive Space to Amplify Youth Transformative Agency in Educational Policy Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents the Learning Lab intervention to address systemic injustice in a predominantly White school district in the U.S. Specifically, we focus on how a diverse group of students, parents, educators, and administrators fostered students' transformative agency amidst contradictions and the diverse motives members brought to the Learning Lab to create district level policy named Protocol for Addressing Harassment and Discrimination. The preliminary analysis indicated that students’ transformative agency was a collective process mediated by cultural perceptions about roles, rules, and norms. Lab members amplified students' transformative agency by: 1) challenging the expert/novice and adult/student dichotomies, and 2) cultivating a collaborative culture. We conclude the paper with students’ reflections on their learning and development during the Learning Lab intervention.

## Introduction

Traditionally, educational decision making tends to  be dominated by adults' perspectives over those of  children and youth. Due to various forms of adultism, racism, perceived expertise and dichotomy of experts/novice, youth’s voice tends to be at the periphery of educational decision making (Bertrand et al., 2023; Calabrese-Barton & Tan, 2019). In this paper, we present a school-community-university partnership in New England U.S. through which a diverse group of students, parents/community members, administrators through a collaborated to create a district level policy that centers students’ perspectives through Learning Lab intervention (Bal et al., 2016). The purpose of this paper is to provide a preliminary explorati on on how the Learning Lab members fostered inclusive space to amplify youth’s transformative agency as legitimate policy actors in the design of educational policy, which in the case of this paper is a Protocol for Addressing Harassment and Discrimination.

## Theoretical framework

This paper is guided by the theoretical framework of transformative agency within cultural-historical perspectives (Haapsaari et al., 2016; Kajamaa & Kumpulainen, 2019). Transformative agency transcends individual capability or characteristics within specific here-and-now actions. Rather, it is a collective change effo rt—a dynamic, complex, relational, and mediated cultural process that continues to evolve and is maintained over time (Haapsaari et al., 2016; Kajamaa & Kumpulainen, 2019). It is also a continuous, non-linear, and tension-laden process, always related to the socio-material context and practical action. Transformati ve agency is evident in  activities where people break away from a given frame of action and/ or take initiatives to transform their activities (Gutiérrez & Calabrese Barton, 2015; Virkkunen, 2006). Instead o f adapting to their environment, agentic actors mak e efforts to transform existing activity and practices for personal or col lective ends (Haapsaari & Kumpulainen, 2019). In this paper, transformative agency is indicated by h ow the Learning Lab members departed from the presc ribed frame of action, such as who has the right to educa tional policy design, who are the experts, and who is knowledgeable, amidst multiple contradictions (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2019; Bertrand et al., 2017).

## Research methodology

Contexts Aleph School District (ASD) in the U.S. is a predom inantly White school district serving approximately  5,000 students across six different towns. As the demogra phic of the school and community grows diverse, so is the prevalence of incidents of discriminat ion and harassment harming historically marginalize d students such as racially minority students, LGBTQIA+ students, reli gious minority students, and students with disabili ties. However, these incidents were not systematically re ported, recorded, and addressed. As a result, many incidents of discrimination and harassment go unreported, mis categorized, and subsequently unaddressed perpetuat ing a cycle of distrust, harm, mental health challenges, and a lack of belonging among historically marginal ized students. The absence of a system to mediate the reporting and resolutions of incidents raised questions about the district's commitment in addressing discrimination and harassment and whether such issues are being adequately identified and addressed. At the same time, in 2019, the state passed a senate bill mandating anti-discrimination protections for students in public schools and requiring districts to develop specific policies to address these issues. However, three years went by, and the district did not have anything in place. Wh ile for the district, the lack of protocol may put the district at the risk of being sued by parents, for families of color, the absence of a system to address the problem is one of the many “blissful ig norance to the everyday discriminations faced by Bl ack and Brown students” (Parent-personal communication). The school district leader ship at that time and some community members were ready to address the problem. In 2022, the author and the district’s Director of Equity began the collaboration.

## Data collection: Learning lab methodology

We employed the Learning Lab methodology (Bal et al., 2016; 2018) (see Figure 1) to guide the design p rocess of the protocol. Learning Lab was an inclusive prob lem-solving methodology that positioned local stakehold ers such as parents, community members, and students, p articularly those from historically marginalized backgrounds, as well as educators and administrator s as legitimate knowledge producers and experts who se perspectives are critical in the creation of school systems such as a protocol to address bias-based discrimination. Lab members met biweekly for 75-90 minutes co-facilitated by the author and the district’s Direct or of Equity. Lab members consisted of three students, two district administr ators, two teachers, five assistant/ principals, one special education case manager, and three parents/community members. Ensuring diverse voice was critical. The first four sessions focused on expanding the compos itions of the Lab members. It is important to note that the focus on transformative agency organically occurred  as Lab members navigated tensions and conflicts (S annino et al., 2016) pertaining to whose voice and expertise mattered in understanding and solving the status quo. ## Data analysis

Data for this paper was obtained from 18 biweekly L earning Lab meetings. We transcribed the  meetings using transcription software and verified their accuracy. To analyze the transcripts, we employed Dedoose data analysis software, utilizing a deductive coding approach (Cr eswell & Creswell, 2018)  within the framework of transformative agency as proposed by Kajamaa and Ku mpulainen (2019). Our analysis began by creating th ree primary codes —deviating, switching, and transfiguring —based on this framework. We then developed child codes within each primary category to better unders tand how these concepts were manifested in the data. These child codes were inductively derived from the data.  For the “Deviating” category, examples of child co des were challenging power imbalances in lab composition, na vigating existing perceptions and practices regardi ng expertise, and voicing frustration regarding power imbalances. Under the “Switching” category, child c odes included creating a safe space for students, indicating allyship with students, and restructuring the group. Finally, for the “Transfiguring” category, child codes invol ved expanding cultures of learning and collaboratio n at the district level and fostering students' voices. Afte r coding the data, we categorized the codes and org anized them into themes, which preliminary results presented below.

## Result

The purpose of this paper is to examine how the Lea rning Lab members facilitated an inclusive space to  foster youth’s transformative agency as legitimate policy actors in designing the Protocol for Addressing Harassment and Discrimination. The preliminary results focus on the collective process (Haapsaari et al., 2016;), wherein Lab members departed from traditional notions of expert s/novices, insiders/outsiders, and adults/students (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2019) to create an inclusive space th at centered students’ transformative agency. Severa l initial findings indicate that Learning Lab members called out injustices that some participants brought with them to the group. Members also cultivated collaborative cultur es where everyone was a learner (Fullan et al., 201 8). The results section concludes with students' reflections on their transformative experiences. Challenging adultism and expert/novice dichotomy Creating the Protocol for Addressing Harassment and Discrimination has the potential to disrupt the established bureaucratic routines of district and school admini strators. During Learning Lab meetings, some admini strators defended their existing practices, asserting that t he district’s bullying policies were effective and that a new protocol was unnecessary. This perspective conflict ed with those of other Lab members, including stude nts who had firsthand experiences of race-based discrimination and harassment. Given their pe rceived authority and network within the district, these administrators posed not only significant barriers to the team’s progress but also harm to the students. Recognizing these tensions, o ther Learning Lab members —particularly adults such as teachers and administrators —challenged the perceived expert ise of these individuals. They addressed the normative knowledge, power, and age dynamics that h indered the meaningful inclusion of student voices and perpetuated bureaucratic thinking. For instance, one teacher, Ms. Virginia, directly addressed the administrators: I don't feel like we're getting a lot of space in t his meeting to talk about the things that we're concerned about. I feel like the administrative per spective is that you guys have it figured out period. We can't get our heads across. And I hope I  can speak candidly. But that's how I felt in the last few meetings.

Here, Ms. Virginia, a teacher, leveraged her positi onality as an adult “inside the system” and her str ong commitment to social justice to remind her colleagu es of the harm caused by their reluctance to create  a policy specifically addressing harassment and  discrimination. Similarly, the District’s Director of Equity utilized his position as a senior leader to remind the Learning Lab members about the necessity of the protocol for the district, more particularly for historically marginalized students and families: The law and the systems we have in place benefit certain people. We have known over and over that laws and systems are created without having pe ople from historically marginalized identities in mind. I just want to put that back on  the table. We have a system, but clearly that system is not working. And if it is working, it's working for a certain kind of people and identity. Here, the district’s Director of Equity explicitly identified that the district had failed to address the needs of historically marginalized students and communities.  The contradiction arising from the differing motiv es of the Lab members mediated their commitment not only to protect student members but also to create space for students to share their perspectives throughout the Learning Lab. Cultivating collaborative cultures The Learning Lab fostered an environment where memb ers could learn from and with each other. For insta nce, we modeled collaborative learning by co-planning and co-facilitating meetings. We allocated time at the end  of each session for members to brainstorm the next meeting’s agenda. Rather than imposing goals or directives, we used prompts to guide members in collaboratively so lving issues. For example, when recruiting Lab memb ers, we started with a small group of six people and pos ed questions such as, “Who do you think should be included in this team?” or “Who is the protocol for?” This approach demonstrated that Lab members were experts in their own contexts and capable of finding solutions to their challenges. This modeling was gradually adopted by other members. Some, in particular, prioritized regularly  checked in with students, inviting them to share t heir ideas. Over time, this practice became embedded in the Lab ’s culture. Gloria, a parent, and Maggie, an assist ant principal, often redirected attention to students b y saying, “I want to hear from the students. What do you thin k about…?” Similarly, Ms. Virginia challenged the dominance of  administrative voices, as described earlier. This collective commitment to justice helped create a safe space for students to contribute. This inclusive culture became evident when Julie, a student, identified a critical gap in the draft protocol. She pointed out that the protocol assumed students were always the perpetrators, failing to account fo r situations where educators might be responsible for discrimina tion or harassment. Julie’s insight, informed by he r own experiences of discrimination in high school, significantly reshaped the protocol and shifted perceptions of student voice. Her contribution underscored that students a re knowledgeable, capable, and essential agents of change. This was a turning point for the Lab. Members learn ed from this momentum and ensured that students rem ained at the forefront of the Lab’s work. As the Lab meet ings progressed beyond the first year of our coll aboration, students presented the final draft of the protocol to the school board, led  district-wide workshops to train administrators on its implementation and continued to be Learning Lab members leading the implementati on of the protocol.

Students’ transformative agency: Students reflections Students’ voices were critical to the creation of the protoco l. However, as described earlier, their involvement came with challenges. While these challenges requir ed time to address, they ultimately had a positive impact on students' learning and growth. Notably, students ex pressed feeling heard, recognizing that they were p art of a larger cause, and experiencing a sense of accomplis hment. For example, Camila and Julie, two of the student participants, collectively reflected: The team itself has always been a support system fo r us as students. No matter what, the team continuously reminds us of the delicacy of every st atement we make, and with that, has sworn to protect us-which I think is especially important…The collabora tive aspect of our work has built a stronger support system of the team as years go by, and we are both eternally grateful to have such a strong foundation when speaking our minds.

Both the students recognized the importance of creating inclusive space through challenging inside and outsider perspectives and centering students’ perspectives throughout. In addition, they also stated a specific structure of Learning Lab that is particularly working for them: Working in smaller groups for portions of the meetings also helped make sure we could solidify what each group felt and how we individually felt. This was a big part of our growth as student leaders as for many of us this was our first time t alking with this many important members of the community. Being able to talk one-on-one or in groups allowed us to feel more comfortable in wider group conversations Here, both students alluded to the power of the Learning Lab members' collective process in upholding intentional spaces, which mediated their transformative agency and enabled them to contribute meaningfully to the Lab’s decision-making and the creation of the Protocol.

## Discussion

Preliminary analysis of this work suggests that students’ transformative agency exists within a dynamic, tensionfilled, complex, and cultural process that requires  intentional mediation (Haapsaari et al., 2016; Kaj amaa & Kumpulainen, 2019). This study hig hlights a significant shift in the traditional role s ascribed to youth in educational decision-making. Fostering students' transformative agency w as a collective effort by Learning Lab members to create an inclusive space where students  could meaningfully contribute, particularly in co-designing the Protocol for Addressing Harassment and Discrimi nation. To amplify students’ voices, it is crucial for adults in positions of authority, such as educators, admin istrators, and facilitators, to intentionally creat e spaces that empower students. As this paper demonstrates, when students are provided with such spaces, they can fu lly activate their agency to challenge and transform entrenched systems within the school district. They were able to confront the whiteness embedded in the district’s policy approaches and establish themselves as legitimate experts in educational decision-making. This study contributes to the literature in the lea rning sciences by showcasing that intentional design is critical for creating sp aces that amplify students' voices. Inclusive space s do not occur by chance; they must be deliberately designed.
