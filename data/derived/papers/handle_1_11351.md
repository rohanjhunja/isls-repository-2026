# What is Data? Youths’ Conceptualizations and Criteria within a Project-Based Data Science Camp

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Data is ubiquitous in young people’s daily lives, yet its meaning and purpose are continually changing in relation to context, infrastructure, and intent. In this pilot study, we explore five 11-to 12-year olds’ conceptualizations and criteria for what makes something “data.” Analyzing semi-structured interviews as part of an embedded assessment where young people were presented with five different data visualizations, we find shifts and consistencies in their conceptualizations and criteria for including or excluding something as data, elevating the attributes of the data (numeracy, increasing volume) and the inferred intents of data producers (related to collection practices and purported purpose) in their decision making. These findings point to the complexity of defining data’s properties and functionalities and the need to develop taken-as-shared expectations and definitions of what data is and can be within educational contexts among youth, researchers, and educators.

## Introduction

Data is increasingly ubiquitous in our daily lives,  from quantified-self data showing heart rate and movement to AI algorithms determining social media feeds to eme rging K-12 data science standards and frameworks. At the same time, data’s meaning and purpose change continually in relation to shifting infrastructures, power dynamics, and lines of inquiry (Borgman, 2017; Neff et al, 2017). Adult perspectives pervade these discourses, while young people’s understandings about how data functions in their lives are largely absent. Existing scholarship focuses primarily on how young  people conceptualize distinct aspects  of data production and functionality. One line of this work addresses how young people consider probability, reliability, and uncertainty in data production (Lubben & Miller, 1996: Metz, 2004) and interpretation (Masnick & M orris, 2008). Another concerns how young people conceptualize the attributes and ethics of data, such as ownership and privacy (e.g., Stoilova et al., 2021; Agesilaou et al., 2022) as well as trustworthiness (Kennedy et a l., 2024). A third line of work identifies underlying expectations and values that explain how and why people gather, interpret, and marshal data toward their goals (Fiore-Neff & Garland, 2015), particularly how these data scripts shape the ways in which young people procure, interpret, and advocate using data (Van Wart, Lanouette & Parikh, 2020). Despite the terms’ ubiquity in curriculum, contempo rary media, and daily life, the research on what young people think data is, what it cando, and what criteria they use to make their evaluation s remains nascent. As scholars seeks to challenge more narrowed visions of data and data science (e.g., Lee, Wilkerson & Lanouette,

2021) and recenter young people’s timescales and vantage points (Yoon & Templeton, 2019), it is critical that we

develop ways of hearing how youth conceptualize what data i s and establish criteria for inclusion and exclusion. In this pilot study, we ask: How do young people conceptualize data? What criteria do young people d raw on as they include or exclude something from being data? Below, we describe the theoretical frameworks, cont exts, methods, and initial findings from a pilot implementation of an embedded assessment of youth conceptualizations of data, concluding with discussions and implications for facilitating and studying youth engagement with data.

## Theoretical framework

Data can be thought of as “collections of related o bservations organized and formatted for a particula r purpose” (Koesten et al., 2021, p. 23). We understand data as social texts, constructed and contested within a larger “ecology of people, practices, technologies, institutions, m aterial objects, and relationships” (p. 56) to esta blish meaning and value necessary for use and reuse across numerous communities (Borgman, 2017). Contrary to depictions of data as objective and neutral, data are always “cooked” by a long lineage of decisions, artifacts, and infrastructures (Bowker & Star, 1999) in pursuit of specified purposes. In this way, data is never collected but always produced, constituted through human, material, and disciplina ry agency (Hardy et al., 2019). As children and the ir childhoods are increasingly ‘datafied’ (Mascheroni,  2020), we seek to center young people’s understand ing of data within this dynamic ecology. We draw on Yoon a nd Templeteon’s (2017) scholarship that elevates th e importance of careful listening to the words, gestu res, and tempos of young people to “support insight s into understanding about children and childhoods often obscured by our assumptions or desires” (p. 57).

## Methods

We analyze data from the most recent iteration of a  design-based research project (Cobb et al., 2002) where 21 11-and 12-year-old youth (14 consented to the study) participated in a 5-day, 40-hour-long data science camp at a science museum in a Northeast U nited States metropolitan area. Youth formulated research questi ons about a place they cared about in their community, used low-cost environmental sensors (e.g., water quality, temperature and humidity, illuminance, and sound) to collect data about their topic, visualize d it in graphs and charts, and shared their work with each other and their familie s. While data was a central component of the camp (e.g., the camp title, the data sensors outputs, creating data visualizations), it was never directly defined for participating youth or among the research and facilitation team. As a result, what exactly comprised data was continually being (re)negotiated, sometimes in contradicting ways.

Interview design For this embedded pilot study within the larger project, five young people were selected for a one-on-one interview with the first author based on their proximity in t he room and availability during activities. The semi-structured interview occurred on the third day of camp and was  split into two parts: first, youth were asked to r espond with text and sketches to three questions about what wor ds, images, and feelings they associate with data; second, youth were shown five images in succession and asked whether they thought each image depicted was an example of data and why. The five images included the city’s baseball team scorecard, a color photograph of the city river, a hand-drawn data visualization showing weekly app use bas ed on the book Dear Data (Lupi & Posavec, 2016), a restaurant’s budget as an Excel spreadsheet, and a spatial data display in GIS (see Figure 1). These images were selected because they exemplified different content, contexts, and degrees of quantification common to daily and workplace data practices. If youth offered short explanations, they were aske d if a specific part of the image led to their classification or what changes to the image would switch their classification. ## Data sources and data analysis

Data sources encompassed activity before, during, and after the interviews. For each interview, we au dio and video recorded youths’ words and gestures as well a s scanned images of their text notes and sketches c ompleted during the first half of the interview. We also col lected additional artifacts and vi deo recordings of data-related discussion before and after the interviews, such as  end of day video reflections, informal lunch time discussions, and responses to specific prompts. Campers selected their own pseudonyms. Analysis occurred in three waves. To begin, the fourth author analyzed the second half of each interview, creating a table of general impressions of each cam per’s response for each image, which the second aut hor completed with transcripts of each student’s  response. Lastly, the second author highlighted examples of the codes in the transcripts, and the third author conducted a comparative case study  analysis for two campers, drawing on data from lun ch discussions, daily reflections, and pre-and post-camp assessments. ## Findings

We first sketch general trends in conceptualization  and criteria across all youth and data visualizati ons and then share insights by focusing on two youth in more dep th. Across all data visualizations, most youth bega n their rationales using the number criteria, highlighting the numerical and quantifiab le aspects that were visible and making associations with data they commonly experie nce in school. In images where numerical tallies we re not evident (e.g., the color photograph, the hand-drawn data visualizations), youth tended to lean more on a purpose criterion, specifically whether the data could be used to answer a question or serve a function. F or example, Running Koala described how the hand-drawn weekly app use sketches required them to “thi nk about it” before deciding if it was data.

Students also provided collection, expansion, and camp criteria, often as a secondary rationale to number and purpose. These criteria occurred most with the baseball sc ores and restaurant spreadsheets, where there was visible evidence of hits, walks, and outs or moneta ry numbers being collected and counted. Expansion was most prevalent for the sketched data use, where it was d eemed data because it was the start of collecting m ore information. Though all students touched on at leas t one of these rationales f or each piece of information, they sometimes decided the image provided was not data (see two instances, Table 2, noted as [excluded]). In these cases, youth’s criteria were used to exclude it as data, such as arguing that the data visualization i s a product of data but not data itself, or that the image could be used to inform data co llection rather than being data itself. Combined, youth elevated their nuanced understanding of how data is produced and transformed, changing shape, form, and function along the way.

## Discussion and next steps

Despite the ubiquity of data in young peoples’ lives, this initial work points to how defining what data is can be a complex undertaking. In this study, we find shifts and consistencies in youth’s conceptualizations and criteria for including or excluding something as data, elevating  the attributes of the data (numeracy, increasing volume) and the inferred intents of data producers (related to collection practices and purported purpose) in their decision making. We also sought to clarify our aims and understandings as adult educators and researchers while listening with curiosity and openness to how young people participating in the camp came to conceptualize data. Together, this makes possible a more taken-as-shared understanding of what data is and can be. Limitations include the timing of the study (at only the mid-point in the camp) which obscured potential changes over time and the impact of the camp. Addit ionally, the current three iterations of the camp d esign have privileged quantifiable data  focusing on addressing community problems  and used specific sensor and data visualization platforms with their own limitations. In the upcoming iterations of this camp, we are eager to further support and expand what data is and what data cando through refinements  in the camp design  and the technological platforms. We see all these aspects elevated by the youth as  important aspects of what comprises data (Koesten et al., 2021) as “collections of rela ted observations organized and formatted for a part icular purpose.” Even in this limited sample, we were surprised by multiple definitions of data that could deeply influence how data science curricula are designed, taught, and assessed. Much depends on further addressing not only on “how” data learning unfolds but also “for what, for whom and with whom” data becomes needed and meaningful within learning (Philip, Bang & Jackson, 2018). We hope this work sheds light on data’s multidimensionality and malleability and on the possibilities of listening and learning from and with young people.
