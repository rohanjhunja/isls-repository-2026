# Mind-Wandering in Education: From Attention Shifting to Inner Engagement

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We explore the phenomenon of mind-wandering (MW) in the classroom. Challenging its traditional negative view, we reframe MW as an engagement with one’s inner world and as a dynamic and transformative process. We analyze case studies of MW experiences of eight students and three teachers across classroom lectures, testing, and group work. Through two interview techniques, and using classroom video recordings, we examine the relationship between activities and MW, MW episode content and types, emotional states associated with MW, and contextual influences. MW is not just a lapse in attention but coexists with and complements ongoing activities. We invite educators to rethink classroom practices as MW is a potential resource for fostering deeper, student-centered learning.

## Introduction

Mind wandering has long been thought of as a shift of attention away from a primary task (Smallwood & Schooler, 2015), often viewed negatively in educational contexts. To date, research in Learning Sciences (LS) has not focused on understanding MW as a phenomenon per se, but at least one paper argued that decreased MW indicated task interest (Wong, et al., 2023). However, this view may oversimplify a complex, adaptive process. For instance, students that choose examples have increased interest and attentiveness (e.g., Reber, et al., 2009). So, we argue that MW can also be triggered for task-beneficial reasons. We introduce insights from other domains into  LS by proposing a paradigm shift. MW can be conceptualized as  degrees of engagement with both the external world, and one’s inner world. Such engagement is adaptive, and depends on context and individual traits. MW is often studied through monotonous tasks that require sustained and focused attention, such as reading or monitoring sequences of letters and numb ers – tasks designed to induce disengagement (W ong, op. cit.). This interpretation is not deliberately theoretical but stems from defining MW in relation to task performance, treating it as a deviation or shift away from the t ask. According to this approach, our minds wander a way from task performance, and that wandering away is also r elated to personality traits (Gozli, 2022). Task-performance approaches do not fully reflect the variety of school tasks, many of which do not demand only sustained and focused attention but  also allow individuals to consider intentions and e xplore future goals  such as planning (Junker et al., 2024), fostering incubation and innovative thinking (e.g. Baird et al. 2012). Viewing MW as restricted to task performance is partly due to the influence of traditional study  paradigms, which overlook how MW can include dynamic, content-context dependent, and often constructive thought p rocesses. Thus, a proposition for the paradigm emphasiz ing personal engagement over mere attention allows a more nuanced exploration of MW and to represent its richness (Gozli, 2022), aligning with the goal of our paper. We present a method that captures MW in all its complexity, without interruptions, support ed by Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis of 8 stude nts’ and 3 teachers ’ interviews (c.f. §Method). We concentrate our attention on three focal topics within the literature: 1) contents of MW  and their types, 2) relationship between contextual classroom activities  and M W, and  3) associated emotions.  In line with other studies on MW, (e.g., Smallwood & Schooler, 2015), our work reinforces the idea of multiple targets of students' attentional processes, which cannot be reduced to a n attentive-inattentive dichotomy. Instead, we introduce the issue of engagement with both internal and external worlds, modulated by context and individual traits. Next, we present a critical theoretical position, our conceptual framework and model to interpret MW, a general research question, methodology, analyses of interviews, results, and conclusions. Theoretical position Mind-wandering is traditionally understood as an attenti onal shift that detracts from task performance. I n educational settings, it leads to decreased comprehension, superficial note-taking, and reduced retention (Smallwood & Schooler, 20 15). This perspective frames MW as a cognitive lapse, detrime ntal to learning outcomes (Mooneyham & Schooler, 2013). Such a conventional task-based perspective overlooks MW’s adaptive aspects, neglecting its nuanced contributions to cognitive and emotional processes. For example, deliberate MW can foster deeper engagement with learning material (Trasmundi & Toro, 2023). It may also play a more complex role, protecting against negative emotions during repetitive tasks and linking to patience, controlled processing, and premeditation (Kruger et al., 2020; Gorgolewski et al., 2014). Additionally, experimental studies often fail to distinguish among MW types — task-related interference, inner speech, visual images, spontane ous versus deliberate, worry, rumination, blank tho ught, dreaming — treating it as a monolithic phenomenon (Seli et al., 2016). This neglects the role of context, personality traits, and the task nature, which all influence whether MW is adaptive or maladaptive (Zhang et al., 2024).

Emerging perspectives propose a more dynamic understanding of MW as engagement with one’s internal and external worlds. Our framework (cf. Figure 1) captures MW as a spectrum, integrating cognitive and emotional functioning. For instance, an individual might initially be distracted by an external stimul us (Figure 1 (a): external trigger) and subsequently reflect on it by way of less defined visual images and future ori ented thought, generally identified as deliberate MW: Figure 1 (b). Figure 1 A Dynamic Understanding of MW as Engagement (Dario, 2024) The individual may then return to their previous activity, Figure 1 (c) — external world — by recalling a similar past situation, Figure 1 (d) — contextually-triggered engagement — which leads them to reflect on their behavior and actions. Finally, the individual may  contemplate the implications of the activity for the future  and move back to deliberate thought (Figure 1 (b)). This dynamic view acknowledges the context-dependent and multi-dimensional nature of MW, positioning it as an integral aspect of cognitive  and emotional regulation (e.g., Järvelä, et al., 2018).

In creative contexts (e.g., poetry or storytelling), MW enhances originality, exploration of new ideas, and problem-solving (Baird et al., 2012). Conversely, during tasks needing sustained attention, (e.g. mathematical exercises, detailed reading), it may impair performance by diverting cognitive resources (McVay & Kane, 2010). Emotional states and personality traits also play a role: positive moods and traits like openness to experience align with more adaptive MW. Negative emotions and neuroticism are linked to mala daptive tendencies (Ottaviani et al., 2013). Given our literature review and our proposed framework, we argue that conceptualizing MW as degrees of engagement with a student’s inner world can provide better understanding of its role in different contexts.

## Method

This paper uses data collected using the methodology first described in Dario and Tateo (2020). We collaborated with three schools, recording and interviewing thre e teachers from technical, vocational and primary s chool (3, 2F; 1M) and eight neurotypical technical high schoo l students (8; 2F; 6M), respecting a person-centered sample size (Squires, 2023). Acknowledging the diversity of teaching-learning activities, we focused on three specific learning situations  in three grades: lecturing, testing, and group work. We analyzed interviews  using the Interpretative Phenomenological Approach (IPA), as described  in §Data Analysis. Informed consent was obtained, and data was anonymized and securely stored.

## Data collection

Data were collected  from April to June 2019. In the Preliminary Phase (1), teachers completed a questionnaire describing the planned activities to help us  understand the learning context. During the 30-minute Classroom Observation Phase (2), two cameras recorded classroom activity: one captured the entire classroom; the other specific student groups. Students signaled moments of MW according to the following instructions: “Please put a marble into the cup when you notice that your mind is beginning to wander, if you are not focused on t he task and/or thinking about something else. This is not u nusual; it happens about 4000 times a day. Remember  we video-record the lesson and we will ask those of you who will be interviewed to comment on the video”.  In the 15-20 min Semi-Structured Interview Phase (3), interviews were conducted individually and separately. Students were interviewed right after class, while teachers were interviewed during their lesson breaks on the same day. Initially, the interviewees first answered open-ended questions. Then they responded to specif ics about MW transitions, their content and modality, and associated emotions (e.g., "Do you think there was an emotion that triggered this thought? How did you feel durin g the MW? How did you feel when it ended?"). Thus, the researcher and participant engaged in a dialogue while reviewing the lesson video, yet made time for unexpected issues. In the 15-20 min Self-confrontation Interview  Phase (4), participants watched segments of the video recordings where they had marked MW episodes on the  part of the students or just remembered them on th e part of the teachers and were asked to elaborate on their thoughts and feelings during those moments (Theureau, 2010).

## Data analysis

We analyzed the interviews using the Interpretative Phenomenological Approach (IPA)  to identify recurring patterns and themes. IPA integrates phenomenology, hermeneutics, and idiography, focusing on how individuals make sense of their lived experiences, viewing them  as “self-interpreting beings” actively engaged in understanding the processes, events, and people in their lives (Squires, 2023). IPA is a dynamic process in which both the researcher and the participant take on int erpretative roles, collaboratively evaluating the n ature of MW and its contextual significance, while in real-time dialogue. The primary concern of IPA researchers is to elicit rich, detailed and first-person accounts of experience and phenomena under i nvestigation. Researchers totally immerse themselves in the data with the objective of giving evidence of the participants’ sense-making of phenomena under analysis. Our approach required: 1) multiple readings of the transcript (translated from Italian) 2 identification of emergent themes, and 3) alignment of them within focal areas. Emerging themes were related to three  focal areas: 1) relationship between contextual classroom activities (i.e., lectures, testing & group work) and MW, 2) MW content, and 3) associated emotions. Furthermore, emergent themes were triangulated with researcher and participant observations.

## Results

Our results give example answers to our research questions, but do not make claims on the quantity of MW in the classroom. In the extracts below, S refers to students, INT refers interviewers, and T to teachers. Contents of mind wandering During MW, five out of 8 students recalled past experiences or planned future events, like going out with friends, realizing dreams, preparing schoolwork, or responding to the interviewer. S12, a 16-year-old girl in chemistry class, reported saying to herself, ("Alright, this hour will end quickly, and then I’ll go to computer science, and so... tha t’s when the thought started at the end "). She  described imagining conversation with teachers and their reactions. Her e MW had the form of an inner speech. Images emerged when she thought about how to organize a presentation: S12: “I was thinking about another class. I was thinking that later, during computer science, I had  to work on the geography assignment, hoping the te acher would also give us the next hour for geography. Because on Monday, I have to study since there's a presentation. I have to study, you know. I organized my day and also my studying. Because I need to start preparing for lots of lessons and tests, and I’m already kind of freaking  out” […] S12: “Then I thought, I'll ask the computer science teacher, but if they say no, I'll ask the geography  teacher. My mind was full of conversations, includ ing asking the geography teacher... It's inevitable, when you need to get something, to imagine the conversation and its possible outcomes: what to say if they say no, what to say if they say yes. I feel more comfortable if  I think about it beforehand; otherwise, I get flustered and don’t know what to say”. A teacher from vocational school (T1) reported MW during student testing, often planning the lesson as they taught it (e.g., how to organize students) or plan a future lesson. T1 says: “Yes, because I was thinking of how to organize the lesson with the students in mec hanics class and I wasn't concentrating on them. When I call on them, no… I write down what they say and the mis takes they make. I can’t stop them because otherwis e they lose the train of thought and so, I wait for the end of their response and I tell them what mistakes they have made”. Engagement and contextual influence This focal area inform s us on the relationship between contextual classroom activities and MW. Participants’ engagement with their inner world seamlessly integrated with the lesson and did not interfere with their activities (e.g.  S12: “While I was thinking about how to organize my study schedule, I was still following the explanation”). Our data also showed that an external trigger frequently initiate d MW, such as a friend's glance, a classmate answering a question successfully, or the image from a book. S9, a 16-year-old boy has a dream to build a school in an African region.  S9: “There’s an image in the textbook that made me think  of Africa. The image showed an African forest seen from above”.

In addition, data showed how participants focused on their inner world or on the lesson depending on the context, the type of subject or its organization. A ccording to S9, the way the teacher conducts the le sson and the type of subject affects the likelihood of having internally oriented thoughts. He stated: “No, as I said, in physics, no. The teacher hands out a sheet to complete while  we listen to the lesson, and I have to keep up. Wh ereas in other subjects like history, you just need to pay attention to the words, and then it [MW] might happen.” Emotional states Six participants described feeling calm during MW but two reported anxiety and concern about future plans. S12, for example, explicitly connected MW to anxiety about upcoming tests, describing MW as a coping mechanism for emotional regulation: “Anxiety. I think about something else if there’s so mething worrying me, like tests or oral exams. Today, I had the English test and was s upposed to have science, but I forgot the book at s chool, and luckily, we didn’t have it. On Monday, I need to study geography for a pres entation. Yesterday, history; today, the test. When I think about these things, I feel anxious, and I have to plan for the whole week. If I organize myself in advance, I feel a little less anxious, but it’s still there. Then there’s boredom too. When I’m bored, I think about what I have to do, and it makes me anxious —it’s all almost connected ”. These insights, in line with focal areas, answer our main question “How does conceptualizing MW as varying degrees of engagement with one’s inner world provide a better understanding of its role in different contexts and among students?”. These examples show that MW clearly varies depending on the surrounding context.

## Conclusions

This study proposes a new paradigm for interpreting mind-wandering (MW), not as a distraction from the primary task but rather as greater or lesser engagement with one’s external world. First, we argue that MW should also be conceptualized in the classroom as a complex, contextually modulated phenomenon, including adaptive forms of thought, rather than  solely as a negative interference with task perform ance (i.e., control-failure hypothesis or executive-resource hypothesis: McVay & Kane, 2010; Smallwood & Schooler, 20 15). Our research shows that unexplored form s of MW emerge in parallel with the external world: the lesson stays in the background and thought related to future planning emerges in the foreground. Such parallel management shows how, in neurotypical subjects, the shift is rapid and motivated by the progression of the lesson. Our data and model show that individuals easily transit from engagement in the lesson to engagement in inner thought. Such tho ught involves words (inner speech) and visual images that do not interfere with subjects’ activities. Secondly, MW is not merely an escape from lessons b ut a form of engagement shaped by contextual characteristics, lesson structures, and students’ emotions. For instance, MW can emerge as a strategic response in situations of low stimulation or content repetition. Students (cf. S9 in §Results) reported during our  interviews that MW happened only in certain types of lessons (i.e., in history but not physics/chemistry).  We must nuance this result as not being inherently related to eith er history or physics, but as we argue, would depen d on context and lesson organization. Regarding emotional states, our students more often report feeling calm, confirming that MW is a self-regulatory process (Kruger et al., 2020). When students are listening and feel disengaged during the lesson, MW created virtual scenarios and a sense of engagement in the internal world (Trasmundi & Toro, 2023). It is therefore necessary to reconsider the idea of  distraction, which does not necessarily imply that learning ha s stopped or become ineffective, but rather that the focus has shifted toward an internal exploration. Our view of MW as a multifaceted and contextually m odulated phenomenon requires a shift in teacher perspectives to adopt innovative and personalized practices that value students’ internal thought (e.g. nondirective meditation, reflection, tasks that integrate internal and ext ernal engagement). Rather than suppress MW, we should take it as an integral part of cognitive-emotional processes and  as an ally for learning. Furthermore, our internal-external engagement model indicates how to further investigate MW’s role as a tool for self-regulation, planning, and creativity. In sum, we invite researchers and practitioners to move beyond the "attentive-distracted" dichotomy by exploring MW as a natural and potentially enriching part of student-centered learning.
