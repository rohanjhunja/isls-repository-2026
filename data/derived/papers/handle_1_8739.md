# Attenuation and Amplification of Agency Through Goal Announcements in a Makerspace

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Agency in makerspaces is frequently in flux, amplified or attenuated through interactions between the maker and individuals around them. Looking at goal announcements in a makerspace designed to provide no predetermined goals, we see that these moments tend to follow triadic dialog patterns and that makers orient to the potential evaluation through hedging and multimodal demonstration. We argue that maker educators need to carefully attend to how interaction shapes maker agency without assuming that open-endedness alone will suffice.

## Introduction

Educators who embrace the 21st century “maker movem ent” often emphasize the pedagogical values of promoting maker agency, designing to support persis tence across moments of failure, and addressing the perceived divide between producers and consumers (D oughtery, 2013; Bevan, 2017). However, recent ethnographic studies of makerspaces have shown that  they fall short of these larger promises. Maker ac tivities often take the form of prescriptive assembly kits (Blikstein & Worsley, 2016) and place undue value on high-tech versus cultural making practices (Vossoughi, Hooper, & Escudé, 2016). Moreover, youth deeply engaged i n making are frequently required to push against dominant narratives of neoliberal entrepreneurship (Greenberg et. al., 2020) and individualism (Vossoughi et. al., 2021). In particular, these works highlight how educative maker programs can reproduce societal inequities (Ryoo & Kekelis, 2018; Elliot, 2021) unless those inequitie s are intentionally addressed (Buchholz et al., 2014; Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2019; Lee et al., 2021). Taking inspiration from recent efforts that attend to the microgenetic processes of learning in makerspaces, we focus on how maker ideals are realized or subverted on a moment-to-moment basis. Focusing on the value of maker agency, this study identifies patterns of intrafamily interaction concerning a critical moment for amplifying and att enuating agency: the public announcement of a goal.  Goal announcements-including statements of goals such as “I’m making a house” and statements of lacking g oals such as “I don’t know what I’m making” - are critic al because they mark a point in time in which the m aker’s intention (which is often studied as a private psychological and cognitive experience) become available to others in the makerspace through verbal and embodied pronouncements. Once a goal has been announced, it becomes a public substrate (Goodwin, 2017) that others can assess, question, or ignore (Pomerantz, 1984). In this way, the statement of the goal has social consequences and r epresents one kind of moment where a maker’s agency is at stake. To this end, we draw on key tenets of discursive psychology to investigate interaction patterns structuring goal announcements in a museum-based maker setting with minimal activity prompts. Building on Shah & Lewis’

(2019) examination of the moment-by-moment amplification and attenuation of power in pair programming

contexts, this analysis examines conversational dynamics of amplification and attenuation with respect to maker agency. More specifically, the study focuses on goal announcements in a makerspace to understand how m aker agency is attenuated or amplified during intrafamily interactions. Based on the existing literature concerning maker agency, goals in education, and power in conversation (Philip & Gupta, 2020), we focus on when goal annou ncements arise, who initiates the goal announcement, the conversational content, and resistance to announcing. To this end, we center the following research question: As a site of potential maker agency, how do public announcements of maker goals unfold in interaction?

## Literature review

This work is grounded in three theoretical spaces: student agency, goal announcement, and power as embedded in moment-to-moment interaction. Agency is often co nceptualized as the socioculturally mediated capaci ty to make independent choices and actions (Schlosser, 20 19). In this operationalization, the sociocultural emphasis refers to societal and cultural structures – ever-present in individual lives – that shape where individuals are able to exert agency. People in social interaction media te these sociocultural structures by endorsing, res isting, and revising them at the moment-to-moment scale of conversation (Philip & Gupta, 2020; Shah & Lewis, 2019). From this perspective, we see individual agency as a fluid achievement of the microgenetic structure of a social context. In conversational moments, agency is amplified or attenuated: increased or decreased in a given situation from an ever-present baseline that assumes tensions around agency. In makerspaces, the decision of what to mak e – the ICLS2022 Proceedings 211 © ISLS maker’s goal – is a key opportunity for the enactme nt of agency, and thus conversations around maker g oal announcements provide a window through which to examine these social dynamics. Goals can occupy the space of private psychological experience, or they can be  shared outwardly within the course of a making act ivity, creating a shared focal point in collaborative work  or simply a point of departure for understanding t he independent actions of others (Strivers & Sidnell, 2016). Because the public presentation of an otherwise private goal might fundamentally change what the interlocutors discuss, what they critique, etc., we would ant icipate a degree of tension between withholding and providing  public goal announcements. This tension brings mak er agency to the foreground of these social encounters.

To even further sharpen our focus on maker agency d uring goal announcements, we layer into our analysis considerations of interactional power. In particular, we examine power in terms of known stru ctural patterns of conversation. Human conversation is str uctured around interlocutors taking turns speaking; overlapping speech is rare (Sacks, Schegloff, & Jefferson, 1974). Turns are sequentially organized with immediate next-turns responding to immediately prior turns, k nown as conditional relevancy. How interlocutors ac cept or resist the conversational themes set by their inter locutor in the immediately preceding turn throws li ght on microgenetic exertions of power (Heritage, 2003). For the purposes of our study, we additionally focus on triadic dialog and hedging as lenses through which power plays out. In triadic dialog, such as initiate-respond-feedback sequences frequently documented in classroom settin gs (Cazden, 2001), the individual who initiates a conversation or topic change holds greater power than the second speaker based on both setting the conversational agenda and later reacting to the second speaker’s contribution (Esmonde & Langer-Osuna, 2013; Hutchby, 1996). In turn, the second speaker might resist the conversational agenda of triadic dialog in the contours of their response (Stivers & Hayashi, 2010), including through hedging. Verbal hedges are defined as “downtoning expressions or expressions of tentativeness or possibility” (Johansen, 2020, p 1), functioning as a mitigation strategy to withhold the speaker’s full commitment to what they are saying (Hyland, 1996). Hedging allows for full acceptance in the first speaker’s conversational agenda but resistance to potential critiques of their contribution.

## Makerspace context

The video data used in our analysis were collected in an open-ended makerspace designed and hosted at a large midwestern science museum between October and Decem ber 2016. Overall, the designers envisioned a makerspace focused on cardboard boxes as the primary material resource. The designers selected cardboard for its perceived familiarity for families, ability to be manipulated with low tech tools such as scissors, and possibility for post-visit family learning, specifically that families would continue making at home with their own cardboard supplies. Just inside the entrance doors, a single sign provided general rules: “Rules of play: Please  do: Build, construct, stack, and mine with the boxes. Please don’t: Climb, Throw, Stand On, or Kick the boxes.” To the left of this sign, a large pile of employee-donated cardboard materials-including a variety of sizes, thicknesses, and colors-provided source material for visitor makin g. The only example cardboard creations in the spac e were pieces left behind by previous visitors. In October  2016, a museum employee wrote “Be Creative” in mas king tape on the black wall of the makerspace; from then  on, the space included visitor-generated tape graf fiti as a prominent visual feature. In terms of design ethos,  the designers strived for unlimited material resou rces (cardboard and tape), easy to use tools (scissors and safety boxcutters), and no predetermined directions in order to foster the maker’s agency and imagination.

## Method

The above-described data set was originally collected to investigate youth (aged 9 to 15 years old) experiences of failure in makerspaces. We selected this dataset for secondary analysis because the maker educators who designed the space placed high value on maker agency. Additionally, the video data collection procedures involved minimal intervention in participant’s authentic maker interaction within a family group. Eight family groups participated in video data collection, resulting in 4.5 total ho urs of video. Family groups were recruited as they entered the makerspace upon their own accord, meaning the parti cipants all self-selected to engage with this parti cular museum experience. Only families with at least one child between 9 and 15 years old were invited to participate in the video data collection. Unfortunately, no dem ographic data was collected beyond the inclusion qu estion during this stage of the original project’s data collection.

## Data collection

Video was collected with chest-mounted GoPro camera s, allowing groups to move through the makerspace without needing to manage the camera. All video cam era placements foreground and background particular features of the environment (Hall, 2000); chest-mounted GoPros capture the wearer’s hands and forward facing context along with the audio record of near interlo cutors, approximating that individual’s experience of the ICLS2022 Proceedings 212 © ISLS unfolding moment (Burris, 2017). Based on the interests of the original study, the 9 to 15 year old child wore one chest-mounted camera, and in five cases, an additio nal family member wore a second chest-mounted camer a. Participating families were asked to spend as much or as little time as they wanted in the space and t o return to the researcher when ready to leave in order to comp lete a short interview. For our analysis, we only i nclude the first question of the interview - “What did you make today?” - because it directly related to goal announcements.

## Analytic methods

This study uses interaction analysis and draws on t he tenets of discursive psychology. Interaction ana lysis (IA; Jordan & Henderson, 1995) is concerned with microgenetic processes and focused on routine patterns of human action-including verbal, multimodal, and tool-bas ed actions. IA is concerned with the situated natur e of knowledge and action, highlighting how people use a spects of the social and material world around them  to sequentially and simultaneously understand one anot her and engage with an activity (Hall & Stevens, 20 15). Analytic attention is directed to social and materi al resources that hold relevance for interlocutors:  participants engaged in interaction/conversation. Where interlocutors place their attention is where researchers pl ace theirs. We focus our IA on the identification of patterns i n goal announcements across the data set, highlight ing the temporal, material, and social aspects of these pat terns. Discursive psychology (DP; Wiggins, 2017) is  an approach that examines how people reference psychological phenomena in conversation and what social purpose those conversational moves accomplish. For example, a student may respond to a teacher’s question with “I don’t remember the answer”; a DP analysis would identify what social action the claim of not recalling performs-such as inviting teacher support-setting aside questio ns about the statement’s accuracy (e.g., whether th e student actually remembers). We engage with DP to understand the organization of goal announcements, attending  to how the interactions shape who has agency over which decisions in the makerspace context. Initial exploratory analysis While content logging the data (at 2 min intervals), we noticed a pattern where some youth makers proc laimed, “I don’t know what I’m making,” while simultaneousl y engaging in physical making actions such as cutti ng, taping, or folding. Exploring the content logs further, we noticed that makers seemed to make this statement after being asked by another person about what their creation was, drawing our attention to goal announcements. We identified clips from the content logs related to goal announcements to conduct an initial exploratory interaction analysis. We selected for diversity in this initial set of clips, specifically choosing ex amples of goal announcements from different families, points in th e making process, ages of interlocutors, and announ cement type (e.g., naming a goal or saying “I don’t know”). In an effort to capture all relevant context, we segmented clips to include significant time before and after the goal announcement. We then transcribed each cli p using Mondada’s (2018) transcription system that blends Conversation Analysis conventions with precise multimodal representations. Multimodal transcription also allo ws us to document how individuals engage with physi cal objects, particularly important for understanding interaction in making contexts (Fasulo & Monzoni, 2009). We iteratively analyzed this set of goal announcement clips through multiple viewings, individually and in group data sessions with other researchers (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). Our viewings focused on the turn by turn unfolding of goal announcements, meaning we explored how these moments were initiated and closed, how they differed from one another, and how they were c onnected longitudinally within a family’s making se ssion. Early in this exploratory IA phase, we began articulating interaction conjectures to describe patterns in unfolding goal announcements that were common across clips (H oey & Kendrick, 2017). To sharpen these interaction conjectures, additional disconfirming cases were identified, segmented, and transcribed. Data selection: Freezing the analytic frame After examining 10 total clips during the initial e xploratory IA phase, we wanted to investigate how c ommon these moments were across the data set. We froze the two interaction conjectures developed in the exploratory IA phase and developed inclusion criteria, specificall y that clips needed to include a) at least two inte rlocutors in order to be considered an announcement, b) (at leas t) one interlocutor claiming that the object being discussed was their creation, c) the object and the maker’s hands (typically) visible in the video frame, and d) interlocutors focusing on object identification (e.g., naming it). To develop a complete clip corpus, we re-watched every video collected, tagging all moments which met the inclusion criteria. These moments were segmented from the larger video; each clip was bound by a change in the conve rsational topic or a substantial break in conversat ion. This process resulted in 88 clips (mean per family group = 11; SD = 5). We then coded features related to our interaction conjectures, including who initiates the discussion  about the maker’s goal, whether the goal is announ ced or withheld, use of a hedging word (Johansen, 2020), and physical engagement with the creation. We recognize that coding interaction data requires deep understanding  of the phenomena and clear definitions (DeLiema et. al., ICLS2022 Proceedings 213 © ISLS 2021; Hammer & Berland, 2014); we are currently engaged in such a concerted effort. For now, we present this coding of the total video corpus to demonstrate the ubiquity of goal announcement interactions in this makerspace.

## Results

Our analysis uncovered two consistent interaction conjectures present across goal announcements:

1. Goal announcements in this makerspace follow an overall initiate-respond-feedback (IRF) sequence.

2. Makers orient to the interaction as evaluative, evi denced by hedging and multimodal demonstration in

goal announcements.

The sole adult in their group – Sara – asks each maker to publicly announce  their making goals. The structure of a question – such as standard Wh-questions, polar questions, “or” elliptical alternative questi ons, and declarative questions (Hobbs, 2012) – cons trains response possibilities, though resistance to the interactional agenda is always a possibility. The question structure in this case requests a statement from the recipien t about the presence (polar yes/no, “do you”) of an  internal (“something in mind”) goal for subsequent and presumed planned actions (“are going to make”). By asking this question, Sara generates a discursive climate in which Emma is asked to provide a polar response to the possibility that they have an intention in their mind that will drive what they make. Instead of adhering to the yes/no response format, Emma responds with the statement “I don know.” In the space of public discourse, this response is relatively ambiguous. Emma could be saying that she lacks a goal or Emma could be saying that she does not know the answer to Sara’s questions. With this analysis, we are not attempting to make a claim about whether Emma did or did not have a goal at this point in the activity. The social implications of outwardly stating a lack of goal ar e the focus of our analysis. Looking at the structu re of their response, Emma delays their response significantly beyond t he 1 second conversational threshold typical of responses (Jefferson, 1989). When they do speak, Em ma moves beyond the “required” response of a polar question, indicating they interpreted the question as a request for an actual goal, not just its prese nce (Stivers & Hayashi, 2010). “I don’t know” as a question response can serve many functions beyond a truthful announcement of lacking information, including avoiding the conv ersational agenda, avoiding assessment, avoiding ex plicit disagreement, or hedging (Tsui, 1991). We cannot gl ean Emma’s individual agenda with their “I don’t kn ow,” but we can point to its situated social action of avoiding further pursuit of Sara’s question about the goal. In response to Sara’s reframing of their announced goal, Lily responds again (line 9), this time crafting a response oriented to the feedback from Sara. First, Lily quickly states “No,” rejecting the specific potential goal stated by Sara. Lily then states their goal of maki ng a “cardboard cute dog” with a micropause separat ing each word. While micropauses can indicate the opportunity to end a conversational turn (Sacks, Schegloff, & Jefferson, 1974), Lily’s lack of closing prosody and simultaneous physical engagement with the cardboard box act as turn continuations, giving Lily a longer conversational turn in order to construct a response. Lily’s word choices – specifically pulling forward the words “cute” and “ cardboard” – create a blend between their original goal announcement and Sara’s reframing. Lily also meets Sara’s request to provide a description of a bounde d thing with the addition of “dog” in their goal announceme nt. Finally, Lily ends their second response with a  hedge – “thing” – which moderates their commitment to the entire goal announcement (Johanson, 2020). After finishing their second goal announcement, Lil y waits for a next turn from Sara, indicated by the significant pause after the word “thing” (line 9; Jefferson, 1989). Sara does not perform the anticipated next turn, ICLS2022 Proceedings 215 © ISLS so Lily repairs the interactional hole with “You know.” Emma further repairs the lack of next-turn from Sara with her own offer of assistance (line 10). However, Lily does not respond to this offer, nor does Emma pursue such a response. However, such offers of assistance provide another type of feedback, signaling that the announced goal is worth pursuing with additional resources.

Analytical synthesis In both cases, we see maker agency amplified by the  deferment to the youth to name their making goal a nd attenuated by Sara’s feedback on those announcements. Following an IRF structure, Sara initiates each sequence with a question, and the sequence is closed with a feedback statement without questioning prosody. Emm a announced a lack of goal, an exertion of agency rel ative to how to approach making, and Sara establish ed the expectation that Emma would need to resolve this omission, attenuating Emma’s agency over that decision. This also left open the possibility for Sara to reinitia te the IRF sequence in the future. In contrast, Lil y announced a goal based on attributes, which Sara framed as an incomplete response in her continuation of the IRF sequence. Lily’s second goal announcement reflected a new orientation to Sara’s feedback, shaping the response to include aspects of Sara’s reframing and hedging the entire announcement with the word “thing.” Both of these m oves reflect Lily’s orientation towards Sara’s feedback as constraining their field of choices, an attenuation of agency. While Sara did not close the IRF sequence with Lily, Emma stepped in and provided a positive feedback turn. These two conversation sequences exemplify our inte raction conjectures and demonstrate how they relate  to amplification and attenuation of maker agency in small-scale interactions. ## Discussion

Power is infused in social interaction, including m oments like naming a goal in a makerspace (Philip &  Gupta, 2020). Looking across goal announcements in this ma kerspace, we see maker agency as frequently in flux, constructed by small but impactful moments of ampli fication and attenuation. Goal announcements typica lly begin with a question from the non-maker. As seen in Sara’s phrasing, the structure of the question may amplify maker agency by framing the maker as the person cho osing the goal. However, the questioner also places themselves in the powerful position of first and th ird speaker in triadic dialog, both setting the con versational agenda and critiquing the second speaker’s response. That feedback turn can attenuate maker agency, as we see in Sara’s shaping of both Emma’s and Lily’s goal formulation. The makers orient to the evaluative nature of the triadic dialogue, using hedges to buffer critique and shaping their response to meet the expectations of the nonmaker. Announcements of lack of goals – such as Emma’s “I don’t know” response – are forms of resistance to immediate conversational agendas, preventing the immediate opportunity to evaluate, but from single interactions alone, we cannot see how amplification or attenuation of agency shapes making across goal announcements. Our next analysis steps aim to examine additional f actors influencing agency in goal announcements, including temporal arrangement, interlocutor ages, and individual or collaborative making projects. Since 2016, the museum designers for the makerspace have iterated on the design of the space to include intentional exemplars ICLS2022 Proceedings 216 © ISLS and activity prompts ranging from assembly style ma king to open-ended tinkering (Bevan, 2017) in order  to support a wider variety of family engagement prefer ences. We will compare findings from across makersp ace designs to understand how the educational design contributes to attenuation and amplification of maker agency. Just because a makerspace is designed to support agency does not mean it is experienced this way. When applying these findings to maker practice, we do not advocate for settings in which people never ask makers what they are working towards. Such a directive would li kely constrain collaboration, limit joint learning,  and undermine other maker values (Vossoughi et al., 2021). We argue instead that supporting maker agency needs to be more intentional than simply not providing goals, which was the design intention in this makerspace. By attending to when a goal announcement is made, who has the power to prompt for that announcement, and perhaps what alternative interaction structures could be us ed to move away from immediate feedback, maker educ ators may develop norms that consistently amplify maker agency.
