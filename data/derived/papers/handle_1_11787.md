# Downshifting: The Nuanced Antecedents of Seeking Easier Content

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning involves challenge, and students respond to challenging content in different ways. This study investigates the antecedents and contexts of downshifting: seeking out easier questions while using a digital learning platform. We study this behavior using student data from Rori – a chatbot-based math intelligent tutoring system that operates over WhatsApp and is widely used in several African countries. Specifically, we use Sequential Pattern Mining to study the behaviors that immediately precede and follow downshifting and Bayesian Knowledge Tracing to evaluate students' mastery of the content prior to downshifting. Results show that the behavior is often preceded by sequences of incorrect answers, suggesting that some students may be adaptively downshifting to avoid failure. However, some students seem to downshift despite being on track to master the current content. We also find that students using Rori in schools as part of their regular curricula are likelier to downshift than those who access Rori independently. We position downshifting within a general taxonomy of behaviors related to persistence in ITSs and suggest further research into the causes of the behavior.

## Introduction

Learning often requires struggle through difficulty and even failure (Bjork & Bjork, 2020; Kapur, 2012; VanLehn, 1988). However, failure can lead students to diseng age in a variety of ways. For example, Baker et al.  (2008) hypothesized that gaming the system – exploiting system features to progress without learning – might be used to avoid failure when problems feel too difficult. There is also evidence that students tend to game the system more while working on ambiguous and abstract content, both features likely associated with problem difficulty (Baker et al., 2009). Similarly, stopout – early quitting of a learning activity –  tends to be preceded by failure (Botelho et al., 2019). These examples suggest that experiences of failure may cause students to stop meaningfully engaging with the material.

Failure may lead a student to seek easier learning activities as an alternative to disengagement and, possibly, as a form of self-regulation (Higgins & Spiegel, 2004). Some platforms allow students to switch to easier content during a learning activity – a behavior we call downshifting. Various motivations may explain downshifting. In some cases, students may avoid cha llenging tasks (Baker et al., 2010), whereas in oth ers, they choose not to persist in the face of difficulty (cf. Botelho et al., 2019). However, in other cases, s tudents may recognize that they are not ready for the current c ontent and choose more appropriate content (Zimmerm an & Moylan, 2009). As such, downshifting behavior is no t necessarily maladaptive or clear evidence of disengagement, as it could be an appropriate response to a misalignme nt between student abilities and content difficulty.

This study explores the contexts in which students downshift to understand the circumstances in which they exhibit the behavior and identify when it is l ikely adaptive or maladaptive. We examine downshift ing in a chatbot-based, math-focused intelligent tut oring system, Rori (1) (described below), which allows students to switch the grade level of content at any time.  Spe cifically, we explore how students use this agency to navigate problem difficulty and respond to patterns of succe ss and failure. First, we use Sequential Pattern Mining (SPM) to explore the behaviors that precede and follow downshifting. Next, we use Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT) estimates to explore the relationship between downs hifting and learning trajectories, allowing us to d istinguish between students on track to have mastered the mate rial had they continued with the lesson and those w ho were struggling to a greater degree.  Finally, we examin e the differences in the prevalence of downshifting  between students using the Rori voluntarily (in independent ly-chosen activity) and students using Rori as part of  their regular school curriculum.

## Background

Challenges and failure often benefit learning (Bjor k & Bjork, 2020; Kapur, 2012; VanLehn, 1988). There fore, persistence – the drive to continue a task until its completion i n the face of challenge or failure – is generally considered the optimal response to failure (DiNapol i et al., 2023; Ryan, 1938; Peterson & Seligman, 20 04). However, students react differently to difficulty, negative feedback, and failure, with some students taking steps to avoid further failure (avoidance motivation) and  others strategizing for future success (approach m otivation) (Elliot, 2013). Avoidance may manifest in gaming th e system (Baker et al., 2009) or stopout behaviors (Botelho et al., 2019).  From this perspective, not all pers istence is productive (Krumm et al., 2015); some times, students persist even when they are not on track to succeed during the learning activity (e.g., wheel-spinning; Beck & Gong, 2013). In these cases, the learner is unprepa red for the current activity and may need to learn more foundational content first (e.g., Falmagne & Doignon, 2012). Thus, there may be times when it is ideal to switch to an easier or prerequisite activity (i.e., downshifting). In other words, downshifting may often be an approp riate self-regulatory learning strategy in which students evaluate their performance and resp ond with foresight about whether to persist (Zimmer man & Moylan, 2009). Research on problem difficulty in intelligent tutoring systems demonstrates that providing students with problems with optimal difficulty can increase engagement and improve learning outcomes (Papoušek et al., 2016; Zhang & Goh, 2021). It is, therefore, plausible that studen ts downshift to find problems that align with their  ability to optimize learning. Furthermore, downshifting may si gnal appropriate emotional self-regulation as psychological factors (i.e., frustration, anxiety, fatigue) can i nhibit their readiness to learn from more complex p roblems, even if they have the cognitive capacity to succeed (Boekaerts, 2011). Therefore, the decision of whether or not to downsh ift is complicated not only by the challenge of a student inferring their readiness and predicting whether  persistence will be fruitful but also by the psych ological experiences associated with struggle. For example, Zhang et al. (2021) found that students who reported feelings of difficulty while using a computer-based learning platform were less likely to use str ategic behaviors in the learning activity. Furthermore, some students may experience failure aversion, leading them to avoid tasks when they believe they may not succeed (Zhang et al., 20 22). Other students seek to avoid effort (Skaalvik,  1997), which could also lead to downshifting when a studen t perceives that the current content is difficult. In order to fully understand downshifting, we must account for the possibility that students may choose this behav ior for a variety of reasons, from maladaptive failure aversi on or limited motivation to persist, to adaptive and positive self-regulation strategies.

## Study context

Rori is a free intelligent tutoring system, adminis tered through WhatsApp and accessible with a 2G int ernet connection, developed to provide students with one-on-one math instruction (Henkel et al., 2024). Unlike many intelligent tutors, Rori interacts with students using natural language processing methods, including large language models. These methods allow students to have an open-ended conversation about math concepts and specific math problems, which Rori presents to students during mi cro-lessons (short problem sets) on specific topics. These lessons are based on the Global Proficiency Framewo rk, an internationally recognized set of math learn ing standards (Ovsyannikova, 2019), and content is available for grades 1-10. Each micro-lesson provides instruction, tailored scaffolding, and worked examples. Figure 1 a presents an example of Rori's interactions during  a lesson on statistics. When students start interacting with  Rori, they are placed into difficulty levels based  on a system-administered pretest a ssessment. However, they have the agency to choose which topics to study and to adjust their grade level anytime by asking Rori for the me nu. Rori reminds them of this option each time they  start a lesson (Fig. 1b). Within this study, we define down shifting as a student moving to a different knowled ge component in a lower grade level of content (e.g., 6th to 5th). Rori is open and free to anyone using WhatsApp. How ever, it was specifically developed for use in Africa and is currently used by over 60 thousand users in Ghana, Kenya, Liberia, Nigeria, Rwanda, Sierra Leone, and Tanzania. Some students use Rori in conjunction  with their curriculum, while others use it indepen dently. A recent randomized control trial conducted in 11 Rising Academy schools in Ghana showed that using Rori during study halls significantly positively impacted math learning outcomes (Henkel et al., 2024). Throughout this paper, we will refer to those in Ghana’s Rising Academies as the Rori In-School sample and all others as the Rori Open sample.

Data The current study uses problem-level log-file data, which include the lessons and topics stu dents worked on, the grade level of the content, students' responses to Rori math questions, and whether those responses we re correct or incorrect. The sample comprises 8,125 students, 5.86% Rori In-School and 94.14% Rori Open. These data were collected between May 2023 and October 2023. Within that time, students submitted 506,858 attempts to 30,5198 questions across 40,149 microlessons.

Analyses Our analyses are primarily exploratory and descript ive as we seek to understand the patterns and frequ encies of behaviors surrounding downshifting. We evaluate thi s behavior using Sequential Pattern Mining (SPM) and Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT).

SPM is a data mining technique that identifies patterns or trends that occur in a specific order over time. It aims to identify more frequent sequences or subsequences of events while maintaining their order of occurrence. SPM is particularly helpful for understanding temporal data, such as a series of actions. In SPM, each data instance consists of an ordered list of items or events. The  goal is to discover recurring subsequences that me et certain prevalence (referred to as “support” in SPM) thresh olds, indicating that these patterns occur frequent ly enough across multiple sequences in the dataset. Our analy sis used the SPADE algorithm, implemented through t he arulesSequences package (Buchta, 2023) in R (Ihaka & Gentleman, 1996). The SPADE algorithm was chosen for its efficiency and ease of implementation for real-world applications (Zaki, 2001). When implementing SPM, we restricted our data in three ways. First we only lo oked at actions of correct or incorrect attempts on  a problem. Multiple attempts on the  same problem were included in the analysis. Second,  we only examined sequences of actions immediately before and after students switc h content – three attempts before the content change and two after. Thus, all the patterns were mutually exclusi ve. Third, we only examined sequences where the maximum time between problem attempts was less than 30 minu tes to avoid considering sequences that span multip le learning sessions.

BKT is a modeling method that tracks a learner's ma stery of specific skills or knowledge – known as knowledge components – over time (Corbett & Anderson, 1995). It applies a Hidden Markov Model to estimate the probability that students know a particular con cept based on their performance on related tasks. T he model uses four key parameters: the probability of learni ng on the first attempt, the probability of acquisi tion, the probability of making correct guesses, and the probability of making mistakes despite already having learned the knowledge components (i.e. a slip). BKT is widely u sed in ITSs to predict future performance (Abdelrah man et al., 2023). The BKT model used in this analysis per formed satisfactorily with an AUC of 0.74 when pred icting whether students get the next problem correct in a sequence of problems on the same topic, in line wit h or moderately higher than BKT's typical performance (M ao et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2023). Fifteen (8.33%)  microlessons had insufficient data for training BKT mode ls (Slater & Baker, 2018) and were excluded from BK Trelated analyses. In our application, we used BKT parameters to predi ct the likelihood that each student knows the knowledge component during each problem before and after downshifting. We then convert this likelihood  to a binary label (“mastery”) using a liberal threshold of 0.80. Although the original use of Bayesian Know ledge Tracing used a threshold of 0.95 to indicate master y (Corbett & Anderson, 1994), subsequent work in so me platforms has chosen a threshold of 0.80 (e.g., Wan g et al., 2022). This liberal threshold can  be seen as distinguishing between whether students are close t o mastering the knowledge component, as opposed to more stringent thresholds used to determine with more ce rtainty that the student has mastered the knowledge component. BKT estimates at or above this threshold are designated as instances in which students either mastered the knowledge component or are likely to master it within the next few problems (likely mastered). Whe n estimates are below this threshold, students have n ot reached mastery (n ot mastered) and may need multiple subsequent problem attempts or alternative instruction to reach mastery.

On average, students downshifted in 15.06% of the m icro-lessons. A substantial minority (18.65%) of student s downshifted at least once while using the platform.  Nearly half (48.05%) of these students downshift m ore than once. Those who downshifted at least  once averaged 3.99 switches. However, this distribu tion was positively skewed (SD = 5.57; skew = 2.82), indicating that some students were downshifting substantially more frequently. ## Discussion

This paper examines downshift – moving to easier content during a learning activity  – which has been underexplored in learning sciences. Almost a fifth of our sample downshifted at least once, often afte r strings of incorrect responses. According to knowledge tracing  estimates, most of these students had less than an  80% likelihood of mastering the knowledge component on which they were working. However, in almost one-third of these instances, knowledge tracing estimates indica ted that students had mastered or were close to mas tering the lesson’s skill. These findings, considered together, suggest that although downshifting behaviors are likely a lowpersistence response to failure, the behavior may be adaptive f or some and maladaptive for others. Furthermore, downshifting prevalence also varied based on whether students accessed Rori as part of their regular curriculum, suggesting that external contextual factors influenced this behavior. We propose that downshifting can be integrated into  a taxonomy of persistence behaviors in mastery learning activities, which involves two dimensions:  the level of persistence and the probability of ma stering the knowledge component when persisting (see Table 5). Krumm et al. (2016) define productive persistence a s a tendency to continue with a difficult task as well as a "good use of strategies." For strategies to be  good, their application must lead to positive r esults (e.g., mastery). For example, persistence is  likely to be productive if a student is highly likely to master the knowledge component. Alternatively, high persistence with a low probability of mastery leads to wheel spinning. Downshifting is  position ed as a lower persistence behavior – between persistence and disengagement -- which can also be divided based on students' likeli hood of mastery. Downshifting with a high probability of mastery is maladaptive, as the student would benefit from pers istence. By contrast, when students have a lower probability  of mastery, downshifting may prevent wheel spinnin g and improve learning. Notably, downshifting is not the lowest level of persistence, as students are likely still engaged in the content; students who downshifted more frequently tended to complete more micro-lessons. There are many plausible explanations for why stude nts downshift even when they have are close to or have mastered the content. Our results show that ev en for these students, downshifting tends to be pre ceded by multiple incorrect responses. This aligns closely w ith the “soft underbelly” conceptualization of down shifting, where students simply avoid challenging content (Ba ker et al., 2010). Failure aversion provides a pote ntial explanation for this behavior (Zhang, 2022); studen ts might switch content to avoid the negative feeli ngs associated with getting problems wrong. Fear of fai lure during learning activities is negatively assoc iated with metacognitive self-regulation (Bartles & Jackson, 2009), suggesting th at failure aversion may disrupt students’ decision-making processes when evaluating whether or not to downshift. Downshifting is also plausibly related to perfectionism, which is associated with anxiety and maladaptive behaviors such as cheating and procrastination, particularly when perfectionism is socially prescribed (Bong et al., 2014). This theory may explain the significant differences in behavior between the mandatory school implementations, where teachers were monitoring student progress and performance, and the opt-in use of Rori, where students were likely using the platform independently. At school, students may have responded to pressure to get problems correct by avoiding micro-lessons where they had previously failed.

Self-regulated learning also provides a framework for understanding downshifting as a learning strategy. In cases where downshifting is an adaptive learning  strategy, students likely reflect on their perform ance with appropriate self-evaluations and have the foresight to downshift, allowing them to avoid unproductive persistence (Zimmerman & Moylan, 2009). However, this process i s likely complex, as self-regulated learning requires students to manage their emotional states and protect their sense of self (Panadero, 2017). Thus, when appraising a learning experience, the student must apply copin g strategies to handle any negative emotions from f ailure experiences (Boekaerts, 2011). In the case of downs hifting, students must balance affective and cognit ive appraisals to determine whether to proceed with the a ctivity. Although downshifting may often be a produ ct of failure aversion, in some cases, it can be a strate gy for mitigating the emotional consequences of fai lure that disrupt the learning process, as well as the prospect of unproductive persistence. Future directions The prevalence and context of downshifting suggest that it is a nuanced learning strategy with adaptiv e and maladaptive applications. Our analyses highlight th at future research is warranted to document and und erstand the interplay of students’ emotional regulation, se lf-efficacy, confusion, frustration, and cognitive abi lities that may cause downshifting. Future research should cons ider variations in when students exhibit this behav ior (e.g., Is it directly after placement into the content by the initial test or does it occur later, perhaps after wheel spinning?). More work can be done to differentiate between unpr oductive failure aversion and more constructive eff orts to avoid wheel spinning. A deeper understanding of why the context of use predicts downshifting—possibly through methods like immediate post-downshifting interviews (Baker et al., in press) —could also help to reveal motivational factors driving this behavior. Finally, it is crucial to understand how downshifting is associated with distal outcomes (e.g., Do students eventually successfully retu rn to the initial lesson? How do they perform on external learning measures?). With a better understanding of the factors linked to downshifting and it s effects on learners, we can guide them in recognizing when downshifting is beneficial and when it is counterproductive.
