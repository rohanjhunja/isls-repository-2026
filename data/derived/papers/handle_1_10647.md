# Clusters of Math Anxiety and Performance in a Digital Learning Game: Differences in Hint Usage and Pause Time

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Math learning research shows that math performance (MP) and math anxiety (MA) are correlated; however, some studies demonstrate less straightforward relationships between them. We explored how students could be clustered based on their MP and MA levels and whether students from different clusters differed in their behaviors in a digital math learning game. Our analysis revealed that students were divided into four clusters of similar sizes. While the profiles of the two groups showed similar patterns that were found in previous research – high MP low MA and low MP high MA, – the other two groups showed a different pattern. Specifically, about one-fourth of the participants showed high MP and high MA, and the rest of the students demonstrated low MP and low MA. Moreover, when solving math problems in a digital learning game, students from most groups differed in how often they asked for hints, and some clusters showed distinct patterns of pause time.

## Introduction

Research has shown that students underperform in ma th, and some connect low levels of performance to h igh mathematics anxiety (MA) (OECD, 2013), which refers to “feelings of helplessness and stress when dealing with mathematics'' (p.87). The relationship between math performance (MP) and math anxiety is widely believed to be linear and bidirectional, indicating that poorer pe rformance can increa se negative feelings towards math, and experiencing math anxiety can lead to increased cognitive load on working memory and harmed math performance (Carey et al., 2016).

Cluster analysis of math anxiety in its relation to math performance Person-centered analytic approaches, such as cluster analyses, offer unique insight of how students may naturally cluster on important factors and how these clusters  predict important outcomes. Past research has used  math anxiety to cluster groups of students and found mixed results regarding which levels of MA were associated with better MP. For example, Fan et al. (2019) divided s tudents into low, middle, and high MA groups and sh owed that the low MA group showed significantly better MP compared to high MA groups. Wang et al. (2018) grouped students based on their MA in combination with math motivation levels and showed that students with high math motivation and moderate anxiety about math exams ha d the highest MP. Orbach and Fritz (2022) grouped t he students based on state MA (more temporary MA), trait M A (more stable MA), and core beliefs toward math. They found the highest achieving profile had low st ate MA, but high trait MA, and positive beliefs tow ard math. In sum, it is unclear what MA level is asso ciated with better MP, but a more important questio n is how the intersectionality between MA and MP may result in u nique clusters, and how these clusters may be assoc iated with differences in behaviors in digital learning games. Math anxiety, math performance, and student behaviors in digital learning game Two important digital learning behaviors are help s eeking behaviors and how long students spend solvin g problems, and these may be affected by MA. For exam ple, even though searching for help in digital lear ning environments might be less anxiety-inducing c ompared to in-person settings (Kitsantas & Chow, 2007), Iannacchione et al. (2023) found no significant ass ociation between MA and help-seeking behavior after controlling for pretest MP. Alternatively, Ashcraft  and Faust (1994) demonstrated that highly anxious students completed math problems almost as quickly as low ma th anxious students, but also provided more incorre ct answers, “sacrificing accuracy for speed” (p. 120). Moreover, Chan et al. (2022) reported that students who showed longer periods of pause time before solving math problems exhibited more efficient solving stra tegies. What remains unanswered is how the intersectionality of MA and MP might differentially explain these behaviors. Current study In this study, we will address the following research questions: ● Research Question 1 (RQ1): How are students grouped into different clusters based on their math anxiety level and math performance level?

● Research Question 2 (RQ2):  Do students from different clusters differ in their  in-game math learning behaviors (i.e., hint usage, pre-solving pause time)?

## Method

Dataset We used data collected through a larger randomized control trial (Ottmar et al., 2023), where 7th grad er students from 11 Southeastern US schools were randomly assigned to participate in one of four digital math interventions during their math classes. We used data from From H ere To There! (FH2T) condition (N = 1,029; 47% girls). FH2T is a game-based platform where students learn to solve algebraic equations by physically dragging numbers within the equations (see Ottmar et al. (2023) for details).

## Measures

We measured students’ MP using ten items from Star et al. (2015). Students completed an algebraic know ledge assessment before the intervention. The mean of the pre-intervention MP was 4.8 (SD = 2.6) out of 10. Students’ MA was measured using nine items in the Math Anxiety Scale for Young Children-Revised (Ganley & McGraw, 2016), resulting in a mean pre-intervention math anxiety score of 13.7 (SD = 6.0) out of 27. FH2T allows users to request a hint for each proble m. For each student, we calculated the proportion o f problems where they requested hints across all the completed problems. The mean hint usage was 0.12 (SD = 0.11).

The pre-solving pause time (hereafter, pause time) refers to the time taken between when the problem was shown on the screen and when the student made the f irst action. Pause time was calculated for each pro blem by dividing pause time before making any action on a p roblem by total problem-solving time, and then averaged across the problems completed for each student. The mean pause time was 43.20 seconds (SD = 5.92).

## Data analysis

For RQ 1, after defining the number of clusters wit h the Elbow method, we ran a k-means cluster analysis using the k-means function from the “stats” package in R using min-max scaling for standardization. To compare clusters by clustering variables, we conducted the Dunn test with Bonferroni correction. For RQ2, we c ompared clusters by their hint usage (using a Kruskal-Wallis chi-squared with Bonferroni correction, due to nonnormality) and pause time (using ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc test).

## Results

RQ1: Clusters based on math anxiety and math performance levels Our analysis revealed four clusters based on MP and MA scores (Table 1, Figure 1; between cluster SS divid ed by total SS was.73 indicating a good fit). All clu sters significantly differed from each other by MP (p <.001 for all, except for Clusters 3 and 4 p =.003). In addition, all clusters significantly differed from each other by MA (p <.001), except for Clusters 2 and 3, which only marginally significantly differed in MA (p =.02), and Clusters 1 and 4 which did not differ in MA.

## Discussion

In this study, we examined student clusters of math  anxiety (MA) and math performance (MP) levels, and  how student profiles with different characteristics dem onstrated distinct behaviors (i.e., pause time, hin t usage) in a math learning game.

First, students were grouped into four distinct clu sters based on their MP and MA levels. Specifically, the cluster with the highest MP showed the lowest M A, and the cluster with the lowest MP demonstrated the highest MA, which was in accordance with previous s tudies (e.g., Fan et al., 2019). However, contrary to most previous findings of the MA and MP relationship, a substantial number of students demonstrated a diffe rent pattern. Specifically, although the students in Cluster 2 showed a high level of MA, they also exhibited a relatively high level of MP, which aligns with a previous finding that reported some students showed high levels of MP and MA simultaneously (Orbach & Fritz, 2022). Possibly,  these students might have been able to control the  impact of their anxiety on their performance. A further stu dy exploring how their behavior differs from Cluste r 3 (low MP high MA) students may provide valuable insights into potential supports to help highly anxious stud ents perform better. Noteworthy, even though their MP level was higher than average, it was si gnificantly lower than the students in Cluster 1 (High MP, low MA). Thus, our results support the idea that increased negative reactions to math could prevent students from performing at h igher levels (e.g., Ashcraft & Kirk, 2001), highlig hting the importance of supporting students to overcome their negative reactions to math. Moreover, Cluster 4 students showed poor math perfo rmance but a low level of MA. Specifically, their MA was not significantly different from Cluster 1 (High MP, low MA) and showed significantly lower MA than Cluster 2 (High MP, High MA). Further research is needed to explore if those students might have devalued math, and their low math value might have prevented them from experiencing MA (Wang et al., 2021). Regarding hint usage, most clusters were significan tly different from each other. Both clusters with higher MP used hints significantly less frequently than those with low MP, which could be explained by their less need for additional help. Interestingly, clusters with low MP (Cluster 3, Cluster 4) showed that students with high MA asked for hints more frequently than students wi th lower MA. If Cluster 4 students devalued math, a s mentioned above, that might have made them less motivated in math learning and, thus, less engaged in searching for help. It is also possible that students in Cluster 4 needed fewer hints than students in Cluster 3, as they showed slightly but significantly higher MP.

Lastly, pause time differed among clusters with low math anxiety; specifically, high MP low MA students tended to spend more time before solving the proble m than low MP low MA students, which is in line wit h previous works (Chan et al., 2022). Interestingly, this was not observed between clusters with high MA. As high MP high MA students could self-regulate more efficiently compared to low MP high M A students (Lyon & Beilock, 2012), it could be that, before approaching math problems, the former group might have spent more time on processing similarly to high MP low MA students,  while low MP high MA spend more time worrying. A further investigation is needed to explore and potentially separate long pauses among students. Building upon existing research, our work further explored relationships between math performance and math anxiety and revealed that students can be divi ded into four distinct groups based on their levels  of these variables, highlighting complex interplay between t hose variables. Moreover, our analysis showed that students from different groups can exhibit different behaviors in digital math learning games. Thus, to personalize support features in digital learning tools, it may be worth taking into account the interplay of math performance and math anxiety of particular students.
