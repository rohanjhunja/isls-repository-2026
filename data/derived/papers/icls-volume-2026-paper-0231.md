# What Children’s Books Do and Do Not Teach About AI: An Analysis Using the AI4K12 Framework

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Although many storybooks introducing artificial intelligence (AI) to young children are now available, their quality and educational value have not been systematically or empirically examined. This study addresses this gap by analyzing how AI-themed storybooks conceptualize and communicate foundational AI ideas, practices, and values to young learners. Guided by the AI4K12 framework, this study shows that while most books make AI approachable through friendly robots and playful visuals, they rarely explain how AI actually works. Concepts such as representation, reasoning, and learning were largely introduced as vocabulary rather than as processes, and ethical or responsible AI use was largely absent. These portrayals risk reinforcing AI as magical or infallible. The study offers implications for designing AI literacy materials for young children that balance engagement with conceptual transparency and critical awareness.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly becoming part of the technologies children use in their daily lives. From voice assistants to social robots and interactive digital media, young children are increasingly exposed to AI-driven systems (Druga et al., 2017). Because formal instruction about AI is still uncommon in both school and home settings, children often construct their understanding through these everyday interactions (Famaye et al., 2025; Williams et al., 2019). Many of these systems are designed with human-like qualities, presenting AI as intentional, responsive, or even all-knowing. While such representations can support engagement, they may also shape how children make sense of AI in problematic ways, including attributing human-like agency to machines or overestimating their capabilities and reliability (Cai, 2024; Druga et al., 2018). These early encounters can be seen as shaping children’s emerging mental models and interpretive frameworks for understanding AI (Norman, 2014; Druga et al., 2017). Supporting AI literacy in early childhood, therefore, involves not only introducing accurate conceptual knowledge, but also fostering more sophisticated ways of reasoning about how AI systems function, what data they rely on, and where their limitations lie (Su, 2025; Su & Yang, 2024). Despite this need, current AI literacy efforts have largely focused on adolescents or adults, with relatively limited attention to younger children.

Within this context, children’s storybooks offer a promising yet understudied pathway for supporting early AI literacy. Children’s storybooks offer a compelling entry point for introducing AI concepts in early learning contexts. Narrative-based approaches have long been valued in education for making complex and abstract STEM ideas more accessible to young learners, using familiar contexts and analogies to support understanding (Banister & Ryan, 2001; Barber et al., 2020; Furner et al., 2018). Stories can function as representational and mediational tools that help children construct meaning by situating unfamiliar concepts within relatable characters and events (Arn & Huang, 2024). The ways in which AI is represented in these narratives may also influence h ow children conceptualize intelligence, agency, and the role of technology in society.

In recent years, a growing number of children’s books have been published with the goal of introducing AI concepts. Yet, there remains limited understanding of how these books actually represent AI, or what kinds of conceptual and ethical perspectives they communicate to young readers. To address this gap, this study uses the AI4K12 “Five Big Ideas” framework (Touretzky et al., 2019) to analyze how children’s AI-themed storybooks present and frame AI concepts. This paper aims to address: (1) What AI concept s are introduced through these books? (2) How is AI conceptually, socially, and ethically positioned for young readers? By doing so, we aim to illuminate current trends and gaps in the design of AI literacy materials for early learners and contribute to the broader discourse on developmentally appropriate approaches to early AI education. We collected a corpus of children’s storybooks explicitly designed to introduce AI or machine learning oncepts. Books were identified through online marketplaces (e.g., Amazon, Barnes & Noble), publisher websites using a 1637 combination of related keywords and synonyms, for example, “artificial intelligence,” “machine learning,” “AI,” “robot/robotics,” “technology for kids,”; these search terms were paired with age-related descriptors such as “children,” “kids,” “storybook”, or “picture book.” Inclusion criteria were: (1) story books intended for children aged 5-8, (2) English-language publications, and (3) explicit reference to AI-related ideas in titles, descriptions, or narratives. In total, 30 books published between 2019 and 2025 met these criteria and were included for analysis (Figure 1). This timeline was selected because 2019 marks the official release of the AI4K12 “Five Big Ideas” framework, which has since guided many educational initiatives in AI literacy. These books were analyzed through a combination of deductive and inductive thematic coding (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006; Braun & Clarke, 2006). The AI4K12 Framework (Touretzky et al., 2019), which outlines five core ideas for AI literacy – Perception, Representation & Reasoning, Learning, Natural Interaction, and Societal Impact – provided deductive categories to guide initial coding of AI-related instances in the storybooks. Each instance in the storybooks was examined and coded according to how it refle cted or related to these ideas with analytic notes recorded to capture contextual features and interpretive observations. This deductive process provided a consistent lens for identifying representations of AI across texts. In the subsequent inductive phas e, the coded instances and analytic notes were iteratively compared and reorganized into broader emergent themes that reflected recurring patterns in how AI concepts were simplified, anthropomorphized, or contextualized for young readers. This combined approach strengthened methodological rigor by grounding the analysis in an established framework while allowing themes to emerge from the data beyond the predefined categories. For children ages 5–8, whose learning is often supported by concrete examples, familiar categories, and scaffolded explanations, these representational choices are especially consequential (Gelman & Kalish, 2008). This section summarizes how AI was commonly introduced and where meaning-making was implicitly encouraged or constrained. Child-centered Design for Engagement.  Across the books, AI was introduced through examples rooted in children’s everyday experiences (e.g., classifying animals, identifying fruits, unlocking phones with facial recognition). This experience-near anchoring reduced abstraction by grounding unfam iliar AI concepts in children’s existing perceptual and categorization schemas. Rather than simply using technical exposition, several books embedded AI logic within progressive and action-based sequences. For instance, one title visualized a decision-tree classifier as a series of yes-or-no questions, for example, starting with “Does it swim?” and branching into different follow-up questions based on the child’s answer, ultimately leading to different animals. By frami ng computational reasoning as a guessing game, the book translated algorithmic comparison into interactive choice-making, aligning with developmentally familiar patterns of categorization (diSessa, 2004). Such framing enables recognition of structure in AI  behavior and establishes a useful foundation for further explanation and guided discussion, even if deeper reflection on the underlying mechanism remains implicit. In 1638 addition, many books incorporated participatory elements, such as multiple-choice questions, mix-and-match activities, or short reflection prompts that encouraged children to relate AI concepts to their own experiences. These features positioned the child not only as a listener, but as an active respondent, suggesting an intent to foster self-explanation or experience-based sense-making rather than passive absorption (Fonseca & Chi, 2011). AI Conceptualized Through Anthropomorphic Metaphors.  In these books, AI was predominantly depicted as robots or anthropomorphic machines, often through brightly colored illustrations intended to engage young readers. However, equating AI to these entities may be misleading. For children ages 5–8, who are actively developing the capacity to reason about others’ intentions, beliefs, and knowledge, such depictions may readily invite social interpretations of AI rather than computational ones (Wellman, 2014). Only a small subset of books made any attempt to explain the reasoning or decision-making processes behind AI, leaving its intelligence portrayed as an inherent and observable trait rather than learned or programmed as a computational process shaped by data or design constraints. Such simplifications may reinforce stereotypical or inaccurate assumptions about AI’s capabilities. Additionally, most books failed to reference more recent AI systems, including generative models like ChatGPT and Gemini. In some cases, the description was outdated, for example, one title in the AI+ME series claimed that “no AI systems have passed the Turing test,” which no longer reflects the sophistication of current conversational AI technologies. Hidden or Oversimplified Reasoning Processes.  Within the AI4K12 Big Ideas, Representation & Reasoning and Learning were the least addressed across the books. Very few books made AI’s internal decisionmaking processes visible and accessible to young readers. For instance, while the AI+ME series (ReadyAI, 2020) briefly introduces decision trees, which already set it ap art from the other books not detailing this conceptual knowledge. However, this is presented as a singular mechanism rather than one of many possible reasoning processes. Explanations of how AI learns were commonly superficial. Most books offered broad statements such as “AI learns from data” or “AI finds patterns,” without clarifying what kinds of data are used, how data is represented, or how learning actually occurs through examples or training. As a result, AI is frequently portrayed as instantly capable or “magical,” with little acknowledgment of its limitations, errors, or dependence on data quality. This absence of process-oriented explanations may hinder children’s understanding of AI as a system that reasons and learns, reinforcing the perception of intelligence as automatic rather than constructed and designed. Similar to many STEM-themed children’s books that introduce scientific or mathematical concepts through terminology, most AI storybooks approached learning by teaching isolated vocabulary words (e.g., algorithm, object recognition, data). Some titles supported this approach with rich contextual cues, such as matching illustrations or hands-on prompts, which can aid meaning-making. For example, one page of a book (Dhoot, 2019) depicts a child and a robot both holding an apple, surrounded by labels such as “stem,” “leaf,” and “red circle,” intending to illustrate that machines recognize objects by learning from features (Figure 2). While this visually suggests the idea of feature-based learning, the mechanism remains implicit. Without narrative scaffolding, young readers are unlikely to understand how these labeled parts relate to a machine learning process. These books relied heavily on images that were visually dense or conceptually abstract, requiring adult mediation or additional scaffolding to be interpretable. Furthermore, AIrelated concepts were predominantly presented as standalone definitions rather than embedded within coherent storylines, making the instructional style resemble glo ssary-style terminology teaching more than conceptual understanding through narrative.

1639 Lack of Ethical or Responsible AI Narratives. Most books focused on AI’s helpful capabilities, emphasizing how AI assists humans in everyday life, particularly in areas aligned with Natural Interaction  and Societal Impact. However, these narratives rarely acknowledged the potential risks or consequences of AI misuse. The “dark side” of AI, such as errors, bias, overreliance, or inappropriate use, was almost entirely absent. Without examples grounded in children’s own experiences with technology (e.g., privacy, screen time, or misinformation), the concept of responsible AI use remained distant and underdeveloped. As a result, AI is positioned primarily as a benevolent helper rather than a powerful tool requiring careful judgment. This study shows that current AI-themed children’s books take important steps toward making artificial intelligence approachable for young learners. Across titles, designers consistently drew on children’s familiar experiences (e.g., animals, food, games, voice assistants) to position AI as recognizable and friendly. This aligns with prior research on anchoring abstract concepts in everyday schemas (diSessa, 2004). However, this accessibility often came at the expense of conceptual transparency. AI was rout inely personified as a robot or helper figure, while the underlying mechanisms of reasoning and learning were largely abstracted away or presented as isolated vocabulary rather than process. As a result, children are invited to trust AI rather than understand it.

These findings point to a broader tension between affective engagement and epistemic clarity in early AI literacy materials. Making AI feel approachable and exciting is important for sparking curiosity, especially among young children. Yet when AI is framed primarily as magical, infallible, or inherently benevolent, children may begin to develop simplified or misleading folk theories that can be difficult to revise later (Cai et al., 2024; Gelman & Legare, 2011). Early mental models matter because they shape how children interpret later information. If initial representations emphasize surface-level friendliness while omitting uncertainty, error, or humandesign, children may come to see AI as an all-knowing agent rather than as a socio-technical system bui lt from data, assumptions, and design choices. This issue is not only conceptual but also ethical. Without depictions of failure, bias, uncertainty, or data dependence, children have limited opportunities to understand AI as partial, situated, and fallible. In other words, the absence of epistemic nuance may also constrain the development of ethical reasoning. Responsible AI literacy requires more than knowing what AI cando; it also requires recognizing that AI outputs are not neutral, universal, or always correct (Ma et al., 2025). Thus, the way AI is narrated in children’s books may shape not only children’s conceptual understanding but also their emerging orientations toward trust, accountability, and responsibility in human–AI interaction. These findings suggest several directions for the design of future AI literacy materials. Rather than abandoning anthropomorphism or playful framing, future children’s AI literacy materials could pair familiar metaphors with transparent process cues by showing how AI learns from examples, makes mistakes, or revises decisions over time. Shifting narration from “AI knows” or “AI cando”  to “AI is learning because…” could encourage modeling AI as adaptive rather than innate. This approach could help children build more accurate mental models of AI as adaptive rather than innate or magical. Such shifts would preserve accessibility while strengthening explanatory depth.

The findings also suggest that the design of AI literacy materials for young children should involve multiple stakeholders, including AI experts, educators, authors, illustrators, and families. A co-design approach may help balance engagement with technical transparency and developmental appropriateness. Such collaboration could support materials that present AI not simply as “smart,” but as explainable, limited, and in need of responsible use (Arn & Huang, 2024; Su, 2025).

To better support conceptual understanding, future AI literacy materials could embed guided prompts or supplementary activity suggestions for parents and educators, assuming some baseline familiarity with AI concepts. However, given that adult mediation ca nnot be presumed in all contexts, future materials may also benefit from interactive formats, such as conversational agents, which allow children to ask questions, challenge decisions, or teach the system rather than passively observe it. Finally, this study focused exclusively on Englishlanguage picture books, limiting cultural generalizability. Future research could apply systematic content analysis to larger corpora to quantify how AI4K12 Big Ideas are represented across children’s AI-themed books, as well as examine cross-cultural variation in how AI is framed for early learners.
