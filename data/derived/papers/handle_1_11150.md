# Four Days to Independent Work: Studying how Professional Coffee Roasters Learn through Proximal Apprenticeship

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This case study examines the first four days of a proximal apprenticeship among coffee roasters. The intense and effective period of learning presented here challenges studies of apprenticeship, where delegations of responsibility occur over many months and years. To understand this transience, we track a changing web of coordinations over four days: who coordinated what? We discuss implications for apprenticeship-like learning and school science.

## Introduction

This paper presents a case of teaching and learning  among professional coffee roasters. Lave and Wenger’s seminal monograph, Situated Learning (1991), sought to widen the field’s perspective on learning  beyond the ways it is designed for schools: with a set curriculum and instructed by professiona l teachers. They sought to reformulate how learning happens in forms that bear  resemblances to apprenticeship. They offered four brief ethnographic cases to exemplify their reformulated concept of apprenticeship: legitimate peripheral participation. Despite the inarguably wide influence of Situated Learning, cited nearly 100,000 times on Google Schola r, the learning sciences research that followed never made  research on out-of-school apprentice ship-like, learning contexts a priority, despite notable exceptions (e.g., Nasir & Cooks, 2009;  Stevens & Hall, 1998). We offer an example of this style of research and suggest that there is much to learn about learning under social and mater ial arrangements that differ from schools, and that there are expanded ways to see knowledge and knowledge-making that have analogs in school subjects (e.g., science  and math). Careful studies of this learning can inform our understandings of how people learn and what they learn, providing comparative perspectives and design insights into ways that learning scientists might design learning environments, in school or elsewhere. As a form of knowledgeable practice, coffee roasting is science-rich work (Aikenhead, 2005) in readily recognizable ways.  Roasters’ work  is science-rich in that it is  guided by what the Next Generation Science Standards call investigations, displaying nearly all of the sub-properties of investigations as  identified in this influential consensus document (NGSS Lead States, 2013); it involves empirically answerable questions and theory-based hypotheses; it involves the collection and identification of relev ant quantitative data understood in terms of variables, and procedures are revised in light of results from investigations (Davey & Stevens, 2023a). Roasting also stretches our understandings of science in ways reminiscent of field studies of scientific and technical work.  For example, roasting involves the  senses, with roasters using information from sight, sound, smell, taste, and touch  (Davey & Stevens, 2023b). Roasters use this sensory information in coordination with information from instruments like spectrophotometers (Davey & Stevens, 2023c). In this, they resemble professional scientists and engineers when studied doing their work (e.g., Goodwin, 1997; Lynch, 1985; Stevens & Hall, 1998). These ethnographic studies expand our understandings of what the actual doing of science or mathematics involves beyond textbook idealizations and armchair formulat ions; our study suggests that coffee roasting is one among many science-using forms of everyday work that can likewise expand our understanding. Even if it does not expand understandings of what “ science is,” it certainly expands our understanding of what science is “used for.” It is a painful irony, familiar to STEM teachers, to routinely insist to  students how useful science and math are but have little concrete evidence to provide beyond strained, unrealistic examples. This is a study of how apprentice roasters learn to trust their senses, extending studies of “disciplined perception” (Stevens & Hall, 1998), wherein newcomers learn through interaction with old-timers to perceive in ways that resemble those of old-timers (e.g., Goodwin, 1997; Maslen, 2015). Disciplining perception in roasting involves all the senses (i.e., including but not limited to visual perception) and therefore provides concrete images of how the whole body can be engaged in K-12 science learning (Varelas et al., 2021). It is a study of learning on the job (Becker, 1972). And while the case bears some important similarities to studies that s ituate learning in ongoing community life and work  (e.g., Lave & Wenger, 1991; Paradise & Rogoff, 2009) in that  apprentice roasters also follow  learning trajectories of  increasing participation in volving increasingly complex and consequential tasks, there is something different going on here regarding the critical variable of time. As we will show, there is a short, intense phase of proximal apprenticeship where an old-timer disciplines the perception of a newcomer, but that phase is surprisingly brief compared to the cases of Navy quartermasters and Va i tailors (Hutchins, 1995; Lave & Wenger, 1991). In the trajector y of learning to roast, after a brief phase of prox imal apprenticeship with an experienced roaster, much of  an apprentice’s learning occurs without persistent human guidance; instead, skilled practice increases through interactions with the materials and tools of the environment. Sociologist Howard Becker (1972) once identified a puzzle about how people learn on the job—wondering how anyone could learn, when the only available human t eachers had little time to “teach” because they had  to get back to their work on which the economic viability of a particular enterprise (e.g., butchering) relied. This case has something to say about this puzzle.

We organize our analyses around five episodes of learning embodied disciplinary knowledge, all representing forms of knowledge that our nearly two years of ethnographic fieldwork have shown us are required to consistently produce high quality coffee that can go to market. At the heart of this case is a learning phenomenon that may be more common and critical in many forms of accomplished practice than has previously been appreciated. Instead of a binary that contrast s the use of the enculturated body with the use of me thods of quantitative data collection and analysis, this case shows that these are thoroughly interwoven in coffee roasting. And learning how they become interwoven in the practices of newcomers is a core question.

## Methods

We began ethnographic fieldwork at Chicago-based roasteries in May 2022. We have participated in and observed (Becker & Geer, 1957)  roasting sessions, sensory evaluations, barista tra inings, coffee classes, curriculum development and implementation, and most pertinent to this paper, three apprenticeships. We draw from a corpus that consists of audiovisual data, semi-structured and video-elicitation interviews (Harper, 2002), as well as field notes, jottings, memos, and annotated photographs. So far, these sources represent roughly 600 hours of video, 300 pages of fieldnotes and collected documents, and hundreds of photographs. We focus here on one apprenticeship relationship. “Mike” began learning to roast with “Corey” on July 14, 2023 and was promoted to full-time roaster on October 20, 2023. Corey organized M ike’s apprenticeship around sessions that occurred every Friday for the first three week s, when the roasters at “Chicago Coffee Company (CCC)” had the least amount of coffee to ro ast. We observed every minute of these lessons, collecting 18 hours of video. Mike began to roast independentl y on August 11, 2023, with Corey increasingly occupied by regular tasks thereafter. We recorded most of Mike’s independent roasts before his promotion (i.e., 120 hours). We build our case of teaching and learning around five episodes drawn from Mike and Corey’s first three lessons and Mike’s first independent roast. We borrow Hutchins’ (1995) concept of coordinations to analyze these four days (cf. Stevens & Hall, 1998). In their work, roasters coordinate actions and representations across humans and non-humans in service of achieving their tasks. As we’ll show, roasters coordinate d information from their senses (using a device known as a trier, figure 1) with temperature information from their machine’s thermocouples and digital timers. In Hutchins’ conception of human activity, coordinations are at the very heart of things: “the real power of human cognition lies in our abili ty to flexibly construct functional systems that accomplish our goals by bringing bits of structure into coordination” (Hutchins, 1995, p. 331). We will show that Corey and Mike constructed a “web  of coordinations” (Hutchins, 1995, p. 289) that changed over time: who coordinated what transformed across the days. Because of page limits, we restric t our analyses to shortened transcripts  from Mike and Corey’s first four days and forego th e inclusion of elaborate transcriptions of talk-in-interaction, though close analyses of these along with the rel evant video segments were the key resource for our analyses (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). Coffee roasting, like any complex, historically evolving practice, involves discipline-specific tools and vocabulary. In this paper, we share only a bit of these discipline specific aspects of roasting practice, hoping that these will make the practice  of roasting sufficiently intelligible  to our readers. Roasting involves nearly continuous, fine adjustments of three key variables: time, temperature, and airflow. At CCC, roasters used digital, handheld timers, and measured temperature via their machines’ thermocouples. They controlled temperature with levers on their roasting machine which adjusted the  flow of gas through the machine’s burners (imagine  a gas stovetop). These adjustments caused chemical reactions to occur at faster or slower rates within and on the surface of coffee beans—coffee roasting is, essentially, the precise control of chemical reactions. We focus on two key phases of adjustment that occurred in th e last minutes of every roast; these phases involved a great deal of coordination between a roa ster’s senses and workplace instruments. The first,  roasters called “first crack ”: a phase when coffee beans switch ed from endothermic to exothermic reactions. These exothermic reactions release d energy into the roasting machine  and could cause a roast to spiral out of control. Roasters responded to first crack by controlling the gas flow through their machines and offset the added energy. Through this control of heat, roasters achieved a “development percentage” for each coffee: the time between first crack and the end of the roast (the “drop”). If a roaster identified first crack at eight minutes and dropped the batch at ten minutes, then the roast ha d a 20% development. Identifying first crack and determining the drop ma rked a roaster’s competence. We build our case around how Mike learned to identify these phases by coordinati ng information from workplace instruments with information from his—and Corey’s—senses. Mike now needed to learn to coordinate information from his senses with information from workplace instruments and make decisions before issues arose—to manage rather than react. He would learn to do this. On day 4, Mike rigidly adhered to temperature values. He said, “I gotta drop it at [380℉] no matter what.” Experienced roasters took a different approach: shoot for a temperature range, but like Corey said earlier, “That’s [time/temp values] just a reference. Remember you always rely on your senses.” Mike relied partially on his senses—he coordinated visual indicators with time and temp erature data—but the temperature data triggered his drop. In the contingent world of artisan coffee roasting where we conducted fieldwork, no two roasts were the same. While important, experienced roasters did not take t emperature as absolute. They coordinated information from instruments with information from their senses. And they prioritized the latter. Over the next two months of his apprenticeship, Mik e learned to trust his senses  in addition to information from instruments. He said shortly befor e his promotion, “I’ve been trying to wean myself o ff of the numbers. Like, I gotta drop it at this temperature.  I’m trying to make it look like the best version o f that roast when I’m ready to drop. Yesterday I did a lot of that.” When asked what prompted these changes, he explained: Mike: You really need to use both: data and your senses. Because I’ve been on both sides of that, where I’m like 80% data. And that’s good when you start because you can turn out a quality batch. Right? You’re looking at your times, your development, your temperature, but it gets to a point where you get so focused on that, that when it looks like it’s not gonna work out, then you’re like, ‘Alright fuck it I’m gonna drop it.’ And it’s a shitty roast. I feel like once I get too data heavy I shut down that other part of my brain that’s like, ‘Wait. You have what you have. Let’s expand it. What can we expand, minimize?’ It’s working with both of those. It’s been fun. I’ve been learning that this week. Mike’s web of coordinations continued to change. With Corey increasingly absent, Mike relied on recorded values from previously successful roasts. Over time, Mike’ s reliance on these “data” caused him to produce “shitty” roasts. When the numbers weren’t working out, his web of coordinations changed. He began to use that other part of his brain, to trust his senses. And shortly after his promotion, Mike decided to st op recording time and temperature data all-together (figure 1). Instead, he relied on ranges that had worked in the past, and when in those ranges, he used information from his senses to let the coffee tell him it was ready to drop.

## Discussion

In this case study of four days of  proximal apprenticeship, after only three days, Corey trusted Mike to handle consequential tasks  and to independently roast coffee. This case of learnin g to roast challenges studies  of apprenticeship (e.g., Lave & Wenger, 1991) where similar delegations of responsibility occur over many months and years. To understand this transience, we track ed Mike and Corey’s changing web of coordinations: who coordinated what? We showed that Corey did much of the coordinating  early in the pair’s interactions. Corey started Mike in learning to smell because that was the hardest to l earn. Corey then taught Mike to coordinate visual indicators with his own. After Mike demonstrated competence in smelling, seeing, and listening alongside Corey, information from workplace instruments becam e the priority. Mike learned to coordinate developm ent percentages with first crack times and drop times. Ideal temperature ranges, and what coffee  should look like in those ranges still challenged Mike; Corey did what he could to guide Mike’s coordinating: he gave char ts and ideal ranges that the team had tested. But Corey re cognized that he couldn’t teach Mike color. The cof fee had to teach Mike; he had to learn to “let the coffee tell us when to drop it.” In the absence of a human teacher, Mike’s learning clarifies puzzles of appre nticeship-like learning: If experts have to get back to their jobs, how does an y apprentice ever learn (Becker, 1972)? The case gave us one answer. Corey represented the information Mike required in workplace materials; Mike used those representations to guide his future actions. The sample of Spott’s that Mike coordinated with  during the roast on day 4 is an example; his notes with “ideal” development percentages and temperature ranges another. We left a few stones unturned that we’re excited to  turn over in the future. Mike made a lot of mistak es on and after day 4. But somehow the team structured  Mike’s days so that those mistakes mattered less. Did they have lower expectations of Mike? We don’ t think so. We find that he roasted coffees that co uld be repurposed depending on how light or dark he roasted them. Mik e made mistakes because —and as he named —he focused on “the numbers”: “I gotta drop it at [380℉] no matter what.” Was this avoidable? We see that Corey advanced this practice during Mike’s first ever roast on day 2: “I’d say go ahe ad and drop it when it hits 379[℉], okay?” Did Corey mean to focus Mike on the numbers? Was th at the only thing he could do? And if so, if an app rentice must learn and then unlearn practices, what opportunities does that afford for shaping the community in return? We started this paper by talking a lot about scienc e and so we might ask what science educators could learn from Mike’s apprenticeship. We learned that the body can’t be removed from learning science; that a coffee roastery’s a place where bodies don’t hav e to be split into  “artistic,” “crafty,” or “scientific” side s. These practitioners and learners bring their whole selves to work. Actually, they understand themselves as having to: “I feel like once I get too data heavy I shut down tha t other part of my brain that’s like, ‘Wait. You ha ve what you have. Let’s expand it. What can we expand, minimize?’ It’s working with both of those. It’s been fun.” Is school science fun? It probably depends on who’s asking and answering the question. But does anyone really seriously ask, fearing the answer? Perhaps n ew ways and places of using science will provide answers to another question that educators have evaded: What’s  so great about science anyway (Feyerabend,  1987)? Roasters’ use of information from their senses in coordination with information from workplace instruments—to produce bodily sensory averages  alongside quantitative measures like  development percentage s—provide preliminary answers from contexts of science-rich activity that is not found in schools or universities.
