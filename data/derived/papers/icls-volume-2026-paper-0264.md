# Towards a Real-time Reciprocal Metacognition Feedback Paradigm in Introductory Computer Science Classrooms

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning is a continuous process, with a student's level of mastery constantly evolving alongside instruction. While traditional assessment methods excel at assessing mastery at a particular moment in time, they are ill-suited for capturing the incremental nature of learning. We propose a novel system that allows students to provide continuous feedback about their perceived mastery throughout a lecture. With this data, instructors could more effectively respond to individual and class-wide misunderstandings in real-time. This new interaction paradigm would also promote metacognitive reasoning skills and allow the students to "dog-ear" moments of confusion during the lecture that could be returned to while studying. Through a series of user studies with both students and faculty, we explored the unique interaction challenges of this design space. This paper describes the key components of the proposed paradigm, drawing on these empirical insights to address design challenges.

We propose a novel infrastructure for capitalizing on students' self-reported metacognition (i.e., thinking about thinking) (Brown, 1987) in conjunction with standard instructor-driven assessments to provide more robust, finergrained, and timelier estimates of student mastery. In this paper, we describe the key benefits of a system designed to both support metacognition and use metacognitive data to improve learning. The unique interaction challenges are explored in a series of user research studies. The main contributions of this paper are a set of user-driven design recommendations for the proposed metacognition feedback system. Instructor-driven assessments have three primary limitations: 1) they are relatively infrequent events in comparison to the ongoing and dynamic phenomena they hope to assess (i.e., learning and mastery), 2) there is an inherent trade-off between assessment frequency and quality, and 3) they may lead students to mistakenly believe that their instructors have a deeper understanding of their individual level of mastery than is practically possible. Given that honest communication about one's mastery is already limited by feelings of embarrassment, what emerges is a system in which a key source of valuable information, a student's beliefs about their mastery, is largely absent. We propose a novel set of feedback mechanisms that foster and capitalize on the metacognition of students as a supplemental data source.

The primary challenges in this design space  are best characterized using cognitive load, which refers to the amount of mental effort needed to accomplish a task (DeLeeuw, 2008). Theorists detail three related types of cognitive load: intrinsic (the effort required that cannot be changed), extraneous (the effort not necessary for the task that can be reduced for greater efficiency), and germane load (additional effort required to construct schemas, see Sweller (2011)). The friction in any such metacognitive feedback system would almost certainly equate to additional cognitive load. This is not unusual, but here the stakes may be higher, as system-added cognitive load will directly interfere with lecture-necessary (intrinsic) cognitive load. There is, however, some amount of systemadded cognitive load that we want students to experience. That is, the act of engaging in metacognition is not extraneous load, but rather beneficial to students. In our case, where the task is monitoring and providing feedback about one's own cognitive state, the skill cannot (or should not) be fully automated. At best, we can hope to structure the task in a way that makes the added cognitive load germane in nature. From a design perspective, the system must walk the fine line of encouraging the automation of procedural activities associated with system use (e.g., pushing the desired buttons) to reduce extraneous  load while maintaining/encouraging any additional germane load resulting from the associated metacognitive activities. We b elieve the design of this nuanced interaction is best accomplished through a careful consideration of the problem space, informed by user research. Audience Response Systems (ARS) (e.g., clickers) allow instructors to poll students in real-time, improving participation, attendance, and metacognitive engagement (Caldwell, 2007; Richards, 2006). However, ARS feedback is largely instructor-driven, with students only reflect ing on mastery when prompted. Student-driven approaches, such as Hickey & Tarimo’s (2014) confusion-reporting tool and Samson’s (2010) LectureTools, allow spontaneous feedback, and have been shown to increase  engagement and equity (Burke, 2019). The current study expands on this promising work in two ways. First, we use a series of user research studies to guide the nature and functionality of our proposed system in a user-driven way. Second, our proposed 1802 system links student-generated response data to structured instructional materials (e.g., slides), generating a rough model of student mastery without the need to engage in resource-intensive knowledge modeling. Learning analytics dashboards (Verbert, 2020) offer another avenue for metacognitive support. Studentfacing dashboards have been linked to improved motivation and achievement (Karaoglan, 2022a, 2022b), though they are rarely grounded in learning theory (Matcha, 2019) and may foster competition over mastery (Jivet, 2017). Participatory, human-centered design is a promising remedy (Buckingham Shum et al., 2019; Michos, 2020). Our work follows this approach with iterative user studies directly shaping system design. Iteration 1: five button response device and dashboard Our preliminary needfinding interviews supported the development of a  system that promotes  more accurate perceptions of mastery (metacognitive skills) and communicate s those perceptions directly to instructors. In this first phase of our design process, we gathered feedback about the first iteration of one such metacognition-focused audience response system  with both instructors (via interviews) and students (via a survey).  The first iteration featured a desk-mounted student response device with five buttons (“confused,” “slower,” “faster,” “I understand,” “I have a question”) and an anonymity switch, paired with two instructor dashboard prototypes: a detailed analytics view and a simplified red/yellow/green “stoplight” display. Semi-structured interviews (~30 min each) with CS faculty were analyzed via affinity diagram, and surfaced four key themes: (1) Student engagement and shyness-faculty valued reducing reluctance to participate but worried the device could let students “hide behind screens.” (2) Data visibility-restricting dashboard access to instructors may prevent students from suppressing honest input, yet selectively sharing data could normalize confusion. (3) Ease of use-faculty preferred richer post-class views ov er real-time dashboards and suggested merging overlapping buttons (e.g., “speed up” with “I understand”). (4) Shifts in practice-the system could enable earlier identification of comprehension gaps and more individualized instruction. We validated the generalizability of the findings from our preliminary interviews  by surveying undergraduate students (n=233) recruited from the online recruitment platform Prolific. The survey respondents (62.6% women, 35.7% male, <1% non-binary) were primarily third (30.8%) and fourth (37.9%) year students, with less representation from first (14.8%) and second (16.5%) year students. The survey asked about the student's note-taking behaviors, the ease and frequency of communication with their instructors,  the kinds of information they would most like to convey to instructors during lectures, and questions about the current iteration of the student response device. Most participants completed the survey in under 10 minutes and were paid $8/hour. There were several key findings. First, 78.1% felt at least “Slightly Uncomfortable” asking professors for help in class and 73.5% felt uncomfortable providing feedback during lectures. The button functionality was largely well-received with over 45% of respondents rating “confused,” “I understand,” and “slow down” as Very Useful. However, 25.7% found the “Speed Up” button to be useless. Interestingly, students favored anonymity (50.2%) while faculty preferred non-anonymity, a tension requiring design attention. Iteration 2: four button response device Building on insights from Iteration 1, the unpopular "Speed Up" button was removed. In response to concerns about privacy and anonymity, three variations of the student response device were tested: 1) a fully-enclosed "pill"-shaped device, open on one side so the student could access the buttons, 2) the same design, except with the top of the pill removed, and 3) a smaller design  resembling a dorsal fin (see Figure 1). Prototype interviews (~30 minutes each)  were conducted with seven (n=7) undergraduate com puter science students at a liberal arts university. Participants were paid $10 for their participation. Participants were asked questions about how and 1803 when they communicate with their instructor, the utility of button functionalities, and their receptiveness to such a device. The following themes emerged from a thematic analysis (Elliott, 2018) of the prototype interviews. Students expressed a strong desire to communicate with their instructors  (request to slow down, clarify confusing material, provide additional examples, or respond to questions)  during class but reported experiencing discomfort (difficult to gain attention, social awkwardness) in doing so. One participant described feeling isolated when, in response to an instructor’s question about comprehension, nearly all peers nodded affirmatively while they themselves remained confused (Participant 6).

Participants noted a fear  of negative evaluation by classmates, emphasizing the importance of limiting the visibility of their inputs to surrounding peers. Some participants noted that audible button presses could reveal their input tonearby students (Participant 5). Six of the seven participants raised concerns related to peer judgment. However, some students were generally less concerned about maintaining anonymity from their professors, suggesting that the data  could provide more personalized support  in class or during office hours (Participant 6). Others acknowledged reluctance to appear “dumb” in front of instructors (Participant 4). Participants also placed considerable importance on the physical design of the SRD. Students preferred a device compact enough to fit comfortably on desks. The “fin” design was favored by five participants due to its smaller size and ease of handling, although some noted it was less comfortable than the “pill” design.  Students noted the overlap between the “I am confused” and “I have a question/comment” buttons. Students also expressed appreciation for newer functions such as “repeat that” and “give an exam ple,” which they found more directly aligned with classroom needs.

Overall, participants indicated strong receptiveness to the integration of SRD technology in their classrooms, highlighting the potential for the system to provide a less intimidating channel of communication with instructors. One participant explained that the “confused” button offered a reliable way to gauge class comprehension, which currently depends on the willingness of a few students to speak out (Participant 4). Another participant emphasized the value of moving beyond reli ance on the “loudest voices” or instructors’ intuition for assessing student understanding (Participant 7).

Proposed metacognition feedback paradigm guidelines Building on the insights gathered from the above user research, we propose the following guidelines for the creation of a metacognitive feedback paradigm in the classroom. The metacognition feedback system includes four key components (see Figure 2). Here we describe each one as they might appear in our proposed system, but any general metacognition feedback system will likely need some version of these four elements: a student response device, a mechanism for syncing instruction, an instructor dashboard, a nd a student dashboard. Student Response Device (SRD).  The student response device allows students to capture a snapshot of their metacognitive state at any time (and in real-time). The student's input to the device is timestamped and stored in a database for two purposes: First, it provides  feedback to the instructor about their perceived metacognitive state. Second, because the input is timestamped, the input can serve as a sort of cognitive “bookmark” (i.e., marking the confusing parts of a lecture).

Instead of the touchscreen of a cellphone, we believe a dedicated four-key keyboard (one key per finger) placed on each desk will allow students who take handwritten notes to write with one hand while keeping the other hand on the student input device. Limiting the device to four keys would allow students to quickly map a particular type of input to a finger press action, promoting automation and reducing the need to look at the device. The logistical and pedagogical reasons for using a physical device are bolstered by a strong preference among interviewed students for a physical as opposed to digital input device.  Students preferred a device that  occupied minimal desk space. Given that peer judgment was the predominant privacy concern among interviewed students, the device should also have side walls that obscure the input of the user from their classmates. We propose the following core functionalities for the SRD, derived from student and faculty feedback:  “I'm confused/Help” should be pressed when the student does not understand a concept being covered during the lecture, or when the student would like to request assistance during practice time.  “Slow down” should be pressed when the student feels the lecture is outpacing their ability to follow it. Pacing was a common concern of both students and faculty. “Repeat that/Give an example ” should be pressed when the student feels like some concept could use further explanation. “I understand / I'm done ” should be pressed when the student is confident that they understand a concept during lecture, or when the student completes an assigned problem during practice time. Mechanism for Syncing Instruction.  While the feedback instructors get from the student input devices has some intrinsic benefit when used in real-time, the real power of the proposed system is in its ability to capture metacognition throughout the lecture for later analysis by instructors and students. The post-lecture analysis of metacognitive data requires that inputted data from the student input devices is temporally synced with the instructional content (i.e., slides). This allows a student to see which slide was being presented when they reported 1804 confusion, or the instructor to see which slides the class was struggling with as a whole.  In our proposed system, we envision this component as either a browser extension (for Google Slides users) or a VBA plugin (for Microsoft Powerpoint users). The basic functionality is the same in either case: Whenever the instructor changes slides, this mechanism logs the new slide number and a timestamp in the central server. Instructor Dashboard. Informed by faculty interviews, the instructor dashboard would be designed to be used both in real-time during the class (if desired) or as a post-class analysis tool. In the real-time analysis mode, the instructor would see a pared down, aggregated view of the classroom. Feedback from the system would be selective (not continuous), alerting the instructor only when appropriate. This simple feedback mechanism addresses the primary communication concern raised by faculty (a lack of moment-to-moment information about student comprehension) while remaining minimally intrusive.  The post-class analysis mode should allow the instructor to 1) track the class's general level of self-reported understanding with respect to specific topics (as they are mapped to slides) and 2) allow the instructor to isolate the metacognitive responses of a particular student. Student Dashboard. The student dashboard will be  limited to the data generated by the specific student using it. Usage during lecture should be discouraged, but students may find it useful after the lecture to have a record of their metacognitive data so that they might easily view which slides they reported confusion during and fill in their knowledge gaps.

The proposed guidelines for a real-time, reciprocal metacognition feedback paradigm leverage insights from user studies to address several key challenges in this space. By empowering students with a low-friction method for providing spontaneous feedback about their metacognitive state, we both address widespread discomfort with communicating with instructors and encourage productive self-regulated learning skills.  Such a system also addresses a central frustration of instructors: the lack of feedback from students about their own perceived understanding as they move through course material. The proposed metacognitive feedback paradigm  would instead provide actionable data about real-time student comprehension to guide pacing and practice. At a system-level, a key strength of the student-driven assessment approach is that it does not require extensive coding of instructor material to link student struggle and misconceptions with specific skills and knowledge. Instead, the proposed system uses the inherent structure already present in a lecture-based course (slides in a slide deck) as a functional proxy for individual concepts. Student responses are synced with individual slides (concepts), resulting in a model of mastery that is less robust, but significantly more portable. The impact of such a system on the development of student confidence, classroom engagement, and questions of anonymity are all directions for future work. Interviewed faculty were unable to confidently predict if, for example, using a Student Response Device would ultimately engender more confidence or be act as additional crutch for already shy students.  The most immediate next step of this work is the development of a functional classroom implementation of the proposed system based on these refined guidel ines. This paper proposes a set of guidelines, informed by user-research, for a real-time reciprocal metacognition feedback paradigm. The proposed system is designed to encourage the development of crucial metacognitive skills and address persistent classroom co mmunication problems. At the same time, the data generated by such a system would offer both students and instructors fine-grain model of student mastery (and confusion) relative to specific instructional content. Importantly, the proposed system accomplis hes this without the need for resource-intensive knowledge modeling.
