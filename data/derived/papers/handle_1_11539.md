# Understanding Pre-service Teachers’ Information Literacy

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
With the rise of social media as a primary source of information on topics like climate change, educators need strong critical information literacy skills to guide students effectively. This study examines how pre-service teachers evaluate the credibility of digital information about climate change. Twenty-one pre-service teachers from a Canadian post-secondary institution participated in this study. Findings show that pre-service teachers prioritize sources they perceive as objective and evidence-based. When assessing social media content, participants considered the content, source, and presentation, showing skepticism toward sensationalized posts and preferring professionally presented information with citations. The study highlights the need for teacher education programs to include training in critical information literacy, especially on complex issues like climate change.

## Introduction: Major issues addressed and potential significance

Developing critical information literacy among the younger generation —empowering them to seek, evaluate, utilize, and create digital information for informe d personal and collective decision-making—is paramount in contemporary education (Osborne et al., 2022; UNESC O, 2023). Young people increasingly use social medi a as a source of science-related information, including topics on climate change (Greenhow et al., 2015; Kresin et al., 2023). While these platforms can inform students, they also expose them to entertaining, commercial, and pseudoscientific content (Allchin, 2023; Höttecke & Allchin, 2020; Mavrodieva et al., 2019). Given this mixed landscape of information, science and climate change education needs to adapt to equip students with effective strategies to evaluate the credibility of climate-related information on social media for informed decision-making (Breakstone et al., 2018; Kresin et al., 2023; Osborne & Piment el, 2023). Teachers play a critical role in support ing students’ evaluative abilities related to climate change information, and they need to be adept at navigating this land scape to effectively guide their students. As primary facilitators of learning, it is crucial that teachers learn how to teach their K–12 students to critically read and write everything, from academic texts to social media (Share et al., 2023). Particularly with complicated topics such as climat e change—which involves not only scientific but also social, cultural, political, and ethical dimensions—making sense of related information on social media is far more complicated than the reductionist idea of simply finding the “truth” (Breakstone et al., 2021; Share et al., 2023). This study aims to contribute to this issue. The primary objective is to understand and analyze how pr eservice teachers evaluate the credibility of climat e change information. Centered around climate chang e, this research seeks to answer the following specific questions: (1) What are pre-service teachers’ main sources of information about climate change ? (2) What are the main criteria and strategies they employ in assessing th e credibility and validity of climate change information encountered on social media? Theoretical and conceptual frameworks Despite youth immersion in a world where media and technology permeate all aspects of life and society, few teacher education programs are preparing educators to help their students critically understand the po tential and limitations of these changes (Share et al., 2023). It is crucial that teachers learn how to teach their K–12 students to critically interact with digital information—being a responsible information consumer and creator for informed decision-making in both personal lives and public discourses  (UNESCO, 20 21). Therefore, teacher education programs must stay current not just with the latest  instructional technology but, more importantly, with critical information literacy. Critical information literacy  emphasizes not only the skills needed to locate, e valuate, and use information but also encourages a deep understa nding of the social, political, and econom ic contexts that shape information creation and dissemination (Tewell, 2015; Tiede et al., 2015). It prompts individuals to question and challenge power structures, biases, and inequalities embedded in information systems. The unique characteristics of social media environm ents frequently lead users to employ cognitive heuristics—mental shortcuts that enable efficient decision-making by selectively filtering information (Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, 2011). Researchers have i dentified multiple heuristic frameworks for evaluat ing scientific information in these contexts. Osbor ne and Pimentel (2023) developed an evaluative heur istic focused on cultivating “competent outsiders” who assess inf ormation through three sequential filters: (1) sour ce credibility, (2) source expertise regarding the specific claim, and (3) consensus among relevant scientific experts. Alternative approaches have emerged from Metzger and colleagues’ work (Metzger et al., 2010; Metzger & Flanagin, 2013), which identifies six distinct cognitive heuristics for online information evaluation: (1) reputation,

(2) endorsement, (3) consistency, (4) self-confirmation, (5) expectancy violation, and (6) per suasive intent.

Complementing these frameworks, Kresin et al. (2023) found that German adolescents (ages 14-16) primarily rely on three key criteria w hen evaluating social media information: (1) conten t-related aspects (“What”), (2) source-related characteristics (“Who”), and (3) presentational features (“How”).

## Research design, methodology, and methods

This study was designed as a case study to explore pre-service teachers’ evaluation strategies regarding c limate change information. Twenty-one pre-service teachers from a Post-Baccalaureate Education program at a post-secondary institution in Canada parti cipated in this research. Each participant was invited to a 45-minute semistructured individual interview aimed at uncovering  their thought processes and evaluation strategies.  During these interviews, participants engaged in interactive activities designed to elicit detailed insights.

## Findings

Variations in climate change knowledge based on educational background All the participants, regardless of their education al background or experiences with social media, agr eed on the importance of staying informed about climate change. They cited various reasons for this shared perspective, such as “[climate change] impacting everyone in many aspects, so we better kn ow about it.” Among the participants, approximately two-thirds (13 out of 21) had educational experiences in science fields such as chemistry, geology, physics, or engineering. Regarding how they kept th emselves informed, it was evident that participants with a science-related background viewed their undergraduate cours es as the main source of knowledge about climate issues. Participants without a science-related background or who had not taken any courses about climate change (8 out of 21) felt “the need to learn more about it ” since they didn’t think they knew enough. It was evident in this study that previous, formal educational experi ences are a major influencing factor on how confide nt preservice teachers feel about discussing and teaching climate change in their future classrooms, as well as being the main source of their knowledge about climate change.

Strategies for evaluating information on social media i.e., TikTok During the TikTok post evaluation activity, pattern s emerged in the strategies that pre-service teachers used to assess climate change information. At the beginning of the activity, most participants acknowledged that it might not be responsible to make quick decisions, recognizing that social media often does not provide enough time for thoughtful evaluation. In evaluating the posts, the pre-service teachers in this study examined all three a spects reported in Kresin et al. (2023): what (content), who (the information provider), and how (the presentation or format of the information). Rather than considering  each aspect separately, these elements were intert wined into specific strategies that the participants employed,  which are detailed below, including (1) skepticism toward content and source, (2) reaction to emotional content, and (3) consistency between message and messenger. Perceptions of information credibility Pre-service teachers demonstrated a strong preference f or information sources they perceived as objective and evidence-based. Scientific data and statistics from reputabl e institutions like NASA were ranked highest in credibility by the majority, especially those with science-related backgrounds. Indigenous traditional knowled ge was also highly valued, with some participants emph asizing its holistic approach to living sustainably  and addressing climate issues. A notable number of part icipants advocated for integrating both scientific data and Indigenous knowledge to gain a comprehensive unders tanding of climate change.  Conversely, celebrity endorsements and editorials or position papers were  deemed the least credible due to perceived persona l biases and potential ulterior motives.

## Conclusion and Implications

This study explored how pre-service teachers evaluate the credibility of climat e change information, particularly in the context of social media. The findings highli ght the critical need for teacher education program s to incorporate training in critical in formation literacy, particularly concerning complex  socio-scientific issues like climate change. Pre-service teachers must be equipped not only with the ability to assess the credibility of various information sources but also with the skills to teach their future students these essential competencies.
