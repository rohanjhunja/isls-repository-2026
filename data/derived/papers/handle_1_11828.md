## Introduction

Science learning environments have been shaped by Eurocentric ideologies that center science as a set of culturally neutral and color-blind systems that have traditionally excluded underrepresented groups from positions of power and knowledge production (e.g. McGee, 2020; Tzou, e t al., 2023). STEM educators are often reluctant to acknowledge the importance of race and culture in their teaching of science (e.g. Morales-Doyle, 2021). However, current science reforms in the United States specify a need for equitable and student-centered science teaching for all students (National Research Council [NRC], 2012; Next Generation Science Standards [NGSS], 2013). These reforms require big steps toward rethinking the pur poses of science education and how science should b e taught in schools leading to considerable challenges in the scope and types of changes required of teachers (NRC, 2015). Many scholars argue that equity is a key dimension for realizing the vision of these standards (Tzou e t al., 2021). However, approaches different people ta ke to achieve equity candiffer. For example, teachers and designers can instill routines that ensure that epi stemic agency is achieved (Reiser et al., 2021). Al so, designers can equip teachers with strategies that help them teach in ways that are more responsive to students’ ideas (Miller et al., 2021). In addition to these approaches, som e scholars argue that teachers should particularly learn how to discuss issues that relate to historical and political contexts in science (e.g. Bang et al., 2013; Morales-Doyle, 2019; Tzou et al., 2021).

Curriculum materials can be part of these efforts to address problems of equity. Curriculum materials are tools designed to help teachers put the ideas of th e reforms into their classrooms (e.g. Cohen et al.,  2003; Remillard, 2005; Sherin & Drake, 2009). In addition to teachers shaping the curriculum, working with curriculum materials can be a site for teacher learning (e.g. Ball & Coh en, 1996). Thus, curriculum materials can help challenge teachers’ ideas and help them to see alternatives. A promising way teachers can engage with curriculum  materials while deepening their pedagogical strategies to achieve reforms is through co-design – a space where teachers and researchers design curri culum materials together (e.g. Penuel et al., 2022; Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Co-design can help teachers achieve more equitable teaching practices and learn key concepts  and practices through moments of deliberations (e. g. Penuel et al., 2022, Pomian Bogdanov, 2022). During moments where design dilemmas occur, team member deliberations stem from the values they bring into the design pro cess (Pomian Bogdanov, 2022). Unpacking design dile mmas during co-design through the lens of values allows us to unde rstand what teachers care about and how they negotiate and (re)define their understanding of these issues. Therefore, in this paper, I explore two cases of how conversations about representing historical and political contexts in science curriculum materials play out within the co-design setting. I track shifts in conversations between design team members as they d iscuss and (re)define values around representations  of historical and political contexts  in science.  In other words, I explore how teachers engage with designing curriculum materials to be more justice-centered by negotiating and redefining representations of science contexts. This study investigates the question: How do teachers negotiate and (re)def ine values related to representations of historical and political contexts as they co-design science curriculum materials? To understand issues that arise, it is useful to lo ok at moments of deliberations or tensions during dilemmas that arise. A useful framework that allows us to identify times when design teams disagree and therefore are faced with more difficult decision-making is the curricular values framework. This framework looks at segments of discussion where deliberations occur around a specific topic  – design dilemmas  – and unpacks contributions that participants bring in terms of t heir values – curricular values (Pomian Bogdanov, 2022). Curricular values (defined in the next section)  are criteria that underlie design team members’ dec isions about how to proceed with resolving design dilemmas (Pomian Bogdanov, 2022). With this framework, we can identify intersections of specific values and how they come together to form possible directions for the team to move in its design of the curriculum.

The purpose of this paper is twofold. First, I unpack how teachers discuss historical and political contexts in ways that challenge traditional ways of teaching  science and lean into more justice-centered ways during co-design. Second, I demonstrate the usefulness of the curricular values framework in identifying and unpacking key discussion segments. The curricular values framework emerged from a larger study (Pomian Bogdanov, 2025) in which I apply the curricular values framework to a co-design process that spanned seven months. For this paper, I focus on a small subset of this data – specifically I focus on design dilemmas during which curricular values are deliberated within two design dilemmas the team had.  I focus on two main design dilemmas – white dude and police heater – in which ideas related to representing historical a nd political contexts  in science are discussed. These were the only two design dilemmas in which ov ert deliberations about historical and political contexts occurred alongside conversation of equitable design  decisions. These design dilemmas are representative of the types of conversations and decisions teachers and c urriculum designers face as they aim to respond to reform initiatives of making science more equitable and relatable for students. I begin by laying out the frameworks that guide this work, introducing the context, and outlining the methods used for analysis, both within the larger s et of data and within this paper. Then, I will dive into the specific design dilemmas segments for a deeper unde rstanding of these conversations teachers engaged i n and how they led to conversations that centered on justice-oriented science perspectives. Guiding frameworks I leverage conceptual frameworks of teacher curricular knowledge (e.g. Ball & Forzani, 2009; Penuel et al., 2023) by posi tioning teachers as working together through the ac tivity of designing curriculum and therefore being transformed by this activity and the people involved (Vygotsky & Cole, 1978; Wertsch, 1991). I use the curricular values framework as an analytical tool that helps us understand deep ly held values that participants involved in co-designing curricula use to make design decisions. Curriculum values reflect a participant’s views about science teaching and learning relevant to curriculum design. Looking at the values that participants bring to the table reveals what they care about and what they think is  important to teach students. When negotiations or dilemmas occur around values, we can see potential tensions between design ideas associated with different curriculum values (Pomian Bogdanov, 2022). The goal of this study is to make sense of how teach ers discuss values related to racial and social justice issues and redefine them, so in this analysis I apply the curricular values framework to examine conflicts between values related to racial and social justice issues.  Curricular values are very deliberate and meaningful to participants and therefore can reveal how teachers discuss issues of racial and social justice by unpacking how they position themselves and the deeply held beliefs they draw. I ground teacher experiences of design for just tea ching practices in a  justice-centered pedagogy lens (Morales-Doyle, 2017). The justice-centered pedagogy lens positions students as transformative intellectuals and calls teachers to engage deeply with underrepresent ed students. To understand how teachers engage with designing for justice-centered pedagogy, I look for instances when teachers grapple w ith historical or political representations.

## Context

I focus on a co-design setting where teachers join curriculum desig ners to write a high school physics unit. My data include weekly hour-and-a-half co-design meetings that span seven months – a total of 35 hours of recorded and transcribed data. These meetings were attended by a group of three teachers, three curriculum designers, and me. I acted as a participant observer – contributing to the work as a curriculum designer while also collecting data as a researcher. This was done within the OpenSciEd  partnership which strives to create transformative science curriculum materials that are intended to be used nationwide and are open source. The three teachers  all worked in the same district of a large Western city in the US. These teachers include: Kristen (white female) who has 15 years of scienc e teaching experience in middle and high school and at the time of the study was teaching at a last res ort school, Matt (white male) who has 13 years of e xperience teaching high school science and at the time of the  study was teaching at a school for LGBTQ+ youth, a nd Ron (white male) who has 10 years of experience teaching high school science and at the time of the study was teaching at a diverse public school (names are pseudonyms).

## Methods

As mentioned earlier, this paper stems from a porti on of a larger set of data. I first outline the met hods used on the full set of data – specifically how the curricular values framework was applied. Then, I outline how the smaller subset of data for this paper was identified and analyzed. Applying the Curricular Values Framework to the full set of data I apply qualitative analysis methods of multiple case studies (Yin, 2013). The multiple case studies include a focus on the three teachers involved as I am tracing how each teacher engaged with racial justice issues during co-design discussions. Using the curricular values framework – I identified design dilemmas that occurred throughout the 35 hours of meetings (Pomian Bogdanov, 2022). Desig n dilemmas began with a question that was posed to the group and had multiple possible ways in which it co uld be resolved. For example, “Are we going with a combination of student activity sheets and science notebooks?” (Matt, meeting 04) is a design dilemma during which the design team discussed the various ways in which students can have material that helps them track their understanding. I characterized a design dilemma when a question was followed by at least five turns of talk in which different curricular values were raised or at least  two actionable choices  were introduced.  Actionable choices were specific ideas and moves that could be implemented in the curriculum  materials. For example, an actionable choice may be “once in a while slipping in a handout that would go in the notebook” (Charlie, meeting 04). This actionable choice positions a student not ebook as the main place for students to collect tho ughts and ideas, but it also offers the option for teachers to pass out activity sheets to complement the notebook contributions. Importantly, the curricular values framework allows us to dig deeper into the driving values that underlie the choices. Curricular values tell us why someone believes a certain actionable choice is the  right one. For example, the choice of including a handout once in a while is driven by Charlie’s belief that “if they’re gonna end up spending five minutes labeling stuff….it seems l ike the tipping point…let’s just hand them a scaffo ld for that because it would take too long to make” (Charlie, m eeting 04). This stems from the curricular value of  Avoiding Burden or Confusion (a curricular value that centers ensuring that there won’t be unnecessary obstacle that get in the way of students to achieve the goals of the lesson) because he does not want students to waste their time doing something that is not critical to their learning.  Using this framework, I examine what curricular values influence the design dilemmas and eventually see whether participants change their stances and beliefs within deliberations around certain dilemmas.

While I identified all the design dilemmas that occurred over the 35 co-design meetings, I identified the actionable choices presented during these dilemmas and the curricular values that were driving the dec isionmaking processes. The curricular values co nsisted of a set of 10 different values that emerge d from the data through processes of close analysis of key segments  (Miles et al., 2013) along with member-checking with participants through stimulated recall interviews (Candela, 2019). The full list of values included some that were externally imposed on the design team like Alignment with the Standards and Making the Materials User Friendly for Teachers. It included age old values such as  Scientific Accuracy and Student Engagement. Not surprisingly, the biggest set of curricular values focuses on students experiencing the curriculum materials including Coherence from the Students’ Perspective, Avoiding Burden of Confusion, Supporting Students’ Reasoning with Tool s, and Conceptions of Rigorous Science. Two values focus on equitable approaches that expand th inking about science to be more subjective and justice-centered to support all student learners and studen t ideas. This includes the values Equity and Societal Historical and/or Political Contexts in Science. I will define the curricular values as I introduce them in later sections.

Using the Curricular Values Framework to unpack a subset of data When the full set of design dilemmas was identified, each design dilemma was coded at the utterance le vel for spoken actionable choices and curricular values. Therefore, I was able to see each utterance that raised the value of Societal Historical and/or Political Contexts in Science. To investigate values related to social justice issues, I focused on design dilemmas that centered delibe rations at the intersection of the last two curricul ar values presented above – Equity and Societal Historical and/or Political Contexts  in Science. Thus, I looked for design dilemmas in which these two curricular values were deliberated alongside each other in a single design dilemma. The value of Societal Historical and/or Political Context s in Science focuses on discussions about how science was/is influenced by social structures, discussing key people or events that influenc ed science. Equity is a value that focuses on being mindful of how science education is culturally and socially responsive and meaningful. From the full set of design dilemmas, there were on ly two design dilemmas that included overt discussions of racial and social justice issues. Th e first is the white dude design dilemma which centers around deliberations of whether and how the design team should include a white scientist, Herschel, in the lesson around an experiment that is credited to him. The second is the police heater design dilemma which centered deliberations around what type of anchoring phenomena would be be st for the unit, one that comes from a clip on a TV  show or one that stems from current a political issue around police guns that use microwaves. In order to trace how the design team grappled with  this value during the design dilemma to understand how the value was (re)defined, I applied qualitative thematic narrative discourse analysis methods (Bloome, 2022; Riessman, 2008) to each design dilemma segment. This allowed me to focus on how the team specifically grappled with the themes of historical and political context, ultimately working toward a more justice-oriented positionality. In what follows, I dive into the white dude and police heater design dilemmas by tracing the curricular values that were used to discuss possible ways of coming to a c onsensus and how, through discourse, participants deliberate these values within the context of constructing curriculum materials.

## Significance

The analysis of discussion segments of co-design meetings using the curricular values framewo rk enables us to characterize the process teachers go through as they grapple wit h designing curriculum materials. Teachers need to reimagine and redefine how they engage students in thinking about historical and political concepts in science. Identifying design dilemmas that specifically revol ved around tensions with these values and unpacking how negotiations are deliberated in efforts to write cu rriculum materials reveal how iterative redefining of ways to address these values occurs and how teachers can move toward ideas that have a more justice-oriented lens. Design dilemmas that specifically included the inte rsections of two  values Societal Historical and/or Political Contexts in Science and Equity resulted in two cases – white dude and police heater design dilemmas. Although these were the only explicit dilemmas related to these issues I observed, teachers likely thought about racial and social justice issues at various times d uring the design process. This is because there wer e design structures in place that explicitly asked the teach ers to imagine the thinking of students of diverse backgrounds. For example, teaching tips within the teacher guide drew attenti on to equity-oriented instructional moves – so as the teachers were writing the lessons they were prompted to think about these issues. Importantly, the two design dilemmas analyzed in th is paper reveal processes of critical shifts within  a conversation. These shifts included teachers reimagining what including historical science contexts in curriculum materials looks like – moving from simply mentioning the scientist responsible for an e xperiment to questioning what science history the teacher knows to be true. Also, the design team started to wrestle with the difficulties of writing materials that are based on real-world phenomena. Specifically, thinking about what ideas can be triggering for students and how to design for that.  These are more often becoming topics of discussion  for curriculum developers and teachers – being mindful of which historical figures and real-world phenomena we choose to represent. This paper showed how such conversations played out. These cases help us see how current teachers can come to think about social jus tice issues naturally as part of a design process. Instead of simply “requiring” teachers to teach in justice-centered ways, we can provide bridges by raising considerations about issues that  teachers have often thought about, and in that way  invite teachers to bring justice-centered teaching practices into their classrooms o n their o wn. One such set of considerations has to do with historical context. T hinking about historical context can lead to reflec tion on who did the research, and in what contexts. Importantly, as we see in this paper, these conversations can lead teachers to reevaluate their positions toward teaching with a justice-oriented lens.
