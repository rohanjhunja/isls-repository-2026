# The Role of Tolerance for Ambiguity in Model-Based Science Instruction

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents findings from an NSF-funded efficacy study of a high school biology modeling program that revealed teachers' Tolerance for Ambiguity (T4A) as a significant moderator of student learning gains. Teachers more comfortable with open-ended, ambiguous tasks facilitated greater scientific sensemaking among students. We discuss the theoretical significance of this finding in the context of science education reform efforts aligned with the Next Generation Science Standards, exploring how T4A rel ates to scientific uncertainty, productive struggle, and epistemic heterogeneity. Implications for professional learning design, equitable instruction, and future research directions are examined.

Despite more than a century of calls for authentic science experiences in classrooms, science instruction remains resistant to meaningful reform making the shift from accumulating a set of static, factual information to creating opportunities for students to take an active role in making sense of the world difficult to achieve at scale (Banilower, 2018). Taking a fresh look at issues that may be at the center of these challenges is a crucial undertaking. In recent years, High-Quality Instructional Materials (HQIM) that have been developed in response to the adoption of the Next Generation Science Standards  often include lesson structures that elicit a range of ideas from students and unfold over multiple days. Instruction like this requires teachers (and students) to withhold closure over time as the group works collaboratively to build knowledge. Such contexts can be messy and unpredictable while also being highly meaningful and authentic. Here we share early research findings that suggest that the Tolerance for Ambiguity construct may hold promise for developing novel approaches to understanding and supporting teachers as they take on these new and sometimes unfamiliar roles.

## Theoretical background: The tolerance for ambiguity construct

During the decade plus since the A Framework for K-12 Science Education (NRC, 2012) was published, our field is still struggling with effective means of enacting change in the power dynamics and distribution of the cognitive load in science classrooms. Previous research has shown that many teachers have productive ideas about the roles of uncertainty in science and productive struggle (Manz & Suarez, 2018; Chen et al. 2024; Starrett et al., 2024) but during enactment "teachers were commonly observed reducing uncertainty …before they were given a chance to grapple with the concept or their interpretation of the concepts" (Starrett, et al. 2024). In the context of model-based inquiry, reducing uncertainty may inhibit sensemaking by encouraging students to view a model as an illustration of the "correct" answer rather than using a model to make sense of a phenomenon (Gouvea & Passmore, 2017; Guy-Gatan, Gouvea, Griesemer & Passmore, 2019). Given that many teachers understand the importance and role of uncertainty, it seems counterintuitive that they would enact practices that reduce uncertainty in the classroom. We propose that the finding that teachers reduce uncertainty during instruction may be driven, in part, by teacher discomfort and lack of confidence in managing classrooms that feature it because of the relationship between uncertainty and ambiguity.  Science classrooms that are implementing curricul a that support productive struggle and endeavor to leverage scientific uncertainty are inherently ambiguous spaces. Anecdotally, teachers often express disbelief and discomfort around the assumptions embedded in the curricular tasks in model-based or other reform-oriented instructional materials. They worry about student engagement and confusion, how they will support students to bring up ideas that are "on the right track", and they express doubts about their ability to manage the meandering progress that knowledge building can sometimes take in the modern classroom context. One way to parse this i s to consider the scientific uncertainty as the desirable external context and the feeling of ambiguity as the affective/internal sense a teacher may have that the next steps are unclear, that the time a teacher should spend on a discussion or activity is not obvious, and a sense that students may say unexpected things, among other concerns. The concept of Tolerance for Ambiguity (also referred to in the literature as ambiguity tolerance, tolerance of ambiguity, or need for closure) has existed for over 75 years, and describes how individuals process, 1762 interpret, and react to information. Frenkel-Brunswik described people with low ambiguity tolerance as having a tendency to resort to black-and-white solutions, and to arrive at rapid and overconfident judgment, often at the neglect of reality. The contemp orary conceptual definition describes ambiguity tolerance as "an individual difference that predicts short and long-term reactions to a spectrum of situational characteristics relevant in a wide variety of life contexts and stimuli perceived as ambiguous" (McLain, 1993, p184). These individual differences are thought to represent a multi-dimensional personality trait (Budner, 1962), with roots in neurophysiological function (Hirsh et al., 2012). Complicating matters, responses to ambiguity are context dependent  indicating that ambiguous stimuli can be perceived as positive or negative depending on the context or expected outcomes (McLain et al., 2015). Reactions can range from extreme aversion to extreme attraction, with aversion resulting in anxiety and avoidance, and attraction resulting in curiosity, engagement, and enjoyment of  novelty and complexity.

Tolerance for Ambiguity has been studied in a broad range of fields, including medical education (Caulfield et al., 2014), engineering education (Mohammed, Okudan, & Ogot, 2006), organization studies (Weick, 2005), English language learning (Yu et al., 2022), and adolescent safety (Tymula et al., 2012). It has been shown to be related to a range of personality traits and behaviors, including need for cognitive closure (McLain, 1993), risk taking and aversion (McLain et al., 2015), creativity (Tegano, 1990), distress tolerance (Veilleux, 2019), cognitive flexibility (Endres et al., 2015), openness to experience (Jach & Smillie, 2019), anxiety and emotional intelligence (Furnham & Marks, 2013). However, there is scant research on how Tolerance for Ambiguity may impact science classroom practice and the explicit connections we are making between this construct in a modelbased inquiry context. T he importance and roles of scientific uncertainty, productive struggle, and epistemic heterogeneity as related to ambiguity tolerance are completely novel. Our study investigates the research question: Does the Tolerance for Ambiguity construct moderate the relationship between a model-based biology curriculum intervention and student learning outcomes? Addressing this question has implications for understanding a previously underexplored challenge teacher s face when enacting reform-oriented science instruction.

Our recently completed NSF-funded project examined the efficacy of a high school biology program that develops scientific understanding by engaging students in modeling. Through a quasi-experimental impact study, the project investigated the Model-Based Educational Resource (MBER). The MBER program is a year-long high school biology curriculum that uses models as core explanatory tools and provides teachers with pedagogical supports for phenomenon-based learning. The study included 5,480 high school biology students  (3,756 in the treatment group and 1,724 in the comparison group) nested within 48 teachers  (31 treatment, 17 comparison). Teachers ranged from 1 to 30 years of experience (M = 9.23 years) and were drawn from districts across California, representing a range of geographic and socioeconomic contexts.  The sample was predominantly 9th graders (80%), with approximately 82% of students qualifying for free or reduced-price lunch. Note that th ese data were collected during the later years of the COVID-19 pandemic when many schools provided free lunch to all students regardless of FRL status.  Treatment teachers participated in a 5-day summer professional learning experience focused on the curriculum's modeling approach and pedagogical supports. Teachers varied in their prior familiarity with the MBER program, ranging from no prior exposure to multiple years of use.  To measure teachers' Tolerance for Ambiguity, we used a subset of items from McLain's (1993) Multiple Stimulus Types Ambiguity Tolerance (MSTAT-I) scale. This instrument has demonstrated reliability and validity in measuring individuals' Tolerance for Ambiguity across various contexts. McLain reported a Cronbach's Alpha of 0.86 for the original 22 items, while we found a similar Alpha of 0.83 using the subset of nine items with the teachers in our study.

Science achievement was measured through assessments of students’ abilities to reason with scientific models developed for this project (Herrmann-Abell et al., 2023). We first tested the main effects of the MBER intervention on student model-based reasoning using a two-level random intercept model, controlling for prior achievement and accounting for students nested within classrooms (Wilson et. al, 2024). We then conducted a moderation analysis using a two-level random intercept model to examine whether teachers' Tolerance for Ambiguity moderated the treatment effect on student outcomes. The moderation model included the treatment indicator, teacher Tolerance for Ambiguity, and their interaction as primary predictors. Student-level covariates included pre-test model-based reasoning, class-mean pre-test model-based reasoning, gender, grade level, free/reduced lunch status, and English learner status. Teacher-level covariates included teacher confidence, vision for instruction, pre-test model-based reasoning, and years teaching high school. The model accounted for the nested structure of students within classrooms through random intercepts at the classroom level. 1763 Prior to the intervention, students in the treatment and comparison groups performed similarly on the pre-test (treatment mean = - 1.15 logits, comparison mean = -1.21 logits, p = 0.428), indicating the groups were well-matched at the start of the study. This study demonstrated a significant positive effect of the program on students' abilities to reason with scientific models (p<0.05, Hedges' g effect size = 0.154). However, moderation analysis revealed that the effect was not uniform across all contexts. We found a significant interaction between the effect of the treatment and teachers' Tolerance for Ambiguity (p = 0.004, coefficient = 0.168). This interaction suggests that the effect of our treatment varied depending on the teacher's Tolerance for Ambiguity. Specifically, the effect of the treatment decreased as teachers' Tolerance for Ambiguity decreased. We also observed significant differences in how teachers' Tolerance for Ambiguity moderated the treatment’s effectiveness. High-tolerance teachers (1 SD above the mean) were associated with treatment effects of 0.37 standard deviations, compared tonear-zero effects for low-tolerance teachers (1 SD below the mean).  Notably, other teacher-level variables included in the model including confidence, instructional vision, and content knowledge did not significantly moderate student outcomes, making Tolerance for Ambiguity (T4A) a distinctive moderating factor rather than a proxy for general teacher quality or preparedness.

Science educators continue to expand approaches to make science classrooms reflect findings from the learning sciences that position students as active, agentive constructors of their own knowledge in contexts that reflect how the scientific enterprise itself operates in a general sense and how students’ ways of knowing and cultural capital are recognized and incorporated into the learning environment. The moderation we found on the T4A construct in our study is important because it provides empirical support for theories that underlie the structure of Next Generation Science instructional materials and learning experiences. Our study uncovered a positive relationship between teachers' T4A and student outcomes in the context of model-based science instruction. Teachers attempting to create environments that are authentic to scientific practice reflect the underlying assumption that knowledge construction in a community involves complex and creative work. This can lead to lack of clarity  about what processes, ideas, and investigations to pursue and how to go about pursuing them — what other scholars have identified as epistemic and content uncertainty (Chen et al., 2024). Teachers and learners then are put in ambiguous situations where the next steps are not well-defined or scripted and the outcome not completely pre-ordained. We think of the scientific uncertainty that we are trying to replicate in science classrooms as a baseline condition that leads to ambiguous social and instructional s ituations. This research tradition continues to draw attention to the power dynamics and inadequate conditions for equity that are pervasive in science classrooms. Issues around psychological safety and comfort, relationality, and care for teachers and students are clearly at play in all classroom-based work (Adah Miller et al., 2024; Alzen et al. 2024), but how they are deeply embedded in and connected to the epistemic dimensions of a model-based inquiry classroom have not been explored in depth. In this context, our finding that variation in Tolerance for Ambiguity among our teacher participants moderated the student outcomes in our study has implications for further research and instructional.

## Conceptual implications

Despite a robust body of theoretical and empirical scholarship around creating the conditions for students to act as epistemic agents, and decades of curriculum and professional development aimed at supporting this shift, the needle has barely moved in terms of what happens in most science classrooms. This leads us to wonder if there are some previously underexplored challenges that teachers face when trying to develop and maintain communities of learners in their classrooms that engage in this version of authentic and meaningful science. Clearly, teachers need support (Manz & Suarez, 2018) and resources to alter the status quo, but the framing and forms of those supports may need to be expanded as we consider the teachers’ feelings and experiences using curricula. Our research begins to explore the relationships between several constructs at play in science education, including scientific uncertainty, productive struggle, and epistemic heterogeneity. Because ambiguity is a given if we create authentic science contexts in classrooms, it follows that intervening on the tolerance the teacher has for such ambiguity may yield important returns.

## Empirical implications

Further evidence is needed on how T4A may impact science teaching and learning, and what professional learning interventions are most effective at developing the trait. As McLain and colleagues state, "the future of ambiguity 1764 tolerance research is promising and it is expected that future studies will yield new insights into individual differences in reactions to the complex, unfamiliar, confusing, indeterminate, and incomplete stimuli that fall within the conceptual domain of ambiguity" (McLain et al., 2015). Several studies have found that exposure to ambiguous tasks can lead to an increase in ambiguity tolerance (Glover et al., 1978; Durrheim & Foster, 1997; Endres et al., 2015), and several researchers have found that ambigui ty tolerance can change over time, particularly with age, experience, and exposure to diverse situations (Helson & Wink, 1992). While the literature lacks controlled experimental studies, the body of evidence suggests that T4A is indeed malleable, and can be developed through repeated exposure to uncertainty, training and education that support decision-making in uncertain situations, or through cultural and social influences, norms, and expectations.

## Practical implications

The findings highlighted here have  implications for the design of effective professional learning and educative curriculum. Given the persistent challenges to reform and equity in science teaching and learning, expanding our approaches as science educators has the potential to be impactful  on curriculum and professional learning designers who are working to make science learning environments more inclusive of diverse worldviews and ways of knowing. Professional learning interventions could be designed to increase teachers' T4A and their effective use of open-ended learning experiences in their teaching. While our study did not directly measure equity outcomes, the literature suggests that ambiguity tolerance may play an important role in fostering empathy, understanding, and acceptance across diverse tolerance concerned individuals' reactions to other people and cultures, and their perceived differences (McLain, 2015). More fundamentally, Allport, in The Nature of Prejudice  (1954), connected prejudice to low T4A, and a tendency to latch onto the familiar and an inability to see all relevant sides. Frenkel-Brunswik (1949) described children who were ethnically prejudiced as also tending to reject ambiguity more so than their peers. While empirical evidence is lack ing, it is claimed that teachers with high T4A  cultivate inclusive environments by embracing uncertainty and encouraging diverse voices. They empower students to express themselves authentically, fostering a sense of belonging and safety (Yu, Wang, & Xia, 2022). Consequently, attention to this construct in science teaching and learning has the potential to support efforts to integrate diverse viewpoints and ways of knowing in the science classroom.

Next Steps What started as an exploratory analysis nested within a larger study has become a promising area for further research. Because of the novelty of T4A as a construct of interest in science education reform and its relationship to key areas of research in sci ence education around epistemic uncertainty and productive struggle, we suggest several avenues for further research. First, we need to know more about how, on a qualitative basis, classrooms taught by teachers with high T4A differ in observable ways from those taught by teachers with lower T4A. And second, we want to know more about the malleability of a teachers’ T4A and the impacts it may have on instructional moves and teachers’ affective state while implementing reform-based instructional materials. Future investigations into the Tolerance for Ambiguity construct in science education may provide a provide a productive step toward understanding the persistent gap between reform aspirations and classroom reality.
