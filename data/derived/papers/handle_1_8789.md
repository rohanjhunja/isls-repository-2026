# Examining Gender Differences in Identity Development Within an Online, Global, Collaborative, Informal STEM-learning Community

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how a learning environment that incorporates aspects of intergroup contact might impact identity development and expression. Adolescent students collaborate on creating STEM-related media projects in this global, virtual environment during weekly video conference calls, which were coded for social and self-identity constructs and analyzed using epistemic network analysis (ENA) to compare differences by gender. Literature anticipated that female students would reflect social identity constructs and male students reflect self-identity constructs, but the opposite was found indicating intergroup contact might defy expected outcomes and complement student identity development among adolescents.

## Introduction

Adolescents shape perceptions of self and others ba sed on lived experiences and socio-cultural norms. The development of self-concepts is essential in how an adolescent views themselves in their educational contexts. By engaging students with intergroup contact, bias between groups can be reduced and potentially form positive group and self-identity concepts that can overcome their societal identity. Allport (1954) recognized that intergroup contact can reduce prejudice and discrimination with four factors to reduce prejudice: (1) equal status amongst group members, (2) common goals, (3) intergroup cooperation, and (4) the support of authorities. Social identity theory posits that an individual’s self-co ncept is informed by their role in a social group o r groups and the emotional attachment one gives to that role (Ta ijfel, 1981). Previous research indicates that soci al identity correlates more so with females, whereas self-ident ity is attributed more so to males (Hwang, 2010).  Social identity consists of three constructs (group self-e steem, self-categorization, and commitment to the g roup) and two for individual self-identity (personal self-esteem and personal identification). This study involves an afterschool community of ado lescent students across four continents who collaborate together on developing media artifacts such as videos and presentations related to science, technology, engineering, and math (STEM) topics of their choice. Students from two or more countries participate i n video conference calls to share ideas and projects (online global meet-ups) supported by a facilitator.  Four components of intergroup contact theory are present: a virtual setting produces a space for students to join as equals, interact in a collaborative manner that creates intergroup cooperation, work towards a common goal of promoting peerled STEM learning, all while receiving support from the facilitators throughout the process. This study explores the effect of intergroup contact on identity development through an initial comparison by gender.

## Methods

The data used in this analysis consisted of discour se from transcripts of five online global meet-ups:  February 2020 (3 females, 7 males), April 2020 (3 females, 3 males), August 2020 (6 females, 7 males) and two in March 2021(one with 5 females, 1 male, the other 1 female, 3 males) with participants from Brazil, Cameroon, Kenya, Mexico, Namibia and the U.S. The transcriptions were coded (see Table 1 for codebook) for three social identity and one self-identity construct adapted from Ellemers et al. (1999) and three identity-oriented constructs unique to the data. Coding was done independently by two raters, followed by social moderation. ENA generates network mod els of the connections among relevant constructs by quantifying the frequency of their co-occurrences within conversations in the data (Shaffer, 2017). For this study, each utterance (separated by turn of talk) was used as the unit of analysis, and each meet-up by date constituted a conversation to which the connections were limited. Only patterns of student discourse were examined.

## Discussion

Based on previous social identity research, it was anticipated that males would demonstrate a stronger association to self-identity constructs, whereas females would show stronger affiliations to social identity (Hwan g, 2010). However, the results found that males showed strong er connections to social identity constructs: group  selfcategorization and group self-esteem while female participants exhibited stronger connections to identity-oriented constructs. The unique context of this learning env ironment may have impacted the hypothesized relatio ns between gender, and social-and self-identity. Using ENA, these insights help show how a learning environment that incorporates aspects of intergroup contact can shift what is expected in identity development.
