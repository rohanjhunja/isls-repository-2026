# When Ideas Take Turns: Temporal Embodiment of Disciplinary Engagement

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaborative problem-solving is central to science education, yet engaging disciplinarily in these settings is fluid, shifting across turns of talk in ways that shape group learning. Capturing these dynamics can provide insights for designing supports that foster deeper disciplinary engagement. This study examines how disciplinary engagement unfolds over time in a game-based science learning environment. Drawing on the Productive Disciplinary Engagement framework, we analyzed 2,415 discourse turns from 24 middle school groups using sequential and temporal analyses. Results revealed distinct pathways and temporal patterns across different groups, shedding lights into differences in learning outcomes. Findings demonstrate the value of temporal-sequential methods for informing scaffold design that supports productive collaboration.

Collaborative problem-based learning environments play a central role in science education by engaging students in authentic, complex inquiry (Dewi et al., 2023). However, group work alone does not guarantee meaningful disciplinary participation or learning success (Koretsky et al., 2021), prompting growing attention to how learners engage deeply with disciplinary content during collaboration (Fredricks, 2023). A key lens for examining such engagement is Productive Disciplinary Engagement (PDE) (Engle & Conant, 2002), which describes learners’ sustained participation in disciplinary practices as they co-construct knowledge and make collective intellectual progress (Engle, 2012; Cheng et al., 2019). PDE highlights four principles supporting productive collaboration: intellectual authority and responsibility, accountability to disciplinary norms and peers, problematizing through critique and questioning, and the use of resources to support reasoning. Because collaborative cognition is socially enacted through discourse (Jeong & Hmelo-Silver, 2016), disciplinary engagement is best understood as a dynamic interactional process. Prior research has commonly used frequency-based coding to relate discourse features to learning outcomes (Yang et al., 2022), showing that successful groups engage more in idea generation and shared understanding (Popov et al., 2018). Yet count-based approaches may overlook the evolving and distributed nature of collaborative thinking (Lefstein et al., 2015). Recent work therefore emphasizes temporal and sequence-oriented analyses, demonstrating that ordered interaction patterns better predict learning out comes than isolated discourse frequencies (Chen et al., 2018). Despite these advances, temporal approaches have rarely been applied specifically to the mechanisms of productive disciplinary engagement and associations with learning outcomes. Addressing this gap, the present study integrates the PDE framework with sequence-and time-based analyses to operationalize engagement as a dynamic process. By examining transitions among PDE principles and comparing trajectories across groups with different performance levels, the study aims to identify mechanisms underlying productive collaboration and inform adaptive learning supports.  To guide the analysis, we ask: what sequential patterns of PDE principles emerge, and how do they relate to group learning differences? how do temporal patterns of PDE principles unfold over time across groups with different learning outcomes?

## Learning context, participants, and data sources

The study was conducted in EcoJourneys (Zou et al., 2024), a game-based computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) environment in which small groups of middle school students investigate the algae-related causes of illness affecting tilapia at a fish farm in the Philippines. The game includes four quests built around authentic scientific problems, in which s tudents alternate between individual inquiry and collaborative activities Deduce 354 and TIDE. This study focuses on Quest Four, a jigsaw-style activity emphasizing scientific argumentation and solution generation. Each student investigates two of four algae-reduction solutions (flocculation, aerators, filter feeders, and harvesters) through videos and background materials before returning to their group as an “expert.” Groups collaboratively compare solutions through chat and face-to-face discussion, submit written justifications in Deduce, and develop persuasive proposals in TIDE. Responses are scored on a 1–3 scale for reasoning quality, clarity, and evidence use; combined Deduce and TIDE  scores serve as indicators of group learning outcome. Figure 1 shows example screenshots from EcoJourneys, including individual inquiry (left) and a Quest Four informational video on flocculation (right).

The implementation took place in two middle schools in the Southeastern and Northeastern United States over 8–9 school days. From 31 recorded groups, 24 groups of four students were selected based on video quality. Groups were categorized as High-Performing (HP) (n = 11) or Low-Performing (LP) (n = 13) based on combined scores. Video-recorded face-to-face discourse was transcribed and synchronized with in-game chat using timestamp alignment, yielding 2415 discourse turns (1347 HP; 1068 LP), primarily from f ace-to-face interaction (1653 turns) with additional chat contributions (762 turns). We first conducted exploratory count-based analyses (Chen et al., 2018), examining contribution percentages, total turns, and average turn length across HP and LP  groups using independent t-tests. As no 355 significant differences were found, subsequent analyses focused on sequential and temporal discourse patterns.

To address RQ1, we applied Lag Sequential Analysis to compute transitional probabilities among PDE principles, examining both direct transitions (lag = 1) and delayed transitions (lag = 2). Group differences were tested using two-way independent t-tests (α =.05). We further conducted Frequent Sequence Mining to identify recurring multi-event discourse chains, analyzing three-and four-event sequences with a 3% support threshold. Analyses were implemented using the LagSequential and arulesSequences R packages. To address RQ2, discourse timelines were normalized by dividing each group’s turns into 24 equal bins, aggregated into Early, Middle, and Lat e phases. Proportions of each PDE principle were calculated within each phase, and a series of two-way ANOVAs tested the effects of Time Phase and Group Type, as well as their interaction (α =.05). Visualizations were produced using tidyverse and TraMineR.

RQ1: Sequential patterns among PDE principles by group types Six transition pairs showed significant differences in transitional probabilities between HP and LP groups (Figure 3). Most significant transitions in HP groups shifted toward Resources as the subsequent move, suggesting that students frequently followed relational or decision-oriented discourse with evidence use, justification, or idea elaboration. The transition Authorit y → Problematizing (p <.05, d = 0.34) was also more likely in HP groups, indicating that expressed ideas often prompted critique and deeper epistemic engagement. In contrast, significant LP transitions more frequently led to Socialize, suggesting greater reliance on socioemotional or off-task interaction. Sequences such as Problematizing → Socialize and Accountability → Socialize indicate that critique or idea solicitation often shifted away from disciplinary inquiry. Lag-2 analysis revealed that HP groups  were significantly more likely to exhibit indirect transitions from Problematizing → Resources (p <.05, d = 0.29), reinforcing the pattern that disciplinary resource use followed inquiry moves either immediately or after an intervening contribution. Frequent Sequence Mining (Table 2) further identified longer discourse patterns differentiating groups. HP groups showed sequences reflecting iterative cycles of critique, accountability, and authority (e.g., AC → PR → AC → AU), suggesting sustained collaborative reasoning. LP groups displayed more repetitive problematizing patterns with limited integration (e.g., PR → PR → RE), indicating potential confusion or weaker idea development despite small effect sizes for multi-event sequences. For Problematizing, a significant main effect of Time (p =.010, partial η² =.56) indicated increased use across phases, with a stronger rise in HP groups. This pattern suggests that HP groups engaged in critical examination later in the task, possibly after case presentations enabled comparison and disagreement. For Authority, both the main effect of Time Phase (p =.053) and the interaction (p =.024, partial η² =.30) were significant. HP groups showed increased authority use during the Middle phase, corresponding to the transition from sharing information to evaluating ideas, suggesting growing ownership and leadership in argumentation. LP groups peaked at Early  and Middle stages but declined in the Late phase, indicating difficulty sustaining an authoritative stance during consensus building. No significant effects were found fo r Accountability, although descriptive increases appeared from Early to Middle phases in both groups. For Resources, a significant Group Type effect (p <.001, partial η² =.28) showed that HP groups consistently referenced evidence across all phases, part icularly early in the task, suggesting stronger disciplinary grounding when group members  shared cases with others. In contrast, LP groups relied less on resources and more on early authority statements, often proposing solutions before all information had been shared, as reflected in qualitative video observations. Finally, Socialize showed significant effects of Time (p <.001, partial η² =.62) and Group Type (p =.009, partial η² =.28). LP groups began with higher levels of socioemotional interaction, followed by a mid-task decline and slight late rebound, suggesting challenges transitioning from informal interaction to sustained task-focused collaboration.

Figure 4 Temporal Pattern of PDE Use by Groups This study used sequential and temporal analyses to examine how d isciplinary engagement unfolds during collaborative problem solving using PDE framework, revealing patterns not captured by traditional count-based measures. Several limitations should be noted. First, post-hoc video analysis showed that statistically identified transitions varied in timing and interactional quality; rapid exchanges sometime s reflected quick rebuttals rather than sustained engagement, suggesting that temporal spacing and interactional dynamics are not fully captured by sequential statistics (Wise & Padmanabhan, 2009). Second, mapping discourse to PDE principles required interpretation, and some interactions did not align cleanly with the framework. Although a Socialize category was added to capture socioemotional and coordination talk, this addition was post hoc and may limit generalizability. Despite these limitations, the study demonstrates the value of process-oriented analyses for understanding collaborative engagement. While count-based measures showed no group differences, sequential and temporal analyses revealed meaningful variations in how engagement developed over time, highlighting the importance of examining when and how discourse moves occur. Integrating quantitative analyses with qualitative video interpretation provided a more comprehensive account of engagement processes and identified transition patterns that may serve as indicators of productive collaboration and inform the design of scaffolds and learning analytics. Future work should examine whether discourse sequences predict learning outcomes, support real-time detection of unproductive interaction patterns, and extend analyses across tasks and multimodal data sources to better understand how disciplinary engagement develops and is sustained in collaborative learning. 357
