# Creating Synergistic Scaffolding Between the Tools of Discourse and Technology

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research has looked at how teachers use discourse to develop aspects of the learning environment such as argumentation or autonomy. However, few studies have focused on how teachers utilize discourse surrounding educational simulations in practice (Erduran, 2019; Tabak & Kyza, 2018). More specifically, attention has not been given to how teachers use discourse to create synergistic scaffolding as characterized by Tabak and Kyza (2018) between tools such as discussion and technology. This case study uses two analytical frameworks, Rasmussen’s Inquiry Oriented Discursive Moves (IODM) and Lidar’s Epistemic Moves Analysis (EMA), to reveal how a teacher uses discourse to create synergistic scaffolding between the discussion and simulations. This case also demonstrates that by creating synergy between discussion and simulation the teacher positions students with opportunities to support shifting practical epistemology around chemical concepts.

## Introduction

Providing students with opportunities to evaluate k nowledge surrounding scientific phenomena can lead to epistemic shifts (Jiménez-Aleixandre & Brocos, 2018). Interactive technologies, such as simulations, have been developed that support students as they engage in these types of explorations by guiding students to reason, defend, and critique claims and explanations about the natural world. Research on educational technologies mainly focus on how designs achieve learning outcomes (Perkins, 2014) or how students interact with the tool (Smeta na, & Bell, 2012; Price, 2019). Similarly, research on teacher discourse is often used to understand how teachers develop student inquiry through argumentation alone, rather than how teachers create epistemic opportunities for students to engage in reasoning. Surprisingly, little attent ion has been given to teacher discourse around the use of educational simulations, even though teacher discou rse plays a role in developing students’ scientific  practice (Tabak & Kyza, 2018). We know that teachers orchestrate the learning environment through their practice choices, yet we have not captured how teachers use tools, li ke discourse, to purposefully integrate technology into the existing learning environment. Here we use two analytical frameworks to observe this interaction with a focusing on question: How do teachers’ discourse choices cre ate synergistic scaffolding between tools in the le arning environment?

Theoretical frameworks Argumentation has often been looked at as a means of replicating scientific sense-making in the classroom (Ford, 2012; Kuhn, 2013; Osborne, 2019). When teachers place students into roles that require them to use evidence to reason toward a conclusion rather than accepting an swers given by the teacher, students participate in  science practices that develop reasoning (NRC, 2011; Erdura n, 2019). This creates the ability for students to engage in evidence-based argumentation that can support revis ing practical epistemology (Berland & Resier, 2011;  Linn, 2018).

Two theoretical frameworks were used to characterize how teachers’ discourse and the role of technology impact students’ learning and potential for epistemological change. The first is Inquiry Oriented Discursive Moves (IODM; Rasmussen, 2008) defined as moves that involved questioning, telling, managing, and revoicing. These moves provide specific attention to discourse related to teacher and student inquiry as they are situated in settings of argumentation. By looking at inquiry-oriented di scourse, we can observe the social setting of the l earning environment created by the teacher. The second framework is Epistemological Move Analysis (EMA) developed by Lidar, Lundqvist, and Östman (2006) defined as d iscourse moves that are instructional, reconstructi ng, reorienting, generative, and confirming that allows us to examine how teachers use discourse to guide and support students in noticing information is important to the construction of knowledge. While the ultimate responsibility for shifting practical epistemology lies with the students, the teacher can, through their discursive and epistemic moves, provide the opportunities for this to occur.

Tabak & Kyza’s (2018) scaffolding functions were utilized to reveal how discourse created synergistic scaffolding to support student reasoning. Specifically, how teachers use discourse to create a synergistic relationship between discussion and simulation tools, in such a way that supports students’ development in scientific reasoning.

## Present study and context

The present study analyzed a subset of data collected during the Connected Chemistry Curriculum (CCC; Stieff,

2019) project that implemented chemistry curriculum  accompanied by visualization software. As part of the

project, video recordings of teachers were analyzed using the Reformed Teaching Observation Protocol (RTOP; Piburn et al, 2000, p. 34). Data of teachers who utilized the simulation while also demonstrating reformed teaching practices were identified and considered for further study. Here we present one case study out of a collection that demonstrates how teachers use inquiry-oriented and epistemic discourse moves to create synergistic scaffolding between the discussion and simulation tools of the classroom. This case takes place during the last 25 minutes of a 90-minute class period where CCC materials were in use. The topic of molarity, or concentration of a solution, is discussed and then observed using the simulation. A screenshot of the simulation screen for this ac tivity is shown in Figure 1. Discussion takes place in a whol e class setting whereas simulation use is done in s maller groups of 2-3 students. The teacher walks around to  engage students in questions about their work duri ng the simulation time.

## Results

The teacher in this case, begins with a whole class discussion to initiate interest in the topic of concentration. The topic of concentration is first connected to an everyday scenario by the teacher asking students if they have ever had a drink that was too strong and had to water it down. Using a telling discourse move the teacher explains this phenomenon is called concentration. The teacher the n uses instructional discourse move to create an ep istemic reconstruction of the task of thinking about concentration in terms of the chemical words, solute and solvent. The teacher does this by drawing a representation of a mixture of Kool-Aid™ and water. The teacher then asks students to label the solute and solvent in the diagram (See Figure 2). This demonstrates how the teacher creates synergist ic scaffolding to connect discussion and the simulation by pointing out that this diagram will b e a reference when using the simulation (See Figure  3). The teacher, using instructional and reorienting moves,  make explicit the terminology is important to unde rstand because it will “come up in the simulation ” (Figure 3). Using Tabak & Kyza’s (2018) scaffoldi ng lens, we can see the function of the notes aim to reduce complexity of using the simulation.

## Discussion

While classroom technologies have been integral in giving students access to scientific phenomenon, we demonstrate here how the teacher makes this technol ogy accessible such that epistemic shifts and stude nt reasoning can occur. By using the IODM and EMA frameworks we see how the teacher creates this accessibility by using synergistic scaffolding between the tools of the classroom such that tools function to scaffold one another. This is created through the deliberate discursive m oves that use the basis of argument to support inqu iry and epistemic agency for the students. How students und erstand observations are revealed by the teacher th rough discourse moves that develop and draw attention to conceptually important details. Using these details, the teacher prepares students to make observations that provide opportunities to reason and create epistemic shifts. Revealing how the teacher integrates discussion and simulation using discourse moves demonstrate how creating synergistic scaffolding can provide Between tools. Students could have made similar observations while using a simulation without the presence of the disc ourse, however, what would be missing are the scaff olds provided from the discussion that students can tap into when attempting to make epistemic shifts. The same can be said in the reverse; that discussion alone may have afforded some students enough to reason through concepts, but by providing the visualization of phenomenon wi th opportunities to create a problematic scenario, students were able to observe a phenomenon giving them a visual opportunity for solidifying their reasoning.
