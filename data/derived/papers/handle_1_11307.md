# From Discomfort to Growth: Embedding Frustration in Makeathon Design for Teacher Development

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research explores the learning design of a three-day educational makeathon for in-service teachers. The makeathon aimed to provide an exemplary learning experience, challenging participants while incorporating learning scaffolds to support them. We examined the learning trajectories that emerged as teachers navigated teamwork-related challenges and group dynamics in a complex project. This paper specifically investigates how these challenges fostered teacher learning and growth, and identifies the design elements that supported participants throughout the process. Our findings present a range of learning outcomes, including teachers’ increased appreciation of diverse team collaboration and, in some cases, disengagement due to sustained frustrations. This study highlights how immersing teachers in challenging teamwork during an educational makeathon candeepen their understanding of maker learning, offering insights into effective teacher training designs.

## Introduction

In the summer of 2024, a pioneering in-service teacher training event was held in Israel—a three-day educational makeathon that attracted 100 in-service teachers. The makeathon’s designers aimed t o offer teachers an opportunity to engage as learners in a model educat ional making experience, allowing them to encultura te its nuanced design elements and deepen their understanding of maker education. To achieve this goal, the makeathon’s design incorp orated various elements, such as heterogeneous grouping of participants, team process management scaffolds, structured reflections on learning, and an inclusive atmosphere to encourage participation from the high ly diverse group of educators. The makeathon’s desi gn intentionally included elements that could provoke discomfort and challenge, reflecting the belief tha t encountering and overcoming difficulties during the maker process is a vital aspect of maker education, fostering resilience, adaptability, and confidence (San Juan & Murai, 2022). Over the course of three days, teachers were pre-assigned to intentionally diverse groups balanced b y varying skill sets and experience in maker educatio n. Teams were tasked with collaboratively designing  and creating innovative, technology-enhanced artifa cts, aiming to  ignite teachers’ curiosity about the potential of maker learning, as stated in the makeathon's opening prompt. The combination of unfamiliar team assignments and an ambiguous, open-ended task became a source of challenge and struggle, a key factor in teachers’ learning throughout the makeathon.

There is a growing need for research on how to trai n teachers for maker education (Bevan, 2017). Educating in-service teachers about the complexities of maker le arning is challenging, as it often differs significantly from traditional school practices (Campos et al., 2019). This makeathon provided an oppor tunity to explore how an intentionally designed, immersive, a nd challenging learning experience for in-service teachers can lead to meaningful insights into constructionist learning, specifically maker education, even within the limited time available for professional development.

## Background

Maker education represents an evolving approach to learning that emphasizes hands-on, experiential engagement, positioning learners as creators—whether as designers, inventors, or builders. In the process of creating artifacts, learners engage with a range of tools, spanning from basic manual instruments to sophisticated digital fabrication technologies (Wilkinson & Petrich, 2017). This peda gogical approach is often considered revolutionary for modern education, with the potential to cultivate i nnovative, t echnology-rich, and collaborative learning experiences for the 21st century (Rouse & Rouse, 2022). Despite its promise, maker education often clashes with conventional educational norms, making implementation challenging (Sormunen et al., 2022). There remains a need for comprehensive teacher trai ning in maker education, as many educators are unfamiliar with its complexities and how it contras ts with traditional practices. As a result, teacher s may implement maker activities superficially, focusing on the construction of artifacts without fully grasping how the designing and building process connects to more pro found learning (Campos et al., 2019). This undersco res the importance of training programs  that equip educators with a thorough understanding of maker learning and its distinction from traditional educational approaches.

Frustration is an essential aspect of maker learnin g, although it is often considered undesirable in traditional education (Resnick, 2017). This aligns with Papert’s (1996) concept of hard fun, where challenging yet engaging activities motivate learners and contribute to their identity development, highlighting the importance of frustration in constructionist learning. Facing frustration and successfully overcoming it can tran sform challenges into valuable learning experiences. Howe ver, structured support i s essential to help participants navigate challenges productively, ensuring that fru stration becomes an opportunity for growth rather t handebilitating. Supporting teachers to experience fru stration or hard fun in making and providing struct ures for teachers to support learners in these processes is crucia l for integrating maker education into formal schoo ling (San Juan & Murai, 2022).

Learning design is the ecology of interacting elements intentionall y used to support complex learning (Cobb et al., 2003). These elements include specifi c discursive practices, task structures, participan t structures, tools and materials, and their interplay throughout the learning course. The interplay of numerous elements creates a complex system that is not easily articulated and conveyed (Koper, 2005). To analyze and articulate the learning design that supported teachers as they negotiated t eam-related challenges in the makeathon, we built on Sandoval’s (2014) conjecture mapping approach. Conj ecture mapping serves as a research framework for articulating and analyzing learning designs. It connects the designer’s intentions, the implemented learning design, the forms of participation it encourages, and the l earning outcomes it produces. By systematically tra cing how design elements influence mediating processes and lead to specific outcomes, conjecture mapping provides a clear and actionable understanding of how learning designs function in practice. While studies have highlighted the value of discomfort and productive struggle in maker education (San Juan & Murai, 2022), few have explored how these el ements can be purposefully designed into profession al development for teachers. This paper addresses this  gap by providing actionable insights into designin g teacher training that intentionally incorporates team-related hardships, fostering a deeper understanding  of the maker learning process, and preparing teachers to support  students through these experiences. We asked the f ollowing research question: How do participants' teamwork-related frustrations in an educational makeathon co ntribute to growth?

## Methodology, data collection and analysis

To explore our research question, the study uses a grounded qualitative approach (Charmaz, 2006) to identify and articulate categories of embodied design elements a nd associated learning outcomes. Data sources inclu ded participants' journals where they reflected on thei r learning experiences throughout the makathon, pre-and postsurveys, and semi-structured interviews with the makeathon’s designer s. These sources provided insights into participants' experiences of teamwork-related frustration and the designers' intentions in incorporating frustration as a learning tool. Reflective diaries, in particul ar, offered a detailed understanding of participant s' frustrations, the design elements that supported their growth, and the resulting learning outcomes. From an initial 52 diaries, we excluded 18 lacking substantial content, leaving 36 for review. Of these, we identified 23 that spec ifically reflected on teamwork-related frustrations. We also included pre-and post-survey data from these 23 participants, representing 11 of the 15 teams.

Two types of categorical analysis were conducted: t he first focused on identifying design elements tha t supported managing frustration and overcoming chall enges, while the second involved a thematic analysi s to define the learning outcomes associated with these experiences. Qualitative data were analyzed using an inductive coding process, allowing themes to emerge naturally. To ensure consistency and reliability, these them es were cross-verified across multiple data sources.

Semi-structured interviews with the designers were analyzed to assess how their intentions to incorporate frustration aligned with participants' experiences.  The findings were integrated into a conjecture map  (Sandoval,

2014) to link design elements, participant responses, and outcomes, thus providing insights into the impact of the

learning design.

To explore participants' learning trajectories from  hardship to growth, we focused on the mediating process of overcoming teamwork-related frustrations. This mediating process served as a lens to understand how the makeathon’s design shaped engagement with challenges and led to meaningful learning outcomes. We mapped specific design elements within the makeathon that contributed to these experiences, presenting our findings as a focused conjecture map that visually articulates co nnections between design features and emergent lear ning outcomes.

The first two authors led the analysis process and worked closely together. Author 1, who served as a facilitator during the event, transitioned to a res earch role only after completing facilitation dutie s to minimize potential bias. Triangulation was achi eved through collaborative researcher discussions, supported by AI assistance (ChatGPT-4), to identify recurring themes and relevant quote s, enhancing the depth of thematic analysis. Ethical approval was obtained from [the U niversity]'s ethics committee, and all participants  provided informed consent.

## Findings

The findings presented here detail the embodied des ign elements and learning outcomes that supported participants in experiencing and overcoming frustra tion, as mapped in the conjecture map shown in Figu re 1. Numbers in parentheses refer to the frequency o f reflective diaries (out of 23) in which these ele ments were referenced.

Figure 1 Educational Makathon’s Learning Design’s Conjecture Map Tracing the learning trajectory that have led participants to experience and overcome teamwork-related frustration resulting in professional learning and personal growth The following embodied learning designs have been f ound to support participants’ experiencing of teamwork-related frustration and overcoming it. Diversely balanced team s (n=18) refers to the deliberate inclusion of participants with varied skills and ex perience levels in maker education. This approach e merged as the most frequently cited supportive element, creat ing teams with complementary competencies that effe ctively addressed the diverse challenges of the makeathon’s creative, technology-enhanced tasks. It enabled participants to contribute based on their strengths while foster ing opportunities for them to learn unique skills f rom one another. However, for some (n=2), this element also  heightened pressure and frustration due to expecta tions of fulfilling a specific role. Team pre-assignment (n=16) refers to assigning participants into teams of seven, where most members had no prior acquaintance. This elemen t played a dual role: while it often created challe nges in negotiating a shared vision and working with unfami liar peers (n=9), some participants valued it for p roviding a sense of the discomfort their students often face, ultimately contributing to personal growth (n=7). Teaching team support (n=9) involved assistance provided by six mentors w ho offered emotional and practical support through timely interventions, helping participants overcome  challenges. Additionally, the event's technical su pport team, composed of students, played a significant role in supporting novices by providing guidance on using t ools and equipment. Work process management (n=9) facilitated growth by fostering discussions on task division and role assignment, allowing some participants to take the lead in these efforts, thereby securing themselves a productive role within the team. Inclusive and playful atmosphere (n=8) including communal lunches, helped reduce barriers to participation, particularly for novices. Structured reflections (n=7) held twice a day, offered space for team-related hardships to be voiced and acknowledged. Pre-makeathon familiarization conducted in an online kickoff ten days before the event, was noted as helpful in building cohesion within preassigned teams. The findings on participants' learning outcomes rev eal that learning to collaborate in diverse teams a nd valuing different perspectives emerged as key outco mes (n=19). These participants came to appreciate t hat diversity within teams can be seen as a strength, w ith one participant noting, “Different ways of thin king among team members provided valuable learning opportunities.” This experience led participants to view group work as a learning process in itself rat her than solely focusing on project completion, wit h another commenting, “The most important thing we achieved was respecting each other and being patient with one another.” Experiencing teamwork challenges firsthand allowed participants to gain empathy, fostering a deeper understanding of the difficulties their students fa ce (n=15). One participant reflected, “I gained a b etter understanding of how children feel when assigned  to work in groups,” while another added, “The emoti onal experience of being in the position of a struggling student will help me connect with students who face difficulties in the future.” Some participants further acknowled ged “the frustrations that aris e—feelings that even we struggled to manage—so how can we expect an elementary school child to handle them?” Participants highly valued design elements that enh anced collaboration (n=10), particularly the opportunity to work in preassigned, non-homogeneous teams. As one participant expressed, “S ometimes I give in to the students and let them choose their own teams, but there’s great value in the challenge of connecting with new people. The contribution from this experience is powerful and significant.” Participants also appreciated the role of mentors, highlighting that “A caring and attentive mentor’s guidance is very helpful.” Interestingly, some participants’ learning outcomes  diverged from the designers’ original assumptions. A few participants came to recognize teamwork chall enges, seeking to prevent them, and at times found the frustration overwhelming (n=4). One participant  reflected on her frustration with the challenges of  teamwork, stating, "I mainly take from this experience of frustration the hope to learn how to prevent similar experiences for my students."

## Conclusion

The makeathon’s design provided in-service educators with direct, authentic experience s of teamwork-related challenges, highlighting both the benefits and limi tations of collaborative learning. By working in pr eassigned, unfamiliar teams, many participants  engaged deeply with the realities of frustration, a llowing them to develop empathy and insights into their students' teamwork challenges. For some, this immersive experience underscored the gap between their own perspectives and their students' experience s in maker activities, positioning the makeathon as a valuable learning event for teachers.

While most participants valued frustration as an opportunity for growth through overcoming teamworkrelated frustrations, others found the experience o verwhelming. They were reluctant to expose their students to similar challenges, indicating some misalignment with the designers' initial conjectures. These findings highlight opportunities to refine the makeathon design, focus ing on balancing the elements that introduced team-related challenges with those intended to support participants through these challenges. These reflections underscore the importance of balancing positive frustrat ion, or hard fun, which stimulates learning, agains t debilitating frustration, which can lead to disengagement.

Despite efforts to balance team dynamics, some grou ps experienced disproportionately high levels of teamwork-related frustration, affecting their overall learni ng experience. This outcome may be unavoidable in preassigned teams, even with careful balancing. Cer tain team compositions can make collaboration parti cularly challenging. To better support participants in thes e situations, it may be necessary to offer opportun ities for engagement in alternative pathways beyond the stric t team structure. Such adaptations could support th e makeathon’s aim to foster collaboration, perseveran ce, and constructive learning experiences while all owing for individual differences in engagement.
