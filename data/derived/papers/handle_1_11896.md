# Designing and Promoting Primary Students’ Knowledge-building Trajectories and Authentic Scientific Inquiry

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigated fourth graders’ authentic scientific inquiry within a knowledge-building environment supported by Knowledge Forum. Designed to cultivate students as scientists, the environment facilitated collaborative inquiry through problem formulation, explanation co-construction, and theory building – key practices aligned with authentic scientific practice. Quantitative analyses revealed significant improvements in both domain and epistemic understanding. Qualitative data, including Knowledge Forum discourse and classroom artifacts, demonstrated how the environment fostered students’ sustained scientific engagement and supported the progressive development of their ideas. The study identified shifts in students’ domain understanding and reflective practices, highlighting the value of tracing knowledge building trajectories to support reflective learning and foster authentic scientific inquiry.

Knowledge building for sustained pursuit and authentic scientific inquiry Science education aims to cultivate  young generations’  scientific literacy through inquiry-based learning, emphasizing doing and reflecting (Bybee, 2004; Flick & Lederman, 2004). However, eve n though students are taught to conduct inquiries by doing science, studies show that not many understand why they are doing it or what science is, leading to gaps in their comprehension of scienti fic concepts (Duschl, 2008; Chinn et al., 2011). Knowledge building, a computer-supported collaborative learning model, mirrors the scientific communities by promoting students’ collective cognitive responsibilities to advance community knowledge (Scardamalia, 2022). On Knowledge Forum, students collaborate to deepen inquiry and improve knowledge, fostering shared epistemic agency and authentic community inquiry (Scardamalia  & Bereiter, 2014). Research indicates that knowled ge building enhances students ’ scientific processes, scientific understanding and epistemology of science (Chen & Hong, 2016),  pointing further exploration into systematically analyzing the knowledge building  designs and trajectories for students’ engagement in authentic scientific inquiry.  Accordingly, the present study investigated the roles of knowledge building in enhancing students’ scientific and epistemic understanding, characterized their KNOWLEDGE BUIDING  processes illustrating authentic inquiry,  examined their shifts towards deeper understanding supported by reflection. Three research questions are included:

(1) Did the knowledge-building approach focusing on  authentic scientific inquiry enhance students’

scientific and epistemic understanding?

(2) How did students engage in authentic scientific inq uiry, and what were the trajectories of growth

supported by knowledge-building designs?

(3) What characterized the changes in students’ scienti fic understanding supported by knowledge-building inquiry and reflection?

Designing the knowledge-building environment Participants included 29 fourth graders between 10-and 12-years old inquiring about Cold and Hot. The knowledge building instruction lasted for five sessions, each 45 minutes, divided into three Phases corresponding to how scientists and the science community work (see Table 1, Figure 1a). The design was principle-based rather than using scripted activities, allowing for the emergence of authentic inquiry  (Scardamalia, 2002), and several fundamental principles, including ‘epistemic agency,’ ‘improvable ideas,’ ‘community knowledge’ ‘transformative assessment,’ were incorporated in instruction. Phase I: Preliminary exploration of Cold and Hot – problem formulation. Students used the Knowledge Forum to write questions related to their everyday experiences, supported by scaffolds that illuminate scientific inquiry processes (see Figure 1b). They visualized their prior knowledge through mind maps and posted research questions on Knowledge Forum. Students collaborated in groups on Knowledge Foru m to build on explanations and conducted simple experiments for evidence. In Phase I, students ’ initial questions and explanations were organized into five Knowledge Forum  Views, aligning with scientific investigation scope s. This process organized and enriched students’ knowledge construction, fostering their authentic scientific inquiry experiences (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014).

Phase II: Building on and deepening inquiry  – explanation co-construction and theory building. Phase II aimed to deepen students ’ knowledge by summarizing and synthesizing Phase I exploration. Students categorized 66 Knowledge Forum  notes into different Views. They summarized notes within the same View, synthesized across Views, and proposed new questions (see Figure 1c). They were asked to draw another group mind map t o integrate personal ideas  into collective views, employing keywords and explanations to visualize their understanding.

Phase III: Reflection into collective inquiry and emerging new questions.  Students were provided with all their previous work to reflect upon their learning trajectories in line with the focus on the “Embedded, Concurrent, and Transformative Assessment” principle (Scardamalia, 2002). They made posters to present their learning trajectories, emphasizing collective conceptual changes. Group presentations highlighted evolving views and understandings. Transitioning to Phase III, akin to an academic conferenc e, students proposed promising questions from Phase II, delving deeper into scientific concepts on Knowledge Forum. This process mirrors scientific inquiry processes and progressive inquiry, promoting a rise in understanding. The collaborative exploration and reflection support continuous improvement and advancement in learning.

Data sources and analysis Data included open-ended questions for scientific understanding at pre-and post-tests. A questionnaire with 28 items on epistemic beliefs with four dimensions (source, certainty, justification, and development) was administered (Conley et al., 2004). Multiple data sources, including Knowledge Forum  notes, classroom observations, and learning artifacts, were included and analyzed to identify significant theme s and incidents that illuminated how students engag ed in authentic scientific inquiry and knowledge-building trajectories. RQ1. Did the knowledge-building approach focusing on authentic scientific inquiry enhance students’ scientific and epistemic understanding? Domain understanding analyses showed statistically significant improvements from pre-test (M = 21.55, SD = 11.66) to post-test (M = 44.28, SD = 12.7, t(28) = 8.56, p < 0.05). Students’ epistemic beliefs are important for scientific inquiry in knowledge building. Analyses showed that students had a significant improvement in overall epistemic belief after knowledge-building inquiry (pre-test: M = 3.42, SD = 0.4; post-test: M = 3.55, SD = 0.28, t(28) = 2.196, p < 0.05). Notably, students showed a significant better understanding of the development (pre-test: M = 3.75, SD = 0.56; post-test: M = 4.29, SD = 0.45, t(28) = 5.269, p < 0.05) and justification aspects (pre-test: M = 3.84, SD = 0.45; post-test: M = 4.30, SD = 0.37, t(28) = 6.138, p < 0.05). In Phase I, students formulated promising questions and explored Cold and Hot through five Knowledge Forum Views (See Table 1), mirroring scientists’ diverse research specialties. Thought initially lacking systematic connections, students used scaffolds to build on each other’s ideas, initiating online discussions based on dai ly observations and experiences. They developed questions, revised explanations, and conducted expe riments akin to scientists. Figure 2a shows a Knowledge Forum note indicating a student’s thinking and inquiry working like scientists generating questions in a new field. While their articulation was not yet scientific, th ey engaged in high-level thinking and inquiry, exploring how materials affect heat transmission. By linking dail y life experiences and collaborating on Knowledge F orum, students demonstrated a progression towards active problem-solving and deeper scientific inquiry, laying the foundation for deeper scientific inquiry and knowledge building. Building on ideas, connections and synthesis for deepening inquiry. In Phase II, students enhanced their collective inquiry by integrating diverse questions, synthesizing connections, and proposing new questions  on Knowledge Forum. T hey collaborated like scientists  in conferences, sharing,  discussing scattered ideas, and pushing the community knowledge frontier. They demonstrated high-level question formulation, deepened understanding in inquiry by organizing complex ideas, citing others and attempting to locate exclusive answers. Commonly, students at such a young age would be con fused when having multiple answers to a single ques tion, but their engagement showed their epistemic understating coevolves with the knowledge building  process supported by the scientific community.

Collective reflection for new questions and new res earch. In Phase III, students generated 15 questions across five subtopics, demonstrating ongoing curios ity about the topic and its real-world applications. As shown in Figure 2b, the student synthesized peers’ ideas to propose new questions, showing their development as little scientists who draw on past knowledge to explore new direction s independently. By citing peers ’ ideas without prompting, students exhibited epistemic agency, cru cial for advancing collective knowledge-building processes. This active engagement reflects students’ ability to autonomously contribute to idea improvements and collective progress in their research within the knowledge-building framework. RQ3. What characterized the changes in students’ scientific understanding supported by knowledge-building inquiry and reflection?

In addition to quantitative data (RQ1), multiple data, including Knowledge Forum writings, classroom artifacts of mind maps, and drawings, were examined qualitatively to illustrate students’ changing scientific understanding throughout the knowledge building process. All groups demonstrated different extents of changes, and we selected illustrated examples to show trajectories and possible directions. From pre-scientific to partial understanding to community-based scientific knowledge Students’ understanding of Cold and Hot  progressed from pre-scientific to partial-scientific, culminating in community knowledge development. Initially, their mind maps in Phase I reflected scattered ideas like heating, cooling, and temperature or color changes. Their prior knowledge was closely related to their everyday experiences  with little to no scientific explanations  (see Figure 3a). Whilst Phase II, students demonstrated their improved understanding by  i ntegrating key concepts like heat generation with simple explanations (see Figure 3b). While not fully comprehensive, this showed a shift towards scientific understanding. By the end of  Phase II, students created advanced mind maps  with logical organizations and detailed keywords, demonstrating their collective conceptual change  and high-level analytical skills. The collective teamwork showed their deepening understanding. These mind maps served as reflective tools, highlighting the evolution of students' thinking processes and knowledge construction throughout the study (see Figure 3c). Collective reflection on learning trajectories recognizing conceptual and epistemic changes At the end of the knowledge-building sessions, students engaged in reflective activities to trace their learning traje ctories. They reviewed their worksheets and mind maps, discussed their learning journey in groups. Figure 3d exemplifies a group’s reflection on their evolving understanding, show ing how their personal experiences sparked motivation in learning Cold and Hot, leading to knowledge construction based on research  in books and the internet. They recognized the shifts in their domain understanding, transitioning from viewing heat as invisible matter to understanding it as a form of motion through group investigation. They regarded  their roles  in learning  as knowledge creators (knowing what they did and why they needed to do it)  instead of information receivers  in a collaborative setting, emphasizing the role of explicit reflection  in fostering conceptual and epistemic change in the knowledge building  setting, enhancing students’ understanding of authentic scientific inquiry and their roles as scientists.

## Discussion

The study investigated and characterized students’ engagement in authentic scientific inquiry within a Knowledge Forum-mediated knowledge-building environment. It expanded on existing research by systematically exploring the design and processe s emulating scientific inquiry, such as problem generation, evi dence-based

## inquiry, theory revision, and collective advances. Results indicated

that knowledge-building pedagogy positively impacted students ’ scientific conceptual and epistemic understanding, aligning w ith scientists’ inquiry practices. The study traced students’ knowledgebuilding trajectory through design phases, showcasing the evolving scientific understanding and the importance of students’ reflection during the learning progress. It emphasized the significance of examining knowledge-building pedagogy, scientific inquiry processes, and conceptual change in collective scientific inquiry, suggesting further r esearch directions for understanding students ’ development and reflections in conceptual change and the impact of knowledge-building on scientific understanding.
