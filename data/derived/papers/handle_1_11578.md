# Coaching for High Quality Mathematics Instruction: Learning to Set Mathematical Goals to Improve Pedagogical Practices and Student Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research explores a school-based middle school mathematics teacher coaching program in which coaches learn to lead coaching cycles specific to mathematics pedagogy with a focus on improving priority students’ conceptual understanding (CU) and teachers’ ability to conduct formative assessment (FA). Results of the study share how coaches: partnered with teachers to set goals for instructional improvement and improved upon those goals through programmatic support.

## Introduction

This study reports on an instructional coaching prog ram focused on enhancing the quality of mathematics instruction for priority students, students testing at least one year below grade level in mathematics. Instructional Coaching for Tech Enhanced Approaches in Mathematic s program (iCoachTEAM) is a school-based one-to-one mathematics teacher coaching program which develops  coaches’ practice to improve sixth through eighth grade teachers’ promotion of conceptual understanding (CU) and ability to conduct formative assessment (FA). iCoachTEAM draws upon Simon’s (2017) definition of CU as knowledge of the mathematical necessity of a particular mathematical relationship, for exam ple, understanding the relationship between the siz e of the denominator of a fraction and the size of a unit-fraction. iCoachTEAM defines FA as the ability to elicit and respond to students’ mathematical thinking (Vande Walle et al., 2014; NCTM, 2014). For students to learn conceptual mathematics, it requires teachers conduc t FA by facilitating instruction in such a way that  students share their reasoning and teachers guide that reasoning towards more advanced mathematics understanding. iCoachTEAM Coaching program Coaches in  iCoachTEAM learn to lead coaching cycles specific to mathemati cs pedagogy with a focus on improving priority students’ CU and teachers’ abili ty to conduct FA. iCoachTEAM is a two-year coaching program with virtual monthly workshops, virtual monthly one-on-one coach-mentoring meetings, and virtual and in-person professional development institutes. Participating coaches learn to implement coaching cycles in which the coach and teacher collaborate to establish a clear picture of reality in the teacher ’s classroom; co-create an instructional improvement goal; select a high-quality instructional strategy to meet the goal; le arn the strategy; and iterate and reflect on the teacher’s progress t owards their goal (Knight, 2018). In this paper, we  focus specifically on the goal-setting stage of the coaching cycles. The goals tea chers and coaches set become drivers for instructional change and provide direction for the remainder of the cycle as teachers select, lear n, and implement new pedagogical strategies in service of their goals.

## Methods

To investigate the outcomes of the coaches’ and teachers’ goal-setting process, we used continuous improvement research methods with iterative cycles of data collection, data analysis, and program modifications. Our research question asked: To what extent do instructional coaches and mathematics teachers set goals which center priority students, address mathematics content, and focus on improving teachers practice with CU and/or FA? We share results from two of the 12 coaches who fully participated in two years of the iCoachTEAM program. Anne and Samantha (pseudonyms, as are all names  in this work) were the coaches who implemented the program with the highest degree of fidelity, and we wanted to understand the optimal level of coac hing and growth iCoachTEAM could offer. The coaches worked in different districts in the United States. We present findings from multiple data sources, includ ing coach interviews, coaching logs, and teacher observations. At the end of each school year, coaches participated in semi-structured, individual, audio-recorded, virtual interviews to share their experiences with iCoachTEAM and the perceived usefulness of its materials. Our team engaged in thematic analysis of the collected data (Terry et al., 2017), using a structured debriefing form to ensure a comprehensive and syste matic evaluation of the data and to identify themes  for the extent to which the coaches and teachers set goals which met the coaching program criteria of: a) centering priority students’ learning, b) meaningfully address mathematics content, and c) focusing on improving teachers’ practice for developing CU and/or conducting FA. We triangul ated the coach interviews and logs and, where possi ble, classroom observations of coached te achers and teacher interviews to further confirm or  disconfirm emergent themes. Due to our continuous improvement research,  we made programmatic adjustments to the coaching program after its first year, which will be discussed in the results.

## Results

Goal setting between the coaches and teachers Through our continuous improvement research, we lea rned after the first year of the program that our c oaches needed more support meeting the goal setting criteria, so our results share: the coaches’ and teachers’ goals during their first year, the programmatic changes we made as a result of our continuous improvement research,  and the coaches’ and teachers’ goals during their second year.

In their first year of the iCoachTEAM program, the coaches and their teachers set a range  of goals they perceived to attend to mathematics content, CU, and /or FA. However, from our analysis none of the goal s set focused on priority students and the attention to CU and/or FA needed to be strengthened. For example, Samantha collaborated with her teacher Colleen to set the goal “all students will participate in mathematical discourse during the Module 1 lessons on Rates, Ratios, and Proportions,” to be accomplished using “think, pair, share.” Samantha and Colleen’s goal was grounded in the mathematics content of Rates, Ratios, and Proportions and inten ded to promote students’ CU by prompting students to expla in their thinking with a partner. Although this goa l has a clear focus on mathematics content, and students sharing their thinking with a partner has the potential to promote CU, we saw a need to strengthen this goal to explic itly communicate how the teacher will promote her s tudents’ active mathematical reasoning and discourse in practice. On the other hand, Anne, set goals with her teachers which were disconnected from mathematics content, CU, and FA by focusing on student behavior and part icipation. For example, Anne and her teacher Alie s et the goal, “in order to increase engagement, 100% of students will verbally participate at least once in a class period,” to be accomplished by the strategy “pulling sticks for asking questions.” While Samantha’s goal was connected to students’ CU, Anne’s goal was disconnected from mathematics content  and lacked attention to students’ mathematical thinking. Across all of the coaches' g oals, we saw a need to strengthen them to explicitl y focus on priority students’ learning of mathematical content  and the mathematics instructional strategies teach ers would use to support their students’ CU or conduct FA.

Our first year of continuous improvement analysis l ed us to consider changes to support coaches to strengthen the goals set for CU and FA and align goals towards CU and FA that were not (e.g. Anne’s classroom management goal). Thus, after our first year implem enting iCoachTEAM, we made intentional changes to the program for the second year to better support each coach and teacher to set goals which met our criteria. These changes included 1) having the teachers and coaches  work together to identify 2-5 students testing one or more years behind grade level as their priority group for the coaching cycle; 2) having the teachers and coaches specify learning goals for this group of priority students that address specific mathematics content; and 3) creating a watch-for checklist with indicators about CU and FA, to support coaches and teachers in attending to the teachers’ current classroom practice and identifying next-steps for improvement. We supported the coaches and teachers to organize each of these components into a single coh esive goal by creating a goal template which states: Priority students will be more proficient on {INSERT STANDAR D/UNIT OF FOCUS} on  {INSERT ASSESSMENT MEASURE} by {INSERT STUDENT DO/TEACHER DO FROM WATCH-FOR CHECKLIST}. After implementing our programmatic changes, all the  coaches and their teachers set goals meeting the iCoachTEAM criteria during their second year. For example, Anne and Alie set the goal “Priority  Students will increase their proficiency using proportional relationships to solve multistep ratio and percent problems on weekly exit tickets by engaging in productive struggle and  persevering when solving challenging or open-ended tasks.” The strategy they chose to use to meet this goal was cognitively demanding tasks. Each goal set in year two of the program explicitly attended to the priority students, the mathematics content t hey are expected to learn, and mathematics instructional strategies teachers would use to support priority students’ conceptual understanding.

## Implications

Shifting from procedural to conceptual mathematics instruction is a central problem of practice within policy and research agendas in the United States broadly and with priority students specifically. One-to-one content-focused coaching, and specifically setting goals for instru ctional improvement, can serve as  a catalyst for driving instructional change. This study contributes empiri cal evidence of how coaches partnered with teachers  to set goals for instructional improvement and improved on those goals with programmatic supports.
