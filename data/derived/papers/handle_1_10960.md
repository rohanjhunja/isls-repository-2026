# PM2.5 Exposure and Student’s Classroom Learning: A Mediation Analysis

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates the influence of short-term exposure to PM2.5 on students' classroom learning. Linear mixed effects models reveal that increased exposure to PM2.5 before class reduces mental effort (standardized coefficient: -12.80%; 95% CI: -2.30%, -23.03%; p= 0.017) of students in class. Mediation analysis shows that cognitive regulation fully mediates PM2.5's impact on students' reduced mental effort. These findings suggest that low air quality may impede classroom learning, requiring greater attention.

## Introduction

PM2.5 is known to have a detrimental effect on cogn itive function and potentially more significant impairments in students, given their higher vulnerability to ai r pollution (Ke et al., 2022; Shin et al., 2019). Although studies have highlighted that PM2.5 exposure hinders students’ cognitive development (Forns et al., 2017), its effects on classroom learning and the underlying mechanism remain to be elucidated. In real-world educational settings, students' experiences differ from artificial laboratory conditions. Classroom learning requires sustained attention, cognitive regulation, and mental effort. Despite the known effects  of PM2.5  on basic cognitive abilities, the influence on advanced cogniti on like cognitive regulation and mental effort remains un known, necessitating further research in authentic educational scenarios.

## Participants

The study involved 22 Grade 10 students (13 males, 9 females) aged 15-16 who participated with consent.  Data was collected over 17 math classes in February-March 2023, with no more than one class per day. Exposure assessment Air quality data  were obtained from the Beijing Municipal Ecological and Environmental Moni toring Center, using data from the  station closest to the sampled school. Meteorological data were retrieved from  the National Centers for Environmental Information.

Student attention, cognitive regulation and mental effort assessment Data on students' self-reported attention, cognitive regulation, and menta l effort were collected using the Experience Sampling Method (ESM) in forty-minute class sessions, and rated on a 1-5 Likert scale (Greene, 2015; Reeve & Tseng, 2011; Paas, 1992). Class difficulty was also rated (Ayres, 2006).

## Data Analysis

A linear mixed effects model was used to analyze th e outcomes, with different lagged values of PM2.5 u p to 7 days. The model included sex as a demographic variable and class difficulty as a covariate due to its known impact on mental effort and cognitive regulation (Paas, 1992). Adjustments were made for confounders like average daily temperature, date, and day of the week (dow).

A mediation analysis was then conducted using cogni tive regulation as the mediator to investigate the mechanism of PM2.5's impact on mental effort, apply ing the quasi-Bayesian Monte Carlo method with 10,000 simulations for Average Causal Mediation Effects (A CME), Average Direct Effects (ADE), and their ratio (Tingley et al., 2014). The statistical analyses were conducted using R, version 4.3.1.

## Results

The PM2.5 concentration throughout the study period  was 63.26 ± 49.87 μg/m3 (n = 40). Self-reported attention and cognitive regulation were 3.65±0.81  (n = 353) and 3.60  ± 0.87 (n = 353), respectively, while self-reported mental effort was 3.81  ± 0.76 (n = 353). Linear mixed effects models demonstrate that increased exposure to PM2.5 before class reduces mental effort (standardized coefficient: -12.80%; 95% CI: -2.30%, -23.03%; p= 0.017) of students in class.  The results remained strong even after various adju stments were made. However, no significant correlation was observed between attention and PM2.5. Mediation analysis shows cognitive regulation fully mediates PM2.5's effect on mental ef fort (as shown in Figure 1). ## Discussion

Our study revealed no significant association between P M2.5 and classroom attention, contrasting with earlier findings of attention declines in children exposed to traffic pollution  (Sunyer et al., 2015), possibly due to participant age and methodology differences.  This pioneering study explores the impact of air po llution on adolescent cognition in classroom settings, used lo ngitudinal data for greater validity and mediation analysis to understand PM2.5 effects on mental effort. However, this study was limited by a small sample size and se lfreporting, which are areas of improvement for future research. Exposure to high PM2.5 levels can reduce students' cognitive regulation and mental effort. Conversely, using air filtration in polluted classrooms could boost learning (Stafford, 2015), suggesting that better air quality may enhance educational outcomes.
