# Student Perspectives to Virtual AI Characters in Teacher Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster explores the use of AI avatars embedded in a virtual environment in the context of teacher education. We present education students’ reflections on their learning experience after a collaborative activity discussing with three AI characters representing different perspectives to climate change. Based on our findings, we present some redesign directions for integrating the activity into a multidisciplinary teacher education course.

Based on our findings, we present some redesign directions for integrating the activity into a multidisciplinary teacher education course. Generative artificial intelligence (AI) and virtual reality (VR) have attracted great interest in educational research and practice, but research on collaborative aspects is still limited (Holmes & Tuomi, 2022; Paulsen et al., 2024). This pilot study, theoretically rooted in experiential and collaborative learning, explores the use of virtual AI avatars in the context of learning about climate change – a topic that might evoke strong emotions and opinions. We designed a collaborative activity involving interaction with AI characters on a VR platform and explored how education students reflected on their experience interacting with the characters (RQ1) and how they perceived the learning potential of the activity (RQ2).

Climate education calls for teaching methods that enable engagement and make information personally meaningful (Monroe et al., 2019). There are indications of VR experiences having positive effects on knowledge and interest in climate topics (Petersen et al., 2020). With regards to fostering empathy, VR has been successful in arousing compassion (emotional empathy) but less effective in enhancing understanding of other people’s perspectives (cognitive empathy) (Martingano et al., 2021). Cognitive empathy would require active engagement that goes beyond viewing emotionally arousing VR content (Martingano et al., 2021). One approach to supporting engagement is collaborative learning where social and cognitive contributions and socio-emotional processes are intertwined during learners’ joint activity towards a common goal (Hesse et al., 2015; Näykki et al., 2021; Scoular & Care, 2020). Virtual reality can create an engaging space for practice and reflection (Paulsen et al., 2024) and promote social presence (Nousiainen et al, 2023; Näykki et al., 2024). Interacting as or with avatars can support building shared fictional realities (see Nousiainen et al., 2023), and this may also involve interaction with nonhuman actors, such as AI characters. While there is evidence that AI chatbots can have a positive impact on learning outcomes (Wu & Yu, 2024), research on AI-powered avatars in 3D virtual environments is just beginning to emerge (e.g., Liu & Siau, 2023), and the use of AI characters in collaborative activities warrants more research.

## Study design and implementation

This poster presents student reflections from a pilot study where education students had conversations with AIpowered interactive avatars using the WondaVR platform. The study was conducted in October 2024, and the goal was to obtain feedback on the learning experience before embedding the activity into a multidisciplinary Environmental Storytelling course for student teachers. The design was inspired by a virtual drama activity previously implemented on the since discontinued AltspaceVR platform (see Nousiainen et al., 2023; Näykki et al., 2024) but adapted to the affordances of WondaVR where it is possible to create AI characters for conversation simulations based on OpenAI’s GPT-4o mini language model. Students (N = 13) worked on laptops in pairs or groups of three and talked to three different AI characters about the causes, effects and actions related to climate change. “Jane”, a mother of a young baby, had been forced to evacuate with her family from their home in Florida due to Hurricane Milton. “Diana” was an experienced climate scientist who focused on Arctic ecosystems and the global impacts of climate change. “Michael” was an activist who had become dedicated to raising environmental awareness and advocating for climate justice when confronted with the reality of climate change as an existential threat. We wrote character prompts based on authentic material (news stories, scientific publications). For example, Jane’s backstory drew upon a first-person account of a hurricane evacuee published in the media. After the activity, we collected written reflections from the participants, asking them to describe their interactions with the AI characters (RQ1) and evaluate different aspects of the learning potential of the activity (RQ2). The data were pseudonymized by giving each student a code such as G3A (Group 3, Student A) and analyzed using qualitative content analysis (Elo & Kyngäs, 2008). In the students’ reflections, we identified 25 mentions related to interacting with the AI characters (RQ1). In terms of prompt design, students felt that different perspectives and backstories made the AI characters feel relatable and believable, while generic answers and lack of detail negatively affected the sense of authenticity: “The woman from Hurricane Milton had a clear backstory, but the activist wouldn’t answer very specific questions about his backstory and neither did the scientist” (G3B). Personal relevance and familiarity also played a role: some students could relate to Jane because her story was tied to a very recent real-life event, while Michael was relatable because of his activism: “I have the same vibe with him and... focus on taking action on climate change immediately” (G2A). In terms of visual appearance and voice, the characters’ “design and animation... contributed to [a] sense of realism” (G1C) but on the other hand, “the characters look[ed] like ordinary people [and did not visually reflect their] expertise or experience” (G4B). Students’ perceptions of the learning potential of the activity (RQ2) were related to three main categories: supporting collaboration (17 mentions), empathy (16 mentions) and learning content knowledge (46 mentions).

• Students expressed positive views on the potential of this type of activity to support collaboration in a

variety of ways: “You [come] up with an idea and the chatbot can support it or give you more information [about] the topic” (G3A), or it could help “students engage in learning as those tools scaffold their communication” (G2B). Several participants emphasized the importance of a well-structured learning design, such as setting clear goals, using different characters to triangulate “information on different contexts of a specific topic” (G4A), and making it possible to “manipulate the environment or cooperate with other avatars [to create] interesting and diverse tasks” (G6B).

• In order to foster empathy, the characters would require depth (“I don’t really relate to any character

since I know they are AI, but having [a] more real backstory based on real life could make it easier to develop empathy for them”; G3B) and need to display emotion (“characters should show empathy and emotion themselves”; G1A). Thus, character design and prompting are key: “if the prompts [are] designed accurately, it could be used to discuss sensitive topics as well” (G6B).

• Overall, the content delivered by the characters was perceived as accurate but somewhat generic and

lacking evidence: “the accuracy made the interaction not only informative but also trustworthy” (G1C), while on the other hand, “we tried to ask for sources and statistics to support their ideas... but unfortunately, they didn’t provide any” (G2B). Some students noted that the way they formulated their questions made a difference: “At the beginning [the characters’ responses] were not very precise but when we [asked] them to be more accurate, they answered well” (G1B). When reflecting on classroom use, students could see the activity as “a good starting discussion for climate change topics” (G5B) or as a way to reflect on previous knowledge, making “complex ideas easier to remember and reinforc[ing] classroom content” (G1C).

The findings provided some guidelines for integrating the activity into the Environmental Storytelling course. From the perspective of collaborative learning, we will keep the AI characters but complement the activity by having students interact in fictional roles with each other as well (see Paulsen et al., 2024). To this end, connecting the activity with other experiential and arts-based methods, such as creative writing and empathized reading, will help us build a more cohesive, shared story across the whole course, supporting the creation of a shared fictional reality (see Nousiainen et al., 2023). To improve the authenticity of the AI avatars, we will develop their prompts to allow for more casual conversation while also exploring ways to make their responses less generic. We believe it will be useful to give all avatars more multidimensional backstories and to formulate more specific objectives (see e.g., Paulsen et al., 2024) to guide students’ conversations with the avatars. Furthermore, as reflection is an essential part of experiential learning (Kolb, 1984), we aim to embed reflection more seamlessly as part of the activity itself both to support cognitive empathy (Martingano et al., 2021) and to provide more insight into the collaborative process during the activity (Paulsen et al., 2024). In this pilot study, we also collected video recordings (screen captures and students’ discussions) and chat logs. We will continue to examine the collaborative process in more depth by analyzing the video and log data alongside the written reflections to better understand how students interacted with the AI characters and with each other (see Paulsen et al., 2024), and how their actual interactions relate to the views expressed in the reflection data. Finally, when the activity is embedded into the Environmental Storytelling course, it will allow for a more holistic view into how a stronger emphasis on cognitive empathy and story-based problem solving will be reflected in students’ experiences and their thinking related to complex phenomena such as climate change.
