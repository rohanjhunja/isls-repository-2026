# Which Collaborative Behavior can Enhance Inter-Brain Synchronization and Group Performance During STEM Collaboration? A fNIRS Hypersanning Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaboration plays a crucial role in STEM education. This study aimed to investigate the neural mechanisms underlying the benefits of collaborative behaviors using fNIRS hyperscanning. The prefrontal cortex (PFC) and right temporoparietal junction (r-TPJ) were targeted as regions of interest. Three key findings emerged from the analysis. First, IBS in the r-TPJ increased during the initial phases of collaboration, while IBS in the PFC increased during the later phases. Second, the frequency of scaffolding and questioning behaviors exhibited a significant positive correlation with the enhancement of IBS in the r-TPJ. Furthermore, the frequency of scaffolding behavior during the initial phases significantly correlated with task outcomes' fluency, integration, and technology scores. Overall, this study sheds light on the impact of different collaborative behaviors on IBS in different brain regions and task performance and proposes a new perspective on learning science using the brain-scanning method of fNIRS hyperscanning.

## Introduction

STEM education places great emphasis on the use of interdisciplinary knowledge and thinking in authent ic situations to develop students’ critical thinking, creativity, problem-solving, and other higher-order thinking skills (Honey, 2014; Li, 2021). As a crucial aspect of STEM education, collaboration involves the exchange of information and the generation of new knowledge. It emphasizes active and social engagement, allowing students to take on various roles such as speakers, listener s, readers, designers, and thinkers (Hurst et al., 2013). With the advancements in neuroimaging techniques, the applic ation of functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS) hyperscanning technology has allowed for the simultaneous recording of brain activity from multiple individuals engaged in social interaction or joint activities. Recent studies in educational neuroscience have demonstrated that inter-brain synchronization (IBS) can serve as a neurophysiological marker of success ful collaboration between teacher-student, children-parent, and student-student pairs, reflecting the quality of these interactions (Nguyen et al., 2020; Pan et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2018).

While previous research has provided initial eviden ce on the role of neural synchrony and influencing factors in collaboration, such as group diversity  (Lu et al., 2021), consensus reaching  (Pan et al., 2021), and the gender of partners (Zhang et al., 2017), there is currently a lack of neuroscientific evidence specifically highlighting the significance of collaboration and identifying neural marks for cognitive engagement on specific collaborative behaviors during STEM problem-solving. Given the advantages of fNIRS-based hyperscanning techniques, the present study aimed to provide a neurophysiological  testbed for the Interactive Brain Hypothesis and i nvestigate the contribution of specific collaboration derived from the cognitive engagement framework during the STEM engineer design process. The research questions were as follows:

1) Does collaboration lead to enhanced IBS and be more effective than individual work in the STEM design

process?

2) Furthermore, if collaboration is effective, which specific behavior in the collaboration process promotes

IBS and group performance?

Specifically, we focused on the PFC and r-TPJ cortex as the region of interest (ROI) to explore the interpersonal neural models between individuals involved in STEM problem-solving. We hypothesized that compared to the independent mode, collaboration between dyads would result in better STEM design outcomes and increased IBS in the ROI. We did not make a specific hypothesis f or the second research question but aimed to explor e the effectiveness of different behaviors through a joint analysis of IBS, task performance, and behavior coding.

## Participants

We recruited 104 adults from several universities (15 males and 89 females, mean age = 21.86 ± 2.99 years). All subjects were strangers and participated in 42 pair s. The members of each dyad did not know each other be fore the experiment. All the participants were right-handed, had no brain disease or mental illness, and  had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Informed written consent was collected from all par ticipants. The study procedure was approved by the University Committee on Human Research Protection of the Beijing Normal University.

## Experimental procedure and tasks

Each pair would finish two engineering design tasks in two experimental co nditions collaborative condition and independent condition. The duration of both two con ditions was 15 minutes, with a total duration of th e experiment was about 1 hour. Specifically, to maximize the use of the 15-minute task time, each 15-minute condition will be divided into three phases including ideation (5 minutes), drawing drafts (5 minutes), and writing comments (5 minutes). The order of two STEM engineering design tasks and two conditions was counterbalanced. The similarity of the task difficulty and experimental materials used in the two tasks better reduced the diﬀerences in IBS due to excessive diﬀerences between experimental tasks. Data acquisition and analysis STEM task performance Participants' performance during the ideation phase (Figure 2. Region 1) was measured using the (1) ﬂuency and

(2) originality of their ideas (Guilford, 1967; Runco & Okuda, 1991). The ﬂuency of participants' ideas was based

on the total number of ideas that each participant generated. The originality of the participant's ideas was assessed using a subjective method (Xue et al., 2018). Besides, the rubrics of the overall rating (Figure 2. Region 2)  were modified from the study by Chonkaew et al. (2016).  They consisted of integration, innovation, technic al, organization, and aesthetics. Two trained raters in dependently rated the 5 dimensions of each STEM work generated by the participants on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 to 5, with 1 being the lowest and 5 the highest. Video coding of collaborative behavior To better understand which behavior best contribute d to IBS during STEM collaboration, two undergraduate students who were blind to the experiment were recruited to independently and manually transcribe and code each group's discussion using the video-recording data. The coding framework for the current study (see Table. 1) was derived from the cognitive engagement framework in Zhu (2006) research, which consists of four dimensions:

(1) question (i.e., group members raise a question to ot hers); (2) statement (i.e., response to a previous message

or provide information relating to the task); (3) reflection (i.e., reflects on changes in personal opinions and behavior).(4) scaffold(i.e., offering suggestions for designing or manage the time) (Bloom et al., 1956). fNIRS Data acquisition Brain activity during the task was measured using t he Brite24 wireless fNIRS system (Artinis Medical S ystems, Elst), which was used to record the oxyhemoglobin (HbO) and deox yhemoglobin (HbR) concentrations for each dyad. The absorption of near-infrared light (wavelengths: 752 and 830 nm) was measured at a sampling rate of 10 Hz. Two optode probe sets (22 channels) were placed over each participant’s PFC and r-TPJ regions, based on previous studies suggesting the involvement of the PFC and r-TPJ regions in group creativity and social interaction (Cui et al., 2012; Lu et al., 2021; Mayseless et al., 2019; Pan et al., 2020). (see Fig. 1).

## fNIRS data analysis

Due to the machine malfunction, the data obtained from two pairs of participants were excluded, and the remaining 39 dyads were used in the data analysis. MATLAB (Mathworks, Natick, MA, version 2022a) was used to process the data, and statistical analyses were conducted b y Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS). The data collected from these ROIs during the two conditions  were used for the analysis of IBS. Before calculating IBS, we took the data of the 1 minute before each task as the baseline and resampled the sampling rate to 1Hz. Next, wavelet transform coherence (WTC) was used to assess IBS from the corresponding ROIs of the two participants in each group (Grinsted et al., 2004).

## Results

IBS in collaborative conditions Through FOI selection and WTC analysis, we observed  a significant increase in IBS of channel 13 during collaborative conditions in the frequency band betw een 0.0115 and 0.0145 Hz (68.96-86.96 s). However, T-test results within this frequency band did not reveal significant differences between the resting and task states. To further look into the IBS during the collaborati on condition, we identified FOI in each of the thre e phases for WTC analysis. In the ideation and drawing draft pha ses, we found significant differences in IBS betwee n the resting and task states in channel 13 within the 0. 0115-0.0145 Hz frequency band. In the ideation and drawi ng draft phases, we observed significant differences i n IBS between the resting and task states in channe l 13 within the 0.0115-0.0145 Hz frequency band. However, no significant d ifferences in IBS were found during the final phase. This suggests that the lack of observed IBS during the collaborative mode in the overall analys is may be attributed to the absence of IBS in the third phase, which consequently diluted the overall IBS findings. However, in the writing comment phase, we observed significa nt differences in IBS in channel 9 within the 0.012 9-0.0258 Hz frequency band.

The relationship between IBS and STEM task performance In the collaborative mode, we conducted a Pearson correlation analysis in each phase to examine the relationship between IBS at channel 13 and each dimension of the  performance assessment scheme. The results reveale d a significant correlation between IBS during the drawing draft phase and the integration and technology dimensions of the group's final work (integration: r = 0.373, p = 0.019; technology: r = 0.348, p = 0.003). Similarly, when we combined the ideation and drawing draft phases, the  IBS also sho wed significant correlations with these two evaluation indicators (integration: r = 0.380, p = 0.017; technology: r = 0.326, p = 0.043). However, in the final phase of writing comments, the IBS located in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) showed a negative correlation with the innovation score of the group's work (r = -0.377, p = 0.018). The relationship between collaborative behavior and IBS In each phase, we conducted Pearson correlation ana lyses to examine the relationship between IBS and t he frequency of four interactive behavioral indicators. Firstly, we found a significant correlation between IBS during the drawing phase and scaffolding behavior during the ideation phase (r = 0.362, p = 0.035). However, IBS in the other two phases did not show any significant correlation with any interactive behaviors. Secondly, we conducted correlation analyses between IBS during the first 2 phases and the behavioral indicators during this period as well as other phases. We discovered a significant correlation between IBS and scaffold and question behaviors during the ideation phase (question: r = 0.369, p = 0.032; scaffold: r = 0.428, p = 0.012), while no significant correlations were found with other behaviors.  These results suggest that IBS exhibits a certain l ag with respect to the occurrence of collaborative behavior, indicating that behavior in the previous phase triggers IBS in the subsequent phase or enhances the average IBS value over a longer duration.

## Discussion

In the present study, we revealed a noteworthy corr elation between the behavior exhibited in the previ ous phase and the subsequent phase's IBS. Th is finding suggests  that collaborative behavior in the previous phase m ay trigger IBS in the subsequent phase, indicating a certain lag between the occurrence of collaborative behavior and IBS. Consistent with previous psychological counsel ing (Zhang et al., 2018) and teach-and-learn processes (Pan et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2018), our study found a relationship between r-TPJ's IBS and questioning behavior Previous research has also identified the involveme nt of the r-TPJ in IBS during interactive tasks, However, the frequency of questioning behavior did not predict t he final design scores. Scaffold behaviors involve offering suggestions or statements to guide partners in disc ussing concepts and designing content materials, as  well as utilizing self-regulating and metacognitive strategies to monitor, evaluate, plan, and control the task process (Xu et al., 2020; Zhu, 2006). This study first found it could promote IBS in the r-TPJ region. The significant correlation between IBS in r-TPJ and the frequency of scaffolding behaviors high lights the role of scaffold behaviors in enhancing cognitive engagement during social interaction. It suggests that by providing g uidance and support, scaffold behaviors facilitate effectiv e communication and information exchange between collaborators, leading to a higher level of shared understanding and coordination, thereby enhancing IBS in the rTPJ region. Besides, previous research has mentione d that the unconstrained brainstorming in the early  stages is crucial for creative group collaboration (Rosing et al., 2018). Correspondingly, the correla tion analysis between behavior and task performance also revealed that scaffolding behaviors during the ideation phase at the beginning of the task positively influenced outcomes including the score of fluency, technology integration, and total score. Future studies could explore the impact of differen t types or levels of question and scaffold behavior s on IBS. Examining variations in these behaviors, such as th e intensity or timing of guidance provided, may pro vide insights into the optimal conditions for promoting IBS during STEM problem-solving.

## Limitation and conclusion

While our results suggest interesting avenues for f uture work, it is important to acknowledge several limitations in the current study. Firstly, gender may influence interpersonal interaction (Cheng et al., 2015; Liang et al., 2022), and the majority of pa rticipants in our study were female-female dyads. In future research, it would be valua ble to explore the collaboration dynamics in groups with different gender compositions, as leadership and cooperation within groups may vary by gender (Lu et al., 2020a;  Liang et al., 2022).  Secondly, the consideration of task difficulty across different experimental tasks is i mportant. The tasks in our study were adapted from interdisciplinary STEM learning materials and were determined through disc ussions with experienced STEM teachers. In future studies, it would be beneficial to carefully consider the age, educational level, and disciplinary background of the participants when designing tasks, and strive to maintain consistent task difficulty between the two conditions.  Thirdly, the duration of the tasks can impact indiv idual task performance and neural activity. Some participants in our experiment may have experi enced increased fatigue over time, leading to decre ased collaborative efficiency in the later stages. Looking ahead, future studies could delve deeper into the neural signatures underlying behavioral dynamics from a developmental perspective. Additionally, exploring additional effects or strategies, such as an individual's interdisciplinary knowledge background, the influence o f external or mutual feedback, and project-based collaboration, would provide further insights  into optimizing IBS during STEM problem-solving.
