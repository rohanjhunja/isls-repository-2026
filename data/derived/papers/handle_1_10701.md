# Addressing Misconceptions in Science through Minecraft

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Misconceptions in science are common among young learners, and addressing misconceptions is an important step in guiding learners towards deeper understanding of concepts in various science domains. One suggested strategy for addressing misconceptions in science is to use scenarios requiring counterfactual reasoning, such as asking “What if the earth had two moons?”. Early to middle-adolescent learners were asked to explore custom designed “What if?” scenarios in Minecraft as part of an informal summer camp experience. Learners completed an assessment of astronomy misconceptions before and after the summer camp. Results showed an overall significant increase in scores on the post-test. Counterfactual scenarios in Minecraft stand as an adequate learning environment to address misconceptions.

## Introduction

Misconceptions in science stem from disconnects bet ween prior knowledge and integration of new concept ual knowledge (Suprapto, 2020). This differs from not knowing the  answer to a question, in that a learner is posing an answer that may sound feasible but is not scientifically accurate. Misconceptions often arises from a feeling of knowing, based on prior knowledge constructed from popular culture or otherwise. Some research has shown that misconceptions among younger learners tend to be lo w, but retroactive interference can lead learners t o develop misconceptions that continue to grow and expand int o other knowledge areas (Thompson & Logue, 2006). Therefore, addressing misconceptions in science, especially common misconceptions, is an important aspect of science education. One possible way of addressing learner misconceptio ns in science is through the use of counterfactual examples that ask learners to think through “what if” scenarios and how a concept might  change (Nyhout & Ganea, 2021). Furthermore, using familiar digital environments, such as video games, can be adapted to address misconceptions and promote conceptual knowledge gai n (Lane et al., 2022). This research aims to understand to what extent counterfactual astronomy examples situated in a popular video game, Minecraft, work to address misconceptions among young adolescents participating in a STEM-focused summer camp. To this end, research questions (RQs) are formulated thus: RQ1. How well does an adapted assessment of astronomy mi sconceptions capture misconceptions among adolescents?

RQ2: What effect, if any, do counterfactual simulations of Earth in Minecraft have on astronomy misconceptions held by middle school students? What factors most associate with  post-test performance?

## Background

Misconceptions in science need to be addressed prio r to learning new material that might build on area s misconceived, and by addressing misconceptions students can progress towards expertise in a domain (Engelmann & Huntoon, 2011). Remediation efforts include making students aware o f their misconceptions, informing students that misconceptions are part of the practi ce of science learning, teaching critical thinking,  and using “What If?” questions to explore correct and incorrect notions. Instruments have been developed and validated to assess the degree to which undergraduates hold misc onceptions in introductory astronomy (Favia et al., 2014). However, analysis of the effectiveness of different techniqu es for addressing misconceptions is relatively unexplored.

One strategy for addressing misconceptions is to utilize “What I f?” scenarios, also referred to  as opportunities for counterfactual reasoning. Counterfactual reasoning can be an effective mean s of learning by providing learners the opportunity to reflect on wh at could be or could have been, akin to cognitive processes associated with regret and hope  (Redshaw & Ganea, 2022). Furthermore, counterfactua l scenarios can provide learners chances to construct causal models of scientific phenomena, such as why the tilt of earth at 23.4º matters for habitability of our planet, and how a change in  the degree of the tilt could impact our ability to survive. Research using counterfactual scenarios with 6 and 7 year olds in two studies divided students into experimental groups reviewing planetary habitability using “What if” qu estions, groups reviewing factual information using examples of other planets (e.g. Venus), and control groups that did not receive instructional materials (Nyhout & Ganea, 2022). Students in a counterfactual group significantly outperformed bot h the control and factual groups on open-ended transfer questions. Open-ended video games provide opportunities to instantiate more instances of counterfactual simulations.

Minecraft has been identified as a game with a lot of potenti al for teaching and research (Lane & Yi, 2017), as well as a means to boost interest in motivatio n across numerous disciplines, including math, scie nce, social studies, and language arts (Baek et al., 2020). However, empirical studies examining learning, espe cially with a focus on addressing misconceptions, when Minecraft is the primary mode of content delivery are still rare. This paper addresses this gap by providing evidence  of misconception reduction in astronomy after learners navigate counterfactual scenarios in Minecraft.

## Participants

Participants are elementary and middle school stude nts (ages 9 – 13) participating across 6 week-long, STEMfocused summer camps using Minecraft as the primary mode of engagement with the STEM content. There were 4 summer camps run in the western United States (n = 49), 1 run in the midwestern United States (n = 17), and 1 run in the eastern United States (n = 6). All camps used the same curriculum, assessments, and featured the same instructors. All participants were provided with laptop, mouse, and access to Minecraft:Java Edition. Consent to participate in the research project was obtained from at least one parent or guardian of ea ch participant. Verbal assent from participants was so licited on the first day of each respective camp. One student declined to have their data included in any analysis and is therefore not included. The total participants are n = 71 (25% Female; 55% White, 25% Black, 17% Prefer not to answer, and 3% Asian).

## Materials and measures

Participants primarily played Minecraft: Java Edition  throughout the 1-week summer camp.  A series of 7 counterfactual simulations were designed in consult ation with an astrophysicist, and participants had the opportunity to explore these simulations in a custo mized version of Minecraft. Examples of counterfactual scenarios include, “What if Earth had no moon?” and “What if Earth orbited a colder sun”. To understand the misconceptions learners have about concepts in astronomy, we adapted an instrument created to measure misconceptions called the Astron omical Misconceptions Survey (AMS; LoPresto & Murrell, 2011). The survey included 25 true/false questions, using commonly seen misconceptions in astronomy. Examples include, “Stars last forever”; “Saturn’s rings are solid objects”, and “Because it is in space, there is no gravity on the Moon”. The targeted demographic for the AMS is early-career college students, therefore we adapted several of the questions to make them clearer for middle sc hool students to comprehend and to closer align them to the specific misconceptions we addressed. Demographic information, including age and race/ethnicity were collected from consent forms, as well as self-ratings of Minecraft expertise and weekly Minecraft play time. Science tool use and observation fr equency are in-game metrics collected  through logs that  indicate a degree of engagement with the digital environment. For a science tool example, typing “/oxygen” into the chat function provides a participant with a given oxygen level at the point they are standing on the map. Observations are open-ended responses participants can write in the chat describing or explaining phenomenon they encounter. For example, if a participant wants to remark about the shape of trees on “No Moon” they type “/observe the trees are short and wide, possibly due to high wind speed s”. Non-game activities were also included to augment Minecraft play, including brief discussions, demonstrations with inflatable planets, and a drawing activity.

## Procedure

Participants completed the adapted Astronomy Miscon ceptions Survey (AMS) in Qualtrics the beginning of the first day, prior to spending any time playing Minecraft. After completing the AMS, participants were intro duced to our Minecraft server and navigated two orientations worlds  where they learned how to  interact with non-playable characters (NPC’s) that provide information, use science tools, and make observations. For all following hypothetical worlds, participants were tasked with using science tools to determine the hypothetical q uestion being asked by the design of the planet. They were also asked to make observations t o describe each world and explain why conditions appear the way they do. Participants spent 25 minutes exploring each world, spread out across the first 3 days of each camp. On the last day of camp, participants were asked to complete the AMS in Qualtrics once again. AMS post-test questions were identical to and presented in the same order as the pre-test.

## Data analysis

Items were scored on a binary scale (True  “1”/False “0”), and pre-and post-test scores were summed for each participant. Internal consistency of items was asse ssed using McDonald’s  ωt for both the pre-and post-test (Malkewitz et al., 2023). McDonald’s ωt uses factor analysis to determine reliability of it ems and allows for differential loading of items, whereas Cronbach’s α does not. Pearson correlations were calculated to understand relationships between variables created by summing item responses. Following this, Student’s pairwise t-test was conducted to assess if there was a significant change in scores between pre-and post-test, and effect size was determined using Cohen’s d, with the pre-test standard deviation used as the denominator.

## Results

Regarding RQ1, McDonald’s Omega, with all items loading onto a single factor (Astron omy misconceptions), was used to assess reliability and showed (ωt  = 0.73) for the pre-test compared with (ωt  = 0.75) for the post-test. A slight increase in reliability informs us that va riance in scores slightly decreased, meaning studen ts answered more correctly in the post-test than they did in the pre-test. The instrument was reliable in capturing Astronomy misconceptions.

Addressing RQ2, Pearson correlations of continuous variables revealed significant positive relationships between pre-and post-scores, which is to be expected, as well as overall science tool use and post-test scores. Science tool use frequency bore significant positiv e relationships with Minecraft expertise, average Minecraft play per week,  post score,  and observation frequency. Learners who utilized the content-related resources more (i.e. Science tools) tended to reduce misconceptions more. ## Discussion

Misconceptions in science can be substantial impediments to learning, as they  are held beliefs or ideas that must be overcome for continued, deeper learning to take place. Many ways of addressing misconceptions have been put forth, yet little work has been done to explore  the effectiveness of these means. Through astronom y-focused summer camps using Minecraft as the main mode of engagement with content,  several counterfactual scenarios asking “What if” questions were explored by learners. Significant differences were found between a pre-and posttest of astronomy misconceptions, indicating that t he overall experience did contribute to a reduction  of middle school-aged students over the course of a week. The adapte d assessment was found to be a reliable measure of astronomy misconceptions for the target age range (9 – 13 years old). At the very least, the initial piloting of the adapted assessment provides a roadmap of misconcept ions that are prevalent among adolescents, as well as an indication that Minecraft is an effective environment for addressing misconceptions in science. As this Minecraft experience is being adopted by planetariums and oth er informal learning spaces, ensuring the continued effectiveness of it to promote a fun environment for exploration while reducing misconceptions is crucial. Correlations provided some insight into the relationships between several variables, including experience and in-game engagement. The use of science tools significa ntly correlated with post-test scores, suggesting that the more learning resources the player used in-game the more likely they were to reduce misconcept ions. Active engagement with the environment is important for learning in this context. Also, having experience and frequently playing Minecraft correlates significantly with tool use, which indi cates that using said resources may be easier for experienced players. Minecraft is a familiar context for many adolescents so being  able to bypass learning curves for control and navigation allows learners t o efficiently access and process STEM-related content, yet those less familiar may still require scaffolds and additional supports to reach intended engagement levels.

## Limitations and Future Directions

While most camp time was spent exploring worlds in Minecraft, there are other activities that do augment the Minecraft experience, such as debriefings following explorati on of each world. Therefore, the reduction in misconceptions cannot solely be attributed to playi ng Minecraft. Also, no experimental conditions were created for this research, so it is not known if exploring the counterfactual worlds in Minecraft is superior in reducing misconceptions compared to discussions or playing other astronomy-themed games, such as Universe Sandbox. Future directions will work on improving the assess ment for adolescents, focusing on removing items containing misconceptions that do not appear problematic. Further analysis will identify in-game play behaviors that most closely associate with or predict performance on the knowledge assessment.
