# Roles of Learning Design Tools in Scaffolding Teacher Participation and Learning in Research Practice Partnerships

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This structured poster session brings together eight presentations of research practice partnership (RPP) projects that have designed and deployed learning design (LD) tools to scaffold teachers’ learning and collaboration as designers. These tools are designed according to the design principles underpinning the respective RPP projects, playing an important role in their implementation. The LD tools presented have diverse features and functionalities, ranging from scaffolding peer review of LDs, visualization of the LD workflow, sharing of LDs for peer adoption, guidance in the design of specific learning activities, design of personalized feedback, fostering collaboration and learning of geographically distributed LD professionals, to the provision of an LD language for shared communication, layered LD decision-making and visualization of design analytics. Such diversities highlight the need for and challenges to establishing LD as a design profession and the potential for LD tools to support the establishment of a learning sciences-grounded designer profession.

## Introduction

Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs) have become the most w idely adopted model of educational innovation implementation in the learning sciences to address persistent problems of practice (Penuel et al., 2013). Moreover, well-designed RPPs would ensure that practitioners would  make creative contributions to the implementation of the targeted innovation as well as the design and interpretation of the research (Henrick et al., 2017). An important challenge to the effectiveness of RPPs in achieving innovation goals is scalability, which includes spread, depth, sustainability, shift of ownership, and evolution (Clarke & Dede, 2009). In recent years, research tea ms around the globe have embarked on the design and deploymen t of technology tools to scaffold teache rs in their LD practices as an integral part of their engagement in the RPPs. LD tools aim to scaffold the communication, sharing, and collaboration of teachers in the learning co-design process by providing a common ‘language’ and operationalizable tool t hat facilitates designers’ interaction, reflection,  joint decision-making, and evaluation of LDs. The LD tools embed the design principles of the res pective RPP they support to enable a more accessibl e appropriation of the design principles by practitioners in their LD process, and to support collaborative co-design as an effective form of practice-oriented profession al learning. By using an LD tool to scaffold practitioners’ engagement in an RPP, researchers adopt a socio-technical co-evolution approach (Law & Liang, 20 20) to designing teacher professional learning systems. Th e features and functions of the technology platform  and resources prioritize the practitioners’ awareness a nd likelihood of adopting specific pedagogical approaches and strategies (Goodyear, 2005). The teachers’ deployment of such systems makes their pedagogical reasoning more open and explicit, which scaffolds sharing and reflections on principles-based practice. In this proposed interactive poster session, eight teams from Canada, China (HK and Beijing), Greece, Israel, Singapore, and Spain will be reporting their efforts in the design  and implementation of LD tools and resources in the context of their RPP projects, the  roles these technological tools and resources play, and the challenges encountered. Each poster addresses the following themes:

1. The context of the RPP project and the underpinning design principles the LD tool(s) aim to scaffold.

2. The key features of the LD tools, including the LD functionalities and design resources (if applicable)

provided to the practitioners and the intended contributions of the tools in implementing the RPP.

3. The observed uses and feedback from the intended pr actitioners, as well as whether and how such

observations align with the design intentions.

4. Reflections on the implementation of the LD tools, including future development plans where applicable.

This interactive poster session will inspire researchers and practitioners to reflect on the collective experience of research teams in leveraging LD tools to scaffol d and scale educational innovations in RPP projects, and to foster collaboration across geographic and educatio nal contexts to advance  this nascent area of research. Such advances will have the potential to foster a design profession in education grounded in the learning sciences. Mediating Co-design and Collaboration: A tool that visualizes pedagogical decision-making Elizabeth S. Charles, Phoebe Jackson, Chao Zhang and Jeremie Choquette Active learning (AL) pedagogy is largely a practiti oner-driven approach to instruction, as evidenced by its popularity in discipline-based education research (e.g., Theobald et al., 20 20). Its philosophical principles are grounded in social constructivist theories of learn ing and its design focuses on student engagement, p rivileging collaboration and group-work. Despite its popularity, the successful use and continued uptake of active learning in postsecondary education faces a major hurdle: th ere is currently no shared language to communicate how and why active learning pedagogy functions in practice.

Our RPP has addressed this challenge by co-designing a Learning Design (LD) tool, CourseFlow, that mediates the development of a shared understanding of the fundament al elements and mechanisms of this pedagogical approach, as represented within AL acti vity patterns.  The RPP is made up of researchers f rom the learning sciences and science faculty as practitioners from several higher education institutions in eastern Canada. Within the RPP socio-cultural perspective, practitioners are not considered merely as compliant partners, instead, they offer valuable insight and contribute to the development of an “a theory of action” (Penuel et al., 2015).

Using a grounded approach, the work of our RPP started with the documentation of a purposeful sampling of AL activities from practitioner. Together with the r esearchers, this team mapped out activities as flow charts that revealed patterns of workflows best  characterized within a theoretical framework of orc hestration (Dillenbourg, 2013). This framework allowed us to map out a coherent pattern including: (1) where the tasks took place (in class, outside class); (2) who was responsible (student, teacher); (3) what social configurations were used (individual, group, whole class); and (4) what cogn itive processes were associated (passive to interac tive).  The LD tool was refined further as more examples of pra ctitioner activities were mapped out until we were able to reliably use it to describe the structure of a wide range of instances of AL pedagogy. CourseFlow visually reifies the orchestration workf lows of this instructional approach and has shown itself to be useful in two important ways, for each of the stakeholders. For practitioners, it provides a language that has been used to mediate pedagogica l discourse within the RPP, as well as co-design collaborations among practitioners, as part of a community of practice. For researchers, these representations generated by the tool reveal the repeated structures of these practitioner-driven instructional innovations. For example, some workflows incorporate pedagogical strategies used in cooperative learning (e.g., jigsaw, gallery walks) or scripts popularized by physics educators (e.g., Peer-Instruction), while others reveal new patterns that  draw on the a ffordances of collaborative learning spaces such as active learni ng classrooms (e.g., distributed problem-solving). Having such patterns elucidated in a theory-and evidence-based framework, allowing researchers to ask import ant questions about these practitioner-generated designs.  Currently, CourseFlow is being used by approximately 100 users, the majority being practitioners. It has been used to d ocument over 190 AL activities, in over 20 discipli nes and continues to develop based on the growing understanding of the ways to align to other curricular level s (i.e., program and course levels).

Community and Data-Driven Strategies in Teacher Design for Learning Davinia Hernández-Leo This contribution presents research focused on providing LD technology tailored through RPP to foster reflective and collaborative attitudes essential in practice-oriented professional learning. The contribution de lves into the dual roles of online teacher communities in facilitating engagement for mutual inspiration and enabling co-design and collaborative inquiry, while also exploring diverse data analytics perspectives to enhance LD. The Integrated Learning Design Environment (ILDE), which seamlessly combines collaboration tools, design editors, and middleware for the deployment o f LDs into learning environments (Hernández -Leo et al., 2018), has been tailored and expanded through RPP to serve as a platform to cater for the needs of diverse teacher communities, from specific schools, teacher training pr ograms, and particular thematic initiatives. This h as enabled us to research different perspectives on th e relevant elements to consider when engaging teach ers in LD community platforms. For example, we have seen that  community platform’s features and incentives, beyo nd individual motivations, significantly influence pre-service engagement and the quality of their contrib utions (Gutiérrez-Páez et al., 2023). Teachers find features that enh ance self-awareness of their actions and the quality of their contributions particularly relevant. To en able teacher inquiry and knowledge building in teac her community inquiry, our research has led to proposals including LD authoring tools that guide teachers in designing research lessons, version control and multi-author management mechanisms, or a design-oriented approach in the learning analytics into their reflective practices (Michos & Hernández-Leo, 2020). In addition to the role of learning analytics in te acher inquiry processes and the redesign of learnin g experiences, through RPP we  have delved into how v arious data analytics perspectives can aid decision-making in LD processes (refer to as the Analytics Layers for LD in Hernández-Leo et al., 2019) and, thus, shape a sociotechnical co-evolution approach. Community analytics sheds light  on the design activities occurring within a community of teachers, fostering awareness, prompting reflection on design patterns, and providing direction for enhancing design practices (Michos & Hernández -Leo, 2020). On the other hand, design analytics eva luates the characteristics and decisions ingrained in LDs, pro moting reflective practices regarding choices made during an LD’s creation and its potential future impacts (Hernández-Leo et al., 2019).  Findings indicate that design analytics visualization not only reduces cognitive load, espe cially mental demand, but also aids educators in se lecting suitable activities without prolonging the design time, ultimately improving the overall quality of a nd helping teachers sidestep design errors (Albó et al., 2022).

LD tools connecting bottom-up and top-down design insights in RPP networks Yael Kali, Ornit Sagy, Tehila Apple and Keren Sarah Levy One of the challenges in networks of RPPs is the sharing and integration of the wisdom developed within various RPPs and its formulation as more general reification artifacts (Aridor et al., 2022). At the Taking Citizen Science to School (TCSS) center, we have developed a set of LD tools to support these processes in the context of school participation in citizen science. Citizen Science is a genre of research where non-experts partner with scientists to advance knowledge, decision making and action regarding real world problems. When schools are involved, these become student-scientist partnerships (Kali et al., 2023). For instance, in the Radon at home project (Golumbic et al., 2023), students help scientists map Radon levels at their homes and raise alert to risky situations. The database created serves both the scientists and the students to pursue their research questions. The TCSS network includes dozens of such partnerships in various domains. Lea rning among partnerships is critical to prevent “re inventing the wheel” for solving similar implementation chall enges of incorporating the citizen science projects  into the school curriculum and practice.

To support such learning, we have developed LD tool s for both bottom-up and top-down learning mechanisms within the TCSS network of partnerships. The bottom-up mechanisms are embedded in INSIGHTS-a tool designed to encourage participants to reflec t on practice, negotiate ideas regarding local inst antiations of the innovation, and co-create reification artifacts that coalesce insights  across settings and expertise. The top-down mechanisms are embedded within EXPANSION – a tool that scaffolds other participants for reusing artifacts developed within the network and adapting these artifacts to their own settings. This poster will focus on findings from teachers’ u se of the EXPANSION tool. The platform provides teachers with ready-to-use materials, developed within the TCSS network, for integrating citizen science projects into their own teaching contexts. Each project includes interactive resources teachers can use with their students, which guide students as they take part in scientifi c research, while also exploring their own research  questions. The set of resources is organized as a sequence of activities that teachers can use, as is, or customi ze while addressing underlying design principles that have been articulated by learning sci entists to support inquiry (Kali & Linn, 2007). The platform includes planning and design tools for making these adaptations, which assist teachers in exploring how to connect the project with local characteristics, constraints, and affordances. Findings indicate that through their use of EXPANSION teachers (a) developed designerly ways of knowing (Cross, 1982),

(b) adopted a Teachers-as-Designers identity (Kali et al., 2015), and (c) inc reased their capacity in designing

coherent sequences of activities suited for their specific teaching contexts. Learning Design Studio: an integrated tool to foster outcome-based aligned decision-making across multiple pedagogical layers Nancy Law and Pakon Ko Teachers in many jurisdictions face pressure to change their classroom practices, adopting collaborative, inquiryoriented pedagogies that engage students in knowled ge construction. This necessitates teachers to assume the primary role of a learning designer rather than an instructor. The Centre for Information Technology in Education (CITE) at The University of Hong Kong has partnered with teachers in iterative co-design cycles in RPP projects to achieve self-directed learning in STEM/STEAM subjects (collectiv ely referred to as In-STEM projects). The Learning Design Studio™ (LDS), developed by CITE, provides a principled design framework, a common design language, shared resources, and a collaborative platform to support teachers in the In-STEM RPP. Creating an LD involves multiple layers of pedagogi cal decision-making, including pedagogical philosophy, approaches, strategy, and tactics level s (Goodyear, 2005). At the course level, LDS helps designers to make these decisions by offering a step-by-step guide that adopts Biggs' (2014) outcome-based approach to course design and provides design patterns as conce ptual resources. LDS prompts designers to define cl ear intended learning outcomes and specify targeted cognitive levels using Bloom's Taxonomy. The Learning Design Triangle (Law & Liang, 2020), e mbedded within LDS, is another conceptual tool that assists teachers in developing an overall course design by generating sequential blocks of learning tasks (referred to as curriculum components, CC for short) by providing a logic for connecting specified outcomes with a selected pedagogical approach and the steps in the work flow  of an authentic problem-solving context referred to as a disciplinary practice. Scientific investigation and engineering design  are commonly adopted disciplinary practices in STEM subjects. Design patterns at the CC level scaffold LD decisions at the pedagogical strategy level. For example, predict-observe-explain (Kearney, 2004) is an appropriate learning strategy when the teacher wishes to elicit students’ misconceptions associate d with  a challenging topic. At the task level, designers can select from 12 task types categorized under four ty pes of learning experiences provided to learners: directed, exploratory, productive, and reflective learning (Law & Liang, 2020). LDS also supports the specification of finegrained task settings, such as time allocation, soc ial organization, e-learning support, and assessment. Designers can explore and select appropriate pedagogical tact ics through the task patterns provided.  A designer dashboard embedded in LDS provides visualizations of the profile of learning experiences if implemented as well as a visual check on the extent of constructive design alignment between targeted learning outcomes and assessment. In the INSTEM RPP project, novice teachers appreciate the shared resources and structured scaffolding, while advanced users find the platform helpful in r efining designs and identifying unaligned outcomes (Chen et al., 2022). Importantly, LDS served as a common design language and technolo gy platform for school-based interdisciplinary collaboration among teachers in t he design of integrated STEM curriculum units and i n crossschool teacher sharing. Ongoing development focuses on abstracting design p atterns that encapsulate the successful operation alization of design principles at connected levels of design dec isions (Goodyear, 2005)  as design resources for more effective scaling of pedagogical practices in learning design and implementation, and in the establishment of teaching as a learning design profession. Teacher Knowledge Building Design Studio: embedding Learning Design Tools in a Knowledge Building Environment Chew Lee Teo, Yee Yin Tan and Guangji Yuan Apart from managing the different constraints and r esources in their daily teaching practice, teachers  adopting knowledge building practices have an additional rol e to creatively support students in negotiating the  world of ideas beyond what is given, and this often includes redesigning the curriculum, environment, and assessment (Ma & Scardamalia, 2022). Teacher Knowledge Building De sign Studio (tKBDS) is a two-day event that brings teachers and researchers into a shared workspace to  ideate and design know ledge building practice (Hossain et al., 2019). tKBDS occurs concurrently with the stud ent knowledge building design studio (sKBDS) - a student holiday programme focusing on real-world sustainability issues in a knowledge building  environment recreated in the university (Yuan et al., 2023). Teachers oscillate between their roles as designers, facilitators, and observers in sKBDS and tKBDS. In this way, tKBDS is likened t o the "research laboratory" for teachers to observe  KB practice in real-time and experiment with designs-the same way in w hich research groups and innovative organisations engage in knowledge creation activities (Tan, et al., 2021). Two technological tools were used in tKBDS. First, teachers and students worked on two symmetrical discourse spaces on Knowledge Forum® (an online technol ogy designed to support community knowledge creation). This technological feature of tKBDS allowed teachers to experience the messy and unpredictable nature of creative problem-solving. More importantly, it enabled teachers to appreciate the students’ knowledge building potential through the rich data. Second, the “Knowledge Building Learning Companion for Teachers (KB LCT)”, an AI chatbot, served as a personal learning companion to support teachers’ individual ideation. The KB LCT provides just-in-time feedback and information, giving each teacher within the community a personal workspace to explore KB principles and practice and, in the p rocess, strengthen their “knowledge-of-practice” (Cochran-smith & Lytle, 1999, p. 250), that is, local knowledge relevant to their teaching contexts. tKBDS centers on authentic and experiential learning.  This immersion situates teachers in the moment; observing actions, dialogues, and emotions, ensuring that KB is not an abstract concept and heightens the role of teachers as designers of a learning environment.  We received positive feedback from teachers, for in stance: “seeing how students go through kb [knowledge build ing] and design, very powerful for us”, “I was neve r so engaged before for a two-full-day workshop”, “the pedagogy chatbot was a nice way for me to bounce ideas”. tKBDS is rooted in research-practice-partnership. Moving forward, we want to further lev erage the learning analytics embedded within the discourse space and incorporate teachers’ feedback to improve the prompt design of the KB LCT, thereby strengthening the feature of ongoing desi gn and feedback at the intersection of researcher-student-teacher communities. Asensio -Pérez, Alejandra Martínez - Monés and Yannis Dimitriadis In the realm of educational research, collaboration s between researchers and practitioners can be fund amental to ground the research proposals both in systematic inquiry and in established experience (Sjölund et al., 2022). This paper discusses the use of a web-based tool, e-FeeD4Mi, that supports the design of personalised f eedback in massive educational contexts. In such contexts, wit h a high number of learners and an asynchronous lea rner-tolearner and learner-to-instructor communication, the provision of personalised fe edback presents a significant challenge (Estrada-Molina & Fuentes-Cancell, 2022). To address this challenge, the moni toring and support of personalised feedback often relies on Learning Anal ytics (LA) approaches, which can scale up the suppo rt to teaching practices by providing data-driven learning indicators (Pardo et al., 2017). Ho wever, effective personalised feedback depends upon the capability of instructors to reflect and make proper decisions upon those LA indicators and connect them with the course design.

With that goal in mind, we propose e-FeeD4Mi, an LD tool that helps instructors to create feedback interventions by connecting learner behaviours  (described by means of LA indicators) with potentia l problems that learners might face during the course run-time through the creation of if-then rules (Ortega-Arranz et al., 2022). In practice, using e-FeeD4Mi, instructors highlight core aspects of thei r course LD related to feedback, thus they are engaged in a reflective ‘feedback design’ process, integrating both research and learning theory and evidence-based approaches. This LD tool builds on a conceptual framework named FeeD4Mi (Topali et al. 2023). FeeD4Mi includes a 5-dimension conceptual structure, accompanied by a process, a set of catalogues (for potential learners’ problems, potential LA-based indicators to identify such problems, and pot ential feedback reactions) and a set of recommendations based on the identifie d learners’ problems. The e-FeeD4Mi tool incorporates the framework components (i.e., process, catalogues and recommendations) aiming to automate the ‘feedback design’ process and to support computer-interpretable designs.

Following Design-Based Research as a methodological approach (Amiel & Reeves, 2008), we document the iterative design and testing process of the tool. e-FeeD4Mi has been evaluated with 6 instructors individually, who used the tool to design personalised feedback interventions. Our results pointed out the perceived usefulness and usability of e-FeeD4Mi and the users’ interest in adopting it into  their teaching practice because it eased the creation of LA-informed personalized feedback in MOOCs. Concretely, we found that e-FeeD4Mi fostered a reflection upon the instructors’ course design, it guided the users through the provided hints and per mitted the creation of automated and timely afforda ble feedback strategies. Our evaluation identified limitations as well, including the need for additional guidance on inter preting some of the catalogue indicators, an aspect  we plan to address in future studies. This research will contribute to the existing body of evidence-based research on the codesign of context-aware LA and LD tools that are grounded in learning theories. Macro-and Micro-recommendation integrated as community features in a Learning Design Tool Eleni Zalavra, Kyparisia Papanikolaou, Yannis Dimitriadis and Cleo Sgouropoulou Responding to practitioners’ vision of a learner-centred instructional paradigm, our research team developed CPELDS (Coherent PErsonalised Learning DeSign), an LD tool supporting teachers as designers of Personalised Learning (PL). Our quest culminated in the context of teacher professional development programs following a 3year-long Design-Based Research approach (Amiel & Reeves, 2008).  This participatory research allowed us to determine design principles of (i) the representati onal format and organisation, (ii) the degree of su pport and guidance, and (iii) the contextualisation level that teachers value in an LD tool (Zalavra et al., 2023), and informed the development and evolution of  CPELDS. Notably, the practitioners highlighted the potential of LD recommendations as commun ity features in an LD tool. Teachers participating in our studies valued two recommendation forms distilling knowledge from the LD community: macro-recommendations and micro-recommendations (Zalavra el al., 2021). The former emerges from peer practices available in the LD community. It conforms to the typical approach that allows acc ess to peers’ designs and enabling overviewing t hem like learning objects in online educational repositories. The latter supports the LD process by recommending specific elements within a LD. The teachers valued embedding recomm endations grounded on pedagogical frameworks related to these elements and referencing relevant peer-based practices. Subsequently, CPELDS integrates the community featu res of macro-and micro-recommendations within its LD representation. The macro-recommendation feature follows a hybrid approach of  case-based, content-based, and collaborative filtering methods (Drachsler et a l., 2015), allowing designers to get recommendations for LDs available in the LD communi ty supported by CPELDS. The micro-recommendation feature is available while authoring a learning act ivity within an LD. It supports two-fold recommendations for theory-and peer-based Personalized Learning activities. The theory-based recommendations derive from PL design principles (Zalavra et al., 2022) inspired by Universal Design for Learning (CAST, 2018). The peer-based recommendations derive from PL activitie s designed by peers within the LD community. Preliminary findings from teachers using a CPELDS prototype demonstrate the potential of integrating these recommendation features into an LD tool: the sharing of holistic design ideas through the macro-recommendations and the scaffolding available during the very challenging task of designing PL activities through the micro-recommendations. Utilizing a Video-Based Social Annotation System for Collaborative Design and Co-Teaching Jingjing Zhang, Xiaojie Niu, Yicheng Huang and Yehong Yang Collaborative endeavors within RPPs often involve a  diverse team of experts from different domains suc h as educational researchers, instructors, IT personnel, coordinators, and teaching assistants. This is especially relevant in the context of online and blended learning, where the responsibility for LD no longer rests solely on individual faculty members. While collaborative co-design can be conducive to teacher learning in communities  (CochranSmith & Lytle, 1999), challenges are often encountered in practice due to limited opportunities for face-to-face discussions, caused by the busy schedules and numerous responsibilities of researchers and stakeholders. Video-based social annotation creates the illusion of wor king "together" online, facilitating focused discussions on shared knowledge (Zhang et al., 2021). It paves the way for the development of innovative learning experiences (Seo et al., 2021), effectively bridging the gap between researchers and practitioners, even when they are geographically dispersed. This paper introduces  the development of an online video-based social annotation system (SAxLearning) designed to promote collaborat ive course design, particularly within the context of emerging teaching practices in higher education, such as the adoption of blended instruction. Employing Design-Based-Research (DBR) methodologies, we document the iterative design process of the SAxLearning system. The five-year-long DBR has established design principles related to (i) ideas, (ii) agency, and (iii) communities that create self-organized communities. Our findings support the potential of social annotation as technological features in a LD contex t. These features were found to significantly reduc e the cognitive load of instructors and learners particip ating in our studies. The SAxLearning system suppor ts LD and encourages co-teaching in university-wide foundational courses, which requires a sizable teaching team. It proved to be effective for supporting co-teaching of a group of 40 faculty members teaching approximately 2,000 university students in a single term. This study contributes to our understanding of how to leverage technology to facilitate collaborative design processes and co-teaching among academics from diverse fields.
