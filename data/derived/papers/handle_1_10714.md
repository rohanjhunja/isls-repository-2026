# Collectively Becoming Racially Literate Through Organizing for Educational Justice

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study reveals how a group of white and/or socio-economically privileged parents organizing for educational justice collectively became racially literate. I illustrate how participants collectively utilize and develop tools, epistemologies, and practices to identify, address, and organize against racism in the socio-political context of their organizing. In demonstrating how becoming racially literate can be a collective action, I reveal how learning resides within the organization rather than within the individuals of the organization. I also illustrate how collective literacy resides within the organizational strategies. This has important implications for how we understand organizing apparatus’ including the organization itself and how collective learning about racial justice occurs.

## Introduction

Recently, learning scientists have called for more attention to how learning occurs through collective  action. This work considers how individuals, networks, and organizations engage in learning through contentiou s practice as people strive to transform power relati ons (Curnow and Jurow, 2021; Philip & Pham, 2021). Despite the increased attention on learning within social m ovements in the learning sciences, few studies have  attended to learning for racial justice within the context o f collective action. Even fewer studies have attend ed to the complex and contradictory racial and class dynamics  that arise when middle-and upper-middle class people across racial groups grapple with their relationshi p to power and privilege in the context of their co llective action for racial justice.  In this paper, I develop the notion of collectively becoming racially literate  to refer to how a group of people notice, norm, and problematiz e racial categories, power, ideologies, and the soc ial, economic, and political forces that shape the urban  District they are organizing within. Collectively becoming racially literate also includes analysis of the gro up’s organizing strategies, tools, epistemologies, and practices. I found that the process of collectively becoming rac ially literate occurred in two distinct ways. First, it occurred through engagement with collective learning. This i ncludes how PFE members created and used specific t ools, epistemologies, and practices, including the develo pment of vocabulary, concepts, and analytical skill s to identify and address racism and to imagine transfor mative ways of being that contest racialized ideolo gies and existing structures of oppression and white suprema cy. Importantly, engagement with collective learning attends to the group of PFE as a collective so rather than focusing on how learning occurs among an individual  in a group setting, the focus on collective here speaks to the learning that occurs among and resides withi n the group. Second, it occurred in political action. Thi s includes the organizing strategies, actions, and politics that were enacted by the group as they took collective a ction in solidarity with racially marginalized and working class communities.

Theoretical approach The Learning Sciences has recently put more attention on  social movements and collective action  (Curnow & Jurow, 2021, with a longer history of research that  has contributed to a rich understanding of how learning is collaborative, and culturally, historically, social ly and politically situated (Engeström, 2016; Gutié rrez & Jurow, 2016). Prior work on socio-cultural theory (Lave & Chaiklin, 1993; Cole, 1996) served to lay the foundation fo r how learning includes social, cultural, and histori cal elements. From a socio-cultural perspective, learning involves shifts in the individual’s understanding o f a concept and in the shared conceptual practices that provided opportunities for learning (Hall & Jurow, 2015). These sociocultural understandings in the Le arning Sciences have also drawn critical attention to how material, political, structural and ideological con ditions organize what we know, how we act, and how we can p articipate in community practices (Nasir & Cooks, 2009; Rogoff, 1994). Given this study’s focus on the ideo logical dimensions of learning, it draws from this critical scholarship of the Learning Sciences that attends t o the ideological, political, and power dimensions of how and what PFE members learn as they construct knowledge related to racial justice together. Rather than fra ming ideology as a rigid systems of beliefs that inhibit  learning, this study follows more recent critical Learning Science scholarship (Gupta et al., 2022; Philip et al., 2016) that frames how all learning is ideological, and ideology involves processes of learning.

Collective racial literacy The concept of collectively becoming racially liter ate builds on interdisciplinary theories of racial literacy developed in the contexts of analyzing legal schola rship, anti-racist interracial parenting, and racialized classroom practices (Guinier, 2004; Philip et al., 2016; Twine, 2004) to consider what the process of collectively becoming racially literate looks like in the context of collective action spaces. The pro cess of becoming racially literate is neither linear nor fi nite. Instead, people learn to see and use associat ed concepts about race and racism in context and the dynamic pr ocess of becoming racially literate is always ongoing (diSessa & Wagner, 2005; Philip, 2011).  I apply racial literacy to the context of how middl e-and upper-middleclass parents collectively make sense of, contest, and rearticulate racialized ideologies that are att ached to education policies. By attending to micro-interactions of collectively becoming racially lite rate, I illustrate how PFE members engaged, contested, rearticulated, and transformed racialized ideologies of educational eq uity through their collective learning and organizing. T his expansion of racial literacy to the collective context of organizing spaces provides insight into how organiz ing tools, epistemologies, and practices for educat ional equity are shaped by the process of PFE members col lectively becoming racially literate as they make s ense of education policies in both the current socio-political landscape and in their consideration of t he historical context that shaped stratification and inequities in education.

## Context of study

PFE formed in 2015, as a group of middle-and upper-middle-class  Oakland Unified School District  (OUSD) parents, who desired to disrupt existing levels of stratific ation and segregation in (OUSD) schools and organize towards a more equitable school district and system. All eight members of PFE hold some form of privil ege whether it be through their race and/or their socio-economic status. The members of PFE are between the  ages of 33-53. Each member of PFE works part-time or full-time and each holds a higher education degree of ei ther a Bachelors or Masters. The racial makeup of PFE incl udes 1 Black woman, 2 South East Asian women, 3 whi te women, and 1 white man. Oakland, California and OUSD  prides itself on its racial and ethnic diversity bu t residential segregation, gentrification, and displa cement cements vast racial, social, and economic in equality. Additionally, OUSD faces a decades-long privatization effort funded by venture philant hropists situated outside of the OUSD community. Since 2009  there have been five superintendents, an infusion o f charter industry money in the school board races, a proliferation of  charter schools and parent organizing groups large ly funded by pro-charter organizations. It is within this socio-political context that I analyze PFE’s organizing e fforts which stand in powerful contrast to the more extern ally funded, pro-privatization parent projects that undergird many of the education reforms in Oakland.

## Methods

I designed a critical case study using the ethnogra phic method of critical discourse analysis to exami ne how middle-and upper-middle-class parents engage in school integration organizi ng, how they navigate the inevitable tensions that arise in their organizing,  and how their learning through their organizing is  shaped by social, economic, and political forces. I employ a critical analysis to my ethnographic methods to con sider how social activities, such as organizing, reveal how e veryday interactions are connected to the social re production and recreation of race relations, informed by hegem onic meanings of race and how people agentically em ploy practices, strategies, and tools to contest, rearticulate, and transform racialized ideologies.

## Data collection and analysis

For the purposes of saturating data and obtaining a  holistic understanding of learning and organizing over time, I conducted data collection beginning in December o f 2018 until May of 2023, spanning over 4.5 years  and 300 hours of observations. To gain nuanced insight into my questions, I cond ucted ethnographic observations of PFE’s monthly meetings, of PFE’s public facing meetings with community me mbers, and conducted document analysis reviewed PFE’s slack channels, publicly shared vide os, surveys, letters, policy proposal powerpoints, and websites. Once data was collected, it was content logged  and coded. I then selected moments in which participants engaged in the process of collectively becoming racially literate.

## Overview of findings

Three data findings illustrate the process of PFE c ollectively becoming racially literate, and how thi s process of becoming racially literate informs their organizing  strategies for educational justice. Each finding focuses on a unique aspect of how PFE collectively engaged in th e process of becoming racially literate. The purposeful selection of data includes  capturing how PFE’s engaged in the practice of distributed expertise, e ngaged in an epistemology of solidarity, and utilized the tool o f grappling with their collective relationship to p ower and privilege, all of which culminated in their collective becoming of racial literacy.

## Finding 1

PFE members engaged in a conversation about increas ing their political power and capacity by recruitin g additional OUSD parents to join their organizing ef forts. As they engaged in this discussion, Eliza, t he newest PFE member, expressed a desire for PFE to create a handbook to help her learn “all the stuff you shoul d know coming into this district”. Sarah, one of the more senior members of PFE, explained how PFE alsoneeds a process of moving new members through learning abou t the history of privatization of the district and how school enrollment works. In response to Sarah’s exp lanation about the need for new PFE members to lear n about privatization in education, Penelope, another  senior PFE member, emphasized the importance for n ewer PFE members to not only understand the role of priv atization of OUSD but to also learn about how to sh ow up as an anti-racist. She explained how a handbook should aim to  “help people understand what the district’s…gone through in the last 25 years” and h ow new PFE members need to know how to “show up at a school site or how school parent leadership is run… which is more of like an anti-racist sort of educational thing.” While this conversation started around an apolitica l subject, recruiting new PFE members, it resulted in PFE members’ offering their unique expertise which culminated in their collective understanding of the ir organizing goals of racial justice in education. I argue that in this conversation, each PFE members  utilized the tool of distributed expertise (Brown et al., 1993), the unique ideas and knowledge spread across each member of PFE culminated in a collective understanding of how  PFE thought about organizing against racism and privatization in OUSD schools. While this result was not a given, I found each member listened to one another, despite their individual differences, and engaged i n substantive engagement and disagreement. These differences ultimately contributed to moments of le arning where PFE collectively fostered collective l earning among all members about topics deeply embedded in n otions of racialized history, racism, power, and ma terial distribution in schools.

## Finding 2

In this study, PFE operated under the dynamics of a  post-summer of 2020 racial uprisings where local distric t leaders and grassroot efforts have increasingly focused on reme dying the “educational debt” for Black and Latinx students (Ladson-Billings, 2006). One specific remedy proposed  as a form of reparations by an OUSD Black student and parent organizing group, OUSD for  Black Students,  demanded for the district to remedy the racialized anti-Blackness the District had historically and contemp oraneously engaged in. They drew specific attention drawn to the disproportionate harm of clo sing majority Black schools in OUSD.   This policy proposal and the political and cultural dynamics of a renewe d focused on racialized equity force d PFE to grapple with how they come to understand an epistemology of solidarity where they organize in support of the Black student and parent organizing group representing over 120 B lack families in OUSD.  In a contentious conversation on PFE’s private slack channel, members begandiscussi ng their stance on supporting this policy proposal put forth by OUSD for Black students. Within this digital con versation, members begandisagreeing with one anoth er over whether the policy proposal went too far in ca lling the District racist. The more senior members operated with a standpoint of solidarity that relied on thei r institutional history of OUSD and their more deve loped analysis of how racism operates as a set of oppress ive structures rather than individualized acts of e xplicitly racist statements. More senior PFE members’ use of a standpoint of solidarity focused on the structure  of the harm rather than questioning if the individuals wer e racist who were responsible for enacting the harm. For instance, Sarah, one of the more senior members of PFE, explained how Black students have been subject  to ongoing discrimination based on their schools dispr oportionately being closed by the district. Sarah e xplained how, “OUSD isn’t a majority Black district and the fact that school closures have always affected Blac k students disproportionately points to some bias…I t hink there is no chance that zero majority Black sc hools get closed, but the question is, when OUSD claims to ce nter equity and address anti-black bias and racism, how is this list acceptable?”

## Finding 3

The data of this third finding captures PFE’s learn ing in preparation for their first public community  forum which was held to increase awareness of the raciali zed nature of the slated school closures and to inc rease opposition to the closures among their audience mem bers. This crucial moment captures how PFE’s organizing shifted as they thought through their individual and collective stance on the closures and how they engaged in the practice of grappling with their relationship to po wer and privilege  as these political events unfolded.  This interaction begins with Naomi, a founding member of  PFE, explaining how a stance against the school cl osures is aligned with PFE’s goal of organizing for a new enrollment policy that would lead to more integrated schools. She outlined the connection between school closures, the expansion of charters, loss of public-school enrollment, and more segregated schools. She sugges ted that PFE “should focus on the enrollment policy solution to the issue of closures. I also think we have this other piece of work to focus on which is if we want to have a vibrant public school system, then we all ne ed to be in, we all need to be choosing public scho ols. So, if they are consolidating schools, we all need to show  up and make sure those schools are wrapped around and supported by their community…and we can tie this in to our enrollment work.…and then coming back to thi s question of what does an equity lens on all of this  look like? What’s our role in ensuring we get to t he fundamental issues that are causing all of this?  Building from the connection between their school i ntegration organizing and taking a stance against school closu res, Penelope then explained how PFE can play a par ticular role in the conversations around school closures wh ich is one of organizing other white and/or privile ged parents against the closures and for parents to sup port a new enrollment process. To make this connect ion between their relative access to power and organizi ng, Penelope drew from historical literature that d ocuments how white parents have been the largest barrier to successful school integration efforts. Penelope suggested that PFE get ahead of the possible resistance to integra tion efforts from white and/or privileged families by building support and momentum for a new enrollment process among these very parents.
