# Integrating Individualized Education Programs into AI-based Tutoring Systems: An Interview Study with Teachers

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
US-based special education teachers use Individualized Education Programs (IEPs) to tailor academic resources for students with disabilities. However, prior intervie ws reveal significant challenges integrating key IEP components – individualized learning goals, accommodations, and progress monitoring – into existing AI-based tutoring and assessment technologies. This paper discusses AI-based tutoring systems ’ m isaligned analytics with teachers’ needs, the level of decision-making AI should have in special education classrooms, and a pathway for adapting AI-based tutoring systems to special education’s needs.

This works investigates how AI-based tutoring systems influence US-based teachers' practices when personalizing curriculum for students with disabilities. AI systems have promising roles in the future of personalized education for students  (Kooli & Chakraoui, 2025; Mohd Amin et al., 2025). However, sole reliance on AI is rare, with teachers actively maintaining separate, preexisting strategies for personalization within their classrooms (Williams et al., 2025). While some prior work explored interventions for specific learning activities and disabilities (Chang, 2022; Garnier et al., 2023; Khamparia et al., 2020; Zingoni et al., 2021), there is a lack of research bridging the functionality of AI-based tutoring systems with the personalization methods teachers actively use to supplement and override AI decisions around students with disabilities (Williams et al., 2025). We present findings from a series of interviews with special education teachers who use AI-based tutoring systems, aimed at understanding how these tools are used within their pre-existing practices. We explore the following research question: How might we integrate personalized goals and accommodations into the design and use of AI-based tutoring software in special education classrooms? We found that current AI-based tutoring systems’ priorities do not match the personalized goals within an IEP and fail to offer useful learning analytics. Further, the desired level of AI’s decision-making in determining student progress varied among special education teachers. This paper presents preliminary findings from  a qualitative investigation of the gap between AI-based tutoring and special education personalization needs.

## Related work

Special Education is an inclusive learning environment for students with disabilities. A dditional resources in the form of assistive technology, modified curriculum, and personalized learning goals are made possible using Individualized Education Programs (IEPs) (Christle & Yell, 2010). In special education classrooms, students often have multiple disabilities and co-learn with others with a variety of support needs. This environment differs from prior scholarship, which has primarily studied personalized support for specific disabilities in isolation (Allman et al., 2024; Cinquin et al., 2019; Panjwani-Charania & Zhai, 2023; Yang et al., 2024).  In this unique environment, special education teachers supplement and override AI decision-making using personalization methods established in individual students’ IEPs (Williams et al., 2025). This work contributes to the scholarship bridging theoretical studies to real-world special education classrooms. We interviewed 12 special education teachers that both used AI-based tutoring systems in their classrooms and had experience with the IEP process.  Five teachers were prior study participants, one teacher was recruited via an education-specific sub-reddit, and the remaining 6 were recruited using Prolific. Teachers who were not previous participants verified their teacher status by providing an official school  email address. We assured all teachers that participation was voluntary, responses would be anonymized, and consent could be revoked at any time. To ensure participants remain anonymous, participant data is reported on aggregate with participant IDs. Semi-structured narrative interviews 800 Our interview protocol  inquires about the mismatch between special education’s methods  for personalized instruction and AI-based learning technologies’ capabilities. The interview was 1 hour long, containing two parts. The first used directed storytelling to understand how AI-based tutoring and assessment technologies are currently used within the IEP process. What role do these tools have from data assessment, establishing a student’s baseline, and creating measurable, personalized learning goals? The second half used design scenarios to: 1) Identify teachers’ needs, privacy concerns, and desire for control of the availability of their  students’ IEP information to an AI-based tutoring system used in the classroom. (2) Identify teachers’ information needs so they can keep track of personalization that occurs.

The interviews were completed and analyzed in two sets of 6. The first six interviews were coded by both coders to establish reliability, the second by only one coder.  Each transcript had two passes: one for the big picture ideas and the second for specificity. Finally, we gathered all the codes for a thematic analysis. Using those codes, we completed an affinity diagram, iterating over clusters to identify themes and insights. Classroom AI provides insufficient detail to support personalized learning goals, leaving teachers with additional labor. The responsibility of AI varies based on teachers’ desired use of the tool Participant attitudes towards the role of AI in special education generally fall  into the following three categories: AI as a tool, AI as a co-teacher, and AI as a collaborator. For AI as a tool, P8 uses AI as a “data collection instrument to measure [progress towards IEP] goals quarterly”. AI for this teacher are tools, not decision makers. P12 does not want the AI to make decisions at all, whereas P5 restricts decision making around defining progress to human educators only. In contrast, AI as a co-teacher values AI-provided decisions. The multi-level nature of P10’s classroom adds value to AI decisions, where tracking each student individually becomes difficult. To match the independent, self-paced environment, AI decisions need to be trustworthy independent of the teacher. AI having additional knowledge from the IEP brings hope for holistic decisions that apply to multiple subjects; however, those decisions should be  transparent. “It is very important for the teacher to know what decisions got made, because [AI] could contribute to teaching strategies in other areas.” AI as a collaborator combines  AI as a tool and AI as a co-teacher. P4 wants  AI to “offer some sort of suggestions on the way forward, but not the actualization itself... final decisions on the actual measures to be taken to define some sort of actual progress should be left for us as educators”. In a collaborative role, AI should assist, not lead. Our work affirms special education teachers'  desire for comprehensive learning analytics and belie f thar current AI-based tutoring systems fall short (Nguyen et al., 2018; Williams et al., 2025). We expand upon the reasoning for AI’s pitfalls: discussing examples of how assessment data isn’t sufficiently granular to be used meaningfully by teachers. O ur study contributes new insight into the responsibilities of AI-based tutoring systems in their classrooms, with teachers divided on the level of power AI should have  in measuring student’s progress.
