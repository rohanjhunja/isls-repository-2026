# Abstracting Design Principles for Indigenous Language Teaching Materials

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

Abstracting Design Principles for Indigenous Language Teaching Materials Zaynab Gates, UCSD, zgates@ucsd.edu Abstract: In the context of a design-based (DBR) study in two teacher training colleges in northern Argentina focused on the promotion of bili teracy practices in the Indigenous and dominant languages, two iterations of language teaching materials were developed for one year and four design principles to develop Indigenous languages teaching materials were abstracted. Design-based research to teach Indigenous languages The research project was centered on biliteracy practic es teaching Indigenous languages in Argentina through a design-based research (DBR) study. Indigenous language teachers face challenges due to linguistic retraction, a small speaker community (~40,000), and linguistic discrimination. Despite decades of Intercultural Bilingual Education, the effects of assimilation policies linger. Newly established tea cher training institutions offer spaces to observe Indigenous educators' efforts in preserv ing language, identity, and culture. This poster is based on the coding of storytelling interviews with 19  language teachers, 20 classroom observations at the  Teaching Institutions, 4 student group interviews, and 4 end-of-iterations reflective interviews. Coding was conducted with a codebook developed iteratively and preliminary analysis was shared with participants for feedback. For brevity, the four principles are exemplified based focused o n one study case: Teacher Pumjwa  (pseudonym). This case was selected as a common case for it encapsulated most of the experiences of teaching observed during the study.

## Conceptual framework

This study is broadly grounded in sociocultural theory to identify learning, the influence of culture, and mediation. Then, it draws on the continua of biliteracy framework which defin es biliteracy “any and all instances in which communication occurs in two (or more) languages in or around writing” (Hornberger, 1990, p. 213) to ma p the fluid context where bilinguals are learning literacy. The research on Indigenous language teaching also provided findings about the importance of  Indigenous knowledges and Indigenous historical per spective, and culturally relevant and Indigenous-language rich environments. Finally, for the purpos e of abstracting design-principles I employed research centered on teachers (Hanghøj et al., 2022; Penuel et al., 2011). Design principles Design principles translate theoretical assumptions into practical orientations, are useful in other projects and places, yet rely on the agency of educators to be adapted and be responsive to dif ferent contexts (Hanghøj et al., 2022; Penuel et  al., 2011). In this experience, we had a few broad guidelines at the start of the research, such as being useful to Indigenous language teachers’ declared priorities, documenting the process for replicability, and nurturing Indigenous  identity and love for the langu age. The main sources to articulate design principles  were observation of current materials in circulation, and conversations sustained during the codesign and reflective sessio ns, including the assessment of the designed artifacts. Four design-principles emerged and were presented to the Indigenous language teachers for their input:

• Respect for Dialectal Varieties.  The first principle emphasizes dialectal varieties and their significance

in identity formation. Teachers advocate d against standardization, preferring materials that reflect linguistic diversity. This could be done by presenting dialectal varieties simultaneously on the page, for example using a slash (/) to show several ways of conveying a phrase or using opposite pages. Also, texts should indicate the dialectal variety being used and add an invitation at the end for readers to research and complement the text with their dialectal variety, leaving space for those notes.

• Indigenous Stance. This principle highlights the importance of an Indigenous perspective developing and

framing language teaching materials, ensuring they reflect Indigenous cultural knowledge and language authentically. Stylistic choices and content select ion should affirm Indigenous identity and engage wi th relevant community issues  and give adequate attention to experiential knowled ge in conversation with academic or systematized knowledge.

• Materials as Building Blocks.  The third principle reflects current uses teachers give to ma terials,

accommodating diverse teaching contexts and student  needs. Teachers valued nonlinear curriculum designs, with multiple entry points to develop a ca pability or approach content. Materials should offer autonomy to teachers, allowing flexibility in curriculum design and adaptation. Clear information about materials' purpose, difficulty, and connections to other resources is essential for effective use.

• Low-Cost, Low-Tech. This principle  advocates for low-cost, low-tech materials that are easily

disseminated and accessible. Emphasis is placed on concise, visually appealing formats that incorporate Indigenous cultural elements. Integration of low-cost, low-tech principles ensures sustainabi lity and wider accessibility of materials.

The study's DBR approach facilitated the development of practical design principles grounded in Indigenous language teaching contexts. While addressing immedi ate concerns, such as respecting dialectal varietie s and promoting Indigenous identity, the principles also aim to guide future initiatives in Indigenous langu age education. However, broader concerns, such as foste ring communicative interaction and community-school connections, remain to be addressed in future resea rch. Overall, the study contributes valuable insigh ts into the development of Indigenous language teaching materials and the  advancement of Indigenous language education within teacher preparation programs.
