# Examining Mentoring in a Professional Society: Early Insights from a New Design Case in the Geosciences

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Mentoring is increasingly recognized as an important developmental process in STEM; however, most research and programming has focused on academic research contexts. Here we examine a pilot mentoring program in a geosciences professional society, investigating situated perspectives on mentoring in this distinct setting. Early findings from surveys and participant observations highlight unique affordances of professional society mentorship for identity development and culture change, emphasizing the importance of this context for design and research.

## Introduction

STEM mentoring relationships can strengthen connect ions to STEM fields, foster solidarities with margi nalized communities, and engage those communities in shapin g more inclusive visions of the field (Ong et al., 2018). Mentoring can be a powerful tool for driving cultural change in STEM (de Vries et al., 2006; Estrada et al., 2018; Thomas et al., 2015). While mentorship is recognize d as vital for developing inclusive STEM discipline s and workforces, systematic approaches to mentoring and mentor preparation remain under-researched (NASEM, 2019). Prominent STEM mentor preparation programs, such as CIMRE and the National Research Mentoring Network (NRMN), focus primarily on academic researc h contexts, particularly undergraduate research mentorship. Yet, mentorship takes many forms across  contexts (Dominguez & Kochan, 2020), encompassing practices like advising, acculturation, role-modeling, identity development, and community building (Dominguez & Hager, 2013; Estrada et al., 2018). Recent schola rship emphasizes the situated nature of mentoring, focusing on how “mentoring episodes” unfold within fluid networks and how these can be systematically designed to foster better development (Dominguez & Kochan, 2020; Soller et al., 2021). STEM professional societies provide opportunities f or mentoring, addressing systemic issues of diversity, equity, and inclusion by reshaping norms and organizational structures, and supporting diverse identities (e.g. women, first-time attendees, non-traditional students; Sardelis et al., 2017; Shelton et al., 2021; Shiffman et al., 2022). Multi-mentor approaches in professional societies help participants build robust support networks and cultivate more equitable environments (Settles, 201 9). And yet, we know even less about the processes and mechanisms that lead to good mentoring outcomes in STEM professional societ ies. These ecologies have characteristic temporal, spatial, and sociotechnica l constraints and affordances that inevitably influ ence how mentoring relations are experienced and how mentori ng roles are  productively enacted. Good mentoring via professional societies may look very different from good academic research mentorship, which has been the focus of the vast majority of recent research on mentoring in STEM. Our purpose here is to present early data on mentor  and mentee perspectives on mentoring to frame a longer-term study of situated mentoring practices as they unfold in the context of the Geological Society of America (GSA), a large international STEM profe ssional society. By investigating perceptions of va lue and relational processes of mentoring in this important context, we hope to learn to leverage mentoring to change the culture and demographic composition of the geosciences and in STEM disciplines more broadly.

## Context and goals

This study is undertaken in the specific design case of the On to the Future (OTF) Program, a STEM outreach and induction activity for new GSA conference attendees  from diverse communities. OTF has been conducted b y GSA annually since 2013. The study team is initiating a five-year program of design-based implementation research (Penuel & Fishman, 2012)  focused on understanding how narrative pedagogies t hat promote processes of individual identity development can be linked wi th co-design activities that promote culture change at hi gher levels. The OTF program matches new conference attendees (“OTF Mentees”) with more experienced and central conference participants (“OTF Mentors”) for a serie s of phased experiences before, during and after th e annual meeting of the society. These phases include pathwa y mapping for identity work and cohort-building, critical reflections on disciplinary experiences, and co-designed activities for changemaking. A key element  of the initiative involves preparing OTF Mentors to break from assimilationist models of mentoring that may r einforce exclusive and oppressive norms that have stubbornly persisted in the geosciences (Bernard & Cooperdock, 2018; Marín-Spiotta et al., 2020). Mentors are oriented toward mentorship practices that are (1) inclusive and dignifying to the diversity of OTF Mentees in ways that strengthen their relationship to the discipline, (2) explicitly oriented toward changing the culture of the field through mentoring practices and relationships, and (3) specifically tailored to cultivate productive relational processes in the  cultural-historical-social-material context of a large, international professional society.

Here we report on early findings from a pilot activ ity focused on identifying OTF Mentor and Mentee perspectives on valued mentoring interactions withi n the context of the professional society. The near-term goal of presenting these early findings is to inform the design of a new mentor development learning community to be launched in 2026, including the development of assessment instruments and novel program components. We also want to show this work to a broader community of learning scientists in hopes of better situating it in the field for productive research and collaboration on mentoring and learning in professional societies.

## Sample and method

A pilot survey of OTF Mentors and Mentees was condu cted to investigate the following research question s: (1) How and to what extent do mentors and mentees see v alue in their interaction within the context of the  OTF program? (2) What do mentor and mentee i nteractions look like in practice? A total of 32 of  130 participants in fall 2024 OTF programming responded to an online su rvey about their experience. Thematic analysis was conducted to “generate (initial) themes” (Braun & Clarke, 2019) from surveys. Survey results were independently coded by investigators. By comparing and discussing  emergent themes and triangulating them with the fi rst author’s notes from participant observation in mentor-mentee activities at the conference, a consensus set of pilot themes was developed. These early themes are discus sed below, and will be used to structure interviews  with a purposive sample of OTF participants in fall 2024 t o further particularize our understanding of mentor /mentee experiences in the GSA program.

## Findings and interpretation

Initial coding highlighted the perceived value of mentoring interactions for identity development, community creation, and disciplinary change. We also identified three particular learning affordances of mentoring in the specific context of professional societies: synoptic perspective, social repositioning, and refining practice. Both mentors and mentees benefit from the synoptic persp ective provided by professional societies, gaining an understanding of diverse disciplinary norms, cultur al diversity, and opportunities for “fit” within th e field. This perspective creates both comparative and normative opportunities for participants to situate themselves within the larger disciplinary landscape,  and facilitates professional and social repositioning. Mentees receive immediate, local guidance and community support at conferences, enabling them to navigate unfamiliar professional networks, and also create new connections to broade r research and academic opportunities supporting th eir professional/scientific growth. Mentors report opportunities to validate and refine their own mentoring practices as advisors and researchers, while contributing to broader cultural and disciplinary transformation. C ontrary to traditional outreach approaches that focus exclusively on the benefits of disciplinary participation, our preliminary analysis suggests that mentors and mentees commonly articulate both an affinity for and critique of the discipline, and that talking about and grappling with this ambi valence fosters familiarity and empathy that contri butes to an inclusive and supportive community.

The presentation will (1) articulate the approach and design conject ures, (2) characterize the population of mentors and mentees, (3) outline initial themes, and (4) illuminate these themes with notes from participant observation and interviews to describe situated mentoring moments that mentors and mentees see as valuable.

## Significance and next steps

The field of the learning sciences has yet to mobil ize a concerted investigation into the situated pra ctices and processes of mentoring necessary to inform better d esign of mentoring programs and ecosystems. This is particularly true of mentoring in professional society contexts, and even more so when it comes to mentoring for disciplinary transformation as opposed to simple as similation or inclusion. By sharing this work with ISLS participants, we hope to refine the focus and conce ptualization of the pilot  mentoring program, and stimulate discussion of the value of both (1) mentoring as a focused line of learning sciences research and (2)  professional societies as critical contexts for learning and disciplinary changemaking.
