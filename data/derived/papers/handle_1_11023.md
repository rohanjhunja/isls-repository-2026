# Learning Engineering for the Professional Science Communicator

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Professional science communicators face a daunting task: communicating science information in an effective manner to a society increasingly polarized over scientific issues and awash in misinformation. Many professional science communicators exist outside the academy and have minimal access to the resources and knowledge needed to develop evidence-based science communication activities. Learning engineering, defined here as the practical use of learning sciences knowledge to design outreach activities, may be a solution. Here I describe a pilot workshop on learning engineering and science communication delivered to professional science communicators. Overall, the idea of engineering science communication activities was well received by this population. Participants left the workshop desirous of more knowledge of the evidence on human learning generated by the field of the learning sciences. Additional work in this space is justified and new opportunities for a bidirectional relationship between learning scientists and professional science communications will benefit both fields.

Imagine that you traveled back in time to 16th century Europe. During your journey, you hear of a family that has a claw from a griffin, the famed mythological beast with a lion’s body, bird’s head, and possibly wings. You gain an invitation to the family’s and view the Griffin’s claw, which was converted into a gilded cup with cover. Today, one can view the purported Griffin’s claw  cup in the British Museum. With a discerning eye, it’s clear that the cup was made with a cow’s horn. The Griffin’s claw cup, and other such wonders  were common occurrence in curiosity cabinets during the renaissance in Europe (Pugliano, 2023). What does a 16 th century fraudulent Griffin’s claw have to do with learning engineering and science communication today? The spread of false information is not a new phenomenon – people have circulated faulty information for centuries. F anciful information is mixed in with the scientific. C onsequently s ifting credible information from tall tales  is a recurring problem. Today the  problem is amplified given the  magnitude of false information run ning rampant on the internet, particularly since the COVID-19 pandemic,  which ultimately resulted in an infodemic (Balakrishnan et al, 2022).

Professional science communicators (PSCs), or non-scientists engaged in science communication, such as those working in informal science spaces, face a non-trivial problem that has a significant impact on society: how can rampant misinformation be countered effectively? Given the psychological basis for falling for and/or believing false information (Ecker et al, 2022), attention to research on human cognition is critical. Here, I discuss a model for countering the infodemic: teaching PSCs to use learning engineering when designing their outreach. Learning engineering and the professional science communicator Although there are many conceptualizations of learning engineering (Lee, 2023), I define learning engineering in this context as an iterative process of applying evidence-based insights and methods from a variety of disciplines to inform the design of an educational experience. Teaching PSCs to engineer their outreach based on evidence from the learning sciences is a practical strategy for designing evidence-based and effective outreach. A learning engineering approach for PSCs is particularly beneficial, given that PSCs often exist outside the academy and have limited time and resources to identify, synthesize, and apply relevant research. Depending on their backgrounds, they may also be ill equipped to digest theoretical frameworks used in social sciences research (Besl ey & Dudo, 2017).  This disconnect ultimately contributes to the researcher-practitioner divide, (Jensen & Geber, 2020). Therefore, teaching PSCs  how to engineer science communication activities using evidence and insight from the learning sciences community may help bridge the research-practitioner gap. To explore the utility of a learning engineering for PSCs, I presented a workshop called Learning Engineering: Applying the science of learning to amplify your science communication endeavors  at the 2022 Association of Science Communicator’s ScienceTalk meeting. The Association of Science Communicators is a professional organization that serves PSCs such as journalists, documentary film-makers, and social media content creators. The workshop lasted 90 minutes and included approximately 30 people. Participants were first introduced to the concept of learning engineering and the field of the learning sciences. After this introduction, participants were split into small groups of 3-5 individuals and assigned a major theoretical perspective of learning 2373 Participants then described how they would design or redesign a science communication outreach activity using that theory. Participants were asked to represent their findings using large sheets of paper. As part of an introduction to the learning sciences and learning engineering, participants split into groups and were assigned a theoretical perspective and asked to use that theory to guide the design of an activity. The problem of misinformation is not new, but the expansion into a technology fueled infodemic requires new solutions. One of these solutions is equipping PSCs, who are traditionally underserved in the arena of science communication training and have limited access to resources, with knowledge of how to utilize learning engineering to design, implement, and iterate on their science communication activities. In this conception of learning engineering, it is a practical tool that can be used by someone outside the learning sciences community to leverage insights on how people learn to generate evidence-based outreach. During the workshop, instruction on learning engineering was accessible and PSCs were able to leverage learning theories into the design of their outreach activities (Figure 1).  Overall, workshop participants were keen to re-design their existing outreach activities and wanted  more information on the science of learning. This suggests that learning engineering as an approach to design evidence-based science outreach activities resonated with PSCs and is a potential avenue for instilling more evidence-based insights into outreach endeavors. Although this work suggests promising future directions for professional development for science communicators, there are limitations. For instance, the sample size was small and likely involved selection bias. These were also PSCs that had the means and desire to attend a professional development conference. At the end of the workshop professional science communicators were optimistic about seeking more information regarding redesigning their activities, the next obvious question is: did they? Therefore, a next step would be to offer more learning engineering professional development opportunities for professional science communicators and follow their outcomes to determine (a) if professional science communicators designed or redesigned outreach activities based on the science of learning (b) how effective they were at doing so. Learning scientists and PSCs can lean on the experiences of each other to both better understand learning in the wide variety of informal science education spaces reached by PSCs  and implement more evidence-based outreach strategies. Overall, there is potential for synergy between these two communities to address the problems of misinformation today.
