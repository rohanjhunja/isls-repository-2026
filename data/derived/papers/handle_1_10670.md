# Creating Our Makerspace: Designing Social and Material Conditions for Community Engagement in the Makerspace

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study presents a case for designing social and material conditions to privilege community knowledge in the makerspace with members from non-dominant communities. I conduct a design-based research where young people and community members worked together to design a new makerspace. I explore how re-mediating the social and material aspects of the learning environment can shift maker education from dominant narratives to relevant and consequential communal learning. I find that intergenerational interactions in story and artifact sharing enabled participants to reconsider the value of community assets in the makerspace.

## Introduction

Making and tinkering serve as means to broaden stud ent participation and enrich STEM learning in  informal learning spaces. Meanwhile, increasing numbers of critics have expre ssed concerns about inequalities that limit youth from nondominant communities from accessing t hese learning spaces. For example, there is rarely public discussion on how to best serve working class stude nts and students of color, and dominant communities are in the position of teaching other communities the know ledge and skills of making in the narratives of the  maker movement (Vossoughi et al., 2016). Equity concerns about narrowed participation and perspectives on knowledge creation in maker education are rooted in a system that centers the experience of dominant populations. Simply addressing these concerns with efforts to expand pa rticipation would fall into the “sameness as fairness”  trap without regard to the historical and present structures that give rise to deeply rooted inequalities. This study presents a case for designing with members from non-dominant communities to disrupt dominant narratives in the makerspace and create relevant and consequential communal learning. It expands on existing literature by introducing a new approach to community engagement within the makersp ace. This is the first step of a design-based ethnographic study in which the researcher, c ommunity partners, community members and young people engaged in the co-design and implementation of an equity-oriented makerspace in a popular science museum. In this first study, I asked the following question: When collaboratively designing the makerspace, what resources do young people and their families take up as valuable? Social re-mediation of learning environments I use a sociocultural perspective of mediated actions to consider activities happening in the makerspace. Learning takes place when participants shift or deepen participation in communities of practices. This requires an equitable organization of opportunities that enables all learners to experience themselves as knowledgeable contributors. In their seminal article, Cole and Griffin (1986) implemented a fundamental change in the way reading instruction serving struggling students in elementary schools is organi zed. Their intervention has proved that re-mediating the learning environment can produce improved readi ng results. This practice of re-mediation was adapted in various design interventions serv ing equitable ends. Gutiérrez et al. (2009) redesigned a literacy curriculum for students from migrant farmworker communities  that historicized the education experience of migrant students. Razfar et al. (2011) situated second-language learners in an activity system where multiple mediational tools were in place and students were repositioned as active agents of knowledge construction. Jurow et al. (2019) mobilized social resources in teacher education to surface new expertise, new design partners, and new means of co-inquiry. They argued that the social and material  dimensions in the knowledge infrastructure  of the learning space  are shaped by historically developed cultural norms and can be reorganized to foreground the assets of communities. In maker education, Vossoughi et al.  (2016) argued for a reconceptualized understanding of maker education grounded in the histories, needs, assets, and experiences of working-class students and students of color. This vision cannot be achieved without changing the social and material organization of learning in makerspaces. The maker movement has been focused on a narrow ran ge of technologies, tools and activities characteri zed by European-American middle-class pursuits. Studies showed that learning environments that are rooted in historical analysis of community realities and are responsive to members’ needs and assets have proven to be consequential for learners (e.g. Shea & Sandoval, 2020). In these studies, the reorganization of instruction, social relationships, materials, and the meaning of doing STEM presents how inequalities can be challenged in STEM practices.

## Methods

I used a design-based research (DBR) approach methodology (Cobb et al., 2003) to r e-mediate the learning environment in naturalistic makerspace settings and tries to generate knowledge about privileging non-dominant communities’ social and material resources in makerspaces.

## Background

The study took place in the makerspaces in two public middle schools: Downtown School and Southwest School, and the makerspace being designed is located at Explore Science Museum (ESM). ESM is located at a city center in the US. They have put efforts in serving communities that  are left out in STEM education. The makerspace in ESM is being redesigned and rebuilt and the new makerspace is positioned as a “youth-driven maker effort”. Both urban schools mirror the linguistic and cultural diversity of the regions served by ESM. Downtown School serves a student body comprised of 80% low-income students and 82% students of color, among wh ich 36% are Hispanic and 25% whose primary language is not English. Similarly, Southwest School enrolls a student population where 81% are students of color,  among which nearly 40% are identified as African American. The Transformed Science Initiative (TSI), a community  organization dedicated to supporting public schools in culturally diverse communities of color, funded and established the makerspaces in both schools and provided curriculum and professional development support for the schools to run their makerspaces.

## Activity structure and participants

The study involved a student design project, three community meetings, and three community feedback sessions. During the student design project, students collaborated in teams over four months t o develop their makerspace prototypes. They met weekly in their school makerspace for warm-up activities led by me, followed by project work in their own pace. Community meetings happened during the first half of student design pr ojects and were attended by students, families, friends, and school staff. In each of these two-hour meetings, we had three activities grounded in participants’ personal, family and community experience with making, and a last activity on their desires and concerns around building a community ma kerspace. Artifacts were produced in these meetings and were presented in the makerspace during the entire intervention. Community feedback sessions, held later in the project, invited community members to provide feedb ack on students' work and showcased artifacts. Whil e I led the activity design, TSI and ESM were involved in all the discussions shaping the design. Twelve students from sixth to eighth grade, with six students from each school, participated in the student design project and all are participants of this study. 10 were students of color representing Hispanic, African American, and Asian. In a total of six occasions where community members  were present, a total of 26 people interacted with the students, with seven of them participating in more than one occasion. 21 were people of color.

## Data collection and approaches to analysis

I conducted participant observation throughout the study. My involvement included introducing the activities and posing pedagogical inquiries relevant to the interv ention, rather than formal interview questions. Add itionally, I engaged in informal dialogues with participants as appropriate. Detailed field notes were taken during the activities, supplemented by analytic memos immediately afterward. Artifacts generated during these events were collected, encompassing drawings and notes exchange d during commun ity meetings, artworks crafted by participants, students' design documents and journals, and prototypes created by students. In my analytic work, I meticulously reviewed my fieldnotes, analytic memos, and visual representations within artifacts to identify recurring themes and categories. Developing a few tentative h ypotheses rooted in the data, I compared fieldnotes and  scrutinized moment-to-moment interactions and their evolution over  time to substantiate these hypotheses. At different points of data analysis, I triangulated tentative themes with community partners (TSI, ESM, and coordinators from the schools) who were present during data collection. When I found potential patterns in my data set, I conduct ed member checks with students by sharing my preliminary findings and inviting their input, offering space for them to affirm, challenge, or contribute additional insights.

## Findings

Intergenerational storytelling makes lived experiences visible When participants were given prompts to reflect on their personal, family and community experience with making, intergenerational storytelling took place as people of different ages shared expertise that resonated with their rich personal and family histories and interests. Across different phrases of the design process, these community-based perspectives on making were taken up by students who built new ways of seeing everyday making expertise. In a story sharing activity took place in the third community meeting in Southwest School, Jose, a Latinx community member, shared a story about making embro idery masks with the help from their mother during the pandemic. When masks were needed in daily life, Jose decided to make them. They investigated into different layers, and their mother taught them to sew the materials together and embroider symbols on the masks. “She was really into it at that time, ” Jose recalled, “…so she was like, I can show you. She helped me find  designs on the internet and showed me.” While Jose was telling this story, Sienna, a Latina student, engaged by asking questions; right after Jose shared the story, she thanked them and extended her arm towards her model in-progress on the other table and said, “we will have an embroidery machine in our makerspace” (fieldnote, 09/03/2023). It seemed like Sienna took up this story of sewing as she immediately offered the tool from this story a space in her imagined makerspace. In her initial design, Sienna did not have a space for sewing; the activities she listed in her design were “sandering, screwing, wel ding, 3D…” (fieldnote, 0 9/02/2023). After Jose shared their story, she seemed to be playing with the idea that an embroidery machine could be imagined into the makerspace. In the following week, I noticed that she added “roped off sewing area?” in her concept map. I pointed to the line and asked where the idea came from. She responded, “It was last weeks’ family thing, we got to hear things like sewing and knitting. Actually, my sister loves to knit and crochet and all” (fieldnote, 16/03/2023). In the las t feedback session, Sienna presented her completed model with “an area where people could have the resources to use yarn and needles and crochets ”. She said she  “ took inspiration from my younger sister ” (fieldnote, 29/03/2023).

In the second example  in Downtown School,  Ling, an Asian mom, embodied her lived experience spontaneously in intergenerational storytelling, making visible a valued practice in her life which was later taken up by her daughter Hannah. In a story exchange, Ling shared her experience of making that involved the idea of material upcycle: when her family lived in the basement, she used to pick up unwanted items on streets and turn them into room decorations (fieldnote, 16/02/2022). Her story demonstrated a recognition of mat erial possibility in daily life, and that attitude towards materials showed up again when she talked about what an instrument could be. In a subsequent activity, students found shared interests in music and tried to figure out the types of instruments for a making activity. Ling commented, “I’m thinking materials that can create sounds …doesn’t have to be an actual cello or drums or something like that. ” She then grabbed a pencil on the table and hit differe nt object to make a series of sounds. Other participants all joined her hitting different materials to try to make music. As they exchanged embodied experiences of sound making with everyday materials, the group not only experimented a new learning approach, but also deepened what the y knew about Ling’s identity. When  a student responded to Ling’s proposal with the name of another instrument as he hit the objects (“Like chimes?”), Ling kept modeling playing music without an instrument and rephrased his word “chimes” to “hanging things you can jing-jing-jingjing-jing”. Evident by these two interactions, her attitude towards material is a stabilized belief and practice. Such stabilized practice, or identity, were taken up by Hannah as she stuck to the idea of  repurposing all the materials in the makerspace since that meeting (e.g., fieldnotes, 23/02, 09/03, 29/03, 20/04/2023). In her completed model, she highlighted three large bins  each named “recycle”, “trash”  “scrapped materials” sitting in the middle of the space and how they will play a crucial role in educating people about upcycling (fieldnote, 28/04/2023). These two examples of intergenerational storytelling demonstrated how community members’ histories and identities were made visible to support meaning-making in the design process. In the first, Sienna connected Jose’s experience with her sister’s and claimed space for this activity that was overlooked by herself,  expanding her original notion of what should be included as authentic making in the makerspace. In the second, Hannah took up a practice in her family history and foregrounded it as valuable experience to teach others about making. Artifact making and sharing present new material conditions for engagement Artifacts served as another mediating tool for people to engage with each other’s ideas. The material environment in the makerspace was enriched with new artifacts shared by community members that told stories about personal, family and community histories and interests, creating new conditions for consequential engagement with making. In the first example  of artifact making, participants from Downtown School were asked to mak e with Play-Doh to show moments in their lives. Tonya and Diamo nd, mother and daughter from an African American family, each made a ball covered with tangled strings depicting the m essy moments of being a mo ther and an intricate colorful donut depicting the happiest moment in her life. In the next activity, they were prompted to talk about their dreams and hopes. Diamond held her Play-Doh donut and talked about how much she likes crafts and hopes to sell them because she doesn’t want her mom to “work so hard” and referred to the tangled ball. Another parent asked Tonya what her hopes were. She laughed and responded, “All I hope is that she does  it and starts that business!” Next, the group were asked to build a space reflecting the group’s interests. Other groups all started by designing an activity, whereas this group started by responding to Diamond’s dream. They compiled a list of resources to support kids running a business in the makerspace, as Tonya joked that having a shop that sells things her kid made can help alleviate financial burdens of her family (fieldnote, 09/06/2022). Family members are socially positioned in ways that are informed by their histories in intergenerational interactions. This exchange of artifacts enabled Tonya and Diamond embodied this built-in historicity: the tangled ball revealed Tonya’s stressful state, which was confirmed by Diamond about her mother’s financial burdens; the food art revealed Diamond’s gift. It represented a hopeful state of their lives, that Diamond is talented and will achieve success if the community supports her dream, such as starting a business within the makerspace. Another example demonstrated how artifacts being shared in the learning environment were taken up by people to discover common interests in making. In the first community meeting at Downtown School, Chandrika, a parent from an African American family, shared the rolling pin as a tool of significance to her  and put down “baking” as her superpower  and “food” among a list of materials for her new imagined makerspace, alongside computers and 3D printers (fieldnote, 09/06/2022). After the meeting, all artifacts, including Chandrika’s poster, were left hung in the makerspace during the co-design activities. Eight months later, in the same space, Patricia, parent of a student from a racially diverse family, was walking around the room when people were trying to look for themes of common interests for the new makerspace. She stopp ed in front of Chandrika’s poster, examined for a few seconds, walked back to the table and said: “Well, it seems like baking and cooking are what everybody’s interested in, I wonder  how would you do that in the space?” (Fieldnote, 16/02/2023). Here, t he artifact from the first meeting became a vehicle for meaning making. Hanging on the wall with other artifacts, it was part of the meaning-making resources of this space and offered insights about the space and people who belong to t he space. With Patricia’s comment, people started a discussion  around how to incorporate cooking as an experience in the makerspace, as they came to believe that it was what “everybody’s interested in”  based on both Chandrika’s and their own poster, whi ch someone also identified cooking and baking as a “superpower” like Chandrika. In th ese two examples, community members’ valued experie nces were presented as sources of knowledge in the form of artifacts.  In the first, the humble material and prompts invited the dyad to bring their family history and lived experience into the space, and the artifacts presented surfaced new potentials for learning. In the second, the poster mediated the construction of perspectives on making. The impact of  such knowledge even extended beyond the immediate confines of the meetings, offering opportunities for ongoing conver sations and cross-pollination of ideas among its members across different times and spaces.

## Discussions

This study argues for  re-mediation for community engagement in makerspaces that positi ons learners’ personal and community experiences as valuable. By attending to community resources through story and artifact sharing, we shifted the dominant narratives in the new makerspace to more relevant and consequential learning for you th from non-dominant communities. In the design, I was intentional on encouraging par ticipants to share authentic stories from their daily lives and experiences that tell wh o they are, without worrying too much about whether  a certain activity is considered “making”. It turned out that in subsequent discussions, what was being shared were usually taken up by other members from the communit y as authentic making  and incorporated in design ideas. This shed s l ight on Vossoughi  et al. ’s (2016) argument about practices from working-class communities are “sources of deep knowledge”  (p.212) for maker education. We understand making as a holistic, cultural event inseparable from the daily experiences of the people instead of a set of novel technical skills that one must acquire. Explicitly conveying this idea to learners shifts the power narratives and encourages them to reconceive their identities in the makerspace as creators and historical actors.
