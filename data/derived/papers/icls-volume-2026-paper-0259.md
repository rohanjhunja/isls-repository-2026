# Building Effective Teacher-Researcher Collaboration Through Thought Partnership

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents a case study of a teacher and researcher who worked together as part of a larger research-practice partnership. Collaborative reflection on the development and success of their work together over time highlighted several critical events in their learning process. The case describes these events as well as common themes that emerged across them and how they supported changes in the teacher’s practice. Implications for teacher learning and effective professional development are discussed.

The first two authors of this paper, one teacher and one researcher, worked together in the context of a research-practice partnership (RPP) focused on disciplinary learning as evidenced in classroom discussions. Researchers partnered with individual teachers as well as the district’s instructional coaches in various professional development efforts that aligned with the goals of the partnership. As part of this work, the two authors engaged in co-design cycles of design-implementation-reflection-redesign (Ko et al., 2022). Once their work in the classroom ended, they reflected on what they had accomplished while  working together. This collaborative reflection led to the realization that their partnership had been especially productive and useful for both the teacher and researcher. This provided an ideal context for exploring the growing interest in evolving models of partnership between researchers and teachers, and in understanding how such thought partnerships can become productive spaces for teacher learning, for reframing how students and their learning are conceptualized, and for rethinking the design and enactment of instruction.  The teacher and researcher’s collaborative  examination of the development of their relationship over time is the focus of the present paper. Theoretical framing RPPs continue to grow as a form of collaboration between research institutions and schools to address problems of practice and improve teaching and learning in schools and districts (Coburn, Penuel, & Geil, 2013). RPPs vary greatly in their goals, scope, and type of work (Penuel et al., 2020). One type of work that is part of many RPPs is co-design among researchers and various stakeholders, such as district or school-level administrators, teachers, or community members. The co-design process may center on elements such as policy, systems, routines, curriculum, or instructional materials.  Research has shown that co-design collaborations between teachers and researchers provide a rich context for professional learning (cf. Goldman et al., 2022). These collaborations often extend over periods of time and involve multiple opportunities for feedback and reflection (Gomoll et al., 2022; Ko et al., 2022). In these partnerships, the teacher and researcher act as thought partners for each other, exploring various perspectives as they grapple with problems of practice and devise ways to address them through the design of instruction (Lawton-Stickler & Bodamer, 2016). These efforts are closely tied to the teacher’s classroom and students and provide ongoing opportunities for professional learning, especially when the relationships are founded on trust and where the teacher and researcher are on equal footing (Henrick et al., 2017; Ko et al., 2022). However, little research has examined how relationships between researchers and practitioners develop and evolve into productive collaborations that advance both teachers’ goals and the aims of the research project. This case study adds to our understanding by examining how a veteran English teacher and a researcher, working as thought partners in an RPP, co-designed classroom instruction and engaged in reflection cycles that led to meaningful changes in teaching practice and student engagement.

## Participants, data sources, and analysis

Author 2 teaches 9th and 10th grade students in a district with a diverse student body (56% Hispanic, 37% White, 3% Asian, 2% Black, 1% American Indian, 1% two or more races; 52% free and  reduced lunch). She had been teaching for 22 years, 14 at her current school, when she started working with the project. For two of the 14 years at her current school, Author 2 worked as an instructional coach, but moved back into the classroom preferring to work as a teacher. She has National Board Certification, a voluntary and advanced credential in the US. During the data collection for this study, Author 1 worked as a researcher on an interdisciplinary team  that worked with 1777 teachers in math, science, and E nglish Language Arts (ELA) in both middle and high schools  to promote productive disciplinary discourse in classrooms. Author 1 worked primarily with high school English teachers collaboratively designing instruction, observing implementations, and reflecting with teachers. years: video and field notes from 15 classroom observation; audio and video recordings of ~75 teacher-researcher meetings of varying lengths; copies of assignments and other classroom artifacts; and audio and field notes from four PD sessions led by the instructional coaches at Author 2’s school. At the end of the two years, the teacher and researcher met to collaboratively reflect on their work together. Both the teacher and researcher perceived the co-design collaboration to have been particularly productive, so the two decided to dig deeper to understand why. They started by separately reviewing  various data sources  and creating narratives of the evolution of their co-design collaboration over the two years. They then responded in writing to each other’s narratives, considering additional points, questions, and elaborations. This back-and-forth contin ued in writing and meetings  across several weeks. As they examined their narratives, i t became clear that there were three critical events that they both agreed had impacted the trajectory of their work. Mertova and Webster (2019) conceptualize a critical event as an event that “reveals a change of understanding or worldview by the storyteller…It is almost always a change experience, and it can only ever be identified afterwards” (pp 73-74). In the present case, the significance of these events emerged at the end of their work together as they narrated and reflected on their shared experiences. Critical events disrupt one’s prior understanding, leading to a shift in perspective that shapes subsequent experiences, interpretations, and actions. Therefore, we consider these events learning experiences that shaped the development of this productive teacher-researcher partnership.

The three critical events that the teacher and researcher considered consequential to the development of their relationship were the following: the first collaborative reflection on the video, mentioned by both the teacher and researcher in their initial narratives; the researcher’s analysis of a list of discussion questions used in a classroom activity, initially mentioned by the teacher; and the teacher’s identification of teaching writing as a key problem area for her, initially mentioned by the researcher. Thematic analysis (Braun & Clark, 2012) indicated several common features of these events: they were classroom-based; the focal problems were relevant to the teacher’s practice; and the teacher and researcher agreed that the instructional practices in use were the source of the problems and addressing them involved redesigning the instruction. Critical events First video co-reflection Prior to the initial classroom observation, neither the teacher nor the researcher had expectations of what would come of the observation or where it might lead. When the teacher volunteered to open her classroom to the researcher, the researcher’s only pu rpose was to observe some classroom discussion as a follow-up to working with the coaches on the PD  that the teacher had participated in the prior year. During the initial classroom observation, the teacher had the students work in small groups to read and analyze a poem. The teacher, researcher, and a videographer walked around observing the groups as they worked. After the class, the teacher indicated that it had gone poorly because none of the students “got” the poem. She blamed her choice of poem, which she was using for the first time. The researcher, on the other hand, was impressed with how lively the student discussions were as they struggled to get at the meaning of the poem. When they watched the video together a day later, the teacher was surprised to see how much deep thinking was happening and how the students challenged each other during their small group discussions. During their collaborative viewing of and reflection on the video, the teacher and researcher entertained the possibility that it was the lack of transparency in the poem’s meaning that afforded multiple perspectives on its meaning, thereby supporting the lively student discussions. In other words, the student challenging texts would likely prompt richer discussion and negotiation of meaning, whereas easier texts might result in quicker consensus and limited dialogue among students.  This was the first of many post-observation reflective discussions that were deeply rooted in what was observed and experienced during the lessons by both the teacher and researcher. It also established a cycle of classroom observation-reflection-redesign that continued throughout their work together. The proximity to the classroom and relevance  to practice of their work allowed for exploration of issues ranging from grading and rubrics to discussion questions and writing prompts. 1778 One focus of the RPP was on supporting disciplinary classroom discussion. The teacher did not perceive this as a major problem in her classroom in that getting her students to talk was rarely a problem. The researcher agreed that there was plenty of discussion in the classroom and much of it was productive. However, during one class in which students were given about 10 cards with discussion questions to talk about  in small groups, the researcher noticed a lot of variability in the types of questions the teacher was asking students to address. The researcher decided to make a table analyzing the questions. Her analysis addressed the following for each question: 1) What will students discuss? What kinds of knowledge might be brought into the discussion?  2) Is there room for productive disagreement? 3) Could this be a deep disciplinary discussion rooted in the text? The researcher shared this analysis with the teacher, who viewed it as an opportunity to reframe her classroom practice. As she put it, By applying this lens, [Author 1] invited me to raise my teacher-game in a few important ways: … [She] showed me that there was something to be gained from looking closely at the kinds of questions I was asking… In my attempt to make good "talking" questions (juicy, controversial, given to different opinions), the questions were verging on “Oprah book-club” territory… I remember [Author 1] pointed out that the text was non-chronologically organized. Students had already noticed this and complained about it being confusing. As a result, I decided to ask them what impact this text structure had on the novel, both in terms of the advantages and disadvantages for the reader. Why do authors write that way at all? Although the teacher had never considered her discussion questions problematic, the researcher’s analysis of the questions prompted her to recognize  the need to be more mindful of how the questions invoked principles and practices of literary analysis and supported students in digging into the literary mechanisms present in the text and engaging in reasoning practices reflective of literary analysis.  In other words, the researcher’s analysis helped the teacher move beyond just wanting students to talk to provoking deep disciplinary thinking and reasoning. Teaching writing During the summer between the two years they worked together, the researcher asked the teacher if there were any specific problems she wanted to focus on in the coming year. After some thought, t he teacher indicated that she strongly disliked teaching writing despite the development of writing skills being the district’s primary goal for 10 th grade English. She admitted that she dreaded having to read and grade her students’ essays. Together the teacher and researcher examined previous writing assignments and explored what the teacher liked or did not like about those assignments and the resulting student products. Throughout the months  of meetings and classroom implementations, they came up with various ways to redesign the writing tasks and classroom instruction around them to better support student writing and ma ke reading and grading the essays less problematic for the teacher. One change they made was to the prompt for a personal narrative writing assignment. Rather than using a formulaic prompt and model (e.g., Write a “This I Believe” essay), they developed a more open-ended prompt that allowed students to draw on their own experiences more broadly and develop narrative essays that were more original in both style and content. Another example was changing  two writing assignments  she gave around a novel: one to analyze the function of a character and the other to write an argument about book banning using sources. These two assignments only partially aligned with district curricular goals, the written products were repetitive and uninspired, and students  showed minimal interest, putting little effort into writing them. The co-design of new prompts centered on addressing these issues. Instead of the analysis of a character, they co-designed a task that allowed students to analyze and refute an argument presented in the novel. Not only did this align better with the district curriculum, but it also gave the students deeper insight into the world of the novel. The second change was in making the argument about banning books a group project, in which students developed a presentation arguing their point and drawing on outside sources. Students were more engaged in both tasks, and the resulting products were more varied and interesting. In both cases, the collaborative co-design led to tasks and instruction that were more meaningful for students and produced writing that the teacher found more engaging. Themes across critical events Through analysis and reflection on these critical events, the teacher and researcher identified several interrelated themes underlying their work: close ties to the teacher’s practice and classroom, focal problems were consequential for teacher-recognized problems of practice, and locating the problem in instruction rather than in the students. Of primary importance were  the close ties to the teacher’s practice and classroom. The classroom observations and collaborative reflection led to uncovering problems and crafting solutions that were specific to her practice and her students. This enabled the co-design work to center problems that mattered in the teacher’s classroom and  therefore from the teacher’s perspective were valuable to address. The co-design process itself 1779 reflected valuing and careful consideration of the multiple perspectives the teacher and researcher brought to the work. They were therefore able to explore various ways of defining as well as addressing the problem. Their joint approach to analyzing and addressing problems of practice they identified was to define the problem as emanating from the instructional practices rather than in the abilities of the students. For the teacher, this shared perspective on students as capable of the work contrasted with what the teacher perceived as a more deficit-than asset-based orientation in the school-sponsored PD and instructional coaching. As she put it, At times, they [instructional coaches] approach issues with the assumption that if students are not completing a task, it must be because they lack the skills, as though motivation had nothing to do with it. The coaching strategy is to “break the task down” into sometimes artificial “steps,” and then teach and model those steps… In some way, this approach exacerbates the problem, because by breaking down the thing [students] can actually do if they wanted to, the task gets less meaningful and more decontextualized. So, the approach, in some cases, may be making the problem worse. It reveals a deficit view and assumes the problem is with the kid (he doesn’t know how to discuss) and not with the adult (the prompt stinks, the text is boring, etc.) It is interesting that, looking back, most of the work we [teacher and researcher] did probably fell under the category of making the assessments more meaningful and aligning them better with standards: not breaking tasks down into smaller parts.

This shared approach to analyzing and addressing problems of practice led to productive changes in both design of instruction and classroom implementation. The co-design work assumed that students were perfectly capable of engaging in deep literary analysis. Indeed, the co-design work led to increasing the difficulty level of tasks while at the same time increasing student engagement.  Both the teacher and researcher perceived the co-design as having been highly productive of changes in teaching practices that deepened students’ engagement in literary The present case study adds to the growing body of research on teacher learning and professional growth in the workplace. It reifies the importance of meeting teachers where they are by focusing on problems of practice that emanate from their day-to-day work in classrooms and that they regard as worth addressing (Ball & Cohen 1999; Borko et al., 2010).  It also highlights the importance of positioning students as agentive in their learning and capable of engaging in developmentally appropriate disciplinary p ractices, provided appropriate instructional supports are in place. Teachers who view students as incapable often redesign instruction by lowering the cognitive difficulty of a task (Bartell et al., 2017; Jackson et al., 2017); however, in this case tasks were redesigned to make them more meaningful to students  and to deepen their engagement in literary reasoning and writing. In other words, the co-design in this case did not “dumb down” tasks; it built on assets students brought and helped them marshal ways of knowing in everyday life to tackle academic tasks (Lee, 2007). It is also important to point out that the co-design case reported here reflects other qualities of successful professional learning, such as sustained duration, collaboration, and ongoing opportunities for feedback and reflection (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017; Desimone & Pak, 2017; Horn & Little, 2010). These characteristics were afforded by the opportunities provided by the RPP in creating a context for ongoing cycles of co-design. Finally, this case study points to the importance of providing teachers with opportunities to reflect on their practice over time and in collaboration with other s (Hall & Goldman, 2022). The perspectives that surface in such contexts  are proving to be critically instrumental in propelling changes in teaching practices that foster instructional agency for teachers, enabling them to support student agency in disciplinary learning.
