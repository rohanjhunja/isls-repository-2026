# Unpacking Paradox in Co-Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Here we examine “paradox,” a particular kind of contradiction inherent in participatory design and co-design. As with any perceived contradiction in human experience, paradoxes may urgently demand resolution, thereby motivating and orienting learning and changemaking through design. However, resolving a paradox involves grappling with an apparent antinomy between equally sound, justified or valued perspectives, processes or solutions. This may drive very rational, inclusive and well-structured co-design processes towards activities and outcomes that may seem irrational, ironically counterproductive and even absurd. We examine examples of paradox in participatory/co-design, showing how participants grapple with paradox in different ways, and how unpacking “paradox in practice” helps us understand (and potentially avoid) undesirable ends. We make the case that attention to paradox is critical for equitable design.

## Introduction

Participatory approaches to the design for learning  and changemaking are fundamental to equity-oriented design and research in the learning sciences. And yet, the y inevitably invite deep internal complexity and co ntradiction among participants and stakeholders. They play out among broader social, cultural and historical conte xts that entangle them in other tensions and issues of non-coherence. Bringing together multiple stakeholders inevitably forces designers and researchers to navigate unexpe cted tensions that often do not exist or are often ignored in normative hierarchical design. In order to act equitably through co-design, we need to develop ways of surfacing, understanding and responding to these diverse tensions. This paper focuses simultaneously on the authentic value of co-design as a way of organizing equitable collective learning and change, while also to the fact that ce rtain kinds of contradictions at the heart of co-design – paradoxes – may persistently subvert outcomes or make co-design counterproductive.

Theoretical perspective Learning scientists have long focused on contradict ions and tensions in theorizing learning, design and human activity. From canonical sociocultural and cultural-historical perspectives, contradictions in object-oriented activity direct and drive cyclical developmental activity towards their resolution (En geström, 1987). Collective processes of participatory design and co-design are theorized as a collective journey throug h a zone of proximal development, aiming for the resolution of contradictions (Wells, 2011). Such fundamentally Vygotskyan notions of contradiction often – but not always – assume that the goal of developmental activity is t o resolve these contradictions (White, 2014). However, some contradictions which clearly demand  action, do not necessarily demand or make possible a solution. Contemporary dialogic (e.g. Bakhtin, 1981; Matusov, 2011) and postmodern (e.g. Law et al., 2014)  perspectives on learning and social change emphasiz e becoming in tension, without resolution. Such perspectives have gained recognition – e.g. dialogic pedagogy (Matusov, 2011). Here we look outside the field of the learning scie nces to organizational and management studies, importing their concept of paradox in organizing and decision-making, a specific type of contradiction that defies resolution, in order to examine breakdowns and coun terproductive outcomes in co-design. Putnam et al. (2016) define paradoxes as “contradictions that persist over time, impose and reflect back on each other, and develop into seemingly irrational or absurd situations because t heir continuity creates  situations in which options appear mutually exclusive, making choices among them diffi cult” (p. 72). Such challenges canderail design pr ocesses unless ways of understanding and managing paradoxes are developed. Putnam and colleagues advise researchers to "sharpen their definitions" (p. 132) of different t ypes of contradictions – including paradoxes – and connect their conceptualizations to the broader metatheories that shape key organizing processes. In the learning sciences, such key processes include de sign, learning, and schooling. Therefore, building on current metatheoretical perspectives in the learning sciences that already structure and justify participatory approaches to design, we want to explore paradoxes in and through design, refinin g our understanding of them, how they manifest in p ractice, and what we cando to engage with, if not resolve them.

## Data and method

Data for this paper are drawn from two different qu alitative interpretive studies carried out independ ently by the first and second authors. The first study was an inter view-based study that deployed a cultural-historical activity theory framework (Engeström, 1987) to analyze tensions in teacher and entrepreneur narratives about design and changemaking in schools (Papendieck and Hughes, 2022). The second study focused on undergraduate co-design of university curriculum, redesigning the curriculum to better center equity and practice. Researchers of study two used frameworks of agency, identity development, po sitioning, and power (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Colli ns & Bilge, 2016; Nasir, 2012; Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2024) to examine processes and outcomes in co-design. While drawing on different methods and theoretical frameworks, both of these studies carefully attended to the presence and role “tensions” experienced by designers in practice. This created a point of comp arison for productive analysis of different kinds of contradictions. The research team discussed cases and findings from each of their studies, re-interpreting them in the context of their reading o n paradox. Through this process, they identified different kinds of paradoxes in their collective data on tension in design. They focused on contradictions that were characterized by: 1) antinomy, 2) persist ence, and 3) a tendency to drive design in very rat ional ways towards irrational or counterproductive ends. By iteratively discussing cases of paradox, the authors selected cases that illuminated the different ways that paradox operates and must be accommodated in co-design.

## Findings and interpretation

Here we use two qualitative interpretive studies to explore the different ways that researchers can examine paradox in design processes. We have identified two broad categor ies of paradox that existed across both studies: 1)  The “Use-Exchange Value Paradox” of Design and 2) The “Structure-Agency Paradox” of Design. In the former, there is a paradox between the use of the design product and the exchange value more broadly across contexts. In the latter, there is a paradox betwe en the authority, agency, and p rivileged status of participants within the design process compared to their roles and status outside the design context, often where the designed produc t is implemented.

The “use-exchange value paradox” of design Here, the participants are grappling with a common paradox in design and innovation: Developing educat ional products with sufficient exchange value on the market is often counterproductive in terms of the use value of that product for the stakeholders and participants in de sign. This persistent contradiction between use and  exchange value leads to participatory design processes that may be exploitative rather than empowering, a disti nctly ironic and counterproductive outcome of a critical participatory process. Teachers, for instance, may be engaged by design fi rms to provide insights and use-cases which inform the design of products that are characterized by bl oated featurism rather than practical use value. In  study one, Kyle, a professional designer, described  routinely seeking out and engaging teachers in the iterative design of a classroom messaging system he was developing as a startup activity. Teacher involvement in design was important in understanding their day-to-day problems, the context in which they taught, and  how the messaging system could present new value to them in their work. Howe ver, when taking the product to market and seeking investment for scaling, Kyle described having to na vigate difficult personal tensions and contradictio ns among educational, social and financial goals: “There's shareho lder value or company value over user value. And ho w do you balance those together to make them worthwhi le for people like myself and other entrepreneurs, to make it worth our time and money to work on thi ngs for users in education and kids and families --we need return on our money.” Kyle felt tremendous pressure to add marketable fea tures to his product demanded by investors in his pilot, largely useless features which he felt enhanced its market value and their return on investment, but decreased its use value to teachers with whom and for whom he  designed the system. He said “I don't want to add features or functionality, or pollute the strength of my product to families, to teachers and kids, to either earn more money or to partner with someone else that doesn't make s ense. Or have the business pressure that [distracts  from] our main mission.” While Kyle resisted this pressure, o ther entrepreneurial designers do not. His story he lps us understand the “use-exchange value paradox” in practice, and why many p articipatory processes of ed ucational design ultimately deploy the labor of teachers to produce products that are not useful to them. A similar experience can occur when involving youth /student in co-design efforts (study two). Youth and student co-design bridges learners' experiences, perspectives and expertise to that of the expectations designed into the educational program (Pitt, et al., 2021; Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2021). Howeve r, broadly speaking, the goal of co-design has historically been to finalize or validate the usability of a product (Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2021, 2024). Typically, with this goal, youth co-designers are involved as end-users, helping to create a more appealing product for youth consumers. In this way, the goal of co-design has been to finalize and/or validate an end product (e.g. curriculum). However, the proc ess of co-design creates a unique opportunity to accurately reflect the needs, identities, and histories of lea rners and, at the same time, challenge implicit hie rarchical structures and inequities embedded in curriculum design and products (Iversen, Smith, & Dindler, 2017; Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2024). Youth/student co-design thus often embodies a paradox of simultaneou sly validating youth voice, seeking their perspectives on designed  products, yet, with goals of exchange value, conti nually positioning youth solely as the recipients of produ cts (curriculum, games, etc.). In this way, youth a re often involved in design either as tokenism of youth invo lvement or to increase the market value of the prod uct being designed, in both cases exploiting youth for what they represent and not for their potentially valuable perspectives as designers.

The “structure-agency paradox” of design In contrast to The “Use-Exchange Value Paradox” of Design where the paradox ultimately undermines the value of engaging participants in design, The “Structure-Agency Paradox” of Design looks at the paradox of participants' value being bounded within the design process, creating unintended longer-term consequences. In study one, teachers often found the individual a gency required to design educational innovations by operating in special technical or curricular spaces, such as gifted programs, makerspaces, “enrichment” curricula, grant-funded projects, and external collaborations. Withi n the context of a school, these “ privileged innovators” (Papendieck and Hughes, 2022) operated with either tacit or explicit support of a uthorities and managers in their schools. Describing her position as a privileged in novator, Ariana – a makerspace teacher in a gifted program – said: I was a little bit ahead of the curve. [My principa l] was very much like me when she was a classroom teacher. She and I worked well together, me being a gifted teacher, her being a classroom teacher, so she's very supportive of what I'm doing. She's behind what I d o 100%, and she just is sort of like, ‘Go do whatever. Just go. Just go. Just don't break any laws. Go.’ Critically, these teachers often reported being vex ed by tensions that arose from this type of privile ged design authority, particularly in relation to their collea gues who were not afforded the same freedom to desi gn and experiment with new teaching methods. Ariana noted that she had to be careful about her special position: If I have to ask her [my principal] for permission,  then she'll have to tell me, ‘You have to do lesson plans like everybody else. You have to do this.’ But if it's more on the level of, ‘Just go. I trust you. I know what you're doing. Go do it,’ t hen it's better. You don't want everybody to know… I am isolated, but partly that's because I do n't tell everybody at my school that I don't have to do lesson plans. They all have to do lesson plans. Ultimately, Ariana said she felt isolated, and like  she did not fit well in school. Her story reveals that even when teacher design agency is officially endorsed and pr ivileged, it can still result in a form of tension and isolation within an authoritarian  and hierarchical school environment. Her story help s us crack open and unpack the Structure-Agency Paradox of Design, revealing how structural empowerment of teachers as designers and changemakers can, in practice, work against their agency and belonging in schools. In study two of this paper, the Structure-Agency Paradox occurs within student co-design, where students are invited to collaboratively design in order to i ncorporate student perspectives into the design of the course, however student voice dissipates once the designed product is taken from the design process and implemented. In this case the paradox occurs because of the bounded  nature of the co-design, with students’ roles and access to consequential decision making within the co-design process, but minim al or nonexistent outside of it, thus ultimately undermining the intended goals of including student co-design. Within the co-design process, students, with facilitator guidance, had opportunities to map out the concepts of the Adolescent Development course and wrestle with how the course should best center equity. Once the co-design team completed the redesign, one of the students (as a research assistant) worked with the facilitator to rewrite the course objectives. At this point student involvement in the redesigned course ended. The redesigned course was then taken and modified by multiple faculty and the redesigned curriculum was impacted by university structures that included needing to keep an agreed upon core assignment (that was less aligned with the redesigned course) and the difficulty of implementing many of the co-design team’s work in asynchronous cours es (which increased in number soon after the redesign was com pleted). While some of the co-design team’s ideas were implemented, students’ bounded agency within the co-design process and the impact of external structure s highlight the structure-agency paradox that can occur within student and youth co-design. Outside of the bounds of co-design, the overarching structures perpetuate normative power dynamics, in which students have little to no roles or voice, creating a paradox that ultimately, and unintentionally, can undermine the initial goals of including students in co-design.

## Significance

Participatory and co-design methods are rightly understood as key to cri tical changemaking in education. As a family of methodological approaches deployed across the learn ing sciences, they are invaluable for developing more just, equitable and dignity-affirming educational experiences and environments.  However, design is complex, and participatory processes invite even mo re complexity, and sometimes even principled, well-organized processes of inclusive design and co-design lead to surprisingly counterproductive outcomes. The cases above show one way of adapting the notion of paradox – a contradiction characterized by antinomy, persiste nce and a tendency to drive design in very rational ways towards irrational or counterproductive ends – to identify and examine how such ironic, counterintuitive and undesirable outco mes emerge in design practices and contexts. In investigating paradoxes more closely, we can begin to understand and grapple with some of these paradoxes, and understand how to balance and respond to persistent antinomies in design, like structure and agency, and use and exchange value. We see value in further investigati ng not just paradox, linking the concept more close ly with dialogic and dialectic understandings of contradict ion that are commonly deployed by Learn ing scientists. Branching out from early cultural-historical work on levels of contradiction (i.e. primary to quat ernary; Engeström, 1987) we also see value in describing wider varieties tensions, typologies of noncoherence, and modes of grappling with them (e.g. Law et al., 2014). We think that by “sharpening our definitions” of the contradictions at the heart of design and learning, we can make better, more reliable progress in organizing for better futures.
