# Parental Multimodal Revoicing: Guiding Children in Computational Thinking Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As family participation in early computational thinking (CT) grows, understanding how parents without technical expertise act as learning partners is essential. This case study investigates how a mother without a STEM background used multimodal revoicing to support co-learning of CT with children during a summer camp with tangible robotics kits. Drawing on interaction analysis of three excerpts from a 64-minute video session, the study shows how revoicing, combining speech, gesture, and material manipulation, functioned as a flexible scaffolding practice that shifted across phases of the activity. Finding indicates that multimodal revoicing sustained attention, clarified instructions, bridged symbolic reasoning with hands-on exploration, and helped the parent to consolidate her own understanding. W hen it became overextended or misaligned with children’s focus, however, engagement declined and opportunities for self-correction narrowed. Extending sociocultural perspectives on scaffolding, the study highlights multimodal revoicing as a powerful resource for inclusive family-based CT education when attending to children.

Computational thinking (CT), defined as the systematic analysis, exploration, and testing of solutions to openended problems (Wing, 2011), is increasingly positioned as a foundational literacy for children’s participation in a digital society. In early childhood, CT is typically introduced through tangible toys and unplugged activities that fit young children’s developmental strengths (Bers, 2020). These benefits are often amplified in family contexts, where sustained parent–child interaction shapes early learning, play routines, and technology use (Lavigne et al., 2023; Xing, 2025). Reviews of CT learning increasingly highlight parents as facilitators, collaborators, and colearners who shape children’s engagement and opportunities to reason computationally (Cai et al., 2023; Campana et al., 2024). Yet we still know relatively little about how parents, especially those without STEM or coding supports matters both for designing more usable, family-oriented CT experiences and for equity: families differ in time, access to programs and materials, and opportunities for informal STEM participation, shaping who can benefit from early CT learning.

One promising lens for examining these micro-moments is revoicing. In classroom discourse research, revoicing refers to repeating, paraphrasing, or expanding a learner’s contribution to acknowledge ideas, clarify meaning, and guide participation (O’Connor & Michaels, 1996). Socioculturally, revoicing is a form of mediated action: an adult takes up a child’s emerging idea and re-presents it in ways that support shared understanding and position the child as an author of meaningful contributions within a participation framework (Vygotsky, 1978; Goffman, 1974, 1981; O’Connor & Michaels, 2019). While initially conceptualized as a verbal strategy, the concept has expanded to include embodied elements in practice. Shein (2012) broadened revoicing from a purely verbal act to a multimodal interaction, where talk and gesture are intertwined in the re-utterance of the child’s contribution in math class. Building on this, Flood (2018) frames multimodal revoicing as a co-operative action that integrates verbal and embodied resources to build intersubjectivity and guide children in organizing experiences in culturally specific ways. In early childhood CT education, multimodal revoicing enables educators to attend and respond to children’s embodied ideas during tangible programming activities (Flood et al., 2022). These perspectives are especially relevant for early CT. With young children, CT commonly takes tangible, hands-on ways, such as using coding robots, manipulatives, or tangible interfaces  that are developmentally appropriate and engaging (Bers, 2020). As children engage in programming or debugging, they externalize their thinking through gestures, manipulation of materials, and trial-and-error behaviors (Yang, 2024). These embodied expressions offer valuable entry points for adult interpretation and pedagogical support  (Wang et al., 2021). In this context, multimodal revoicing serves as a key pedagogical tool that enables adults to recognize and extend children’s embodied ideas. By transforming children’s physical actions into verbal or ges tural representations using CT language, adults help make implicit strategies explicit and foster conceptual clarity (Vartiainen et al., 2020).  For example, Flood et al. (2022) observed preschool teachers reformulated children’s gestures by focusing, recontextualizing, and decontextualizing them to support CT learning practices, suggesting that CT practices can be embodied and co-constructed. Emerging evidence suggests similar discursive work occurs in family learning, where parents rephrase prompts, clarif y steps, and sustain activity flow in informal 1089 maker contexts (Zimmerman et al., 2022). However, systematic analysis of parental revoicing, especially its multimodal forms entangling speech, gesture, and materials, remains limited. This study addresses that gap through a case study of one family participating in a CT summer camp, focusing on a mother’s parental multimodal revoicing: a form of verbal mediation that combines revoicing with embodied action (e.g., pointing, demonstrating, manipulating materials) to co-construct meaning with children during tangible coding activities. By analyzing the functions, forms, and effects of parental multimodal revoicing in CT co-learning sessions, this study deepens our understanding of how parents support CT learning in everyday contexts and informs the design of family-oriented CT learning experiences. The two research questions are: (1) How does this parent incorporate multimodal revoicing to support CT co-learning with children through tangible CT toys? (2) What impact does such revoicing have on children’s learning processes? This case study draws on data from the 2023 “Learn CT Together” summer camp (Xing, 2025), a five-week program hosted at a nonprofit urban center in the Northeastern U.S. Two families participated in nine sessions that introduced core CT concepts (e.g., sequencing, looping) using Thames & Kosmos Coding and Robotics (TKCR) kits. Families solved coding challenges by assembling arrow cards to program a participant-built robot to move across a grid of map cards. All family sessions, each lasting 50–90 minutes, were video recorded, resulting in approximately 19 hours of footage.

The focal family (n=4) compris es Mary (a White mother with no formal STEM background) and their two adopted African Americandaughters, Kay (age 5) and Mia (age 6). This family was selected because Mary frequently and flexibly used revoicing while co-working with her children. For in-depth microanalysis, Session 3 (64 minutes) was selected because it captured Mary’s first encounter with the kit’s number cards and the idea of loops, offering an analytic window into how parents new to CT learn the toy’s logic while scaffolding children’s understanding.

Guided by revoicing frameworks (O’Connor & Michaels, 1996; Zimmerman et al., 2022), I first deductively identified moments when Mary revoiced the instructor’s or children’s verbal/embodied contributions, then inductively developed emergent categories and relationships. Eleven episodes (3:1–3:11) were categorized by the prompt source (Table 1). Interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) was conducted on three representative excerpts centered on number cards. Although number cards are not themselves a CT concept, they materially instantiate abstraction (compressing repeated steps) and algorithms (pairing a function card with a repeat value). Transcripts captured speech, gesture, gaze, and object manipulation using conventions adapted from Jeffersonian transcription (Flood, 2021). Multimodal revoicing was treated as an embodied mediation practice that (a) positions learners as authors, (b) coordinates talk– body–objects to make thinking visible across agents, and (c) progressively formalizes everyday sense-making into shared disciplinary understandings. Excerpt 1: Revoicing between children and the instructor as a mediator Excerpt 1 takes place during the initial introduction of number cards in coding (gray cards labeled with specific numbers), which illustrate abstraction by shortening coding sequences. Mia placed the number card three after the second straight card  (as shown in Fig. c)  and then test ed it, expecting the robot to move straight five times (=2+3) to reach the targeting place on the map  (initial goal of the challenge). Out of her expectation, the robot only moved straight four times, where excerpt 1 starts. Excerpt 1 Understanding How the Number Card Works (S3:1 6:48-7:22) In Excerpt 1, Mia’s confusion (“Mommy:: What happened?”) initiated a repair sequence in which Mary acted as a learner-mediator. She revoiced Mia’s vague question into a targeted inquiry to the instructor—“So does this not move it three spots up?” —while pointing to the number card (lines 2–3). When the instructor’s initial explanation remained indirect, Mary “thought aloud” through counting and sequential pointing, making her reasoning public (lines 6–9, see Fig. a); Kay contributed “Go three times.” The instructor then moved into the family’s ecological huddle (Goffman, 2008) and provided an embodied demonstration by pointing to the cards and moving the cheese manipulative to emphasize that the number card functions at the “and THEN” step (lines 11–15, see Fig. b). Mary consolidated this multimodal explanation with a brief summary and a negative contrast (“It doesn’t mean…three more times”), then shifted into a teaching role by translating the idea into child-friendly language and gestures (“This and this are directly connected”), explicitly surfacing the key rule that the number card modifies only the immediately preceding command.

Analytically, Mary’s multimodal revoicing unfolded across three linked layers: interrogative revoicing that transformed a breakdown into a clear question; self-explanatory revoicing that externalized her sensemaking through coordinated speech– gesture–materials; and translational revoicing that stabilized the rule for children through simplified phrasing, repetition, and deictic pointing. In doing so, she functioned as a “learning broker” (Zimmerman et al., 2022), bridging instructional discourse and child-c entered sensemaking despite limited 1091 technical background. Children’s participation was differentiated: Mia stayed visually and physically engaged, while Kay engaged peripherally—tracking the demonstration, briefly contributing, and then returning to independent coding—suggesting that the mat erial-rich, multimodal interaction supported both direct and observational learning (Wang et al., 2021).

Excerpt 2: Revoicing the child’s coding After Excerpt 1, Mia temporarily abandoned number cards and successfully solved the challenge with five straight cards; Kay also succeeded. When the instructor prompted them to reintroduce number cards (“Where should we place the number cards?” “How many times does it repeat?”), Mia focused on repetition but omitted the required straight/arrow card (Fig. d), triggering Excerpt 2.

Excerpt 2 Scaffolding the Coding Process (S3:2 9:38-9:54) In Excerpt 2, Mary’s scaffolding shift ed from direct correction to recasting. She first identified the missing step (“It needs an arrow first”) and explained why the robot could not act without a directional command. She then reformulated Mia’s partial code into an algorithmic sequence: the number card (“five”) only works in relation to an arrow card, “first read the arrow, then repeat it five times,” aligning with multimodal revoicing that transforms children ’s embodied attempts into more disciplinary forms (Flood, 2018). As she spoke, Mary physically inserted the missing arrow card (Fig. d→e), a directive intervention that can reduce opportunities for child-led self-correction (Sobel et al., 2021). Yet the interaction remained productive: Mia paused to think, closely tracked Mary’s revision, and immediately tested the revised program, while Kay learned peripherally by watching and later applying the number card correctly.

Together, this excerpt shows how multimodal revoicing can turn a procedural error into a brief debugging lesson: Mary made the breakdown visible (no arrow, no action), stabilized the pairing rule (number modifies the prior action), and foregrounded how the robot interprets commands sequentially, supporting the development of algorithmic reasoning central to CT.

This case shows that parental revoicing in CT activities is more than repeating children’s talk: it is an embodied pedagogical strategy that coordinates speech, gesture, and materials to sustain attention, connect symbolic code to physical action, and deepen engagement with CT ideas. Multimodal revoicing served a dual purpose: helping parents consolidate their own understanding while scaffolding children’s learning to create a shared, artifact-mediated zone for sensemaking. At the same time, when revoicing became overextended or misaligned with children’s cues, it reduced opportunities for self-correction and exploration, underscoring the need for flexible responsiveness in the moment.

The findings also reinforce the value of material-rich environments: number cards, the robot, and manipulatives anchored explanations,  shaped gestures, and supported both direct and peripheral participants. Importantly, parents without coding expertise could still act as effective learning brokers, suggesting that scaffolding depends less on technical knowledge than on embodied responsiveness and relational coordination. Although based on a single case, this study suggests CT programs should position parents as co-learners, design tasks that invite multimodal interaction and debugging, and attend to peer dynamics in family participation.
