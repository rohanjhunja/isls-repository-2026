# Student Perceptions of Learning Supports in an Educational Game

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research examined middle school student feedback about the design components that helped them learn from an educational game centered on science learning about predator-prey relationships. Students frequently cited interspersed instructional content (delivered as comics), authentic visual media, and interactive game play as driving their learning. Results suggest that students perceive value in engaging instructional content that serves to reinforce game learning and respond positively to authentic content integrated into gamified experiences.

## Introduction: Educational games for science learning

Previous work has focused on establishing the efficacy of educational games (de Freitas, 2018), including benefits to cognitive and motivational aspects of student learning (Kalogiannakis et al., 2021). Other research has explored the impact of educational game components on student learning outcomes  (Chen, 2020), with some evidence suggesting that embedded prompts and feedback support learning from games (Law & Chen, 2016). However, less is known about how students themselves perceive the value of varied features in educationa l game s. We examined students’ feedback about their learning experiences with an educational game implemented for learning about predatory-prey relationships in middle school science.

## Method

Data was analyzed from 387 student surveys completed by sixth graders afte r completing a science learning activity focused on an educational game; 288 free text responses contained sufficient content for coding. Students were distributed across seven teachers and seven schools in the Western United States (including 2 rural schools and 2 Title I schools). One school had high enrollment of underrepresented students (greater than 50%); two schools had substantial enrollment of economically disadvantaged students (40% or greater). ## Results

Students most frequently cited the educational comi cs as helping them learn (32.3%), followed by the digitized specimens (28.1%), and game play interactions (24.3 %). A small percentage of students  cited game feedback (10.4%), with rare mentions of reflection questions (4.2%) and game scoring (4.2%).

## Conclusions

Students most frequently cited the multimedia instructional content that supported the game (i.e., instructional comics) as helping them learn, noting that the engaging comics helped them understand the underlying science of the game. Students also frequently cited the visual  media (i.e., images of real entomology specimens) as helpful, suggesting strong appetite for integrating authenti c content into simulated game experiences. Integrating game components that students perceive as valuable and e ngaging for learning may help developers create eff ective opportunities for learning with  educational gam es. S tudent feedback suggests potential value in prioritizing designs that balance interactive game play with compelling instructional materials and authentic visual media.
