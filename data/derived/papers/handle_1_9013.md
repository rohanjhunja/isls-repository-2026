### Abstract
Learning in and through fieldwork usually occurs as apprenticeship in a situated, real-life context. This Interactional Ethnography of an ecology fieldwork initiative in Hong Kong adopts a microethnographic discourse analysis (ME/DA) using video recordings from point-of-view (POV) glasses. Analysis of university ecologist and secondary biology student micro-interactions illustrates how the ecologist shuttled between two sociomaterial ‘presences’ weaving scientific reasoning with the physical manipulation of natural objects in the field.

## Introduction

Learning in and through fieldwork usually occurs as apprenticeship in a situated, real-life context. Students in the field encounter authentic problems across a range of tasks supporting development of expertise across knowledge, skills, and dispositions in a community of practice (CoP). Sørensen (2009) argued, however, that this traditional framework does not fully account for how learning transpires as “knowledge emerges at different places” due to “the relation between circulating humans and immobile references” (p. 180). Studies in the materiality of learning are examining interactional and spatial “heterogeno us assemblages” (Fenwick, 2015). This Interactional Ethnography (IE) addresses the 2022 ICLS call for “elucidating theories and processes of learning and teaching” with a microethnographic discourse analysis (ME/DA) of one ecology fieldwork session in Hong Kong involving secondary school students and university ecologists  (Green et al., 2020). Ecologists view the skill of  species identification as the basic prerequisite for understanding biodiversity and environmental issues; however, students can normally exhibit such skill only at higher taxo nomic levels, not at species levels (Randler, 2008). Mentally relaying species names and morphology is a key goal  for apprentice ecologists (Kontkanen et al., 2016). This study of the sociomateriality of fieldwork asks, how do university ecology experts circulate between materiality and scientific reasoning to facilitate students’ species identification in fieldwork and their thinking like ecologists?

## Methodology

IE is founded on sociocultural conceptualizations o f learning in situ. This ME/DA study adopted Sørensen’s

(2009) concept of materiality as a lens to examine the spatiality of learning and “sensitively account ” for the

multiplicity of “forms of knowledge and presence” i n a natural environment (p. 189). Analysis centers on an excerpt from the second of two field-trip components (mid-2021) in a place-based secondary biology initiative in Hong Kong. Field trips involved a rocky shore transect survey (~1 hour) at an intertidal section of a beach in close proximity to the school. Senior secondary students (n=21-22; 15-16 yrs) were grouped with 2-3 pre-assigned peers. Six university scientists specializing in rocky shore ecology (4 ecologists, 2 research assistants) led the fieldwork with two schoolteachers. Group materials included s ampling quadrats (1 gridded, 1 blank), clipboards, record sheets, a species ID sheet, a graphical food web, m agnifier, forceps, and small box. Participants cons ented to recording with four tripod-mounted static cameras at the backshore capturing transect group macro-interactions while personal, first-person point-of-view (POV) gl asses and audio pens worn by selected group members  and the university ecologists captured micro-interactions (~26 hrs total). Transcriptions (Cantonese to English) were reviewed by an interdisciplinary team of educationa lists and ecologists. An event map (Figure 1) with spatial mapping of macro-level geographical distributions traced the historical roots and trajectory of a telling case study of one university ecologist and a senior secondary student co-constructing knowledge for identification (Table 1).

## Analysis and results

The identified rich point (Table 1) illustrates a c omplex intertwining of talk and materiality. ME/DA unpacks synchronous, micro-level discourse and proxemics. I t begins at 26:11 when the Cantonese L1 ecologist (pseudonym Jenny) picks up an Echinolittorina radiata (Obj 1) within the quadrat and, holding it in her palm, asks a group member (pseudonym Kelvin, Cantonese L1) to identify. He offers an incorrect response, “塔結節濱 螺” (Echinolittorina malaccana). In the absence of a species ID sheet as a cognitive prompt (26:16), Jenny enters a deductive process to enunciate the differences between the two species of the same genus, initially by focusing on species-level features (surface colors of spires; aperture features; 26:18). She picks up a second organism (Obj ICLS2022 Proceedings 2040 © ISLS

2) located outside the quadrat as a contrasting example. Holding it in her fingertips in front of Kelvin, she points

out specific species features. When Kelvin incorrectly identifies the organism again, Jenny moves it closer to his face (26:48) asking him to carefully ‘read’ the key characteristic of Monodonta labio. Assessing that he was yet to attain the identification skill at species level, Jenny finally redirects him to find his species ID sheet. Adopting Sørensen’s (2009) framing of three forms of presence (authority-subject, collective, agent), the ‘authority-subject presence’ as delineated by norma tive teacher-student power relations and reliance o n a validated teaching instrument (species ID sheet) is  transformed into ‘agent’ presence through the rhyt hmic intertwining of progressively closer proxemics aligned with schema-structured discourse. By engaging the student in the situated, discursive process of connecting features of discovered field organisms (Objs 1 & 2) to the expert’s internal dichotomous schema for identification (Tab le 1; Figure 2), the ecologist facilitates the appr entice’s (re-)performance of knowledge in collaboration with  the expert (ibid). When this fails, she re-employs  the standardized picture-based identification key to support ‘imitation’ (26:51), which is less cognitively challenging. By repurposing the classroom object in the field, they return to an authority-subject presence. In the apprenticeship of ecology, fieldwork provide s the expert a familiar space for creating differen t presences to facilitate learning and to assess an a pprentice’s proximal development. This ME/DA tellin g case study illustrates an ecologist ‘shuttling’ between two sociomaterial ‘presences’ by weaving scientific reasoning discourse with the physical manipulation of natural  objects and learning artefacts in the field. It co ntributes to theorizing micro-processes of apprenticeship in eco logy and supports the inclusion of university scien tists in school-based fieldwork.
