# Actual and Vicarious Participation in Deliberative and Disputative Argumentation: Effects on Conceptual Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Theory and research suggests that deliberative argumentation is more likely to result in domain-specific content learning, compared to disputative argumentation. Yet, direct evidence is scarce. In the present work, we report on two controlled, randomized experiments to test these conjectures. In Study 1, undergraduate learners (N = 58) participated in a computer-mediated discussion with a disagreeing confederate on their understanding of a scientific concept they studied (natural selection). The peer confederate’s verbal behavior was scripted to evoke critical discourse on the explanations, while controlling for content, length and type of dialogue moves, but differing in discourse style (disputative/deliberative). In Study 2, learners (N = 88) read a disputative or deliberative discussion between two disagreeing learners. Results confirmed that deliberative argumentive discussion produced superior conceptual learning, both when learners participated in and when they read the discussion. Data on the learners' interaction perceptions provide some insights into reasons behind these differences.

## Theoretical background

In spite of some unavoidable differences in detail and in branding, there is considerable scholarly consensus about the type of argumentive dialogue that is both normatively desirable, as well as supports learning and development (e.g., Asterhan & Schwarz, 2007, 2009; Berland & Lee, 2012; Larrain et al., 2021; Nussbaum, 2009; Resnick et al., 2015; Reznitskaya & Wilkinson, 2017; Wegerif e t al., 1999): It is dialogue in which students refl ect upon, explain, and articulate their own thinking. They seek to clarify misunderstandings. They scrutinize, challenge and compare different ideas, but do so in a collaborative, supportive atmosphere. Thus, even though criticis m and challenges are an essential and even defining feature of argumentive discourse (van Eemeren et al., 1996; Walton 2006), models for productive classroom argumentatio n emphasize that these should be truth-centered (Reznitskaya & Wilkinson, 2017) and issue-driven, as opposed to person-driven (Keefer et al., 2001). The ultimate goal is collaborative, deliberative sense-making, as students search for the m ost reasonable answer (Reznitskaya & Wilkinson, 2017). This type of argumentive discourse  has been coined deliberative argumentation (Asterhan & Schwarz, 2016; Felton et al., 2009).

In contrast, instructions to discuss one's own perspective, solution or views with a disagreeing peer may also evolve into critical discussions that are void  of collaborative construction and exploration, but  characterized by interpersonal competition and persuasion. This d ebate-like type of dialogue has been termed disputative argumentation (Asterhan & Schwarz, 2016; Felton et al., 2009). Disputative and deliber ative discussions may include similar numbers of critical dialogue moves (e.g., critical questions, queries, rebuttals, counterarguments). In disputative argumentation, however, these are ma rked with distinctive rhetorical features that emph asize interpersonal repartition, ego-enhancement and/or an increase in face-threat during disagreements (Asterhan, 2013). Others have found that it is often characterized by shorter dialogue turns (Felton et al., 2015; Mercer, 1996) and by one-sided arguments that repetitively support one's own, original claims without dealing with the opponent's (Asterhan, 2013; Berland & Reiser, 2011; Felton et al., 2009). Theoretical accounts of  learning through argumentation  contend that the seemingly subtle differences between dispute and deliberation are important, sin ce the latter is likely to promote learning, wherea s this is substantively less likely to be the case for the former (e.g., Asterhan & Schwarz, 2016; Keefer et al, 2000; Mercer, 1996). For example, a focus on the interpersonal, compe titive dimension of social interaction may reduce a person’s openness to considering alternative viewpoints, as well as thei r willingness to publicly articulate and share their own incomplete ideas. Since these actio ns are believed to be at the heart of learning thro ugh argumentation (Asterhan & Schwarz, 2016; de Vries et al., 2002; Keefer et al, 2000; Nussbaum, 2008; Osborne, 2010), by not engaging in them, learners are expected to forego important opportunities for learning. Yet, in spite of the broad conceptual consensus about the desirability and advantages of deliberation over dispute for content learning, the number of studies  that have directly studied the outcomes of rhetori c style (disputative vs. deliberative) on learning from arg umentation are few. Different goal instructions (to  persuade or win vs. to learn) have been shown to result in diff erences in discussion type (Authors, 2010; Felton e t al., 2015; Garcia-Mila et al., 2013), essay writing (Felton et al., 2 009; Vi llarroel et al., 2016) and student perceptions (Darnon et al., 2007, 2009; Sommet et al., 2015). Y et, effects on content learning outcomes are rarely  targeted directly. In a controlled confederate experiment, A uthors (2015) provided proof of concept that partic ipation in deliberative (vs. disputative) argumentation indeed  results in superior conceptual learning gains on a  complex science topic from the conceptual change literature  (i.e., diffusion in liquids and gases). However, these results have yet to be replicated in a different content domain and with a new sample. Effects of rhetorical style on learning processes Moreover, even though different reasons have been proposed fo r why and how rhetorical style affects learning from argumentation, very little is known about what the exact motivational, cognitive and/or emotional processes and states that could account for the hypothesized effects of learning from deliberation and dispute. Several process indicators were included in the Asterhan and Babichenko (2015) and Asterhan & Hever (2015) studies. Yet, surprisingly, effects could not be attributed to any differences in  observable learning behaviors that were directly related to exposure to, nor generation of content (such as different time-on-task measures, number of external information resources accessed, or number of substantive propositions made). The only observa ble difference between conditions was that learners tended to be less will ing to share their own (incomplete) understanding with a competitive discussion partner (Asterhan & Babichenko, 2015). This latter, tentative finding corresponds with res earch in social and organizational psychology where scholars have explored the role of conflict and dis agreement in work group settings and negotiations t asks (De Dreu & Weingart, 2003). In this line of research, e xpectations about an upcoming interaction are typic ally manipulated, without actual interactions. Nevertheless, t heir findings are noteworthy, as they often show th at competitive goals and task settings tend to result in more narrow-minded thinking and cognitive rigidity (Bar-Tal et al., 1989; Fisher et al., 2017). For example, Butera and Mugny (1995) showed that individuals that were led to believe they were about to compete with another per son searched for more confirmatory evidence on a Wa son card type task. Carnevale and Probst (1998) showed that anticipating a competitive (vs. a cooperative) negotiation task negatively impacted individuals’ cognitive fle xibility and performance on individual problem-solving tasks. In contrast, however, there ar e also reports of positive effects of competitive f raming on cognitive functioning (De Dreu & Nijstad, 2008; Tjosvold & Johnsson, 1978). For example, De Dreu and Nijstad (2008) argued that a competitive mental set may motivate the mobilizatio n of extra cognitive resources which are, in turn, directed toward task-related aspects that are directly relevant to the resolution of the conflict.

## Vicariously observing disputative and deliberative discussions

In the present study, we also seek to explore whether argumentive  discourse style also affects learning from vicariously observing argumentive  discussions within the context of a learning tasks. Expectations regarding effects of reading a disputative vs. a deliberative discussion on learning are less st raightforward than for actual participation in them: On the one hand, the disputa tive tone may vicariously induce anxiety and cause the reader to cognitively invest less, resulting in an adverse effect on learning, similar to the direct participation settings. On the other hand, however, reading a disputative discussion cou ld lead to higher arousal and increase motivation and emotional investment, while not offering direct  face threat to the reader (unlike in active partic ipation). The more heated tone of a disputative discussion could also highlight the d ifferences in explanatory models that two different discussants use to explain the scientific  phenomenon, which based on findings from contrasti ng cases research, could in turn aid learning in conceptual change tasks. In previous work (Asterhan & Hever, 2015), we showed that rhetorical style of online small-group discussions on a value-laden, socio-political topic (illegal immigration) affected how much readers learned about the topic. However, it has yet to be tested on a conceptual change learning task and in a science learning setting. The present study The main objectives of the present work a re as follows: First, we aim to empirically test co mmon assumptions about the advantages of deliberative argumentation over disputative, persuasive argumentation for conceptual learning in a randomized controlled experiment setup. We will test whether first findings confirming th is advantage (Asterhan & Babichenko, 2015)  can be replicated in a different content domain of conceptual change in science education (i.e., natural selection). Sec ond, we aim to shed light on the reasons behind the expected differences in learning gains. Specifically, we explore the effects of deliberative vs. disputative argumentation on the discussants' socio-cognitive perceptions during and about the interactions. Third, we test whether the effects of rhetorical style on conceptual learning extend t o situations in which learners participate vicariou sly in argumentive discussions, that is: reading a discussion between two other learners. Study 1: Learning from participating in a deliberative/disputative discussion

## Participants

Fifty-eight undergraduate and pre-university students (Mage = 23.67, SD = 2.96) were recruited from Social Sciences, Law, and Humanities programs at a large university campus (82.7% female). Eligibility criteria included native-level language proficiency and no prior enrollment in life sciences programs. Participants received  either course credit or monetary compensation. A power ana lysis using G*Power 3.1 indicated that to detect a small to moderate effect size of partial η 2 =.03 at a significance level (α) of.05 with power (1 -β) of.80 and an assumed correlation of r =.60 between repeated measures, a sample size of at least N = 54 was required. We increased this number to account for an estimated 10% attrition.

## Design and procedure

A between-subjects experimental design was used, with participants randomly assigned to either a deliberative or disputative argumentation condition. Conceptual und erstanding of natural selection was assessed at two  points: after the instructional phase (pretest) and again 7–10 days later (delayed posttest). All participants read the same instructional text (453 words in Hebrew) explaining the principles of natural selection, followed by learning assessments and other measures (see below). They then solved two transfer tasks (e.g., evolution of webbed feet in ducks, non-functional eyesight in cave salamanders). Next, the y were informed they would participate in a text-based discussion with a same-sex peer (in reality, a confederate) to collaboratively solve the two tasks. Participants r eceived condition-specific dialogue goals: to win and persuade (disputative) or to deepen understanding through critical discussion (deliberative). After entering the chat room, the confederate—randomly assigned as discussion leader via a bogus coin toss—used a scripted set of prompts. The participant was  instructed to respond. To ensure consistency, the confederate’s behavior was tightly scripted so that only rhetorical style (deliberative vs. disputative) varied, while controlling for dialogue length, sequence, move typ es, and domain-specific content. The confederate introduced commonly held misconceptions distinct from the part icipant’s, representin g an alternative mental model. Two content versions were prepared to ensure conceptual contrast. In both conditions, the confederate followed a set sequence: prompting participants to state their solution, asking for clarification, challenging the explanation usin g a misconception, presenting an alternative explan ation, and inviting reflection —repeated for both items. The linguistic framing differed b y condition: disputative style included assertive phrasing (e.g., “I don’t think s o. It’s clear that X is wrong”), whereas deliberati ve style used tentative language (e.g., “I’m not so sure… Could it really be X?”). After the discussion, participants completed a filler problem-solving task on an unrelated topic, followed by post-discussion survey items. The delayed posttest occur red 7–10 days later. All phases were conducted via Zoom, with the experimenter, participant, and confederate present (video off, microphones muted during the chat interaction).

Materials Demographic information included gender, age, relig ious affiliation, degree of religiosity, and prior biology education. Attitudes toward biological evolution were assessed using items adapted from Shtulman (2006). Conceptual Knowledge.  Conceptual understanding of natural selection was m easured pre-and post-discussion using two parallel, validated assessment tools (Authors, 2020). Each version included both open-ended and forced-choice items focused on two animal traits. For each  trait, a brief description explained the species, a physiological change, and its relevance for survival. Participants responded to 11 statements (6 for one species, 5 for the other), indicating whether each was true or false and justifying their response. Statements reflected correct principles or common misconceptions, with balance maintained across test versions. Cronbach’s α indicated good reliability for both pretest (.78) and delayed posttest (.86). Test versions were counterbalanced across conditions. Responses were coded based on a previously validate d codebook developed alongside the natural selectio n assessment tool (e.g., Authors, 2020). Each written  response was evaluated for accuracy and alignment with key principles of natural selection (see Table 1). Scoring followed a three-point scale: 0 for omissions, misconceptions, or major errors; 0.5 for partially correct response s; and 1.0 for fully accurate answers addressing th e targeted principles without misconceptions.

For true/false items, both the selected response and the accompanying written explanation were evaluated. Full credit was given for a correct sele ction paired with an adequate explanation. Zero poi nts were assigned for incorrect choices with incorrect justifications. If the choice and explanation were inconsistent, scoring was based solely on the explanation.

After a training phase, two independent coders rated 15% of both pretest and posttest responses. Interrater reliability was satisfactory (Cohen’s κ =.72), and discrepanci es were resolved through discussion before coding the full dataset. Total conceptual understanding scores were calculated by summing ite m scores and converting them to percentages (0–100%).

Participants rated their willingness to engage in a  future discussion with the same partner (0 –100 scale), their preferred format for a future lea rning activity on natural selection (dyadic vs. individual), and their willingness to revise their original answers (0–100 scale). In addition, they responded to three open-ended prompts: (1) describing their discussion part ner, (2) explaining whether they would want to continue working with that partner and why, and (3)  recalling as many of the partner’s discussion cont ributions as possible (free recall task).

Distributions of learning gain scores were inspecte d for outliers. Residuals were assessed for normali ty via skewness and kurtosis (acceptable range: -1 < x < 1) and Q–Q plots. No significant differences emerged between conditions on any of the three contr ol variables: attitudes toward evolution, religiosi ty, or perceived understanding. Pre-test scores showed moderate positive correlations w ith attitudes about evolution (r =.29, p =.029) and initial perceived understanding (r =.30,  p =.021), but not with religiosity (r <.01, ns).  None of these variables correlated with learning gains. The correlation between pre-discussion and posttest scores was strong (r =.70, p <.001).

Study 2: Learning from participating in a deliberative/disputative discussion In Study 2, we explored whether the effects of part icipation in a disputative vs. deliberative argumen tative dialogue would extend to a setting in which learners did not actively participate but observed a discussion between two other learners. The setup and materials used in Study 2 are otherwise identical to Study 1 and the discussions that participants read in this study were obtained and adapted from the Study 1 dataset. To account fo r potential effects of what the main discussant contributed content-wise (i.e., the conceptual models that participants in Study 1 discussions used to explain the phenomena), we created two versions of disputative and deliberative discussions: In the first version, the main discussant's contrib ution reflected a canonically c orrect and full account of natural selection. In the second version, the main discussa nt's contributions reflected common misconceptions about natural selection (i.e., that adaptions occur in reaction toneed, that acquired changes are passed on to offspring).

## Participants

The sample included 88 students from a large public university, between the ages of 20 and 43 (M = 27.89, SD = 4.78; 62.2% female). They were recruited through online platforms for research participation. Ethics approval (IRB) was obtained from the institutional ethics co mmittee. Participants voluntarily participated in the study and gave their informed consent, in return for financia l reimbursement  (approximately 14 EURO). Only students without formal schooling in biology or chemistry beyond the standard high school curriculum requirements were recruited for participation.

To compute the required sample size for a study on the effects of learning from vicariously observing (as opposed to directly participating in) a discussion, we set the power analysis calculation to an effect size of partial η2 =.03 (half of the Study 1 effect size). To detect an interaction effect between discourse style condi tion and time at a significance level (α) of.05 with power (1-β) of.80, and a correlation of r =.7 between pre-and posttest measures (based on Study 1, a sample size of at lea st N = 42 was required. The final sample size we aimed f or was doubled due to the addition of two discussion v ersions within each discourse style condition:  One  in which the querying discussant represented a misconceived understanding and the answering (main)  discussant represented a correct one, and one in which this was reversed. This additional factor was added to control for the possibility that any effects of rhetorical style wo uld be conditioned by the salience of misconception s or correct explanation in the read discussion.

## Design and procedure

A between-subject experimental design was employed. Participa nts were randomly assigned to one of four conditions, according to two factors: rhetorical style  (disputative or deliberative argumentation)  and the correctness of the main discussant (showing full co rrect understanding or incorrect and based on commo n misconceptions).

The materials and procedure were identical to Study 1 with a few exceptions: Instead of participating in a discussion with a confederate, following the lear ning phase, participants read a textual dialogue between two disagreeing female learners, which they were told had participated in a previo us version of the experiment. Four different version of textual discussions were prepared for each of the four conditions. They were adapted from the actual discussions obtained in Study 1 and aligned such that they were of equal length (approximately 790 words), had an equal number of turns and that the content o f the main discussant’s contributions (the one resp onding to the confederate’s prompts) was similar within correctness condition. Materials The same coding book for coding conceptual understa nding of natural selection was used as in Study 1. After a training period on a separate data set, two trained coders coded 21.9% of the Study 2 data set, blinded for condition and test occasion. Inter-rater reliability was satisfactory, Cohen’s κ =.83. One coder coded the remaining data. Several post-interaction items were added to this study: A self-report questionnaire to assess whether participants in different conditions captured the d ifferences in rhetorical style, adapted from previo us work to fit the setting of respondents having read a discussion between two learners (Author, 2018). It contained two separate scales (r = -.29), namely deliberative argumentation style (4 items, α =. 79) and disputative argumentation style (4 items, α =.85). Respondents indicated their agreement with different descriptions of the discussion on Likert scales, ranging from 1 (does not describe the discussion at all) to 7 (describes the discussion very well). Examples are The discussants tried to critique the ideas togethe r and The discussants tried to show the other side why th ey are wrong.

In addition, participants indicated who of the two discussants was more convincing in the discussion, they evaluated the quality of each of the two discu ssant's understanding of natural selection (rating scale of 1 to 10), and they were asked to describe in their own w ords how each of the two discussants explained the workings behind evolutionary changes in animal traits.

## Discussion & Implications

These findings suggest that a disputative tone nega tively influenced participants’ perceptions of the questioning discussant and positively biased them t oward the main speaker, irrespective of the actual conceptual quality displayed. Yet, these perceptions did not account for the learning effects reported earlier, as no interaction effects between discussant accuracy and learning ga ins were found. No differences were observed in gen eral preferences for collaborative versus individual learning, χ²(1) = 1.35, p =.246, nor in participants’ willingness to revise their initial responses, F(1, 84) < 1.

## Discussion

Results from two randomized controlled experiments that isolated rhetorical style from content, length, epistemic nature of dialogue moves and other potentially relevant dialogue factors, confirmed theoretical conjectures about the advantages of deliberative over disputative argumentation for conceptual learning. Deliberative argumentation resulted in higher learning gains, both when learners participated in an actual discussion (Study 1) and when they observed a discussion between two other learners (Study 2). The results replicate earlier findings on the effects of rhetoric style on conceptual learning from argument ation (Asterhan & Babichenko, 2015) and extend it to a different science topic (natur al selection) and with new samples. They also exten d the findings to a vicarious learning setting in which learners read an argument ive discussion on the correct explanation of natura l selection processes. Finally, they extend previous research on the effects of reading a disputative or deliberative discussion on a socio-political topic (Asterhan & Hever, 2015; Fisher et al., 2017) to conceptual learning in science education domains.

## Limitations and future directions

Future research should further explore the states a nd processes that mediate the effects on learning. The finding that students in the deliberative condition reported more overall willingness to change their answers resonate with previous findings (Asterhan & Babichenko, 2015), as well as with research from organization al psychology on effects of competitive mental sets on cognitive fle xibility (e.g., Carnevale & Probst, 1998). Systemat ic research that explores these hypothesized processes in a mor e direct fashion seems a logical next step for future resear ch. Future research should also further explore potential boundary conditions of effects of deliberative and disputative argumentation (e.g., does it extend discussion of socio-political and socio-scientific topics). Systematic research on the outcomes, processes and antecedents of deliberation and disputative argumentation is of importance for the study of lea rning through social interaction. However, it is also important in a broader context: Citizens around the world are  increasingly exposed to polarized, combative disco urse on a plethora of complex issues.
