# Tracking Agency in Open-Ended Making with Young Children

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Creative open-ended making highlights agency. We use a temporal lens (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998) to understand how agency is expressed, distributed and supported in family making workshops for young children. We found that children expressed their agency across temporal modes, shifting over the design and making process. Parents and educators dynamically supported agency, often supporting shifts across temporal modes. Children showed frequent linguistic markers of agency in their discussions of their projects, and parents often narrated their children’s agency. We argue that open-ended making with adult support that centers children’s agency, supports children’s engagement in the design processes of ideation, problem-solving and iteration, and their sense of agency in their design and making work.

## Introduction

Hands-on making puts learner agency at the center  in visible ways: makers construct their ideas into physica l reality.  Educators in the maker movement have emphasized the sense of agency involved  as makers tinker with materials, discover possibilities, decide what to w ork on and try to create it (Halverson & Sheridan, 2014; Clapp et al., 2016; Sang & Simpson, 2018; Godhe et al., 2019). Yet the inability to make an idea work in physical reality can be a salient blow to one’s sense of agency. To address this, educators may design situations where makers are more likely to succeed. But, depending on their design, these supports can constrain learners’ ideas, decisions and actions, lessening their sense of ownership of the work (Konopasky & Sheridan, 2015). Given both the centrality of agency and its potential fragility, it is import ant to understand how agency is expressed, distribu ted and supported in educational making environments.

Agency is often defined as the capacity to produce an effect (Kaptelinin & Nardi, 2006).  A learner is agentive when they make decisions and execute actions.  In open-ended making, we see this agency when a learner decides on a design or course of action, or physica lly takes an action such as manipulating or constru cting some material. In our analysis, we use a temporal lens on agency (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998; Holland et al., 1998) to highlight the ways agency may be expressed, distributed and supported across temporal modes. A temporal view asserts that to meaningfully make decisions and act in the prese nt, actors need to envision potential futures and draw on resources from their habitual past.  Tracking agency  across these temporal modes helps us better understand how scaffolding may support or thwart learner agency and attend to the contributions of diverse actors in open-ended creative work (Sheridan, Zhang & Konopasky, 2022; Sheridan & Zhang, 2023). Studio arts and design environments have a long his tory of navigating the tension between open-endedness and structure in supporting agency in creative open-ended work (Sawyer, 2017; 2018).  A ktas et al.

(2023) focuses on the obstacles inherent in design processes and the kinds of creative agencies used to overcome

them.  They argue the material nature of obstacles (in their case for graduate level weavers) spurs the development of creative agency  as makers strive to get around them. Collaborative making projects highlight how agency is distributed among partners, shifting over time. Svhila et al. (2019) use the construct of “framing agency” to identify how team discourse reveals how meaningful design decisions are developed and supported.  Sheridan et al. (2022) describes how though high school art educators prim arily support learners’ autonomy in creative processes, they occasionally shift to exerting cont rol over aspects of the process in service of learn ers’ broader agency.  These shifts to control are strategic, temporary and often linguistically hedged by narrating the learners’ agency in the process.

Tracking how agency is distributed, expressed and s upported gives insights into learners’ experiences, may provide insight into how to support learners without undermining their sense of agency, and a more expansive view on how to credit work on a shared creative pro ject. Using a temporal theoretical approach to agency (Emirbayer & Mische,  1998; Holland et al., 1998) we ask the question:  How is children’s agency in an open-ended co-making project  expressed, distributed and supported?

## Methods

This is a component of a multi-year design-based implementation research study (Fishman et al., 2013) of museum and library workshops for hands-on making for families with young children (aged 6-10) in partnership with a museum focused on children’s hands-on learning and a suburban library system in a larg e culturally and socioeconomically diverse metro area on the U.S. east coast. Workshop content varied, but each were 1.5-2  hours long, involved structured exploration and open-ended making and were conducted by a museum educator assisted by a community member who works in a STEM field. In this analysis, we focus on the open-ended making portion of the workshops.

## Participant selection

Families in the workshops were recruited purposively: All families had at least one child aged 6-10 who showed an interest in informal STEM participation  and recruitment was guided by a principal of maximal heterogeneity in order to include diverse perspectives. The current analysis includes the sub-set of 15 families who participated in a minimum of three sequenced workshops. These families represent more than 15 different race/ethnicities, but have less occupational and educational diversity: nearly ½ of the families had at least one parent who was educated or worked in a STEM field. Other parental occupations included priest, police officer, legal assistant, teacher, kitchen staff, and nanny.

## Data collection

Data were collected using an overall video-recording of the workshop, which periodically focused on each family as they worked, an audio-recorder on each family’s worktable, and the educat or and community STEM professional each wore a microphone.  In addition, researchers took detailed fieldnotes (typicall y one researcher per 1-3 families) occasionally asking questions about their process  while making, and conducted 10-15 minute interviews with each family following each workshop session.

## Data analysis

Analysis first involved integrating the multiple data sources for a particular family to create a detailed time-based account of their open-ended making work process. We first divided the making experience roughly into episodes connected to shifts in the design/making process —such as coming up with an idea, planning how to enact the idea, revising or refining idea, asking for help, t esting out designs.  We then look at how agency is expressed or supported within each of these episodes by looking at all the actions and interactions (verbal and non-verbal) of the different participants involved in the design (e.g., child/ren, parent(s), educator, STEM professional). We use a temporal model of agency (Emirbayer & Mische,  1998) and linguistic markers (Konopasky & Sheridan, 2016) to organize these actions and interactions into how agency is expressed across the temporal mo des of habitual past, practical-evaluative present and future projective. All data analysis was reviewed by at least two researchers.

## Findings

During the open-ended making experiences, we found that agency is expressed, distributed and supported across the temporal modes, by diverse participants in dynamic ways over time (See Tables 1 and 2).  Agency is reflected through actions, non-verbal communication, and language —including on a grammatical level. Each family had distributions of expressions of agency across temporal modes and across participants. In the habitual past  mode, parents most often supported agency by reminding of immediate past resources—such as tips the educator gave, or what they learne d in the prior hands-on activity.  These were sometimes framed in directive, command form s (e.g., “Remember what she told you about what you n eed for a circuit”).  Sometimes parents supported children’s habitual past agency through  “recognition of type” (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998) —that is, a more generalized principle or situation.  For instance, when a girl was designing a flying tent car for her stuffed animals, her mother reminded her to think about their size when planning her design. Here the parent implicitly highlights the principle that designers need to think about the qualities and nee ds of intended users.

Educators used similar strategies as parents across  temporal modes, but their interventions were more periodic, often in response to a frustration or to appreciate a creation.  They frequently used t wo important linguistic devices that support agency: 1) Conditional rather thandirective language when talk ing about ideas or decisions (e.g., “Maybe you could…” or “You might think about…”). This phrasing indicates the educators were offering possible courses of action or consideratio ns, not making design deci sions. Direct statements  were reserved for describing material constraints (e.g., “These motors aren’t very powerful.”). 2) Suggestions framed from a personal perspective (e.g., “When I don’t have an idea of what to make, sometim es I like to ask myself ‘what do I need?’;” “One thing I do when I’m frustrated is just set it aside for a bit and work on something else.”) This language positions child and educator as fellow makers and suggestion as a possible option, not as a direction they must follow, both of which serve to support children’s agency. These findings are consistent with studies of teachers’ support of agency in studio arts classes (Sheridan et al., 2022). In family interviews,  children most consistently e xpressed primary agency in ideation, with most ideas (either initial or revised) generated by a child pr imarily on their own, or co-developed by siblings.  Occasionally ideas were reported as collaboratively created by c hildren and adults.  Very few ideas were  primarily credited to an adult.  Problem-solving to enact the idea was reported as more coll aborative. In families’ responses to “How did you make it work?” fewer than half of the instances focused primarily on the child’s actions and decisi ons, whereas more than half focused on collaborative pro blem-solving.  There was only one instance where an adul t was described as the primary problem solver.

Her mother regular ly asks if she needs help, but Ana rejects her offers. That’s one of the things that I was thinking about when I make my invention. I really like birds, so I made a bird over there. You like dancing and you made an invention about dancing.” Her language frames Ana as a fellow maker, and aims to build a more generalized habitual past resource  that inventors often are inspired by their interests. When Ana demonstrates her invention, the educator comments, “That’s a fantastic way to use the motor.” When asked how she might wor k on the project further, Ana responds: "I’m going to put some lights, make little chairs and then make an au dience in front of it."  Again we see a tight link between the future projective mode in the  improvisation on her envisioned idea and the practical-evaluative present in her decisions needed to execute it.  In her language, actions, and envisioning  throughout the project, Ana expresses her agency across all temporal modes.  Her mom and other adults only provide minimal technical assista nce at her direction, and offer praise that frames her making in a broader context of invention. This re-evaluation of their own prior experiences was not uncommon for parents without a background in STEM.

## Conclusion

This study identified a range of ways children’s ag ency is expressed, distributed and supported across  temporal modes in open-ended creative work. All children expressed agency dynamically across te mporal modes as they engage in the creative design and making process. As children characterize the problems they experience in their design, make decisions and execute actions in the p resent, these decis ions and actions are shaped by their improvising with their design ideas in the future, and drawing on past experiences and knowledge that inform their design. Siblings and other family members often show distri buted agency across temporal modes.  Parents and educators often supported one temporal mode whi le the child worked in another  and were more likely to contribute to making ideas work than they were to generating ideas. Looking across the three examples from one workshop we are first struck by their distinctive approaches to the design problem, each rooted in the interests  and ideas of the child /ren.  Tracking the temporal shifts in agency in these cases allows us to see variations in how their agency is expressed and supported.  Ana fluidly expresses agency across the temporal modes with min imal support. Aaron’s mom provides considerable sup port to move him from improvising in the future projective to enacting his ideas in the practical-evaluative present mode.  Luis posed a difficult challenge for which varied adults  provide support of habitual past resources to support his decisions and actions solving problems in the practical-evaluative present. An examination of agency support suggests that pare nts and educators often narrate the children as agents, across the temporal  modes, also often minimizing their own contribution s.  In their questions and comments, parents and educators  articulate children’s agency: the ideas children have developed, the decisions they have made, the resources they have drawn upon.  In addition to narrating the children’s agency, parents and educators often strategically provide support that help move children from one temporal mode to anothe r.  For instance, when Aaron is excitedly inhabiting the fu ture projective of his ideas for his excited ice cr eam cone, his mother’s scaffolding works to shift this to include  the practical-evaluative present, asking him to make more specific design decisions and plans. When Luis is stu ck on problems in the practical-evaluative present, the educator and community professional suggest habitua l past resources, such as the concept of resistance and th e practice of systematic testing.  Lastl y, though these are young children, the educators r egularly narrate them as engaging in practices akin to adult professionals: Ana is like other inventors, Aaron is a scientist, and Luis solves problems like all the “electrical people.” In children’s accounts of their making, not only do we see dynamic shifts across temporal modes as they work, we also see frequent linguistic markers of agency: when children describe their work and process, they use personal pronouns combined with frequent mental, ma terial and action verbs and specify direct objects of their actions (Konopasky & Sheridan, 2016). This study also raised some questions for further r esearch.  Though not the explicit focus of our current analysis, there seemed to be i nteresting dynamics around the expression and support of agency when experiencing obstacles.  Aktas et al. (2023) found obstacles productive for graduate level designers’ sense of agency; future research should explore the relationship for  young designers and makers. Across the cases discussed and the full set of fami lies studied, children frequently experienced frustrations and obstacles in their making and design. Some frustrations, like Aaron’s rolling ice cream cone, the sibling’s Ferris Wheel, and Luis’ water sensor were never fully reso lved.  Yet, when prompted to describe what they made they express considerable agency: children identified problems, described strategies that they used, and next steps they were going to take, thus engaging in meaningful design processes and their language showed frequent markers of agency. This aligns with other research that suggest  open-ended projects that allow makers to make meaningful design decisions support agency, even when the make rs are not yet fully able to enact their intended visions (Konopasky & Sheridan, 2015; Svhila, 2019).  This is in contrast to more “kit” or making of pre-specified designs, where makers are more likely to show fewer markers of agency--they are more likely to narrate their projects with a collective pronoun “ we,” use fewer mental verbs, and more modals of obl igation (e.g. “We had to..”) (Konopasky & Sheridan, 2015). In describing their projects, nearly all the children in the current example of openended creative work  frame themselves as both the primary thinker and th e makers of the work and as highly agentic problem solvers.
