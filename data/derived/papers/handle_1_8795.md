# How the Shape of the Earth is Argued into Being

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Claims that depart from Western scientific consensus, such as the relationship between vaccines and autism or the flat shape of the earth are often disparaged. We aim to cultivate greater convergence with scientific consensus, from a stance that rejects attributing alternative explanations to heedless partisanship or emotional susceptibility. We focus on the ‘flat earth’ explanation, and examine argumentative moves, attention to evidence and knowledge co-construction in YouTube discussions between supporters and critics of ‘flat earth.’ We found that Flat Earth supporters engage in argumentation and meta-talk about evidence, even at least as frequently as the critics. These findings suggest that cultivating greater convergence on scientific consensus requires deep and nuanced approaches, because alternative explanations can be couched in scientific, deliberative, and argumentative structures.

## Introduction

"We’re not just fighting an epidemic; we’re fighting an infodemic” said the WHO Director at a conference on the COVID-19 outbreak (WHO, 2020). Indeed, scientific m isinformation and disinformation on well establishe d scientific topics such as climate change, vaccinations, and the shape of earth are being challenged and disputed. Specifically, in contrast to Western scientific can on, the Flat Earth movement argues that the earth’s  shape is planar, doubting government agencies’ evidence that the earth is spherical (Garwood, 2008). In light of declining trust in science and institutional providers of information (Barzilai & Chinn, 2020), the proliferation of alternative theories  (Landrum & Olshansky, 2019), such flat earth, foretell increasing divergence from scientific consensus, some to detrimental effects (Sinatra & Lombardi, 20 20). Although ultimately our aim is to encourage pu blic engagement with science, and to support greater con vergence with scientific convention, we reject appr oaches that attribute the adoption of alternative explanations to deficit, heedless partisanship or emotional susceptibility. Instead, we contend that a deeper understanding of the internal logic of such theories can provide more productive avenues for deepening belief and trust in Western science (Kienhues et al., 2020), and increasing the convergence between publicly held beliefs and scientific conven tion. Therefore, the current study proposes to eluc idate how alternative theories are communicated and maintained by discourse practices on social media. We focus on discourse in post-video comments on YouTube that appear following videos in support of Flat Earth. Unlike other alternative theories, such as concerns over specific vaccines or schedules of vaccinations, where there is controversy within the scientific community, there is a strong consensus over the spherical shape of the Earth, and the idea that the Earth is flat is a radical departure from scientific canon. We chose YouTube as the medium for our analysis due to its key role in the Flat Earth movement. A Flat Earth Conference in Raleigh, North Carolina, November 2017, revealed that many of the participants came to accept the alternative theory of Flat Earth after watching videos about it on YouTube (Olshansky et al., 2020).

## Theoretical background

Discursive approaches to knowledge A discursive approach to knowledge and knowing assu mes that social groups and cultures develop shared meanings that are ascribed to practices, artifacts, and systems of signs (Gee, 1990; Kelly & Green, 1998) and that these shared meanings are reflected in interaction and communication. Argumentation is a specific form  of discourse in which participants make claims, support these claims with evidence, or try to refute or discount claims raised by others (van Eemeren et al., 2015). Argume ntation can be disputational in which participants have persuasion goals, or it can be a form of knowledge construction, referred to as deliberative argumenta tion (Asterhan & Schwarz, 2016) or collaborative argumen tation (Chinn & Clark, 2013) in which participants are mainly driven to understand an issue. In this study, we focus on scientific knowledge and understanding from a community and discursive perspective (Mason, 2007). That is, rather than examine the declarative conceptions of specific individuals through interviews, written te sts or tasks, the study examines naturalistic discu ssions concerning the shape of the earth as these discussions occur spontaneously in the comments section of YouTube videos. This approach may limit our ability to unde rstand individual cognition, but it provides insigh t into the ways in which knowledge is used in practice in the world. Specifically, by examining arguments between those ICLS2022 Proceedings 243 © ISLS that advocate that the earth is flat and those that  advocate that the earth is round, we can examine t he ways in which each of these groups engages with the practices and theories of formal western science. Alternative conceptions and public engagement with science Science education has a long history of examining learners’ conceptions and the ways in which they align with or depart from scientific conventions, variably referred to as misconceptions or alternative conceptions (Chi et al., 1994; DiSessa, 2014; Duschl & Jiménez-Aleixandre, 2012; Linn & Eylon, 2006; Linn, 2008). Examining public engagement with science, there are increasing conce rns over the proliferation of alternative conceptio ns that depart from canonical scientific explanations of natural phenomena or that cast doubt over scientific innovations such as vaccines (Sinatra et al., 2014; Sinatra & Lombardi, 2020). The advent of the Internet and social media has brought about greater access to both conventional s cience information as well as to alternative positi ons, and people struggle with reconciling these conflicting resources (Tabak, 2016). One approach to alternativ e conceptions sees these alternative conceptions as a  deficit on the part of those who hold these altern ative conceptions (Brossard & Lewenstein, 2010; Seakin & Hobson, 2017; Stocklmayer & Bryant, 2012), implying that if the public were less ignorant of science they would not maintain such conceptions. These approaches may be more pronounced as research on post-truth sugges ts that people are highly susceptible to adopting misinformation online, and that they tend to engage in immediate surface processing of information (Barzilai & Chinn, 2020; Brashier & Marsh, 2020; Kahan et al., 2017) rather than engage in deeper deliberation (Bago et al., 2020; Sinatra & Lombardi, 2020; Tabak, 2020). In recent years, the deficit model has been critized as an inaccurate and denegrating protrayal of public understanding o f science (Brossard & Lewenstein, 2010; Sturgis & A llum, 2004), and there are increasing calls for gaining a  better understanding of the nature of the public’s  scientific knowledge and skills and of how they are used in everyday contexts. The Flat Earth community In contrast to current scientific convention that the Earth is spherical, there are those that maintain that the earth is flat (Olshansky et al., 2020). Within the community that adopts a flat earth position, referred to by some as Flat Earthers’, there are different perspectives over how gravity works, whether the earth is surrounded by a sheet of ice, and other ideas concerning celestial bodies be yond the earth (Garwood, 2008; York, 2017). Althoug h Flat Earthers have existed for at least as long as spher ical earth theories emerged, there has been a resur gence and expansion of this community in the last few decades, considerably influenced by videos posted on YouTu be promoting the flat earth perspective (Brazil, 2020; Olshansky et al., 2020). Dating back to at least the 1970’s and continuing today, members of the flat earth community offer justifications for their position that include references to religious scripture as well as those that are ba sed on observation, measurement and experimentation  (Vogt, 1979; York, 2017). The relationship between conventional Western science views and religious views and their role in adopting flat earth perspectives is an area  of study and of mixed results, where religiosity, sensitivity to conspiracy ideology and science literacy were all implicated in a tendency to adopt alternative theories, and a flat earth perspective (Landrum & Olshansky, 2019). In this study, we seek to better understand the foundation of flat earth perspectives by focusing on how flat earth id eas are constituted through the discourse and argum entation that takes place within the flat earth community and between Flat Earthers’ and their critics in post-video comments on YouTube.

## Methods

We used several search and filtering strategies in order to obtain the YouTube videos that were used in our analysis of post-video comments on videos advocating a flat earth approach. We initially searched for “flat ear th” on YouTube and selected the top 20 by number of views. Afterwards, we extended our search using information we obtained from other sources such as documentaries a bout the Flat Earth community, search trends and po pular sites as shown on Google Trends. We searched YouTube for videos based on names of leaders in the community, and on the use of experimentation in support of the  flat earth theory. We collected a database of 20-3 0 videos. Our first filter criteria was videos that advocate for the flat earth theory, next we eliminated video s that had comments that consisted mostly of derogatory interc hange. Finally, we selected 4 videos published on t he “YouTube” platform that advocated for flat earth, had extensive comments and “likes,” and that represented two types of videos typically found in the database we collected, as described below. We sampled 1278 post-video comments out of these 4 videos. Each video represents a stance supporting the Flat Earth theory, from a different perspective: two videos explain and discuss common questions regarding the theory, and two videos present experiments. We assessed each comment as a unit of analysis using a four-fold coding system: ICLS2022 Proceedings 244 © ISLS

1. Supporters vs. critics. We coded statements for bei ng expressed to support or to oppose to Flat Earth

theory.

2. Argumentative Discourse Moves. A coding system was developed based on Lu, Chiu, and Law (2011).

Comments were coded along three main dimensions for  argumentative discourse moves. The first dimension of the code considered how the present co mment evaluates previous comments; namely, whether it expresses disagreement, a counter claim, agreement, or a question. The second, classified each comment as having grounds to warrant their comments. Third, classified each ground for its type (e.g., religious, or scientific).

3. Meta-talk about evidence. A coding scheme developed by Shi (2020) to examin e changes in use of

evidence-based claims during argumentative discours e. This scheme conceptualizes three categories: solicit evidence (e.g., “what is your evidence?”), evaluate source of evidence (e.g., “tell us where y ou get that evidence”, and evaluate content of evidenc e (e.g., judgment of evidence quality and evidence comparison).

4. Collaborative knowledge construction (CKC). We used Gunawardena, Lowe, and Anderson’s (1997)

coding scheme which assesses the process of knowledge construction in a series of successive (though not necessarily strictly sequential) phases that are viewed as moving from lower to higher levels of group collaborative interaction: (1) Sharing/Adding (e.g., a statement of observation or opinion; definition, description, identification of a problem); (2) Nego tiating meaning (agreement or disagreement by providing restatements, by identifying inconsistenc ies, or by asking for clarification); (3) Elaborati ng (building on and expanding previous statements and meanings); (4) Proposing synthesis (synthesis of the discussion with the prospect of testing it and fina lizing it); and (5) Consensus/Applying constructed knowledge (stating a summary of agreements, application of new knowledge, metacognitive statements of changes in knowledge or ways of thinking).

## Discussion

The broader research program in which this study is  embedded aims to understand how people reason abou t science in everyday contexts to inform formal and c ommunity-oriented science education. We further aim  to advance greater trust in, and alignment with formal (Western) scientific practices and knowledge. However, we work from a grounding assumption that alternative p erspectives arise from a conceptual ecology and ref lect an internal logic (Chinn et al., 2020; Demastes et al., 1995; diSessa, 2018). Therefore, we aim to uncove r and understand the internal logic underlying alternative theories, as they are “talked into being” (Caronia & Chieregato,

2016) and constituted through naturalistic discourse (Kelly & Chen, 1999). In the present study, we examined the

Flat Earth community as a test case, analyzing and characterizing the discourse moves of YouTube post-video comments.

Our findings reveal that in open public discussion venues, theoretical and ideological commitments are shaped and sustained not only through supportive and co-constructed discussion within the community but also through defense of these ideas against critics. The  YouTube information ecosystem attracted and gave v oice to Flat Earth theory supporters and to its critics. Ir onically, critics, who seem to have the goal of era dicating flat earth ideas, may in fact contribute to perpetuating these ideas by providing supporters a need to “close ranks” and defend their perspective (White et al., 2018). In our analysis, Flat Earth supporters achieved higher collaborative knowledge construction levels than critics.

Moreover, the patterns that we found in these pro/con flat earth debates in YouTube post-video comments differed from patterns we found in other, less char ged, science-related post-video discussions. The Fl at Earth debates included (among supporters) higher levels of collaborative knowledge construction and of expressions of disagreement than those found in less charged discu ssions, which were predominantly characterized by n eutral claims (Dubovi & Tabak, 2020). This supports the notion that Flat Earth supporters are driven toward Flat Earth preserving argumentative moves. In addition, it suggests that research examining scientific discussions on social media should pay closer attention to the social-emotional weight of the issues that are discussed (Dubovi & Tabak, 2021), because differences along these dimensions could affect argumentative patterns. If we consider the flat earth critic participants a s reflecting a stance more aligned with formal scie nce, then we see that Flat Earth’ers are more similar thandifferent, discursively, to “scientifically aligned” speakers. Both Flat Earth supporters and critics engaged in argumentative discourse and meta-talk dialogue about evidence. However, there were two main patterns that distinguished supporters from critics, which could inform education. Flat Earth critics and supporters incorporated warr ants for their claims with similar frequency, but t hey based these claims on different grounds. Flat Earth supporters tended to call on religious evidence, while critics relied more on scientific warrants.

The similarities in argumentative moves between supporters and critics point to the difficulty in resisting alternative scientific explanations. Prior research demonstrates that people are sensitive to texts and discourse that connote “scientificality,” and consider the more scientific-seeming texts to be more reliable (Bromme et al., 2015; Thomm & Bromme, 2012). The discussions following videos that presented experiments in support of a flat earth are especially pertinent, because of the combined e ffect of the use of experiments and the higher prop ortion of argumentative moves. Therefore, through their “scientificality,” alternative theory presentations on social media may seem reliable and convincing. The similarities in argumentative structures and moves between suppo rters and critics suggests that extending experiences with scientific argumentation may not be sufficient for supporting greater alignment with scientific consensus. The di fferences in warrants suggest that developing a dee per understanding of the nature of evidence and what constitutes scientific evidence versus other forms of evidence (Chinn et al., 2020; Duncan et al., 2018) may be a step in the right direction.

## Conclusion

People who maintain alternative scientific beliefs are often cast as adopting these positions from unr easoned responses to emotional manipulation or to group affiliations. The Flat Earth discussions that we analyze suggest that at least those members of the Flath Earth comm unity who participated in the discussion were reasoned and deliberative, and that much of their argumentative moves were aligned with scientific moves. These fin dings suggest that cultivating greater convergence on sci entific consensus requires deep and nuanced approac hes, because alternative explanations can be couched in scientific, deliberative, and argumentative structures.
