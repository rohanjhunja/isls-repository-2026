# “The Big Payback”: Using Culturally-Centered Curriculum to Retain Historically Marginalized Students in STEM

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Students from historically marginalized communities frequently struggle to connect to STEM at the postsecondary level. This action research study looked at the impact of developing STEM curriculum for historically underserved STEM majors at a Midwestern university. Using culturally based instruction, the results show that the knowledge of marginalized students can be used as a resource in STEM learning environments.

## Introduction

While many historically marginalized students have studied STEM fields, many drop out and study non-STEM fields (Chen, 2013). This loss of STEM majors has contributed to a STEM education diversity gap in universities and colleges. STEM interventions have been extensively studied to increase student engagement and participation. Participation can indicate STEM engagement, but the se programs do not guarantee STEM retention for underrepresented students. Studies suggest that his torically marginalized students struggle with STEM courses without culturally centered educational resources (Hayes & Patton 2019). Given this, educators and researchers must create and use curricular resources that prior itize and empower historically marginalized STEM students' voices, experiences, and needs. Thus, this study is  part of a larger project to retain and engage STEM  students from marginalized communities in higher education. This study asked: How do STEM students from historically marginalized demographics respond to a culturally centered STEM curriculum?

## Conceptual framework

Using sociocultural learning theories (Vygotsky, 1997) and the Funds of Identity conceptual framework (EstebanGuitart & Moll, 2014) this study views the acquisit ion of cultural knowledge as a necessary component of the learning process, as it enables individuals to cons truct knowledge. Using these frameworks, the use of  culture as a frame for learning emerges as tools and signs that a re used to make sense of the knowledge that is deve loped. Ultimately the use of these frameworks here proposes that individuals do not come to educational settings with a blank slate, but rather acknowledg es the significance of cultural repertoires as impo rtant assets, in contrast to previous deficit-oriented models applied to marginalized communities.

## Research design

This study examined STEM program participants at Yellow university. The action research study implemented a culturally-centered curriculum with 13 first-year STEM majors from marginalized communities. The  researcher focused on five-weeks of curriculum activities that addressed acade mic and psychological endeav ors that could impact STEM students' experiences (see Table 1). The study used semi-structured interviews with students to gather qualitative data on their STEM perceptions, support networks, and prominent identities. After five weeks, thematic analysis was conducted.  The author transcribed all data and created a coding scheme to capture students' STEM attitudes, beliefs, and interests. The externa l auditor reviewed the codes, which were then itera tively refined. The codes collapsed, followed by more iter ations. This process revealed themes from the study 's categories and participants' voices.

## Findings

Our analysis resulted in  three themes: that students’ beliefs of STEM evolved over time, students dealing with internal conflicts  and external outcomes, and adapting to change while maintaining uncertainty. In the theme students’ beliefs of STEM evolved over time as students participated in the course, shifts in their understanding of STEM over time were revealed. These relatively minor modifications enabled students to reassess their views on STEM by employing their knowledge, ideas, and belie fs as a guide.  As students attain knowledge, they turn inward to reflect on themselves. A participant shared, “With the knowledge I now hav e about STEM that I previously didn’t, I can approach these challenges and overcome them easier than I would’ve without this course” (post-interview).

Within the theme students’ confronting internal conflicts and external outcomes when students attempted to merge their identities into STEM subjects, they indicated some difficulties. These internal disputes arose as a result to seeking recognition for their STEM talent s and abilities from peers and faculty members.  “When I was told that I was expected to struggle in my chemistry course because I was a quiet student, I felt insulted and looked down upon” (pre-Interview). As students continued throughout the culturally-centered curriculum, they disclosed that seeing others, who are sharing the same experi ences has motivated and encouraged them to continue  in STEM. For example, one student shared, their ability to persist in STEM is  now informed by the new resources (i.e., peers) they can employ to overcome any adversity:  “You do not have to go through everything alone and make way for yourself in this field” (post-interview).

In the final theme, adapting to change while maintaining uncertainty, students were able to embrace changes by means of cultivating new perspectives  about STEM that they were reluctant to  do prior to the study.

As students participated in the curriculum, they were able to build their confidence by connecting STEM to their expertise. “This course is for you to understand who you are a nd who you can be. If you put all those troubles away from you and out of sight, you can accomplish anything and everything” (post-interview). Despite the positive outlook received across the study, students indicated discontent about the curriculum. One student noted how they felt the curriculum topics were offensive toward others. They stated, “ I am finding it very hard to just immediately think of it as “white men in lab coats. ” Again, I did not grow up thinking like this, nor did I spend enough time in the white community to make such broad generalizations” (post-interview).

## Implications

In general, the curriculum allowed learners to investigate the reasons behind their sense of empowerment in STEM by incorporating their cultural knowledge. The stud ents' attitudes underwent a significant transformat ion as they engaged in the culturally focused curriculum. The r esults support the idea that when students are prom pted to relate the curriculum to their personal experiences, it can lead to an increase in self-awareness, which in turn can help them shift their focus towards STEM subjects. Although the shifts in thinking are beneficial, stu dents encountered difficulties in forming their own ideal s, beliefs, and perceptions of STEM. As a result, t hey resisted new knowledge and maintained narrow viewpoints in t he field of STEM.  Moreover, this study has implications for STEM educators and researchers to rethink how they candevelop STEM content that is applicable to students' lives.   Consequently, as more faculty strive to create an inclusive environment, this has the potential to help retain and support historically marginalized students in STEM.
