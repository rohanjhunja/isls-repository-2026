# The Role of Color in Debugging Lego Structures

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study blended the fields of debugging, spatial thinking, and change detection. We explored 12 K-2 students’ debugging skills as they fixed a Lego structure by using multicolor or grayscale manuals. Students in both groups were most likely to debug the bugs on the top and bottom of the structure, while the multi-color group was more successful at debugging bugs in the middle of the structure than the grayscale group, possibly because they were also more likely to trace the steps in the manual and use an understanding the structure strategy. Overall, students used debugging strategies similar to those found in programming, providing initial evidence that young students can successfully engage in debugging during unplugged, non-programming activities such as Lego building.

There has been increasing attention to the importan ce of computational thinking (CT) across the elemen tary curriculum in the United States. Two key aspects of CT that are found in unplugged activities are abstraction and debugging. Abstraction involves focusing on importa nt components by ignoring unnecessary details, and debugging is identifying and fixing problems (Caeli  & Yadav, 2020; Wing, 2008). In spatial activities such as Lego building, abstraction and debugging might be u sed in the process of 3-D shape composition and decomposition. In fact, one area where both CT and mathematical practices align (and debugging can pla y a significant role) is in analyzing and comparing thr ee-dimensional shapes in different sizes and orient ations and composing and decomposing three-dimensional shapes (Brennan & Resnick, 2012; Lye & Koh, 2014; National Governors Association Center for Best Practices & Council of Chief State School Officers, 2010; Kotsopoulos, et al., 2017). To demonstrate that CT is an encompassing framework we alsoneed to understand how children use CT in domains other than programming. Building on t he popularity of spatial, block building activities in early childhood, we investigate CT in unplugged, non-prog ramming-based activities by targeting debugging in Lego structures using a visual perception lens with a specific focus on how color plays a role in students’ debugging. Debugging in programming, offline, and visual-change-detection tasks The greatest difficulty for novice programmers is d ebugging their code (Fitzgerald et al., 2008; Murph y et al., 2008; Wing, 2006). Students have been successful when debugging familiar code (Katz & Anderson, 1987) and easily found obvious bugs (Fitzgerald et al., 2008). Unplugged spatial activities such as block building and puzzles (e.g., Bell et al., 2012; Bofferding et al., 2021; Dietz et al., 2019) can also involve or support deb ugging. Bofferding et al. (2021) asked first and third graders to debug a non-standard shape puzzle, where students had to layer tiles on top of each other to create a compos ite design. More students debugged the puzzle on the posttest by using their spatial thinking skills, such as visualization and rotation of the tiles. Likewise, to complete change detection tasks (i.e., tasks where students must identify what is different between two scenes), students draw on visualization skills (Shore et al., 2006). Thus, spatial activities could support emerging debugging skills. Finding bugs in spatial tasks requires visualization skills, such as detecting visual differences in change detection tasks. In such tasks, participants must determine what is different between two pictures. In one version, they see the original and changed picture side-by-side, similar to “Hidden Pictures” in the children’s Highlights magazine (https://www.highlightskids.com/games). Prior studies have shown that visual differences are easier to detect if they are central to a scene (Rensink et al., 1997) and when there are fewer elements in the scene (Shore et al., 2006). Further, changes in object location or orientation were easiest to spot, followed by changes in color, and deleted parts were the most difficult to find (Rensink et al., 1997; Shore et al., 2006). Learning how children debug block structures could influence task design and bring this CT practice into classrooms. Debugging strategies Two main debugging strategies include forward reaso ning and backward reasoning (Katz & Anderson, 1987). Forward reasoning involves searching for bugs start ing from the initial lines of code or while paraphr asing or representing the code; whereas, backward reasoning involves searching for incorrect behavior by mapping output to lines of code (Katz & Anderson, 1987). Novice programmers and those unfamiliar with the code use forward reasoning more than backward reasoning (Fitzgerald et al., 2008, Katz & Anderson, 1987). Murphy et al. (2008) investigated novice programmer’s debugging strategi es, adding different categories to the classificati on of ICLS2022 Proceedings 1449 © ISLS debugging strategies. These include strategies such as tinkering, tracing, and pattern matching. Tinkering refers to unproductive or random changes, pattern matching  is recognizing differences by focusing on looking,  and tracing is physical or mental checking of outputs w ith inputs in a linear fashion. Further, some young  students rewrite the entire program instead of fixing buggy lines of the program (Bofferding et al., 2022). Our Study Our study blended ideas of debugging, spatial think ing, and change detection. We used a version of a s ide-byside change detection task by giving students a complete Lego structure and asking them to fix the Lego structure to match a modified version of the structure. Our t ask involved students debugging multiple differences in one structure. In addition to exploring the features of size (e.g., a 6 x 2 versus a 4 x 2 block), thickness (e.g., flat versus standard block), and location or orientation, we al so explored the extent to which seeing the pictures  in color versus grayscale play a role in students’ change detection and debugging. Finally, we explored the role of being able to see the larger picture in smaller chunks by providing students with a picture of the Lego structure versus a manual that shows how pieces are put together. Our main research questions were (1) What strategies do students use to debug a Lego structure? and (2) Which bugs (i.e., changes to the structure) do they fix? For each of these questions, we considered the role of seeing the manual in multi-color versus in grayscale.

## Participants and study design

We worked with four kindergarten, four first grade,  and four second grade students during their afters chool program at a Montessori school in the midwestern United States. We targeted a balance of genders. All names in the paper are pseudonyms. The data in the study comes from the first out of three sessions of a project where we explored students’ composing, decomposing, and debugging process through Lego building activities. During this session, students composed a Lego structure using multi-color or grayscale pictures versus step-by-step manuals. The focus of this paper is on the debugging activity that came next. Starting from their completed Lego structure (see Figure 1A), we wanted students to use a new manual to find and fix the differences (see Figure 1B). The new manual had a picture of a modified structure on the first page, followed by the steps needed to correctly make the Lego structure (see Figure 1C and 1D). This version  of a change detection task involved 10 bugs or cha nges (labeled in Figure 1B) including missing pieces (bugs 1 and 2 in Figure 1B), incorrect pieces (bugs 4, 5, 6, and 7 in Figure 1B), incorrect placement (bugs 3, 9, and 10 in Figure 1B), and incorrect orientation (bug 8 in Figure 1B). Because some students saw the modified manual in grayscale, we did not include color differences as bugs. We worked with students individually and video recorded each session with two cameras. ## Results

Overall, students debugged 65 bugs out of 120 (M=5.4 out of 10). Interestingly, first graders found the most bugs (33 bugs), followed by kindergarteners (17 bugs) and second graders (15 bugs). Second graders’ low performance may have been due to disinterest in fixing a Lego s tructure they had already put together. Students using multicolor manuals were more likely to debug than those using grayscale manuals (42 bugs versus 23 bugs, respectively). Further, students were most likely t o debug the bugs on the top and bottom of the Lego structure.  For example, bugs 1 and 2 (see Figure 1 B), new pieces in the structure, were fixed by all six of the students in the multi-color group and three students in the grayscale group. Likewise, four students in each group debugged bug 8, which had an incorrect orientation,  and bugs 9 and 10, which had an incorrect placemen t (see Figure 1B). Bugs in the middle of the structure were harder, especially for students using grayscale manuals. Overall, nine students debugged by keeping the main structure intact as much as possible. However, two students (Carter A., a kindergartener from the gray scale group, and Yellow, a first grader from the mu lticolor group) used a rebuilding strategy and started over using the manual. Further, five students in the multi-color group, compared to two students from the grayscale group, used an understanding the structure strategy by turning the whole structure to identify bugs. This strategy might have been particularly useful for helping students notice bug 3 (see Figure 1B) because the pieces were no longer  flush on the backside. Seven students relied on tracing, starting with the bottom of the structure (i.e., first step in the manual). Most of the bugs occurred symmetrically on left and right sides of the structure, encouraging students to use pattern matching.  At one point, a few students started to remove the pieces symmetric to the locat ion of bugs 4 and 5 (see Figure 1B) until they real ized that those were already correct. Three students used a combination of pattern matching and tracing.  For example, one first grader, Ashley, alternated between identifyin g changes in the picture and going through the step s of the manual. First, she noticed the uppermost difference s in the structure (bugs 9, 10, and then 8 in Figur e 1B), and then she fixed bugs 1 and 2 (see Figure 1B) while starting to go through the manual.  After fixing the bugs at the bottom, she went back to the picture of the final structure and identified and fixed most of the middle bugs (bugs 4-7 in Figure 1B).  Finally, she found and fixed the final bug (bug 3 in Figure 1B) using the manual.

## Discussion and significance

Overall, students’ success at identifying the bugs at the top and bottom of the Lego structure is interesting because in the visual change detection tasks, students have the easiest time identifying changes in central features (Rensink et al., 1997). If central means “toward the middle,” students should notice bugs 4 and 5 (see Figure 1B) most often.  However, students may have interpreted the top pieces as central to defining the structure or the base as central because it holds up the structure. Future work could incorporate student interviews about the nature of the structure and their impressions about the structure to better understand what parts they might consider central. Another possible reason for students’ focus on the top and bottom bugs is that they were easier to spo t visually, consistent with programming studies (e.g., obvious bugs are easy to find, Fitzgerald et al., 2008).  In fact, bugs 9 and 10 were under two visually striking pieces (a bird and a flag). However, based on prior change detection studies (e.g., Rensink et al., 1997; Shore et al., 2006), students should have had a hard time identif ying missing pieces (bugs 1 and 2). Having the manuals that highlight pieces to use at each step likely reduced the difficulty of identifying the missing parts. Of particular intere st were the differences demonstrated by students in  the two groups: multi-color versus grayscale manuals.  Students who did not identify the missing pieces were a ll in the grayscale group. Further, two of these three students did not look at the manual’s steps, debugging by looking at ICLS2022 Proceedings 1451 © ISLS the picture of the final structure. Identifying the  bugs in the middle of the Lego structure was more difficult, especially for the grayscale group.  One reason may have been because the line designating a break between pieces was black, which may have been more difficult to discern in grayscale pictures. The results of this study provide initial evidence that young students can engage in productive debugging tasks that are unplugged and that CT skills are broader than programming. Further, students’ processes in working with the materials suggests a few implications for promoting debugging skills. First, children should be encouraged to understand Lego structures by rotatin g them and even investigating how certain pieces ar e put together. Second, Lego structures could involve both symmetric elements to encourage pattern matching as well as sections that do not lend themselves to pattern matching in order to encourage students to question how pieces are put together. Further research could then inves tigate to what extent debugging in these Lego envir onments might correspond to students’ debugging in tangible, block-based programming environments.
