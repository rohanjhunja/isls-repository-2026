# Supporting Students’ Uptake on Peer Feedback in Collaborative Argumentation: A Design-Based Research

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Students’ uptake of peer feedback is closely related to their learning improvement in peer feedback activity. However, the uptake of peer feedback remains challenging for students. To address this challenge, this study conducted design-based research to facilitate students’ peer feedback uptake practices. Three cycles of iterative designs were implemented to develop, implement, and evaluate a tool to scaffold the peer feedback uptake in classrooms. The findings indicate that the reflection tool effectively enhanced students’ uptake of peer feedback. The iterative design practice added value to the existing literature of peer feedback literacy and fine-tuned pedagogical scaffolds for peer feedback uptake.

## Introduction

Peer feedback involves students evaluating one another's work whereby they  identify the strengths and learning gaps, and provide suggestions for improvement. It enhances learners’ motivation and performance, fostering their resilience evaluation, and argumentation  (Archila et al., 2022). The feedback can be useful only when it is processed and acted upon by students.  F eedback receiver’s meet difficulties  in understanding the feedback received and translating feedback into action can influence feedback uptake. Current studies mainly explored the factors impacting feedback uptake. For instance, Wu & Schunn (2020) explored the relationship among feedback features, feedback perception, and feedback impleme ntation, and found that increased understanding and agreement of feedback predicted higher implementat ion rates. There is a lack of empirical efforts  to address the uptake issues in secondary school context. To support students in making sense of information a nd using it to inform their later work, this study developed a peer feedback intervention to enhance students' understanding, making decision, justifying, and acting upon peer feedback. This paper describes the design and improvement of the peer feedback intervention and investigates students' experiences and perceptions on it. Design principles to support peer feedback uptake The current design to support peer feedback uptake is based on four principles that were derived from  feedback literacy and reflection  on feedback literate. Student feedback literacy framework contains apprec iate feedback, make decision, manage affect and take action  (Carless & Boud,2018). By reflecting, the receiver would further interpret feedback, and that involves decoding and internalizing feedback messages, and evaluating and adapting the learning process based on feedback (Carless,  2006), which can result in behavioral changes, readiness  for application, and commitment to action  (Boud et al, 1985). (P1, P = Principle) to (P4) indicates key information that informed the design of each of the principles. Current studies find understanding, agreement and planning influence students’ acting upon feedback (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). When authors fail to understand a comment, they generally choose to reject it (Patchan et al., 2016). Notably, students always meet difficulties in understanding feedback (P1), and their ability to act upon feedback is hindere d by lacking the skill to interpret comments received (Robinson et al., 2013) (P3). Besides, Vander Pol et al. (2008) found that students’ agreement with peer feedback was significantly correlated with the uptake to  revise their work. Increasing understanding and agreement of feedback predicted higher implementati on rates (Wu & Schunn, 2020) (P2). Question prompts can be used to help students’ reflection, which asked students why they would use or not use the re ceived feedback significantly increased the number of accurate feed back comments students applied and decreased the implementation of inaccurate comments (Jurkowski, 2018) (P3). Another useful strategy is providing explicit guidance. Students may not know which useful strategies they should use to implement feedback. They may find it difficult to engage with feedback because they lack an understanding of the academic terminology used within feedback (Jonsson, 2013). (P4) Thus, from the cognitive perspective, we should design collaborative learning that supports students’ understanding and acceptance of feedback,  and planning how to respond to  feedback based on the following design principles: P1: Help students understand peer feedback; P2: Help students to decide whether to uptake feedback; P3: Help students provide justification for accepting or rejecting feedback; P4: Providing students with explicit strategies  (i.e., exemplars)  is useful for acting upon feedback. Utilizing the aforementioned design principles, this study crafted a peer feedback intervention to facilitate students' processing and implementation of feedback. It is important to invite teachers as co-investigators and analyze classroom questions so as to understand the reason for failure and success, and to take steps to refine the design in both theo retical and practical aspects after the DBR (Collins, 1992). The main question focuses on  how to improve students feedback uptake. It has been split to two sub-questions: RQ1: What is the rate of students’ feedback uptake?  RQ2: How do students perform in the process of feedback reflection?

## Methods

Design-based research The study goes through three stages: preliminary research, prototy ping, and assessment. The preliminary stage includes context setting and needs analysis, literature review, and development of guiding principles. Prototyping involved three cycles that consist of design, implementation, and evaluation stages. Assessment consists of openended questions on students’ perceptions of their peer feedback experiences.

## Participants and learning task

This research was conducted at a coeducational gove rnment school in Singapore, focusing on the English language subject and the mode of lessons were face-to-face computer-supported collaborative argumentation. The study involved three cycles exploring the following topics (each topic per cycle): “Fame causes more harm than good. How far do you agree? ”, “ Volunteering causes more harm than good. How far do  you agree? ”, and “Artificial intelligence chat-bots cause more harm than good. How far do you agre e?”. Data were collected from a single class of 26 secondary three students, averaging 15 years old. The teacher organ ized students into seven groups, each comprising three to four students, wit h participation being entirely voluntary. Written i nformed consent was obtained from each participant between January and May 2023. For collaborative argumentation, students utili sed the AppleTree platform  (https://appletree.sg/home), which offers a graph-based argumentation workspace, scripted collaboration procedures, and group management (Wenli Chen et al., 2023). The AppleTree platform integrated the SMCKI （The spiral model of collaborative knowledge improvement ）collaborative script, encompassing five phases: (1) individual ideation, (2) group synergy, (3) peer critique, (4) group refinement, and (5) individual achievement (Chen et al., 2021). The peer critique phase involved inter-group activities, with students visiting other groups to assess and comment on their peers' argumentation diagrams. In this paper, the a ctivity was facilitated using Google Worksheets, pr oviding functionalities for self-assessment, peer assessment, feedback provision, and feedback uptake. Notably, feedback uptake is done through iterative cycles which are the main focus in this study.

## Data collection and analysis

We collected peer feedback and students’ reflection s on their feedback uptake.  For the uptake rate of feedback, the information of uptake of feedback was obtained from the log rep orts from the AppleTree platform, which tracked students' revision history and compared it with the feedback. We calculated the uptake rate of  feedback by using the number of feedback acted upon divided by the number of feedback should be acted upon. We use feedback quality and implementable indicators to shelter the feedback should be acted upon. The assessment stage of the DBR included a survey to examine the students' perceptions of the peer feedback tool. As shown in Figure 1, we conducted three cycles to improve the tools on improving feedback uptake. In First cycle, the intervention includes  a series of prompts to guide students in processing feedback  received, see Figure 1 column “Version 1”. For RQ1, we calculated the uptake rate and found that in the first cycle, there were a total of 25 high-quality feedback data, of which 14 were adopted by students, resulting in an uptake rate of 56%. For RQ2, we investigated students’ performance in the feedback reflection process. Two patterns of student uptake of peer feedback emerged. The first pattern involve d understanding → acceptance → planning → uptake, representing the general mechanism of feedback uptake. The second pathway includes understanding → rejection → justification → change of view → planning → uptake, primarily driven by argumentative discourse triggering changes in perspective. The majority of non-adopted feedback consists of acceptance without action, and it is also observed that students spend a significant amount o f time in areas where reflection is unnecessary, such as feedback that has been accepted and praise-type feedback. For RQ1, the uptake rate was 61.9%, with 21 high-quality feedback and 13 feedback implemented. For RQ2, analysis of reflections revealed a prevalence of ina ccurate feedback in cycle 3.  Insights from student interviews indicated that the argumentation topic i n this cycle posed the greatest difficulty, attribu ted to limited prior knowledge and experience in the field, thereby impeding their responsiveness to feedback. The interventions in this cycle exhibited diminished efficacy, and th e correlation between helpfulness scores and feedback uptake remained ambiguous. Moreover, the transition between the Google workshe et and the AppleTree collaborative platform surfaced as a factor hindering students’ effective uptake of feedback. Assessment To assess the prototype, open-ended questions were collected from the students. Users appreciate its organized structure, clear comment display, and the color-coded interface. Students commented that it “makes peer feedback engaging”, “gives a comprehensive scale of my strengths and weaknesses”, "easier to read and interesting.” Some students also shared that “the sentence openers were helpful for us to form sentences in peer feedback and convey what we were trying to say.” Meanwhile, it is worthy to point out that some students also posed some challenges of using the peer feedback uptake scaffolds, mentio ning that it’s "a bit too complicated” and “can be improved aesthetically.”

## Conclusion

Following the design-based research process, this study iteratively designed the intervention to support feedback uptake in the peer feedback activities in secondary  classrooms. This iterative process highlighted the  trajectories of optimizing the pedagogi cal scaffolds for peer feedback uptake practices in  authentic classrooms. The design, implementation, and evaluation in the three cycles contributed to a scaffolding that effectively enhanced students’ understanding of feedback, decision making, reflecting and planning after receiving peer feedback.
