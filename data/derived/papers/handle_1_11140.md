# Creating Thirdspace: Indigenous Learning Lab for Survivance

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Racialization of school discipline is an enduring systemic problem in the United States. This formative intervention study is based on a collaboration with 14 school stakeholders: American Indian students, parents, community members, and educators at a rural high school in a community-driven problem-solving process called Indigenous Learning Lab (ILL). Members analyzed the root causes of the racialized school discipline and created a new behavioral support system. Utilizing critical geography and decolonizing methodology, this formative intervention study aimed to unpack how ILL members formed Thirdspace—a space of resistance, possibilities, and hopes—to address enduring racial disproportionality in school discipline. Members challenged race-neutral representations of space, mapped out dystopia, revitalized American Indian epistemology, and mapped out a real utopian vision of schooling.

Racialization of school discipline in the U.S The U.S. school system has been a settler-colonial space since its inception, in which First Nations students and families struggle over cultural and linguistic eras ure, recognition, and educational sovereignty (Grande, 2015; Lomawaima & McCarty, 2006; Sab zalian, 2019; Wolfe, 2006). Indigenous ontologies, epistemologies, morals, and educational practice were/are pathologized by t he settler-colonial state (Lomawaima & McCarty, 2006). School discipline has historically been a tool for the settler projects of forced assimilation and cultural genocide masqueraded as “education.” American Indian youth experience multiple forms of marginalization in the settlercolonial school system. Particularly, exclusionary school discipline (e.g., out-of-school suspension, ex pulsion, and referrals to law enforcement) has been used as a tool of exclusion through which Indigenous bodies are erased from the learning space and white settler-colonial privilege and property are reinforced and perpetuated. Despite complexity in disproportionality patterns across states, districts, and schools, nationally American Indian students are more likely to receive frequent and harsher sch ool discipline compared to their white counterparts  (Whitford et al., 2019). Racial disproportionality is a byproduct of settler-colonial practices that spatially arrange geography of opportunity (Tate, 2008) based on racial lines a nd is also a tool of erasure to uphold settler-colonial power privileging whiteness over indigeneity. To disrupt the racialized sch ool discipline system, it is imperative to investigate how power, privilege, and marginalizati on are indexed in local social, spatial, and histor ical contexts simultaneously (Soja, 2010; Tuck & McKenzie, 2015).

To address settler-colonial violence embedded in disciplinary policies  and practices, a formative intervention, Indigenous Learning Lab (ILL), was im plemented in a close collaboration with an Anishina abe Tribal government at Newhope High School (NHS) (Pseudonyms for all name of places and people in this paper) in a Midwest state between 2019 and 2024. As a comm unity participatory design project, the ILL facilitated a critical design praxis of local school stakeholders —i.e., reflecting on systemic tensions a nd contradictions entrenched in the existing school system and taking collective action to co-design locally meaningful solutions in response to the needs, goals, and visions of school  stakeholders (Afacan et al., 2021; Ko et al., 2022; Ko et al., 2023). The ILL was a systemic transformation through an inclusive prob lem-solving space that center ed the multiple voices, perspectives, and histories of Ame rican Indian students and families, who together wi th school professionals design ed a new school behavioral  support system that reflect ed Indigenous epistemologies and ontologies (Ko et al., 2023). This study documents how ILL members leveraged their individual and coll ective histories and experiences in a dialogic space to di srupt: The settler-colonial school space that is socially, historically, spatially, and politically constructed to u phold white power and privilege; and color neutral and technocratic diagnosis of and solutions to racial d isparities in behavioral and academic outcomes. Thr ough the ILL, part icipants, in particular Anishinaabe members, formed Thirdspace —a generative space filled with survivance, transgression, and new imaginations (hooks, 1989; Soja, 1996; Vizenor, 2008).

## Theoretical framework

To address racial disproportionality within social-spatial-historical contexts of settler colonialism in the Anishinaabe nation, this study was epistemologically grounded in cultural historical activity theory (CHAT) to racial disproportionality in school discipline s temming from the intellectual legacy of Vygotsky and social constructivism (1993; 1978). CHAT positions learnin g and humandevelopment as inseparable from the soc ial, historical, and geographical contexts where people learn and develop as participants of multiple cultu ral activity systems such as family, community, and education pr ofessionals (Engeström, 2016; Rogoff, 2003). CHAT considers social, historical, and spatial underpinn ings that materialize in the form of cultural tools  used in disciplinary processes (e.g., a school’s behavioral expectations, rules about referral processes, personnel involved in the process, artifacts, and division of labor). Within the cultural-historical approach, racial disproportionality is a byproduct of systemic discrimination: both a result and tool that sustains settler colonial power (Lomawaima & McCarty, 2006). The overrepresentation of American Indian studen ts in discipline is inseparable from the historical landscape of settler colonialism materia lized in day-to-day and institutional interactions, such as the struggle over land and water, and Indigenous sovereignty. Coupled with the cultural-historical approach to racial disproportionality, this study centered Indigenous educational sovereignty through an interdisciplinar y epistemological grounding informed by decolonizatio n theory (Brayboy, 2005; Tuck & McKenzie, 2015) and critical geography (Lefebvre, 1991; Soja, 1996; Vel ez & Solórzano, 2017). The cross-pollination of these frameworks is powerful to investigate how settler c olonialism historically produces and perpetuates ra cialized space through everyday schooling practices. This th eoretical synthesis helped identify how Indigenous local stakeholders exercised their transformative agency to explore the new spatial possibility of resistanc e and sovereignty represented by the systemic design process. Thirdspace We utilized the thinking of Henri Lefebvre (1991) and Edward Soja (1996)  to establish the basis of critical geography, specifically the concept of Thirdspace. Lefebvre's trialectical relations of space, which encompass perceived and lived spaces, serve as a foundation f or understanding of Thirdspace as a hybrid space wi th radical openness. Perceived space, also known as spatial pr actice (Lefebvre, 1991) or Firstspace (Soja, 1996),  refers to the tangible, everyday environments that individuals experience through their senses, such as parks, schools, and sidewalks. These spaces are often taken for granted and shaped by social technocrats, such as urbandesigners and policymakers. Conceiv ed space, also referred to as representations of sp ace (Lefebvre, 1991) or Secondspace (Soja, 1996), encompasses the conceptual imagination of space by those in positions of power, such as architects and planners or school policy makers who design and  assign space based on their expertise and knowledg e. This creates orders that users of space typically accept without much resistance, as exemplified by the physical layout of schools or cities, and the proximity of reservat ions to educational resources. The lived space (Lefebvre, 1991) or Thirdspace (Soja, 1996), is the mental imagination in physical space. It is the space of sense-making. The lived space encompasses the perceived and the conceived s pace yet simultaneously transcends them. Soja, buil ding upon Lefebvre’s spatial trialectics, situated Lefebvre’s  lived space or spaces of representation as a hybri d space (Bhabha, 1994); a gold mine of counter space where “…resistance to the dominant order arising precisel y from their subordinate, peripheral or marginalized posit ioning” (Soja, 1996 p. 68). This counter space is w hat Soja termed Thirdspace.

Tribal epistemology We paired the concept of Thirdspace with TribalCrit. TribalCrit recognizes the liminality of American In dian students and families as well as their historical a nd everyday challenges to settler-colonial practices. Resistance to settler-colonialism may create emancipatory space or the Th irdspace of sovereignt y that transcends control over lands (Bruyneel, 2007). Sovereignty is a diale ctical relationship between resistance and place: “ The ability of a group of people to make their own decisions and control their own lives in relation to the space where they reside and/or that they envision as their own” (Bru yneel, 2007, p. 23). The Thirdspace of sovereignty surpasses the dichotomy of colonial binary rules: Indigenous people’s location as inside or outside of the system. Given the context of the Newhope community, in this paper, we document how ILL members leveraged their individual and collective agency utilizing their lived experiences  embedded in their specific socio-spatial-historical context to form Thirdspace, within which they challenged the existing settler colonial power and privilege embedded within school disciplinary policies and practices and desi gned a locally meaningful school level behavioral s upport system.

The present study Socio-spatial-historical contexts of Newhope High School American Indian students in NHS are overrepresented  in exclusionary disciplines. In the 2019-2020 school year in which this study occurred, NHS’s enrollment was 70.6% white, 21% American Indian, 3.2% Latine, 1% Asian, 0.1% Black or African American, and 3.7 % from two or more races (Department of Public Instruction, 20 20). While American Indian students constituted one-fifth of the student enrollment, they received 64.3% of in-school suspensions and 62.8% of out-of-school suspensions (Newhope High School Discipline Data, 2019). What are visible as school discipline problems in N HS have socio-spatial-historical roots that are undetachable from Indigenous, in this case the Anishinaabe’s struggle over rights to space, ancestral land, control and exercise of treaty rights, political autonomy, economic-self-sufficiency and a desire to revive and preserve cultural heritage; struggle over recognition and control over one’s own life (Silvern, 1995). The Newhope region itself is often known as the “deep north”. In the 1980s, NHS and the city of Newhope was the epicenter of an antiIndian movement known as the “Walleye War”, triggered by treaty rights dispute (Nesper, 2002). White protestors chanted slogans such as “ Save a walleye, spear an Indian ” during protests at boat landings at lakes where t he Anishinaabe spearfish. The Anishinaabe community members risked their lives fishing. The dysfunctional sociospatial-historical climate at the community level  accumulated to deep distrust and broken relationshi ps between Natives and non-Natives at NHS. Grounded in the socio-spatial-historical context of Newhope, this paper is aimed to investigate how the ILL members centered the racialized day-to-day and institutional experiences within and outside of school in their collective efforts to create a new culturally responsive behavioral support system.

## Method of data collection and analysis

ILL had four phases: design, implementation, adaptation, and sustainability. In this study, we examined the design phase which took place in the 2019-2020 academic year. 14 school community members participated in the study (See Table 1).

As such, in the facilitation of each sess ion, careful planning was undertaken to ensure that facilitation  techniques, artifacts, and discussion prompts stra tegically reflected the principles of TribalCrit and Thirdspace, focusing on the socio-spatial-historical lived experiences of ILL participants, particularly the Anishinaabe memb ers. As a democratic inclusive design process, ILL was a community-driven systemic design methodology through which di verse local stakeholders collectively and critically examined oppressive school systems contr ibuting to racial disparities. The process of ILL f ollowed a dialectical process of problem identification, and systemic design (Bal et al., 2018) (See Figure 1). ILL members analyzed the school’s existing behavior system, modeled their envisioned system, examined the new model, then prepared for implementation of new policies and practices. For the data analysis, we collaboratively transcribed the video recordings of the 10 ILL sessions and use the transcriptions as our data. We followed an iter ative approach for data analysis, guided by key the oretical frameworks and our research question: How did Indig enous Learning Lab members create Thirdspace —a space of resistance—to address racial disproportionality in school discipline? We utilized a three-leveled coding strategy for data analysis. Initially, each author individually conducted open coding for the transcriptions of ILL meetings, generating initial codes informed by theories: Thir dspace and TribalCrit. Subsequently, weekly meeting s were convened to discuss and refine the initial coding, allowing for creativity in relation to the research question and theoretical frameworks. Notes were taken on each au thor’s initial coding, and a list of potential code s with definitions and examples of quotes was created, for ming the basis for the codebook  (Miles & Huberman, 1994; Saldaña, 2021). The first round of data analysis le d to the creation of a codebook informed by critica l geography and TribalCrit, as well as the data.

In the second round of coding, we collectively reviewed and refined the initial codes and their definitions, using examples from the ILL sessions and supporting literature (Creswell & Creswell, 2018). Disagreements in coding were resolved through discussions and consensus-building techniques, such as proposing code mergers or expansions. Coding reliability was ensured by establishing inter-coder consensus through ongoing discussions in research group meetings to resolve differences, fol lowing established guidelines. In the final coding process, we refined and identified the themes discussed in this paper as the ways ILL members leveraged their lived experience to create Thirdspace and (re)claimed their space in education as knowledge makers and designers to address racial disproportionality in school discipline.

## Results

The present study theoretically guided by cultural-historical approaches to racial disproportionality, TribalCrit, and critical geography as we sought to answer our r esearch question: How did ILL members form Thirdspa ce to address racial disproportionality in school discipl ine? We identified four strategies employed by ILL members:

(a) challenging race-neutral repres entations of space, (b) mapping out dystopia, (c) r evitalizing tribal

epistemology, and (d) mapping out a “real utopian space.” Challenging race-neutral representation of space ILL members collectively engaged in a democratic pr ocess of challenging the assumption of neutrality of school disciplinary and academic achievement data. In the first ILL session, the researchers as the ILL facilitators asked the NHS principal, Mr. Archer, to present academic and behavioral data. This presentation served as a stimulus in the questioning stage. During his presentation, the principal was firm that NHS was the best school among the schools serving a similar demographic of youth in t he Newhope region because NHS had the highest average ACT score, and American Indian students had the hig hest graduation rate. Mr. Archer situated NHS as a raceneutral space: All students regardless of racial/et hnic groups thrived in the school. However, an Anis hinaabe member pushed for a deeper look at the data, situating the academic and behavioral achievement within the deeply embedded racialized practices of teaching and learning: Diana Prince: Do you have this broken down by the feeder schools? Kevin Archer: No, no. We could, I suppose, Diana.

Here, Ms. Prince challenged a race-neutral representation of data by requesting further disaggregation of the data. Mr. Archer proceeded to present school discipline data with two categories of students: American Indian students and the complete student body. Ms. Prince requested  the principal to present the school’s di scipline data disaggregated by race for all racial/ethnic groups,  not just for American Indian students to see the d istribution of causes of disciplinary referrals across racial/ethn ic groups. The members concluded the first session of the IL L meeting by acknowledging a need to present disaggre gated data for reasons of referral such as threaten ing behavior, aggression, or harassment data in the nex t ILL meeting. When discussing the suspension data which directly compared American Indian and white students rather than American Indian students to the whole student body, NHS administrators started to develop a diffe rent perspective towards the school, in particular the experiences of American Indian students in NHS: Moving from the school as the b est school in the region to the school as a tough place for American Indian students to attend. The humility of the NHS administrators to develop a different perspective by listening to the request  from American Indian members became a catalyst for further exploration of data with the purpose of challenging  the representation of the school as a neutral spac e. Members also indicated ongoing resistance against the neutr ality and collectively voiced out their concerns until data is disaggregated and disparities is visible (Brayboy, 2005; Soja, 1996). The critical pushback from the ILL members regarding the presentation of data had revealed a r eality that the school administrator may not have b een fully cognizant of or may have been refusing to see.

Mapping out dystopia We referred to mapping out dystopia  as a process wherein the  ILL members collectively delineated everyday school disciplinary practices from referral to disc iplinary consequences as well as carefully unpacked  racialized practices across interconnected contexts of school and community, history and present. Mapping out d ystopia foregrounding NHS as a physical site of American In dian struggle and historical trauma continued as ot her members collectively shared data. For example, when the school’s American Indian mentor Mr. Jeremiah shared his perspectives regarding racial disproportionality in school discipline, he situate d the overrepresentation of Anishinaabe students in NHS as inseparable from the historical trauma and distrust lingering in the community of Newhope at large.

We as Native people have struggled for many years, and I see it. I have PTSD at that school because where I sit is probably 20 feet from where I had my first confrontation with non-members over treaty rights. And I tell my students that where you are is a hotbed of racism that was in the school. Drawing on the settler colonial history, Mr. Jeremiah recognized the socio-spatial-historical contexts of Newhope not simply as a background, but the ground where in equities take place (Tuck & McKenzie, 2015). That i s, the school is a complex social and physical site where Indigenous students face ongoing struggles, reflect ed in dayto-day relationships marked by political tensions over treaty rights and pervasive distrust. Revitalizing tribal epistemology The third strategy ILL members utilized to form Thirdspace was by recognizing and integrating tribal knowledge, and value systems into the ILL process. It is important to note that the utilization of Anishinaabe cultural practices was far from appropriation or exotism. Rather they are key cultural artifacts suggested by American In dian members of ILL as critical tools to ground the meetings. We always  asked for permission and suggestions from Mr. Jeremiah and other elders if using certain cultural practices was appropriate for the ILL. An example of the integration of the Indigenous value system was through the smudging ceremony. Mr. Jeremiah as the American Indian mentor and a Native  Anishinaabe agreed to perform the ceremony at the commencement of each ILL session. Anishinaabe members explained that smudging—burning sage and directing the smoke over one’s body and face–was critical for two reasons: First, smudging symbolically cleansed negative energy and thoughts, preparing for dialogue that mi ght cause the ILL members to recollect memories of racism, trauma, and pain. Second, smudging signified bringi ng positive energy to a meeting space, encouraging honesty and bravery to heal the historical trauma of schooling, and building a politicized alliance among the ILL members. Mr. Jeremiah addressed the significance of performing the smudging ceremony in the school (space): I’ve never thought I would be able to do this in this room here. This was the principal’s room when I went to school here. It’s a first for me and it’s an honor to bring these items out to a place that never really had good feelings here at school. We’re breaking ground and we’re breaking things that needed to be done a long time ago.

In the history of the school, this was the first time the school has had a smudging ceremony in the ad ministrative space. That in and of itself signifies an enormous gap in the relationship between the Anishinaabe community and the settler colonial school. Encouraging and partic ipating in Anishinaabe cultural practices in the school space signified hope and readiness to change and heal.

Mapping out a “real utopian space” A utopian space is an ideal yet unreal space (Foucault, 1984). In this study, instead of “indulging in utopiandreams we must accommodate to practical realities” (Wright, 2010, p. 4).  Rather than solely critiquing the s ystem and dreaming about the ideal school discipline system, ILL members began to create the ideal school discipline system that they envisioned. Mapping out the “real utopian  space” of a new, behavioral support system was the stage where members spent the majority of their time in I SLC. Between session four to nine and one sub-committee meeting, ILL members designed a new school disciplinary system t hat was responsive to Anishinaabe students’ and families’ diverse goals, histories, cultural practices, and needs. Members worked in small groups ensuring the inclusion of various roles (e.g., student, teacher, administrator) in each group to map out their ideal school system (see Figure 2) using multiple stimuli such as the school’s exist ing school discipline system, discipline data, and participant perspectives. Mapping out a real utopian space include d mapping out transformative or big dreams and hopes such as the new culturally responsive school discipline system as well as everyday dreams or suffering (la petite misère) (Bourdieu et al.,1999).

Figure 2 Newhope High School’s Culturally Responsive School Behavioral Support System The new behavioral support system (the big dreams) that the ILL members designed for NHS consisted of three layers encompassing community, school, and  classroom spaces (see Figure 3). Members defined t hree pillars of the culturally responsive behavioral support system as:  (a) relationship building within NHS among students, teachers, and staff, as well as beyond th e school space, particularly with tribal entities a nd families; (b) prioritizing prevention of perceived bad behaviors rather than the old system of “wait and punish” (i. e., wait for behavioral incidences and use punishment as a react ion to the perceived problematic behaviors); and (c) communication in response to demands for improving connection and relationship-building among educators, administrators, students, and families. Broken relationship s and distrust were foundational to the schooling experience of Anishinaabe students and therefore, tackling this barrier was a collective priority. In addition to the big dreams and hopes, members al so identified suggestions to address the everyday dreams through day-to-day interactions. For example, students and educato rs, who have formed allies with American Indian students and families asked for genuine day-to-day interactions in school. Ms. Jane stated: “My hope was that all students will feel heard and represented and seen. I think a lot of times there are kids who don't feel seen in the school”. It appeared that  what ILL members deemed as a crit ically needed change could be understood as relationship building that should have been done in the very first place. ## Discussion

The design of school disciplinary systems and other  local-level school and policy reforms have traditionally marginalized the perspectives of local stakeholders, including school-level educators, students, and parents. This exclusion stems from the historical conceptualization of school disciplinary systems as conceived spaces, wherein bureaucratic authorities such as school districts, states, and ext ernal experts are viewed as the sole architects of reforms and behavioral norms. Within this conceptua lization, school discipline is often perceived as n eutral, devoid of spatial, historical, and contextual consi derations. However, this paper argues that school d isciplinary systems can transition from exclusive technocratic spaces to inclusive environments where historically marginalized groups, particularly Indigenous commun ities, are recognized as experts and active partici pants in shaping their education al experiences. By challenging conventional spatial  concepts as either imagined or material, ILL members introduced new spatial imaginaries that o ffer alternatives to settler-colonial educational systems. They demonstrated that school behavioral s upport systems and local policy reforms can transce nd the confines of settler colonial norms. While school di scipline remains influenced by conflicting ideologi es and aspirations, it also possesses the potential to cre ate a representational space known as Thirdspac e, wherein negotiation and collaboration can occur.

This paper contributes to the field of learning sci ence by emphasizing the importance of foregrounding socio-spatial-historical contexts, not merely as geographical loc ations, but as dynamic settings where social interactions, power dynamics, struggles, hopes, and  dreams unfold in research design, data collection,  and analysis. Additionally, it advocates for interdisci plinary and transdisciplinary perspectives to delve  deeper into the nuanced processes of collective transformative decision-making.
