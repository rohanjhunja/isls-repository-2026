# Comparing Teachers’ Perceived and Observed Learning in Asynchronous Online Professional Development: The Role of Self-Regulated Learning Strategies

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teacher learning in asynchronous online professional development (PD) is often assessed through self-reported instruments, despite uncertainties regarding teachers’ ability to accurately self-assess their learning gains in such settings. This study compared middle school mathematics teachers’ self-perceived and observed learning gains in content knowledge (CK) and pedagogical content knowledge (PCK) after participating in an asynchronous online PD program. By analyzing data collected from 57 teachers, we found a weak and nonsignificant correlation between teachers’ perceived learning gains and observed learning gains. Teachers who frequently monitored their understanding of the learning content tended to underestimate their improvement of CK from the PD program while those using cognitive learning strategies (i.e., organize and elaborate) often overestimated it, controlling for their baseline knowledge level. These findings highlight methodological concerns in using self-reports to evaluate online learning outcomes and the potential distortion in identifying effective learning strategies.

## Introduction

Teacher professional development (PD) is one type o f learning opportunities for in-service teachers to develop knowledge and skills necessary for quality teaching  (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017). Changes in teacher knowledge and practice are key indicators of the ef fectiveness of teacher PD programs, which are typic ally assessed through self-reported instruments (e.g., questionnaires) or obje ctive measures (e.g., knowledge assessments, classroom observation). Self-reported surveys are particularly prevalent in large-scale asynchronous online teacher PD (e.g., Erickson et al., 2012; Gri ffin et al., 2018; Reeves & Pedulla, 2013; Sheridan  & Wen,

2021) due to its cost-effectiveness, ease of administration, and less anxiety-provoking format. However, to which

extent does teachers’ self-reported learning reflect their actual learning in an asynchronous online PD program? The question has not been explored yet.

When we discuss which asynchronous online PD progra ms have a positive impact on teacher learning (i.e., changes in teacher knowledge and skills), neglecting the differences between measures used to capture such learning can be problematic. Firstly, if self-reported and objective measures of teacher learning  are capturing different constructs, relying solely on teachers’ s elf-reports may result in outcomes that don’t truly ref lect what we aim to understand. Self-reported learning reflects individual’s evaluation of their current or improvement in knowledge and skills about one particular construct  (Sitzmann et al., 2010), which essentially reflect s ones’ confidence or feeling of knowing something (Sangster et al., 2013) rather than their observed cognitive learning. Secondly, if both self-reported and objective measures indeed capture the same construct (e.g., teacher content knowledge) but differ significantly in accuracy, us ing just one tool might overestimate or underestima te the program’s effectiveness on teacher learning and further hinder our effort to i dentify features that characterize effective asynchronous online PD programs or learni ng strategies that scaffold online learning. Moreov er, the accuracy of self-reported knowledge may also depend on indiv iduals’ current knowledge level as research has revealed that people with low expertise tend to ove restimate their ability, which is also known as the  DunningKruger Effect (Dunning, 2011). Doubts about the use of self-reported instruments for assessing individuals’ cognitive abilities have been widespread across fields (Bacon, 2016; Krauskopf & Forssell, 2018; Sitzmann et al., 2010). Yet, only r ecently teacher education researchers have begu n to pay attention to whether teachers could accura tely self-assess their learning in PD programs (Copur-Gencturk & Thacker, 2021; Gardner-Neblett et al., 2020). A recent study reported a barely zero correlation between self-reported and direct assessments of 545 teachers’ ma thematical knowledge gains from 24 traditional face-to-face PD programs (Copur-Gencturk & Thacker, 2021). In the context of asynchronous online PD programs, we found only one study compared teachers’ perceived and observed knowledge, in which the authors found no correlatio n between early childhood teachers’ knowledge about  dual language learners and what they believe they know before the PD and a very weak correlation between teachers’ perceived and observed knowledge after the PD (Gardner-Neblett et al., 2020). However, the focus of knowledge measure in the study was on current level of knowledge rather than improvement in the teacher knowledge after the PD. The subtle difference between the focus of knowledge measure matters, given prior work found t he correlation between self-assessments and direct assessments of cognitive ability decreased significantly when the measure was focused on knowledge gains.

Additionally, how teachers regulate their learning processes in asynchronous online PD programs may influence both the accuracy of their self-perceived learning and their observed learning from  the program. Self-regulated learning (SRL) is generally defined as le arners’ management and regulation of their learning  process through strategy use (Greene & Azevedo, 2007; Pintr ich, 2004; Winne, 2001). SRL strategies, particular ly metacognitive monitoring, could involve teachers in  monitoring and evaluating their own understanding about certain knowledge and thus become more aware of the ir understanding and recognize gaps in their knowle dge. Theoretically, it can be hypothesized that teachers  who frequently monitor their understanding of the learning content may ha ve more accurate evaluation of their learning gains  from PD programs. However, empirical research is scarce to substantiate this hypothesis among in-service teachers in asynchronous online learning environments. It is also worth noting that although some studies have explored the relationship between teachers’ SRL and self-perceived or directly assessment knowledge in onlin e learning environments, they did not take consideration of teachers’ baseline knowledge level (e.g., Fan et al., 2021; Huang & Lajoie, 2021).

To address limitations above, this study aimed to e xamine the alignment between middle-school mathematics teachers’ self-perceived and observed learning through participati ng in an asynchronous online PD program. Teacher learning is a multi-faceted construct, in this study, we only focused on changes in teachers’ CK and PCK as teacher learning outcomes from the PD pr ogram. In addition, we investigated how teachers’ engagement in SRL activities (i.e., cognitive learn ing strategies and monitoring) related to both thei r perceived and observed knowledge gains, as well as the alignm ent between these two outcome measures. The study contributes to current understanding of the assessm ent of teacher learning in asynchronous online lear ning environments and how teachers’ employment of different SRL strategies relate to their online learning outcomes. Asynchronous online PD differs from in-person PD greatly, primarily due to the dynamic nat ure of interactions among teachers, PD instructors, and the learning en vironment. Such variations may impact the way teachers perceive their learning, as well as their observed learning (Sitzmann et al., 2010). With the increasing demand for effective asynchronous online PD for teachers and t he methodological issue regarding using self-reported instruments in evaluating traditional PD, it is crucial to investigate the differences in outcome measures to ensure a common basis when we try to understand which asynchronous online learning opportunities are more effective for teachers.

## Conceptual framework

Teacher learning in PD programs can be multifaceted. In the study, we only focused on changes in teach ers’ CK and PCK as teacher learning outcomes from the async hronous online PD program. CK and PCK are crucial f or the quality of their instructional practice and students’ learning opportunities (Baumert et  al., 2010; Hill & Chin, 2018). In this study, teachers’ CK is conceptualize d as an understanding of the conceptual underpinnin gs of the mathematics content being taught at school and the ability of solving mathematics problems in the scho ol curriculum by reasoning and evaluating different mathematical situations (Copur-Gencturk & Tolar, 2022; Krauss et al., 2008). PCK is conceptualized as the knowledge that make mathematics content comprehensible for students (Ball et al., 2008; Shulman, 1986), which involves the knowledge about students’ mathematical thinking  and instructional strategies for effective mathematics teaching (Ball et al., 2008; Copur-Gencturk & Li, 2023; CopurGencturk & Tolar, 2022). Self-regulated learning (SRL) has been recognized as an important factor influencing learners’ learning outcomes in online environments (Broadbent & Poon, 2015). SRL refers to a process of regulating one’s motivation, cognition, and metacognition by employi ng different learning strategies (Pintrich, 2000; W inne, 2001). In this study, we limit the scope of the res earch to teachers’ employment of cognitive learning  strategies and metacognitive monitoring in asynchronous online  PD programs. We define cognitive  learning strategies as the strategies teachers use to organize and interpr et learning content targeted by PD (Pintrich, 2000). We define metacognitive monitoring as the teachers’ behaviors  to assess and evaluate their understanding of the learning content during the PD learning process. According to Pintri ch (2000), cognitive learning strategies (e.g, organization, elaboration) aid in handling and understanding of information. Meanwhile, metacognitive activities such as monitoring helps people frequently assess t heir understanding of learning content by evaluatin g the discrepancy between current knowledge level and the  desired knowledge level. Therefore, we expected th at teachers who engaged in more frequent usage of cognitive learning strategies and metacognitive monitoring would be more accurate in their self-assessment of learning from the PD program and produced better learning outcomes measured by direct assessments, depending on their baseline knowledge level. Current study The study used data collected from 57 grade 6 and g rade 7 teachers who participated in a fully asynchr onous computer-based teacher professional development program that provides opportunities for  teachers to interact with and receive feedback from a virtual pedagogica l agent based on natural language processing. The s tudy aimed to answer two research questions:

(1) To what extent is teachers’ self-reported learning gains aligned with directly asses sed learning gains of

content knowledge and pedagogical content knowledge in this asynchronous online PD program?

(2) To what extent are teachers’ self-regulated learning strategies (i.e., cognitive lear ning strategies and

metacognitive monitoring) related to the alignment between teachers’ learning gains of content knowledge and pedagogical content knowledge measured by self-reports and direct assessments?

(3) What is the relationship between teachers’ self-regulated learning strategies (i.e., cognitive lear ning

strategies and metacognitive monitoring) and their self-reported knowledge gains and directly assessed knowledge gains respectively?

The asynchronous online teacher professional development The PD program was developed to enhance middle scho ol mathematics teachers’ CK and PCK of proportional reasoning, a content area where teachers typically struggle with and need additional support for effective teaching (Copur-Gencturk et al., 2022; Izsák &  Jacobson, 2017; Orrill & Brown, 2012). This program  consisted of two modules targeting CK and PCK, respectively. The CK module has five submodules with a total of 35 activities to enhance teachers’ understanding of the key concepts  related to ratios and proportional relationships. The PCK module included three submodules (i.e., planning, i mplementing, and assessing and reflecting on a less on) with 18 multi-stage activities such as selecting and adapting tas ks that were aligned with learning goals, analyzin g students’ work and responses, and reflecting on the ir instructional strategies. The program was operat ed in an intelligent tutoring system derived from AutoTutor, in which teachers received tutoring on each learning activity in natural language through a virtual agent supported by natural language process ing (see Copur-Gencturk et al., 2024 for details about PD system design).

## Participants

We recruited middle school mathematics teachers acr oss states in the U.S. by contacting teachers via s chool emails, as well as an anonymized link on social med ia. We invited qualified teachers (i.e., teaching g rade 6/7 in the academic year of 2021-2022, indicating the availability for completing the PD pro gram in 2021 summer) to participate in this study. The analytical sample included 57 teachers who provided self-evaluation of their learning gains from the program and completed both pre-and post-assessments on their knowledge. Most of the teachers in the analytical sample are female (82.40%), White  (70.18%). Over half of teachers have a specialized  teaching credential in math (57.89%) and never had experience participating in online PD programs (61.40%).

## Measures

Observed knowledge gains Teachers’ CK was measured by using a subset of item s on ratio and proportional relationships developed  by the Learning Mathematics for Teaching (LMT) Project (Hi ll et al., 2004). These items are typical school ma th problems related to key concepts in ratio  and proportional relationships. The measure include d 25 items (the maximum score in the test is 25 points), with a rel iability of.70 (i.e., Cronbach’s alpha). Teachers took the same test prior to and after completing the PD program. The difference between teachers’ pre-and post-test scores was used as an indicator of their observed gains in CK (M=0.94, SD=2.38). Teachers’ PCK was measured by using two multiple-choice items developed by the LMT project (Hill et al., 2004) as well as eight constructed-response items developed by Kersting (2008) (Cronba ch’s alpha was 0.59 for the 10-item scale; the maximum score in the test is 26 points). For each constructed-response item, teachers were asked to watch a short video on teach er-student interacting on a problem about ratio and proportional relationships. They were then asked to  analyze the mathematical understanding of  students in the video clips or provide suggestions to improve teaching shown in the video clips to facilitate students’ learning of the concept (i.e., two key components of PCK). Teachers took the same test prior to and after completing the PD program. Te achers’ responses were blind-coded by two raters using a 4-point rubric based on prior research capturing teachers’ PCK (Copur-Gencturk & Li, 2023). The data were blinded before coding so that raters were unaware of the teachers’ group assignments or wheth er the responses were from the pre-or post-test. A Cohen’s kappa statistics of 0.81 indicates a strong agreeme nt between two raters. The difference between teach ers’ preand post-test scores was used as an indicator of their observed gains in PCK (M = 0.82, SD =2.63). Self-reported knowledge gains To capture teachers’ self-reported knowledge gains from the PD program, we created a survey instrument with 3 Likert-type items (Cronbach’s alpha=0.83). To enhance the alignment between what was captured through self-reported instruments and direct assessments, the self-reported survey items asked teachers to indicate ch anges in specific areas of knowledge they utilized while responding to items in direct assessments. For example, we asked teachers to self-report changes in their understanding about student s’ mathematical thinking and learning of proportional relationships, which is one aspect of PCK teachers drew on when answering items in our di rect assessments (i.e., items asking teachers to analyze  students’ mathematical thinking shown in the video  clips). Specifically, teachers were asked to rate the exten t (1 Not at all-5 Great extent) to which this PD program improved their knowledge of ratio and proportional relationships (i.e., CK), understanding of how children think and learn about ratios and proportional relationshi ps (i.e., knowledge of students’ mathematical think ing), and knowledge of instructional representations and strategies to teach ratios and proportional relationship). Teachers’ scores on the CK item indicated their self-reported knowledge gains in CK. Teachers’ total scores on the othe r two PCK items indicated their self-reported knowledge gains in PCK. Test-survey alignment measure We first standardized teachers’ self-reported and test scores on the improvement in CK and PCK, then calculated the test-survey alignment by subtracting teachers’ standardized survey scores from their standardized test scores. Thus, teachers with positive test-survey alignment scores provided a lower self-rating of knowledge gains relatively to their observed knowledge gains. In co ntrast, teachers with negative test-survey alignment scores provided a higher self-rating of knowledge gains. An alignment score  equal to zero indicates scores for self-reported measure and direct assessments are the sam e standardized distance from their respective means (unstandardized self-reported CK gains M=3.39 on a 1-5 scale; unstandardized self-reported PCK gains M=6.63 on a 0-10 scale).

Self-regulated learning strategies We measured two sub-scales of SRL strategies ↓ cognitive learning strategies and metacognitive mon itoring, through a self-reported survey instrument after teachers completed  the PD. The survey comprised eight Likert-type items, adapted from Pintrich’s (1990) Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire. Each item was rated on a 1-4 scale (1=strongly disagree; 4=strongly agree), as king teachers to indicate their level of agreement with statements about the use of specific learning strategies. Specifically, the scale of cognitive learning strategies had five items on teachers’ use of organization and ela boration strategies with a reliability of 0.74 (e.g., I review my notes and try to find the most important content in  this online PD). The scale of metacognitive involv ed three items with a reliability of 0.80 (e.g., I monitor and evaluate what I unders tand by pausing at a regular interval or whenever needed while studying for this online PD).  We calculated an average score for the items to re present the extent of teachers’ employment of cognitive lea rning strategies (M=2.92; SD=0.60 on a 1-4 scale) and metacognitive monitoring (M=3.20; SD=0.61 on a 1-4 scale), respectively. Analytical approach To investigate the alignment between teachers’ self-reported knowledge gains and directly assessed know ledge gains, we conducted a Pearson correlation analysis between teachers’ scores from the self-reported instrument and those from direct assessments on CK and PCK imp rovement, respectively. We then performed two sets of regression analyses for CK and PCK, respectively, to explore the extent to which teachers’ usage of SRL strategies are related to the alignment between teachers’ self-reported and directly  assessed knowledge gains. In the regression model for CK, the test-survey alignment is predicted by teachers’ usage of cognitive learning strategies and metacognitive monitoring, controlling for their  self-reported learning gains in CK and their baseline level of CK. Similar analysis was conducted for PCK. Lastly, to explore the relationship between teachers’ SRL strategies and their learning gains captured by two different measures, we conducted regression analyses for each outcome measure (i.e., self-reported and directly assessed knowledge gains in CK a nd PCK), in which cognitive learning strategies and metacognitive monitoring are variables of interest and teachers’ baseline level of CK and PCK are controlled variables.

## Results

The correlation between teachers’ self-reported gains and their gain scores based on direct assessments capturing the same construct was weak and insignificant (r = - 0.19, p = 0.16 for CK; r = 0.21, p = 0.12 for PCK). The result indicated the way teachers perceived their own knowledge gains did not reflect the knowledge gains measured by direct assessments and the underlying constructs assessed by these two measures may be different. Table 1 displayed how teachers’ usage of SRL strategies is related to their knowledge gains captured by their self-reports and direct assessments, as well as the alignment between scores on these two outcome measures. Overall, the results shown teachers’ u sage of cognitive learning strategies and metacogni tive monitoring were significantly related to observed CK gains and the alignment between teachers’ self-reported and observed CK gains. Specifically, while taking into account teachers’ initial level of CK, teachers who frequently used cognitive learning strategies appeared to have less observed learning of CK from the PD program (β = -1.45, p = 0.01). In contrast, teachers who frequently monitored their understanding of the learning content in the PD program seemed to have more observed learning of CK (β = 2.50, p < 0.001). Meanwhile, neither of the two SRL strategies showed a significant relationship with the observed improvement in teachers’ PCK after participating in the program, nor with their self-reported learning of both CK and PCK. In terms of t he accuracy of teachers’ self-assessments of learning gains, we found when controlling for teach ers’ self-reported CK gains and their initial level of CK, teachers who frequently use cognitive learning stra tegies had a tendency to overestimate their learnin g of CK from the program (β = -0.54, p = 0.03). However, teachers who frequently monitored  their understanding of the learning content in the PD program had a tendency t o underestimate their learning of CK from the progr am (β = 1.01, p < 0.001). Neither of the two SRL strategies was sig nificantly associated with the alignment of teacher s’ self-reported PCK gains and observed PCK gains.

## Discussion

This study compared 57 middle school mathematics te achers’ self-perceived and observed improvement of content knowledge (CK) and pedagogical content knowledge (P CK) from a fully asynchronous online professional development program. In line with previous research that compared adults’ self-reported and directassessed knowledge in workplace training (Copur-Gencturk & Thacker, 2021; Sitzmann et al., 2010), our study also found teachers’ self-reported improvement in CK and PCK did not accurate ly reflect their observed improvement in CK and PCK in the context of a fully asynchronous online PD program. The results s uggested that teachers’ self-reports and direct assessments captured different l atent construct. The absence of a significant correlation is concerning because an asynchronous o nline PD program deemed effective through teachers’  selfreports may not show the same level of effectivenes s when evaluated using direct assessments as the ou tcome measure. The body of research on traditi onal teacher PD programs often relies on large-scale PD impact studies that are based on teachers’ self-reports (e.g., Garet et al., 2001) to identify key features that characterize effective PD programs. However, in Kennedy’s (2016) systematic review on how teacher PD improves teaching, she found many popular design features are not associated wit h program effectiveness. As researchers in teacher education explore effective designs and delivery of online PD for teachers (e.g., Bragg et al., 2021; Reeves & Pedulla, 2013), this empirical research underscores the significanc e of selecting the outcome measures to evaluate the  impact of online PD programs.

Findings also suggested that SRL strategies could d ifferently influence various aspects of teacher learning (i.e., changes in CK and PCK in this study) from PD programs. In this study, we found that teachers’ use of cognitive learning strategies and metaco gnitive monitoring was significantly associated onl y with observed gains in CK, rather than PCK, when controlling for teachers’ initial level of observed knowledge. It i s possible that CK is often more factual and straightforward, making it easier to acquire through employing SRL strategies. In contrast, PCK is more complex, integrating both pedagogy and content, and may require more nuanced learning approaches. More specifically, our findings reveale d that teachers who regularly monitored their compr ehension of the learning content in the PD showed greater im provement in CK compared to those who rarely used monitoring as a learning strategy. This observation  aligns with previous research in the field of teac hers’ SRL in digital context (Huang & Lajoie, 2021; Kramarski & Michalsky, 2010). Unexpectedly, we observed that teachers who frequently adopted cognitive learning strategie s seemed to show less improvement in CK than those who infrequently used such strategies. The results migh t indicate that the cogniti ve learning strategies (i.e., organization and elaboration) captured in our study  were not effective for developing CK in math, lead ing to a situation where frequent use resulted in less impro vement. Since our assessments on CK emphasizes conc eptual understanding of math concepts over procedural knowledge (e.g., steps to solve a math problem), the specific cognitive strategies captured in this study such as  taking notes in general might not contribute to mo re learning. For example, if a teacher only took notes on proced ures for solving a problem without understanding co nceptual rules behind math problems in different situation, he/she may not gain a higher score in our assessmen ts after taking the PD program.

Lastly, we explored how teachers’ use of metacognitive monitoring and cognitive learning strategies was related to the alignment between their self-reported and observed improvement of CK and PCK. We  included teachers’ baseline knowledge and their self-reported knowledge gains as covariates to help us bett er understand the role SRL strategies plays in the accuracy of teachers’ self-assessments. Similar to findings above, the strategies were only significantly related to the alignment of  two outcomes measures on CK rather than PCK. Specifically, teachers who regularly monitored their comprehensio n of learning content tended to underestimate their improvement in CK. This finding is similar to the Dunning-Kruger effect (Dunning, 2011) suggesting that people with lower cognitive ability at a task often overestim ate their ability, whereas those with higher abilit y tend to underestimate it. Teachers who regularly monitor and reflect on their learning might become more aware  of the complexity of mathematics concepts  and their knowledge gaps (Pintrich, 2000; Winne, 20 01), leading them to underestimate their own improvement. It is also possible that as teachers engage in self-monitoring, their standards for personal performance also improve, which could result in a feeling that current improvement is not sufficient, even when they have made significant progress.

Overall, findings in our study have significant imp lications for assessing teacher learning, particula rly within asynchronous online environments. The absenc e of a correlation between teachers’ self-reported learning and directly assessed learning highlig hts a critical reminder for policymakers and practi tioners: paying attention to how outcome measures of PD programs are defined and operationalized when selecting appropriate professional training resources. Additionally, researchers should acknowledge the limitations inherent in different outcome measures when designing impact studies for asynchronous online PD programs. Neglecting to consider outcome measures might lead to erroneous conclusions about essential characteristics of successful PD programs and mislead practitioners’ choice of participating in P D programs. We recommend the use of multiple measur es to assess the desired outcomes of PD programs, as this approach may provide a more comprehensive and accurate understanding through data triangulation. We also acknowledged a few limitations in our study. First, the sample size in this study is small; Thus, the findings sho uld be interpreted with caution as it might not be generalized to a larger teacher population. Also, this study only focused on a few aspects of SRL strategies by using self-reported instruments. Yet, teachers may adopt varied learnin g strategies in response to different learning acti vities within an asynchronous online PD program. Our method of me asuring SRL was not able to capture th ese dynamic and real-time processes because the current learning system for the PD was not designed to understand or suppor t teachers’ SRL. There is a possibility that teachers could not accurately report their use of SRL strategies as well. Thus, there is a need for future research to delve deeper into the range of learning strategies that t eachers might use in asynchronous online learning environments by integrating system features that could record teachers’ realtime SRL activities. This e xploration is vital to understand and optimize teac her engagement and efficacy in diverse professional training settings.
