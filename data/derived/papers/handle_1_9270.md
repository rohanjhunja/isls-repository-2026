# What Happens When Collaborators Are Not In-Synch?

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Dual eye-tracking research has shown that during collaboration, “togetherness” is important. This togetherness can either be attentional and/or cognitive. Previous research has shown that convergence or synchronicity is beneficial for collaborative learning outcomes. However, during collaboration divergence or asynchronicity can also be fruitful. In this paper, we present an analytical shift to understand the collaborative processes during these asynchronous episodes. Such episodes are defined by lack of joint visual attention (JVA) or joint mental effort (JME) or both. JVA and JME are defined using Dual eye-tracking data. We analyze the actions and dialogues during these episodes to highlight the key differences between high and low performing dyads while they co-create concept-maps. Using the results, we propose JME-based gaze-aware systems to potentially increase the collaborative effectiveness.

## Introduction and background

Collaborative learning is a goal-oriented activity that requires coordination and conve rgence (Fischer & Mandl, 2005; Roschelle & Teasley, 1995). Interpersonal coordination occurs at multiple interre lated levels (behavior, language, representations, emotions, physiology, neuronal activity), and relies on the dyna mic perception-action coupling between interacting partners. It requires individuals to synchronize with what their partners are looking at, doing, saying, feeling, thinking, etc. Interpersonal coordination serves many cognitive and relational purposes, such as grounding, transactivity, intersubjective meaning-making, social cohesion and group regulation (Clark & Brennan, 1991; Vogel et al., 2016; Suthers, 2006; O’Donnell & Hmelo-Silver, 2013; Soller et al, 2005). Coordination and convergence go hand in hand. Individuals synchronize and coordinate their perceptions, actions and knowledge in order to converge towards a common goal. Convergence does not necessarily mean similarity. Convergence occurs when individuals build a shared representation of the s olution to the problem at hand, but also when they build a shared representation of what they perceive, do and think relative to the others (Hollingshead et al, 2009). Group members cannot be in permanent synchronization throughout the collaboration. The degree of convergence fluctuates from one level to another (e.g., at the same time, individuals may be looking at the same thing but have different representations of it) and also at e ach moment of the interaction. A collaboration is successful when partners generate and maintain sufficient tension between the convergence necessary to achieve the common goal and the divergence is necessary to pr oduce new and innovative ideas (Jorczak, 2011; Kapur et al., 2006).

Moreover, the collaboration to be beneficial, like with any skill, students must lear n how to collaborate (Rummel & Spada, 2005), making it important for learning technologies to provide socia l support in addition to the cognitive learning support (Weinberger et al, 2005) that students need to be succ essful. When students are collaborating, they do not all start with the same expectations about collaboration, a nd adaptive collaborative learning support (ACLS) may be needed to provide students with the correct support at the correct time (Walker et al 2011; Magnisalis, et al, 2011). ACLS can be used to adapt to the collaborati ve learning environment to provide appropriate support for the students by assessing student interactions, compa ring them to a set of productive interactions, and providing interventions that will guide students closer to a  productive interaction (Soller, et al, 2005; Walker, et al, 2011).

Researchers have used dual eye tracking (DUET) to study the creating and maintaining of common ground between collaborators. Common ground is one of the most crucial aspects of succe ssful collaborations (Clark & Brennan, 1991). Using eye-tracking, previous studies showed the relation be tween gaze and dialogue (Meyer et al, 1998; Griffin and Bock, 2000; Allopenna et al, 1998; Richardson et al, 2007). I n other studies, the mechanisms by which the collaborators create and maintain the common ground between them had been studies and was found to be correlated with the teams' performances (Jermann and Nuessli, 2012; Gergle and Clark, 2011; Cherubini et al, 2008;). DUET has been used as a tool to explain the socio-c ognitive mechanisms underlying collaborative learning (Jermann and Nuessli, 2012; Sharma et al, 2018; Olsen et al, 2018). Information extracted from DUET data has been used to explain collaboration quality (Schneider et al, 2019), collaborative taskperformance (Sangin et al., 2011; Jermann and Nuessli, 2012; Sharma et al, 2017), and collaborative learning gains (Olsen et al., 2020). Duet has also been used to explain certain proc esses related to collaborative learning as well, for example, repairs of misunderstanding (Cherubini et al., 2008), shar ed understanding (Richardson et al., 2007), and coordination (Brennan, et al., 2008). This motivated researchers to us e DUET as a method to provide collaborative awareness to the peers attempting to solve a given pr oblem (Schneider and Pea, 2015, CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 100 D’Angelo and Begel, 2017; D’Angelo and Gergle, 2016). In most of these studies, the basic outcome or the working hypothesis is that Joint Visual Attention (JVA) is a decent proxy of col laborative mechanisms. All of these studies emphasize a social extension of the eye-mind hypothesis (what you see is what you process) to “looking together is processing together”. However, this notion has not been verif ied in certain studies over the past few years (e.g., Belenky et al, 2014). Sharma et al (2021) proposed a novel metric for computing the similarity in the mental effort being exerted during the collaborative problem solving namely, Joint Mental Effort (JME). This metric computes the cognitive load similarity from the pupil data (Sharm a et al, 2021). Further, Sharma & Olsen (2022) showed that there is a causal relation between the joint visual attent ion and the joint mental effort for the high performing dyads but, there was only a correlation between the two m etrics for the low performing dyads. The above two mentioned contributions show that there is more to collaborative work than the joint visual attention (Sharma et al, 2021; Sharma & Olsen, 2022).

All the above works highlight the fact that there should be certain level of s ynchrony, attentional or cognitive, during collaboration and they also show that the groups/dyads with higher synchrony ac hieve high performance, high learning gains and have high collaboration quality. One collaborative learning theory is that students are able to assess and update their mental models of the domain by working with peers (Chi & Wylie, 2014).  Through the process of co-construction, students can reflect on their own ment al model to make repairs, incorporate their partner’s ideas into their model, and construct new knowledge by bui lding upon their partner’s ideas (Hausmann, Chi, & Roy, 2004). However, this does not always happen in the moment s of synchrony as these tasks include different moments of role-based and task-based division of labor (DoL, Jermann, 2004). These DoL strategies have not always been associated with moments of synchrony, att entional or cognitive (Jorczak, 2011; Kapur et al., 2006).

In this study, we are interested in moments of interpersonal asynchrony during interaction and their relation to collaborative learning. Specifically, we focus on moments of attentio nal asynchrony, i.e., moments when collaborative partners are not looking at the same object at the same time (the level of joint visual attention is low), and/or when they do not allocate the similar cognitive resources to meet the demands of the current task (the level of joint mental effort is low). Visual attention refers to the physiological and cognitive mechanisms that sort all incoming visu al stimuli to process only those that are relevant. Mental effort is the “in tensive aspect of attention” (Alnæs et al., 2014, p. 2). When the demands of the task increase, e.g., when attention to multiple items is required at the same time to solve the task, mental effort, i.e., the attentional resources required to complete the task, increases (Paas et al, 2003). Recent results have shown that the jo int visual attention (JVA) does not necessary correlate with Joint Mental Effort (JME), again challenging the social extension of the eye-mind hypothesis to “looking together is processing together” (Sharma et al., 2021; Sharma and Olsen, 2022). We use JVA and JME to define the episodes of attentional and cognitive synchrony and asynchrony; and further analyze these episodes based on dyad’s actions and discourse across different performance levels. Specifically, we address the following research question: What is the relation between pair performa nce and asynchronous collaborative signals at behavior, cognitive, and discourse levels?

## Participants, procedure, and data collection

A total of eighty-two master's students (16 females) participated in the pr esent study in pairs. During the study, all participants watched two videos about "resting membrane potential", a topic they were unfamiliar with before participating. The videos lasted 17.05 minutes in total. Videos provided the information needed to create concept maps. Participants were free to control the video player and there w ere no time restrictions while watching the videos. In average, viewers spent 20.05 minutes watching (SD = 3.54 minutes). Using IHMC CMap tools, peers collaborated to build a concept map based on the videos. Concept-maps were deve loped collaboratively over a period of 10 to 12 minutes. In this case, each student was working on their own computer, and they were able to communicate while their screens were synchronized, i.e., the students were  able to see what their partners were doing. Pairs were required to connect 14 preloaded concepts with their correct relationships using the Concept Map tool. It was also possible for them to add new concepts if they so desir ed. We collected the following data from our interaction with the concept map tool: 1) Eye-tracking data using two SMI remote eye-tracking devices (SMI RED 250) at a sampling rate of 250 Hz. For each participant, we use  a 5-point calibration and a 5-point validation mechanism. The BeGaze software was used to identify the fixa tions and saccades. 2) Students' dialogues using the system audio from one of the computers. 3) Concept map actions carried out by the dyad. Measurements Cognitive Similarity (Joint Mental Effort, JME): We calculated the students ’ JME, a measure of the cognitive load similarity using pupil diameter from the eye-tracking data. To calcula te JME, we first use the method CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 101 proposed by Duchowski et al. (2020) to compute the individual cognitive load.  Next, we discretize cognitive load to an integer value between zero and ten. Using the cognitive load for both peers in the dyad, we compute a crossrecurrence between two time-series, using the method proposed by Richardson et al (2007). Attentional Similarity (Joint Visual Attention, JVA): Joint visual attention (JV A): JVA is a measure of how similar two individual gaze patterns are. The similarity between the gaze pa tterns of two collaborating students is the similarity between the two proportionality vectors using the rever se function (1/(1+x)) of the correlation matrix of the two vectors (where x is the distance between the two pr oportionality vectors). The proportionality vector is created using the proportion of time spent on each concept  on the screen. A similarity value of 1 shows complete similarity between the two gaze patterns during a given tim e window. A lower value of similarity shows that the two participants spent less time looking at a similar set of objects on the screen during the same time window.

JVA-JME Episodes: We computed the JME-JVA episodes based on whether th e JME was high or low in a given time window of ten seconds, and whether the JVA was high or low in  the same time window of ten seconds (Sharma et al, 2018).  where this duration was deemed suitable for  defining such episodes. The high or low JME was determined by using a median-cut for the JME for each dyad. Similarly, the high or low JVA was determined by using a median-cut for the JVA for each dyad. Finally, there were four types of episodes: high JME low JVA, high JME high JVA, low JME high JVA, and low JME low JVA. Division of Labor: Division of labor is computed using the number actions taken on spec ific concepts by one member of the dyad and definition provided by Jermann (2004). Specifically, we c ompute the Sum of Differences (SD) and Sum of absolute differences (SAD) between members of a dyad using the formulae (1) and (2).

When the DoL strategy is classified as role-based, it means that one student did all the actions within a certain time window, implying that the other student had either been acting as a free-rider or was acting as a navigator. In other words, a role-based strategy has SAD between [0.5, 1] and SD between [0.33, 1] or [-1, -0.33] indicating that one student did everything within a certain time frame. In order to classify this DoL strategy as concurrent, the SAD must be in the range [0, 0.5] and the SD must be in the range [-0.33, 0.33] between the beginning and end of the time window. This indicates that the students were equally participating on the same concepts and making connections between them. There is also a third type of DoL strategy that is categorized as task-based if the SAD is within the range of [0.5, 1] and the SD is within the range [-0.33, 0.33] during the timeframe, which is an indication that each student was taking action on the concept map, but on a different concept, dur ing the time period. Taking a look at the actions that are being taken on the different concepts and links, we can explain these divisions of labor. As a result of a role-based DoL, the members' roles in the dyad differ from one anothe r. As an example, this can be seen as a leader-follower situation, where one peer acts on the conce pt map while the other peer provides instructions to the other peer in order to implement it. During task-based pe rformance, each peer is involved in acting on a different concept and link at the same time. In conclusion, the concurrent DoL is a set of concepts and links in which both peers are acting simultaneously on the same set of concepts and links. Dialogue Codes: We The dialogues were transcribed and coded using two different coding dimensions. According to the Cronbach Alpha, the correlation between two coders was 0.73. One of the dimensions to consider is whether the dyad is discussing the concept map tool and aesthetics (CMAP) or about the content of the concept map (KNWL). As an example, "Let's write something to remove the question marks" would be coded as CMAP, whereas "Resting membrane potential is the equilibrium between Na+ and Cl-" would be coded as KNWL. In the second place, it is important to determine whether the dyad was engaged in negotiations (NEGO), cooperations (COOP), or explanations (EXPL). For example, “so, to change this membrane  potential... or this is a constant, here” would be coded as NEGO, “let’s join these two concepts” would be coded as COOP; and “so the K channel permits the K ions to go out” would be coded as EXPL. Finally, we also c oded the silent (SLNT) episodes, in which none of the peers in a dyad spoke during the episode. Dyad’s Learning performance Levels: Concept map correctness is the lear ning performance for this activity. Two domain experts created a map using the same 14 concepts. This expert map was compared with all the participant maps. The map evaluation scheme was as follows: 2 points for correct links and correct labels; 1 point for correct links without labels; and 0.5 point for correct links with incorrect labels. The dyad's performance score was calculated as the sum of all its points. The dyads were then divided into high and low performance groups using a median split.

## Data analysis

In this paper, we are interested in the collaborative episodes where at lea st one of the JME or JVA is low. Therefore, we only focus on the high JME low JVA, low JME high JVA, and low  JME low JVA episodes. To examine the direct relationship between eye tracking measures and performance, dialogue codes, and DoL, we used a set of ANOVAs. We tested for the normality and homoscedasticity conditi ons using Shapiro-Wilk and Bausch-Pegan tests, respectively. In the case where the normality was violated, we normalized the data, and in cases where the homoscedasticity was violated, we used a Welch correc tion. We also tested for the pairwise interaction for all the variables using ANOVA. For multiple tests we applied Bonferroni corrections.

## Discussion and conclusion

The high-performing dyads have higher proportions of high JME-high JVA and high JME-low JVA episodes than those for low-performing dyads. On the other hand, the low-performing dyads have higher proportions of low JME-low JVA, and low JME-high JVA episodes than those for high-perfor ming dyads. This is in line with the previous works showing that the high-performing dyads have higher proportions of high J VA episodes, and the recent work done by Sharma et al. (2021) that showed high-performing dyads having a higher proportion of JME episodes as well. Moreover, we observed that it is implausible that high-perf orming dyads have both joint measurements marked as low. These findings reflect similar implications as  in Sharma et al. (2021), where both JME and JVA significantly differentiated between the high and low-performing dya ds. However, JME had a higher effect size than JVA. In the rest of this discussion, we will focus on the three episodes where at least one of the two joint measurements was marked as low.

First, we focus on the division of labor types and dialogue codes during high JME and low JVA episodes. Having higher proportions of the role and task type division of labor for high-perform ing dyads and a higher CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 104 proportion of the concurrent type of division for low-performing dyads indicates better action and focus planning from the high-performing dyads. For example, while being engaged in task-base d division of labor, typically, there is no requirement of having high joint visual attention and having a high menta l effort from both the participants in the dyad shows that they are performing the planned actions in  a better manner than their low performing counterparts. Similarly, while engaging in a concurrent type of divisio n of labor, it is essential to maintain a high level of joint visual attention. The low-performing pairs, while engaged in a concurrent division of labor, have low joint visual attention but high joint mental effort. This shows that even though they are exerting an equal amount of mental effort, they are not following each other's acti ons which indicate poor coordination management. Moreover, considering the dialogues during high JME and low JVA episodes, the high-performing pairs are either silent or provide an explanation about knowledge points or negotiate about the concept map. This goes in line with their type of division of labor. one possible explanation is that when the high-performing dyads engage in task-based division of labor, they are negotiating the structure and the aesthetics of the concept map. Whereas when the high-performing pairs are in a role-based division of labor, one partner is explaining some of the knowledge points to the other partner. Another possible explanation is that there is not much dialogue between the high-performing pairs when they're engaged in a role or task-based division of labor. Therefore, they have low joint visual attention, but they are engaged in their actions by putting in a similar level of mental ef fort. This is a working hypothesis that we will explore in future venues. During such episodes, we observe that the low-performing pairs are engaged in a concurrent type of division of labor where it is important to have both joint measurements marked as high. This again indicate s poor communication and coordination management by low-performing pairs. This is also reflected in the dialogues that the low-performing dyads engage in during low JME and low JVA episodes. They are negotiating about the knowledge points but they're not putting an equal amount of mental effort into the negotiation. Moreover, they coordinate the structure of the concept map and explain the structure and aesthetics of the concept map to each other without looking at a similar set of objects during the same time window. This again reflects th at they have poor communication and coordination management. Finally, we observe the division of labor and dialogues durin g the low JME and high JVA episodes. During such episodes, the low-performing pairs are engaged in the role and task-based division of labor, both of which require high joint mental effort to complete the planned ac tivities properly. Once again, the difference between high and low-performing pairs based on their actions shows poor communication on the part of low-performing pairs. Furthermore, looking add the dialogues of low-perform ing pairs, they engage in all the dialogue categories about knowledge points, and once again, one of the partners is exerting lower mental effort than the other in low-performing diets, which indicates poor communication management. In a nutshell, from our analysis and possible implications, what emerges is a working hypothesis for the explanation of the behavior of low-performing pairs is poorer communication and coordi nation management by such pairs than the high-performing pairs. Analyzing such moments provides us with a unique set of opportunities to design adaptive collaborative learning support systems (ACLS), which might be  useful to train students’ collaborative skills (Rummel & Spada, 2005). For example, in the past, resear chers have shown the benefit of using the gaze awareness to support collaboration in the terms of high collabora tion quality (Schneider & Pea, 2015), and high task-based performance (D’Angelo & Gergle, 2016; D’Angelo & Begel, 2017). The results from CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 105 our contribution and the contributions from Sharma et al (2021) and Sharma & Olsen (2022) make a  compelling argument for designing gaze-aware tools incorporating Joint Mental Effort (J ME) to provide adaptive collaborative support.

The JME based ACLS can be design at the different levels of group-awareness as proposed by Soller et al, 2005. Soller and colleagues, in a literature review, show that there are three different levels of group-support: mirroring, metacognitive and guiding. We argue that JME can be used at all the three levels. First, JME can easily be computed from the eye-tracking data in real time and therefore can be us ed within a JME-awareness tools in the same manner as the gaze-awareness tools mirror the joint attention of the partners. Second, in the cases where the system observes the JME to be critically low, or one of the peers havi ng extremely high or extremely low mental effort, the ACLS can provide the dyad with the content-based help  (Fisch, 2005) or propose to watch the video again or discuss the content of the activity (Sharma et al, 2021). Fin ally, the JME can also be used at the metacognitive level of support.  One candevelop a model explaining how JVA and JME evolve with time and actions, and based on the behavior of high performing dyads, suggest remedial action (mirroring or guiding). This can be the adaptability that we propose through our results and methods. In this contribution, we showed how JME and JVA can be used together to understand the asynchronous episodes of the collaboration and based on the results how one candesign ACLS. However, we are using the attent ional and cognitive similarities as two separate measures. In the future we will attempt to develop a unique indi cator that combined JVA and JME. Moreover, we are also not considering the temporality of the different epi sodes – which can be done by using Markovian model. For example, transitions from one type of episodes to another are not considered in this paper but we aim to apply temporal methods to understand asynchronous episodes during collaborati on. This will also help us build the JME-awareness tools in a better manner.
