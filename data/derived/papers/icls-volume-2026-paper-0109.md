# Speculative Learning Through Conference Participation: On the Affordances of Rogue Poster Sessions

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While some education researchers have begun to explore the learning that happens at academic conferences, this work largely examines the praxes of sanctioned participation (e.g., presentations, organizing, networking). In response, we use this paper to explore the learning that occurred during “rogue” poster sessions that the authors conducted at the 2025 ISLS annual meeting focused on a poster conceptualizing learning at the conference itself. Analyzing video recordings of four rogue poster prese ntations, we iterate a model of speculative and participatory learning that builds from the content of the poster to reimagine the borders of the learning sciences and the conference itself. Beyond our analysis of learning within (familiar) poster presentations, w e propose the embrace of unsanctioned conference participation as a means to amplify marginalized voices and ways of knowing in the field while also acknowledging the inherent barriers that come with challenging social norms in this way.

Participants at the 2025 Annual Meeting of the International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS) may have noticed something strange: a seemingly normal poster displayed in unusual places around the venue. At various times, two presenters and a handful of interested onlookers could be seen with a poster taped to a railing on a stairwell balcony, hanging next to the bathroom down a ground level  corridor, or attached to the marble facade underneath an awning while rain splattered on the concrete a few feet away. We describe these encounters as rogue poster sessions, “unofficial” presentations that exist at the margins of the conference (Hennessy Elliott & Woods, 2025). Our intervention sits within a continuum of sanctioned and unsanctioned activity, with the former representing all the events organized and defined by the conference committee and the latter encapsulating all actions participants take beyond “official” co nference programming. In staging these events, we draw attention to the precarity of learning at conferences and the possibilities they pos e as sites of relationship building. T he value of academic conferences often grows outsi de of official presentations or events and in  those moments of interpersonal interaction as we wa lk from one session to the next,  talk over lunch at a nearby restaurant, wander around the host city  after the conference day has “ended,” and more. While these unsanctioned activit ies and others have always been the norm at conferences, this category of participation can include more unexpected and disruptive activity as well.  Rogue poster sessions sit within the liminal space between the sanctioned and unsanctioned, mirroring prearranged and predetermined forms of conference participation while challenging the need for conference committees to organize that interaction for us. We use this paper to explore the possibilities for learning that come from participating in rogue poster sessions, both as a sanctioned/ familiar form of conference interaction and an unsanctioned/unusual intervention. Poster sessions provide an especially potent space to  explore the boundaries of conference participation because of their simultaneous positioning as tools for exploring emergent knowledge s and marginali zed contributions within academic disciplines  (D’Angelo, 2010). In doing so, we engage the learning sciences in the analysis of academic conferences as educative contexts (an overlooked site of research within the field) and invite the society itself to engage in a dialogue around supportive learning praxes at the annual meeting. Drawing on participatory (Lave & Wenger, 1991) and speculative (Garcia & Mirra, 2023) conceptualizations of learning,  we present an ways scholars engage in and contribute to forms of learning in the discipline while also propos ing modes of participation to challenge the underlying assumptions of the conference. This includes a chal lenge to the institutional power embodied by academic conferences, illustrated through  the marginalization of knowledge presented via poster presentations (and, simultaneously, the marginalization of those scholars who present these works) (Swales, 2004; Swales & Feak, 2000). At its core, this analysis presents an opportunity to uplift marginalized forms of participation and reimagine the academic conference as a site of learning  via sanctioned events, the intentional enactment of unsanctioned activity, and engagements in the in-betweens. To further explore participation across a sanctioned to unsanctioned continuum, we first consider the kinds of learning that happen within conferences  as they are currently conceived. We also acknowledge that conference 972 structures, and subsequently the learning therein, remain constantly in flux. This positioning begins with an overview of the literature on learning within conference settings before moving on to an expansion of our dual theoretical contexts (participatory and speculative learning). Conferences as sites of learning As noted by Campbell et al. (2021), conference participation leads to the production and acquisition of four kinds of knowledges: disciplinary  (ideas produced by the field), professional (learning “how to be” a  member), sociocultural (e.g., understandings of diversity and belonging), and relational (gaining awareness of interpersonal connections). While distinct, these knowledges interact and support each other. For example, disciplinary learning feeds into the networking practices at the heart of professio nal and relational k nowledge production (Fakunle et al., 2019). We extend this theorization by proposing that organizations and individuals conceive of  learning broadly happening through three kinds of activities: lecturing or direct instruction, such as paper presentations or keynote speeches; creative production via workshops; and the social interaction that occurs during sanctioned socializing events and moments  of interaction in liminal spaces at the margins of the conference (e.g., between sessions) (Hennessy Elliott & Woods,  2025). While lecturing remains  the dominant means of  knowledge exchange within formal conference activities (Belliappa, 2020; Henderson & Bur ford, 2020), we emphasize the value of unsanctioned socializing outside of designed and curated activities throughout this paper. Building on this foundation,  c ritical scholars have challenged extant conference structures and their implicit assumptions of  learning, arguing that a reliance on lecture-style pedagogies  replicates status quo knowledges while delegitimizing  marginalized (e.g., racialized, gendered)  ways of knowing (Henderson & Burford, 2020; Pereira, 2017). For many, poster presentations represent a space where new members can interject emergent knowledges into the field (Swales, 2004). But this framing positions posters as “for graduate students or new members” and secondary to spoken presentations and papers, marginalizing the influence they have within disciplines (D’Angelo, 2010; MacIntosh-Murray, 2007; Swales & Feak, 2000). Treating posters as secondary forms of knowledge production reinforces the institutional power embedded within academic conferences, one that results in limiting the influence  of certain members and their ability to fully participate. Exploring the value of posters in disciplinary construction can help, but more is needed to challenge embedded forms of institutional power, asking how scholars can legitimize previously overlooked voices and knowledges. Embracing new and creative forms of participation (especially emergent forms of hybrid participation) can amplify normally unheard participants, generating new kinds of knowledge production and exchange beyond the university’s colonial logics (Belliappa, 2020; Black et al., 2020; Seidenberg et al., 2024).  At ISLS, scholars focused on Queer and Trans learning (Uttamchandani et al., 2025), hybrid symposia design (Keifert, et a., 2023), and “decolonial and postcolonial possibilities” (Curnow, et al., 2025) all represent parts of the learning sciences community invested in new forms of learning at the conference. Such practices represent modes of learning centered on the production of community within the discipline and the discipline itself (Burford et al., 2020; McDonald & Star, 2008). Participatory and speculative learning at conferences This critical research into the educative potential of  conferences considers the learning that happens within academic conferences as they are and as participants push the boundaries of what conferences can be. In terms of what learning happens within the usual activities of the conference, we align with McDonald and Star (2008) who argue that conferences provide  spaces to produce communities of practice in the form of academic disciplines. The creation and maintenance of these  communities represents a form of learning  where new members join, become ingrained in the community by constructing situated identities and knowledges, and then contribute to situated knowledge production as veteran members by re-defining and maintaining the boundaries that define the community (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Learning thus occurs through community participation. Within academic conferences, participating in sanction ed and unsanctioned activity creates opportunities to develop disciplinary identities (Belliappa, 2020; Burford et al., 2020) and knowledges situated in the discipline (Campbell et al., 2021), a process that  occurs in tandem with community building.  In this way, powered relations are developed and maintained through the designation of whose voices get privileged in sanctioned conference activities and the community developed vision of what those activities represent. But learning also happens as participants imagine new forms of participation that do not yet exist. Garcia and Mirra (2023)  define this process of imagining new ways of being into existence as speculative learning, a cultural practice where participants deeply investigate the past, critically analyze the present, produce knowledges that lead tonew ways of being, and embody the affective relationships that exist in becoming understandings of equitable community  (Holbert et al.,  2020; Lizárraga, 2023;  Mirra & Garcia, 2020). Importantly, speculative imagining represents a tangible process that  comes from active and hands on community participation (Jones et 973 al., 2025; Perez, 2025). This same kind of learning can happen within conferences as participants  – particularly participants who challenge undergirding assumptions about what/who belongs – and organizers imagine new ways of being in community through knowledge production and exchange (Abbas, 2023; Black et al., 2020). To further explore participatory and speculative forms of learning within  conferences, we draw inspiration from both design-based research  (DBR) (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012) and research-creation (Loveless, 2019) to explore new possibilities of engagement and performance within our discipline. We initiated the iterative design cycle behind DBR with our poster submission to ISLS  2023 (Woods & Hennessy Elliott, 2023). Taking a novel and process-oriented approach, we conceived of and created this submission, one centered on the ethics of care behind deadline extensions, entirely within the 24-hour deadline extension offered by the conference that year. Based on comments from participants at the conference and our own reflections, we then engaged in a second round of reimagining academic production vi a research-creation (Hennessy Elliott & Woods, 2025). While we took a more traditional approach to writing and researching our conceptualization of learning within ISLS in this iteration, we presented our work via rogue poster sessions throughout the conference  by displaying our poster in three different unsanctioned spaces: a balcony in a stairwell, outside of the building underneath an awning, and next to the entrance of the ground floor bathroom. We hung the poster for an hour each time and discussed the poster with anyone who decided to independently approach us. These open-ended and unstructured conversations serve as the foundation – and data corpus – for our analysis. In alignment with interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), we filmed and audio recorded each of the four conversations that occurred during our rogue poster presentations. Interaction analysis aligns with our theorization of learning at conferences by center ing the meanings that emerge from participants themselves and the environments they create for each other through interpersonal and affective practices (Vossoughi et al., 2020), thus providing a valuable methodology for our  study. Our four recorded  discussions lasted 15-20 minutes and included five different participants: Josh (early career scholar, assistant professor), Jack (early career scholar, assistant professor), Kevin (early career scholar, doctoral student), Ellen (late career scholar, professor), and Emily (mid-career scholar, research scientist). All participants agreed to being recorded before conversations began. To analyze the data, we watched the recordings together,  discussed moments from the data that felt significant, and began tracing the shifting foci of the conversations. We then individually coded the videos through an open and iterative approach to descriptive coding (Saldaña, 2015). We logged all moments  where the conversation moved from one topic to another  and who enacted that shift while coding the focus or topic of this new phase in  the conversation. In this process, we  specifically centered on moments of ideational conflict (i.e., whether something counts as  learning) and congruence (i.e., immediate agreement over forced socializing) to understand the sensemaking we did with participants. We then engaged in Harry et al.’s (2005) consensus building process, reviewing each  other’s codes and creating a shared codebook. Finally, w e combined our codes into broader themes related to the context of the conference through pattern coding (Miles et al., 2020) a nd selected one moment from each conversation as an interactional hotspot to provide a focus for analysis. Importantly, we come to this work as two emerging learning scientists and early career researchers. In acknowledging our position, we recognize our own personal interests in establishing ourselves in the field, pushing the learning sciences in new directions, and developing our understanding of the present and future of the conference. This lens undoubtedly but also productively shaped our approach to the rogue poster presentations and our subsequent analysis. Additionally, Peter personally knew four of the five participants (with Kevin being introduced by Jack at the beginning of the ir joint conversation) and Colin  had met Josh  and Ellen previously. While this aspect of our study, along with the small number of participants, creates a limitation in terms of the generalizability of our findings, we focus here on theory building as opposed to representative statements that apply to all conference participation. While conversations with people we did not have previous relationships with may have produced different results, we also position this aspect of the intervention as a valuable point of data in itself: the fact that only people familiar with us came to discuss our poster shows a pattern in the kinds of participation that happen at conference sessions, sanctioned or unsanctioned. Through our analysis, we developed three larger themes and six subthemes related to the various topics discussed (and pivoted towards)  during our rogue poster presentations (see Table 1). First, we discussed details from the poster and the participants’ reflections on ISLS, creating space to co-construct knowledge inspired by the poster’s contents and our immediate environment. We categorized these codes under the “object” theme, as they centered 974 on tangible artifacts and concurrent experiences. Second, we talked about the relationships that emerge at conferences or through research and membership within the learning sciences. These codes comprise the context theme, representing collective disciplinary learning about the broader social landscape surrounding our discussion and ISLS. Finally, we explored other models of conferencing outside of ISLS and possible ways of modeling participation in the future. We combined these codes under the future theme, represent ing a speculative learning praxis by imagining how we might “do” conferencing in the future. To map these conversations,  we present diagrams that illustrate each conversational shift that occurred (see Figure 1). In the following sections, we begin with written vignettes to situate and then illustrate our interactional hotspots. These vignettes take on slightly different forms depending on the aspect of participation  we aimed to highlight, with each showing different  yet equally important aspects of our interactions. In the first, we focus on our use of gesture to orient our conversation with Josh, emphasizing the use of the physical poster as a site for conversation. In the second, we center the ways that Jack and Kevin built on each other's bids for interpretation of conferencing  as an example of the dialogic nature of the conversation. In the third, we emphasize the synchronicity between our speech and Ellen’s to illustrate knowledge development. In the final vignette, we focus on Emily’s gestural work with the poster to surface an implicit divergence in conceptualization of what learning is. While the poster communicated disciplinary knowledge that the participants had not encountered before, discussions about these ideas or learnings  rarely served as the main focus of conversation. Discussions were, instead,  an opportunity to support the construction of professional, sociocultural, and relational knowledge  in alignment with Fakunle et al.’s (2019) analysis of learning at conferences. T he poster thus acted as a tangible material through which speculative learning could occur, similar to the description of speculative props provided by Perez (2025). For example, Ellen’s gestures towards the possibility of new ways of engaging in conferencing that existed in the negative space of the poster (see Figure 3, line 11) created a moment of speculative learning by linking the present moment and future possibility (Holbert et al., 2020; Lizárraga, 2023). Even in Ellen’s challenge towards the participatory framing of learning we presented in our theoretical model, the presence of the model itself created a means towards imagining what can occur within the context of academic conferences. Ellen’s conversation, in contrast with the other three, offers an interesting counter example of what happens when there is not an agreement on an implicit conceptualization – in this case of learning – as participants discuss a poster. Responding to her pushes on what learning can and cannot be created a powerful opportunity for us (the authors) to further iterate on our conceptions of learning in the conference and beyond. At the same time, other conversations also offered opportunities for refining our conceptualizations: Emily specifically asked what’s best for our collective learning, Josh invited us to fully articulate our argument, and the pair engages us in talking about what we think it took to belong at ISLS. In all instances, the participants invited us to co-construct new ideas through our discussion. These findings therefore support the work of critical scholars pushing for  emergent and relational pedagogical practices within conferences that embrace becoming ways of knowing within academia (such as the unexplored ways of “doing” conferences provided by our participants) (Black et al., 2020; Seidenberg et al., 2024). These findings have implications for designing and developing conference structures  that amplify the voices of traditionally marginalized scholars,  emphasizing the need to consider how underrepresented ways of knowing emerge in sanctioned and unsanctioned conference participation alike (Belliappa, 2020). Drawing out another comparison, t he divide between early and mid/late career scholars in terms of participatory learning. For Josh, Jack, and Kevin, navigating conference participation  through their early career identity represented a crucial part of the discussion as they questioned what it means to be a learning scientist and what participation in the field can or should entail. But for Ellen and Emily, both established scholars in the field (with established careers), the conversation never turned towards learning sciences membership and instead more prominently focused on ways of enacting speculative conference practices. This shift in the focus of learning maps 978 on traditional models of communities of practice described by Lave and Wenger (1991) a nd others. Those closer to legitimate peripheral participation may engage with conferences to better understand how they might fit within the discipline and how their ways of knowing may intersect with that social context (Belliappa, 2020; Seidenberg et al., 2024). But more veteran community members might engage with learning by shaping the boundaries of the field, as their belonging is already secure and they have the opportunity to shift what participation (and, subsequently, learning) can mean for others  (Abbas, 2023; Black et al., 2020). Who belongs, and what belongs, at ISLS is an ongoing, community-wide, negotiation that those already comfortable being labeled as Learning Scientists might unintentionally dominate.

While the above points of discussion can apply to any sanctioned poster presentation, we also acknowledge the unique aspects of this intervention that may not apply to all traditional conference participation. The fact that the poster was about the conference itself clearly directed the conversation towards a reimagining of participation within the learning sciences. Yet, as Jones et al. (2025) show, all forms of community participation can engage individuals and communities in the civic practices that lead to speculative reimagining. Additionally, the unsanctioned aspects of our presentations may shift the ways that participants engage (and, more specifically, whether they engage at all)  with the work despite being almost identical to most poster presentations aside from the location and time frame. As participants revealed in their discussion of the rogue nature of these presentations, a tension emerged between both inspiring engagement (the weird location created a sense of intrigue that drew participants in, as discussed by Jack and Kevin; additional time allowed Josh and Emily to engage in e xtended because they knew the authors).  This tension contributes an additional layer of nuance to previous research, showing that new ways of engaging in conferencing can bring marginalized forms of knowledge exchange and production into the discipline (Belliappa, 2020; Black et al., 2020; Seidenberg et al., 2024). By embodying an unsanctioned form of conference participation, we gave up the familiarity that comes with a sanctioned event like a poster session where social norms encourage participants to talk to strangers. This assertion challenges those working to reimagine conference s to carefully consider the ways that unsanctioned parti cipation may unintentionally undermine the ability of peripheral participants to engage, furthering the unintended consequence of preventing already marginalized individuals from participating fully in speculative praxes and thus reinscribing more dominant ways of knowing.

We continue to ask: what are the logics of learning at our conference and in our ongoing collective work as a field? Rogue poster sessions, and the analysis above, are just a start to this important inquiry for the learning sciences. In our analysis, w e address the ongoing positioning of poster  presentations as marginal forms of sanctioned participation (D’Angelo, 2010; MacIntosh-Murray, 2007; Swales & Feak, 2000), a framing  that replicates issues of institutional power for marginalized participants through the relegation of new knowledges and identities to the fringes of our field. We also show that valued forms of learning that exist within other aspects of conference participation (Campbell et al., 2021) emerge through poster presentations, with the unsanctioned nature of our particular intervention opening up space for new relationships to form (even though our study also revealed the barriers that come with new modes of participation). But we are not the only ones doing this work. In a related development, ISLS 2026 is offering submissions to an art gallery, a new move towards what belongs at a learning sciences conference. Further, we have already seen new forms of symposia form in sanctioned ways (i.e., hybrid) and unsanctioned (i.e., posters as papers in symposia). Our analysis above emphasizes the notion that who belongs, and what belongs, at ISLS is an ongoing, community-wide, negotiation that is braided with societal and local power-dynamics. Such is also a matter of how learning happens and what we can imagine in resistance to the problems of social power and domination inherent in academic spaces and higher ed institutions. Further, as higher education across the globe contends with the rise of fascism and targeted defunding of research and infrastructure, how might we need to change as an international community to learn what solidarity and resistance looks like ?
