# Breaking Rules and Breaking Machines: Kindergarteners’ Negotiation of a Mathematical Playworld

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how kindergarteners negotiated their teacher’s established playworld and the mathematical implications that followed. Drawing from the literature on playworlds, we consider how children’s opportunities for defining playworlds, particularly in ways that are mathematical, are more or less constrained by the logics of K-12 schooling, the context of our analysi s. Employing classroom video data, we examine the dimensions of one play-based task as they are reflected, extended, or challenged by three focal children, connecting those dimensions to the ir opportunities for mathematical learning. We find that children were generally more willing tonegotiate contextual, social, and material rules of the playworld, but hesitant to play with mathematical expectations, thereby limiting the scope of their inquiry. We end by advocating for the inclusion of children’s negotiating power in school-based playworlds, as such contexts are arguably the most reluctant space s to follow children's leads, particularly in relation to disciplinary content learning.

After a brief whole-group mathematics lesson on the carpet, three kindergarten students —Tiana, Nathan, and Naomi—excitedly got to work exploring combinations of five at their small-group table. Today’s business was serious work as the three children entered into a playworld filled with popsicle factories, magical recipe books, and two plus three equals five—but cert ainly not patterns and definitely not popsicles that were “out of order.” Tiana: Squishes body up in excitement. Throughout the task, the three children continued tonegotiate what was “allowable” in their playworld. Indeed, and as play scholars have long argued, rules are central to play, as they permit children to establish a common fantasy in order to keep the action going (Rakoczy, 2007). These rules are not merely children’s attempt at imitating adults or “growing into the intellectual lives of others,” (Vygotsky, 1978, p. 97) but a “literacy” that children choose and use to make sense of the world (Wohlwend, 2018). In this way, play is both a context and a “text” as children create a “tangible space that other players can see, touch, manipulate, and resemiotize, always contingent on the continued agreement of other players” (p. 313). Of course, in order to settle on these agreements, players must smooth over contradictory meanings—creating disparate storylines that may not always make sense, but allow the action text to persist and, at times, become even more mutually engaging. As Wohlwend contends: When players share an understanding of an imaginary, they draw on their individual (and sometimes conflicting) knowledges and repertoires of cultural practices to guide their use of their bodies as they  interpret, negotiate, and respond through their shared performance. This [context] both supports and weakens a play context: by making it more lifelike and recognizable and by making breakdowns, ruptures, and tangents more likely as multiple  players’ ideas diverge and add more texture. (p. 313) However, when it comes to shaping the rules of formal schooling, the context of Tiana, Nathan, and Naomi’s mathematical playworld, “younger children have historically been left out of this conversation” (Ferreira et al., 2018, p. 261). In fact, despite being a space for children, K-12 schools more often reflect the priorities of adults, as children are “colonized” or “brought under control as [they] fall into line on a mandated and complicated 216 path” (Dyson, 2013, p. 421). This is particularly true for mathematics, a discipline with a history of prioritizing accountability logics and prescriptive norms (Gresalfi et al., 2009; Horn, 2018), as opposed to children’s own desires and aims (Yoon & Templeton, 2019). The result is that students who “venture off” this adult-dictated path, and who are disproportionately racially minoritized and economically disadvantaged, are often stratified into low ability groups and thus denied access to advanced math coursework in future grades (Cobb & Russell, 2015). While such practices are often associated with the high-stakes testing environments of upper-elementary and secondary grades, the performance pressures of public schools have incited an “accountability shovedown” (Bassok et al. 2016) in K-2 classrooms by r eplacing young children’s pursuits with prescriptive participation expectations and short-term skills. As a result, one of the first messages that students learn in school is tha t what they have to contribute does not “belong” in mathematics. Therefore, as we observed Tiana, Nathan, and Naomi’s playful narrative unfold, we wondered just how much of this mathematically driven playworld, though seemingly transgressive compared to business-as-usual K-2 mathematics instruction, actually “belonged” to these children. In the following analysis, we attend to this tension by examining the possibilities and implications of school-based playworlds, particularly when children are more or less invited to shape the construction of those worlds. In doing so, our analysis is guided by the following research question: How do kindergarteners enter into and negotiate a school-based playworld, and what are the implications for their mathematical participation?

## Literature review

Logic of control in K-12 schools Math classroom logics, defined as the norms, values, and assumptions that dictate how students should participate in math classrooms (e.g., Acker, 1990), are often driven by a “staircase” model of learning, where  one mathematical skill must be mastered in order to move on to other mathematical ideas (Brenner, 1998). This means that formal spaces of mathematical learning are driven by strict prescriptions, often by a scope and sequence from which teachers can rarely deviate (Parks & Bridges-Rhoads, 2012). Moreover, these narrowly defined skills hide the mathematical expertise that children already bring to school  (Franke et al., 2024). Preschoolers in Brenner’s

(1998) study, for example, more often encountered money by going shopping with their families. Consequently,

dollar bills were the most salient and meaningful piece of currency for these children. However, traditional lessons might dictate that students have a strong understanding of pennies, nickels, dimes, and quarters before a dollar bill comes to have meaning. This approach takes the mathematical experiences that children encounter in their everyday worlds as irrelevant, while also limiting the potential for children’s own creative inquiries to contribute to the making of generative mathematical learning opportunities beyond the ones set forth by adults (Dyson, 2020). Thus, in controlling how students are able to engage with mathematical ideas (i.e. by following particular trajectories), K-12 schools also control what mathematical ideas are made relevant to students. Play and mathematics On the other hand, children’s play spaces and peer networks have their own autonomy outside of adult intervention (Corsaro, 2008; Dyson, 2020; Paley, 2007).  Through play, children not only imagine alternative roles and possibilities, but they also raise critical provocations of the adult world (Wohlwend, 2018; Silin, 2016). Therefore, designing mathematical spaces to incorporate play could be a way for children to  take control over their mathematical learning experiences.  Indeed, scholars in out-of-school and early childhood contexts have long demonstrated the relationship between play and children’s geometric discourse (Gejard & Melander & 2018), number sense (Ginsburg, 2006; Ramani & Siegler, 2008), and broader mathemati cs practices (Parks, 2015). However, because of the historically constraining context of math education in formal schools, the field lacks empirical examples of mathematical play past the preschool context. Herein lies the tension of this current work: in formal K-12 settings, are children truly able to play with math? Or do the interests of the standards and curricula override opportunities for children to play and shape their mathematical learning through co-opting the term as a means to disguise the same old forms of control? This paper closely e xplores these concerns by examining one play-based mathematical activity designed by an experienced kindergarten teacher—and her students’ uptake of that activity—in a K-8 school. In doing so, we extend the literature on mathematical play into formal schooling.

## Theoretical framework

This analysis centers on the role of children in shaping play-based mathematical experiences in schools. Therefore, we take up Lindqvist’s (1996) concept of playworlds, defined as “imagination carried out in action,” in order to “create new meanings” (p. 6). In their own leveraging of Lindqvist’s work, Ferholt et al. (2021) write,  “In playworlds, adults and children enter into a common fantasy” (p. 96) by integrating imagination with reality. We extend this definition in the present analysis through the combining of mathematics with a pretend scenario, 217 described in greater detail in the Methods section. Moreover, and central to this shared fantasy, is the establishment of a joint imaginary context, which inevitably requires rules. Yet, as Nicolopoulou (1993) maintains, “...these are rules recognized and accepted as necessary by the children themselves, not simply handed down by adults” (p. 45). Thus, and as Ferholt et al. (2021)  claim, children’s play is not merely a sense-making medium for growing into the status quo adult world, but for reconstructing it. We maintain  this stance  in the present study as we examine how, or whether, children negotiate a teacher-initiated playworld in the context of formal mathematics— a space with a history of reinforcing status quo logics of what it means to be mathematical (Gresalfi et al., 2009). Though seemingly paradoxical to rules, Lindqvist (1996) additionally stresses the necessity of ambiguous conditions in enabling children’s playworlds to unfold, as ambiguity liberates children—and even adults—to step out of their institutional roles. By facilitating open-ended spaces of uncertainty, adults make space for children’s untidy plot threads to start, stop, restart, and overlap with personal desires  and emergent inquiries (Wohlwend, 2018). Furthermore, these collisions create opportunities for “productive tensions” that uncover “something of importance and enable a moment of critique, a need for renegotiation, or the emergence of a new local” (Medina et al., 2022, p. 99). In this way, the “truth” or knowledge that arises from children’s playworlds can often reveal new meanings that are even more consequential than the “truths” of everyday reality (Rushdie, 2021). In the following analysis, we conceptualize “reality” as the unambiguous institution of K-12 mathematics education, and one kindergarten teacher’s integration of play into her instruction as an opportunity for “ambiguous conditions” to unfold. In what follows, we leverage Lindqvist’s (1996) and Ferholt et al’s. (2021) concept of playworlds as a lens for interrogating the tensions, but also potential new meanings, that can emerge from enacting such mathematically ambiguous opportunities in the highly constrained context of U.S. public schooling. This study draws from a longitudinal research project exploring the integration of mathematical play in elementary classrooms at a racially and economically diverse K-8 public charter school in the southeastern United States (65% Black, 35% white, 48% economically disadvantaged). Eight K-2 teachers, alongside their assistant teachers, participated in the broader project, collaborating in professional development sessions designed to support their playful mathematics instruction. During these sessions, teachers analyzed curricular standards, including their school’s scope and sequence and existing lessons, engaged with playful activities, and made deliberate design modifications grounded in their own classroom knowledge and pedagogical goals.  Although professional development sessions were  a collaborative endeavor, and teachers often consult ed one another for design ideas and feedback, teachers also had considerable agency to adapt play-based tasks and lessons for the particulars of their classrooms or toward their own pedagogical aims.

As part of the broader study, w e gathered data across two contexts:  the collaborative professional development sessions and whole-class and small-group video recordings of student s’ mathematical participation during  the redesigned lessons. The present analysis centers on one teacher, Ms. Lane, and her kindergarten classroom. Ms. Lane, a Black woman with over twenty years of teaching experience, brought deep pedagogical expertise to her practice. Her classroom instruction follow ed a deliberate sequence: whol e-group introduction, small-group play centers, and whole-group discussion. She demonstrated a unique blend of care and humor throughout her teaching. Of all the participating teachers, she often exhibited playfulness herself, whether through role-playing or silliness. Hence, her classroom offered a generative site for exploratory analysis of playworlds. We sampled Ms. Lane’s  “popsicle” task from Year 1 of the study as the focus of this analysis, as it featured a richly constructed imaginative context among other activities. Co-designed by Ms. Lane, two other kindergarten teachers, and our research team, this activity positioned children within a  pretend popsicle factory. Children were playfully invited to create recipe books for their popsicles using crayons and two recording sheets with seven popsicle  molds composed of five blank squares (see Figure 1).  Children were instructed to use two colors to fill the popsicle molds and to write an addition number sentence for each recipe, hence exploring various combinations of 5. To align with the school’s standards and scope and sequence, Ms. Lane and her colleagues had proposed a “no mixing colors” rule during the professional development session, thereby discouraging students Figure 1 Sample Popsicle Mold 218 from switching back and forth between colors. T eachers conceptualized this rule as an intentional scaffold for achieving the curriculum’s goal of addition with only two addends (i.e. 2+3=5 as opposed to 1+2+2=5). tracking camera. 360 cameras documented three small groups engaged in the popsicle task at student tables. As a research team, we independently watched one small-group video and content logged our own observations  of children’s pretend play and mathematical play engagement with the popsicle task. Prior to viewing video, and in alignment with the literature on playworlds, we had initially expected to observe children stepping into, exploring, and reshaping the popsicle task  in personally meaningful ways. However, our initial viewing suggested that students were willing tonegotiate certain dimensions of the popsicle task, but not all. After reconvening to discuss our observations across all three groups, we selected one  focal group for closer analysis —Tiana (Black girl), Nathan (white boy), and Naomi (white girl)—due to the analytical richness of their rule negotiations, as the three children in this group made frequent references to Ms. Lane’s established play rules. Given these continual callbacks, we conceptualize this group as a  “paradigmatic case” (Flyvbjerg, 2011)  of the possibilities and constraints children may face when attempting to shape mathematical playworlds within formal K-12 contexts.

## Data analysis

In line with our theoretical framework of playworlds as a common fantasy in which adults and children enter (Ferholt et al., 2021), we began our analysis by identifying the teacher-initiated rules of the popsicle factory. We conceptualized these rules as Ms. Lane “inviting” her students into a playworld. As a team, we thematically organized (Miles et al., 2014) these rules into four categories: rules concerning context, mathematics, materials, and social interactions. To consider whether, or how, children reconstruct ed this teacher-initiated playworld. we then individually coded the small-group video for instance s where children reflected, challenged, or extended, Ms. Lane’s established rules, and whether fellow group members accepted, rejected, or negotiated those bids (see Table 1). While coding the small-group video, we  crafted individual analytic memos (Saldaña, 2021) of salient moments that were illustrative of these rule interactions. We met as a team to refine our own understandings of the groups’ uptakes (or not) of the playworld rules. Overall, we considered these bids, and consequent uptakes, as evidence of children creat ing new, child-directed meaning throughout the mathematical task  (Lindqvist, 1996). Importantly, Ms. Lane not only rejected Tiana’s mathematical challenges, but in both scenarios where children negotiate d the rules, Ms. Lane leveraged  children’s contextual extensions of the playworld to maintain their mathematical compliance (i.e. in order to make “real popsicles” they needed to work hard). However, we emphasize that, in this moment, the rules of the popsicle task directly conflicted with the children’s participation by restricting their mathematical engagement. Pragmatically, to make popsicles with only two colors that could not be mixed, the number sentences omitted the possibility of 0+5 or having more than two addends. Thus, the children quickly exhausted all mathematical possibilities, as the only combinations available were 1+4, 4+1, 2+3, and 3+2. The mathematical rules also revealed a material tension: children were given sheets that provided seven popsicle templates but the mathematical rule only allowed for four possibilities. While we cannot be sure, Tiana mixing colors for her last popsicles might have emerged from this tension: she had no remaining combinations and attempted to resolve the problem by challenging the mathematical rule. Neither Ms. Lane nor the children treated this  mathematical rule as open to revision, even when it limited the scope of their mathematical inquiry. Ms. Lane routinely redirected the children back to the mathematical rules, even when they were in the middle of extending the playworld context. This episode highlights that children were eager tonegotiate the context of the playworld, but both children and teacher treated the mathematical rules as less flexible, even when those rules acted as a constraint to their math participation. The present analysis contributes to understandings of mathematical play through its unique attention to the contextual landscape of formal K-12 schooling. While the mathematical play literature has long demonstrated the role of play in supporting children’s learning (Clements & Sarama, 2017; Ginsburg, 2006; Vogt et al., 2020), little remains understood about whether or not, or in what ways, these same findings might hold true in traditional schooling contexts, spaces that are known to incentivize certainty and control, as opposed to ambiguity and choice (Yoon & Templeton, 2019). Indeed, one of the preeminent benefits of play that researchers in out-of-school settings point to is the opportunity for children to make choices by testing out strategies or pursuing new ideas (Su, 2020; Vogt et al., 2020). The present study adds to the literature on mathematical play by leaning into this tension in the context of formal learning. In doing so, we found that Tiana, Nathan, and Naomi certainly exercised agency within the boundaries of the established playworld, and oftentimes those choices were mathematical in nature. However, when it came tonegotiating the playworld itself, the three children appeared much more willing to challenge or extend contextual, social, and material rules, as opposed mathematical expectations. We argue that this may have  limited the children’s opportunities to play with the discipline, given that Naomi’s challenges to the mathematics could have provided the three children, or even the whole class, with ideas and strategies beyond the scope of the task. Yet, we refrain from characterizing this analysis as a commentary on Ms. Lane, but rather, as an illustrative case of how, even in a play-based classroom, the high-stakes, accountability logics of formal schooling (Horn, 2018; Yoon & Templeton, 2019) facilitated a playworld that was comfortable with children’s negotiations, so long as those negotiations were not mathematical in nature. Indeed, Ms. Lane encapsulated these pressures during her initial project interview as she shared, “What if I don’t get these concepts delivered right to the kids?” However, we see Ms. Lane’s exit interview, after nearly three years of play experience, as her own challenge  to mathematics, as she noted, “There's so much that goes into playing…If you build that strong foundation on the front end, they'll get the other stuff later.” As demonstrated by this analysis, we maintain that when children are allowed to play, they not only “get the other stuff later,” but may actually play with more mathematical ideas. Moreover, and in line with our theoretical framework, these mathematical ideas are likely to be child-driven, rather than handed down by adults (Nicolopoulou, 1993). Future work can leverage these promising insights to examine the opportunities that arise when children are explicitly invited to shape their mathematical playworlds and when their mathematical challenges and extensions are accepted in K-12 schools.
