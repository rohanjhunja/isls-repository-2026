# Addressing Cognitive Load and Mental Schema Development for Graduate Research Methods Knowledge

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This design-based study examined a graduate research-methods course for 14 doctoral students, grounded in cognitive constructivism, situated learning, and cognitive load theory. Using backward design and cumulative assignments, the course scaffolded proposal writing in professional contexts. Coded reflections and semantic network analysis showed that students valued structure and iterative feedback but struggled with alignment, template confusion, and workload. Two revisions are proposed: a weekly visual “you-are-here” organizer and consolidated cloud-based templates to reduce extraneous load and strengthen workplace transfer.

Our project is situated in graduate research methods coursework for education practitioners. The course uses a scaffolded approach to developing research proposals: complex research tasks are deconstructed through backward design and sequenced in practice-based chunks that build toward student-designed projects. Transfer from class to professional practice is emphasized through examples from real-world educational settings and opportunities for students to ground content in their own workplaces. This sustained connection supports accommodation as students reframe perspectives on data and develop new approaches to practice. The theoretical framework included cognitive constructivist learning theory (Piaget, 1952), situated learning theory (Lave & Wenger, 1991), and cognitive load theory (Hutchins, et al., 2013; Sweller, 1988, 1994; Paas et al., 2003; Wickens et al., 2012). To gether, these perspectives assume that learning involves schema formation, that schema development is strengthened when intrinsic and extraneous load are minimized, and that schema formation is most effective in authentic contexts.

The study involved one semester of a doctoral program evaluation course at a large Southwestern University with 14 students. The course addressed program evaluation for continuous improvement, including its purposes, procedures, theories, and models. Students developed a program evaluation proposal for a problem of practice in their professional context. Incremental assignments represented smaller sections of the final proposal and supported formative learning. End-of-course written reflections asked students:

1. What aspects of the incremental assignments building to the final program evaluation proposal worked

well to support your learning?

2. What aspects of the incremental assignments building to the final program evaluation proposal did you

struggle with?

3. What aspects of the incremental assignments building to the final program evaluation proposal can be

improved?

Data sources and analytic procedures This design-based research study employed iterative cycles of implementation, evaluation, and refinement. Primary data consisted of student reflection responses coded at the sentence level using MAXQDA Analytics Pro. Coding used a priori categories (“What Worked”, “Struggles”, and “Theories”), in vivo codes, and axial coding to identify relationships among categories (Thornberg, 2012). Semantic network analysis used correlation matrices at p <.05, p < 0.01, and p <.001, imported into UCINet and NetDraw. T he Girvan-Newman algorithm identified clusters, while betweenness centrality identified network leverage points (Lam et al., 2021). Descriptive statistics and the Spring 2025 network map (Figure 1) identified persistent themes and emerging patterns for iterative course improvement.

2656 Descriptive statistics revealed prevalent themes in students’ reflections. Common “What Worked” codes included course sequence and structure, feedback, and improved learning process. Students valued the incremental design: “Each module let me first explore the concepts through readings and then immediately apply what I learned in the weekly assignments.” Feedback was also central: “The weekly feedback on each assignment was constructive and helpful. Your reflections encourage me to revisit and refine earlier sections as I learn more.” One student described the final model as effortful but useful in designing a strong program. Students also reported challenges. Common “Struggles” codes included difficulty aligning weekly assignments with the final proposal, time-consuming workload, and confusion with tools and templates. One student noted, “One challenge I faced was aligning each week’s assignment with the overall vision of the final proposal.” Others noted the evaluation matrix format: “I think it was confusing to have it converted to a doc format rather than keeping it in Excel.” Another wrote, “Module 9 was also hefty,” reflecting workload imbalance. Semantic network analysis identified four clusters at Q = 0.657 (p <.001; Figure 1). One cluster emphasized course structure and improved learning; another linked feedback with increased confidence and selfefficacy. Additional clusters highlighted assignment alignment, tool usability, and time management struggles. Overall, the scaffolded design and feedback supported meaningful learning and confidence development, but alignment, tool clarity, and workload issues contributed to extraneous cognitive load and informed proposed revisions.

## Implication and conclusion

The network analysis revealed two extraneous-load issues in the scaffolded design. First, although weekly assignments built on one another, they did not always preserve full context for the final project. Some students struggled to connect weekly work with the complete proposal. The molecular perspective of individual assignments was sometimes disconnected from the molar perspective of the full proposal, increasing extraneous load. Second, standalone weekly templates required students to transfer accumulated content from prior assignments, adding effort not directly tied to the weekly learning objective. In response, two design elements are proposed to reduce extraneous load and increase germane load for schema formation: an advanced organizer and consolidated assignment templates. Although the final project was overviewed in week one and mapped to each assignment, students had to return to that content for orientation. A weekly graphical organizer (e.g., “you are here”) will highlight each learning objective within the larger course goal and preserve the whole-part relationship.

Individual templates will also be collapsed into fewer consolidated templates. Students will work in the same document, shifting from section to section. Moving templates to cloud-based documents (e.g., OneDrive, Google Docs) will enable iterative revisions without transferring content, reducing extraneous load and increasing capacity for germane load.
