# Professors Exploring Large Language Models

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores specific insights of university professors using large language models (LLMs) across their different roles. Using think-aloud protocols, we interviewed ten Norwegian professors from mathematics and natural sciences actively engaged in a project integrating these tools in teaching. The study surfaces emergent applications of LLMs in higher education with professors’ reflections on using LLMs in teaching & research practices.

## Research Participants

After ethical approval from the university’s ethics  committee, 10 of 18 Norwegian university professor s from mathematics and natural sciences involved in brainstorming ways to integrate LLMs in teaching joined the study. The first author engaged as a collaborative researc her. At project launch (Aug‘23), most professors ha d tried at least one LLM-powered tool (ChatGPT, Bing, Bard); 3 had not tried  any. Acknowledging LLMs and tools they power are not the same, we use their names interchangeably.

## Procedure, data sources, and analysis

Think-aloud Interviews in mid-October were conducted in English for 45-60 minutes in each participant’s university office once they used a Zoom meeting link and their computer. Professors launched their LLM tool and were asked to prepare for a task relevan t, authentic, and linked to their university respon sibilities. They shared their LLM screen interactions to enable data captur e. They were asked pre-first LLM prompt to describe LLM use goals: “Before seeing the answer, what are you anticipating from the LLM’s response”. As LLM interactions continued—led by the profs or first author prompted —they were asked: Now that you’ve seen the model’s response, what are your initial thoughts ? Questions were follow-up questions to their answers. To wrap, participants described details and rationale for their vision of ideal LLM responses for their stated goals. Data sources included video transcripts from Zoom s creen-sharing recordings. Video captures of professors were not collected; transcripts were rev ised for accuracy and associated with an anonymized  ID to ensure confidentiality. Participants’ prompts were incorporated in video transcripts to situate their LLM interactions. Professor prompts ranged from two to 18, average 6.7. All 10 interview transcripts, cont aining transcripts and LLM prompts used were merged into a  single document, reviewed by both authors  for acquaintance with each participant's tasks using LL Ms. Insights were shared at a first meeting and mad e into a table. The first author marked participant contributions later reviewed by a second coder for agreement. In another review, the first author  exported transcripts into a shared Excel file, high lighting relevant contributions. The second coder and first author applied emergent descriptive codes to participants’ utterances (e.g., “general,” “can’t trust,” “word predictor,” “etiquette”), discussed  and merged overlapping codes (e.g., codes assessing  LLMs’ responses, such as “vague,” “not specific,” “genera l” were combined into “shortcomings in responses”) until consensus. In a third review, the first author appl ied interpretive annotations to coded segments and prepared a table shared with the second author with categories, codes, frequencies, and two or more representative examples for discussion until they indicated key overarching themes.

## Preliminary findings and discussion

We highlight five prominent themes from professors’  reflections about using LLMs in their academic activities. Professors unveil practical, critical, and ethical considerations related to the academic community’s values on cognitive augmentation through LLMs for academic wo rk. These reflections can inform future R&D directi ons in LLM technology, identifying opportunities for enhancing their affordances for scholarly work. Benefits in delegation but critical oversight needed Professors acknowledged efficiency gains experience d from delegating (Latour, 1992) tasks to LLMs whil e emphasizing the indispensable roles of human oversi ght for quality control, noting concerns about the core probabilistic nature of these models. Recognizing a  number of LLM affordances to support research, tea ching, and learning, including writing support, coding, and finding patterns in qualitative data, the value of LLMs in triggering or organizing thoughts and ideas stood o ut, as it was consistently valued as a springboard for further ideation. Profs appreciated how LLMs proffered a fo undation to build upon without starting from scratc h. “A good starting point” was a phrase nearly every prof essor echoed, regardless of model or task. Prof 9 appreciated how an LLM could outline key points and help struct ure his thoughts when outlining a scientific paper introduction. He stated: “... kind of a starting point to trigger my thoughts around this (...) I had all these points in mind, but maybe a little bit blen ded together (...) This to me is a good way to pres ent things. So this kind of schema would be pretty useful, I think.” But he not ed that while the LLM content seemed reasonable, it often lacked depth, was sometimes meaningless, out of con text, or not cohesively linked with casual relation ships, needing frequent verification: “This breakdown it p roposes is I think it's reasonable. But now I need to check if the content is meaningful.” In general, as professors found some comfort in deputizing the LLM for fulfilling some aspects of their professorial work activities, they  critiqued their deputy  for not performing up to expected standards with appropriate refinements, references to verified scientific literature, and accurate cal culations and facts. Yet such emergent patterns suggest the budding of a productive co-evolution of human-LLM performance, with each partner playing complementary roles.

Professors’ system mental models and diminishing returns Through reflecting on using LLMs, profs revealed a nuanced mental model, recognizing LLMs lack understanding and merely “put words together”, yet can still be u seful, e.g., Prof 1’s sentiments: “You know, there' s no understanding here. It's just statistically…putting together sente nces. Of course, there's no understanding of my intentions, or it's just a statistic though. Someti mes it's good and impressive.” From this recognitio n of the probabilistic nature of these models, a number  of other system assumptions follow, such as the nee d to verify outputs-given their propensity to being wrong, incomplete, unmeaningful-and the need of further iterations to achieve useful outcomes. Prof 1 reflected on prompt engineering: “you have to work a lot with the prompts to get what you want.” In general, professors’ experiences highlight limit ations in achieving improvements through iterations alone, attributed to the increasing costs of further interactions. This is exemplified by Prof 7, who, while using the LLM to design a new course from scratch, remarked: “I can run based on this data and it was just not really getting better. Then I thought, okay, this is something tha t I cando myself cause it's not really improving. So kind of reached a plateau”. This notion of the technology h itting a p lateau was experienced by many professors, who reported disengaging from the LLM once perceiving that their inputs yielded diminishing returns, especially when it began to produce irrelevant content or only refl ected the professor’s own ideas. Most professors wo uld then stop and take control, observing that the LLM could n’t give them more, thanks to its intrinsic limitat ions or if they took up the LLM offerings as response to their first few iterations. Yet Prof 6 saw the technology’s potential despite its initial imperfections and further iterated: “I would just keep going and debug a little bit myself. Usually I would have to…you know, actually have to write al l of this…well it would probably take me an hour to  even write the first version, right? Figure out small issues like how to make a mountain range. I'd then google and like 15 min you find out about this program "noise" and how to implement that. This and that in here (inaud ible) But in theory, at least, you, you could tell, oh, imple ment probably noise for those parameters (inaudible)... so that is quite useful and that usually works well. It's kind of back and forth.” Considering Hutchins et al. (1985) concepts of gulf s of execution and evaluation in systems interfaces, we may reason that if LLMs narrow the gulf of execu tion by enabling users to specify intentions in nat ural language, they widen the gulf of evaluation by necessitating that humans interpret and verify the LLM’s outputs. Yet, this challenge is further compounded by another gulf, that of envisioning (Subramonyam et al., 2024), which can be thought of as the distance between the human’s initial intentions and their formulatio n of a prompt that foresees how LLM capabilities and training data can  be leveraged to generate high-quality output. Professors’ experiences and reflections underscore that the nar rower this gulf is, the lower the cost of interaction, as people are typically attuned to efficiency. That said, in interactions between LLMs and humans, it seems esse ntial to develop an adequate system mental model in order to achieve intentions at reasonable costs. Human-AI reliance anxiety Reflections revealed apprehensions by profs and the ir students to LLM use, academic integrity concerns, critical thinking skills erosion, and legitimate assistance boundaries. Prof 2 shared anxiety in using LLMs for  teaching since she might not fulfill her educator role: “Maybe this is fear that I, you know, would be cheating (...) Because I'm not really doing my job. Is it fine to let this  tool help me too much? I'm planning to just…take t his and send it to the students & this is what we are going to d o. I think there is still this feeling for me that using  it is a bit cheating. She worried re: balancing LLM use and pre serving human thinking: “I don't know, so maybe in tasks that would require higher thinking skills, you know, like. I mean, I don't know what people use it for (...) If you're kind of asking the tool to think for you. Because I think the only thing we have is thinking.” Turning to students’ anxieties, Prof 4 noted the importance of encouraging exploring LLMs to determine how they can provide benefits if used thoughtfully.  She addresses anxieties felt by students who refra in from using the tools, fearing their use may be perceived as cheating or believing it prevents real learning: “Yeh, I think so. I think that could be something that actually s eparates students because you have those students w ho see the benefit, who use it, and you have those students wh o don't even try because they think it’s cheating. And maybe this can help see that I don't need to cheat using it, but I can use it to learn. And if it can manage that, I think I can help. For instance, I was planning on giving them a simple code just to run this first equation in class as a start for the assignment and maybe it can help because some s tudents are afraid of using it. Yeah, I have severa l students who say I would never touch it, because I feel that that's kind of like they don't learn.” Minimal experiences threshold for LLM utility Professor reflections on LLM interactions highlight  having a “minimal threshold of experiences” for LLM utility(Zamfirescu-Pereira, et al., 2023). Prof 6 noted that while LLM s could offer partial answers, foundational understanding was needed to use its outputs and to critically assess their reliability and complete ness. His assignments embody his insight as he encouraged stu dents to use ChatGPT as a quasi-teaching assistant: “My assignment uses a prompt for ChatGPT to get an answ er that is at least 70% there along with annotation s and explanations of what the different parts do. Just almost like another teaching assistant. I don’t know if they actually used it. I think it could be a good idea for them to use it. Because, as I said, they will get 70% of the way. It works somehow, but not completely. But you still have to know what you’re doing.” Prof 4 emphasized the technology’s ability to generate relevant keywords and provide o verviews (which aid formulating follow-up questions) still requires foundational knowledge to critically evaluate outputs (which ris k mistakes): “It gives me quite a lot of material like I expected. So when it's defining stu ff, it usually gives me the right keywords, so it g ives me something to kind of ask next. And it gives me a ki nd of good overview. It does make mistakes. So it a lso tells me that you have to know stuff. You can’t rely just on the first question because now we did some changes and then we and I had to ask again and then it realized it was wrong and so it also confirms my initial idea that it's not too useful if you don't know anything. So by having knowledge, it's helping because I can say this is not right and then give me the right one. So then I can use that further, but if you don't have that knowledge, you will struggle.” These reflections yield the key insight that while LLMs are powerful cognitive and learning aids, thei r utility soars when one has foundational experiences and  subject understanding. This occasions productive L LM interactions by enabling educators and students to discern the accuracy and reliability of information these models provide. From a human-computer interaction perspective (Subramonyam et al., 2024), while LLMs could be potentially useful in reducing costs in formulating intentions through natural language dia logue leading to desired output, they increase the costs of perceiving, interpreti ng and evaluating the output given  by the LLMs’ probabilistic process, which can lead to inaccuracies or incompleteness. “Too human” Professors offered intriguing reflections on interacting with non-human entities. Some felt compelled to be polite despite awareness of communicating with an inanimat e object—no human was ‘behind the curtain’ (Wizard of Oz trope). Prof 2: “OK, I've never t ried being rude. I think I feel like I have to be p olite. Even though I know it's like there's no one there. I kind of think I feel l ike I have to be polite.” Yet Prof 6 voiced concern s of being too courteous to a non-person, reasoning he wanted to be aware of the bot's non-human nature: “I kind of started just dropping all the courtesy when talking to these mac hines because I'm trying to distinguish between tal king to humans and talking to a computer. That's kind of weird. I think, if we ask it, please do. No, I did, but I dropped it off. I make a conscious effort. Because you have to completely cut it out since it's not a person.” Professors reflecting on conversations with machines raise fascinating questions. One concerns how we must precisely consider how we want machines to approach us. If we prefer to be approached politely, we should train and interact with AIs politely. Yet t here’s the danger Aronsson (2020) depicted in her s tudy with social robots in Japan elder care, that “As we start to tr eat machines as if they were almost human, we may b egin to develop habits that will have us treating human beings as almost-machines” (p.451). Professors' reflections on the need to be ever-conscious when we are interacting with machines raise further questions. It's not only about how we interact with machines; there are tangible dangers in overly humanizing them. Assigning human traits-either through language or appearance-to technology can lead people to trust them more th an is warranted. Yet attributing beliefs and emotion to non-humans is not only recognized as a universal featur e of our cognition (Reeves & Nass, 1996; Shevlin, n.d.), but there is evidence of its advantages in facilitating learning (Cheng et al., 2024). As professors shift their thinking to the design of technologies powered by large language models, how to reconcile their impressive conversational abilities  in ways that do not exaggerate their capabilities and entra p us over time into god-like attributions (Lanier, 2011)? How human is too human?
