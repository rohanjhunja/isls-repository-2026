# Intertwined Development of Interest Pursuits

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Whereas interest research has long focused on one specific interest within one context (e.g. mathematics interest in class), students pursue multiple interests in parallel. As such, development of interest pursuits can become intertwined. The current study explores such intertwined developments by using a qualitative ESM smartphone application and interviews with 30 students (aged 14-25 years) in different educational programs. Analysis of the interviews shows three main intertwined developments: 1) object genesis, where one interest develops out of another, 2) parallel engagement, i.e. two interests co-occur or their engagement is mediated by similar skills or knowledge, and 3) related meaning-making, i.e. two interests have a similar meaning or gain meaning in relation to each other. Whereas interest theory typically speaks of interest development as a process of deepening of a single interest, the results of the current study reveal how interest development can also reflect broadening and connecting of multiple interests.

## Introduction

Whereas interest research has long focused on one specific interest within one context (e.g. mathematics interest in class), in recent years there is increasing attention for multiplicity of interest (Akkerman & Bakker, 2019; Hofer, 2010; Krapp, 2002). Small-scale studies such as Akk erman and Bakker (2019) have demonstrated how adolescents pursue multiple interests in parallel, which concern a high variety of domains (academic a nd nonacademic) and encompass topic-, activity-and ev ent-based objects of interest. Given this multiplic ity, as a student moves through daily life, they take with th em all their previous (interest) experiences into t he current moment (Dewey, 1938; Roth & Jornet, 2014). As such, connections between interests are likely to arise, whether unintentionally or prompted by others, and these in terest pursuits may (temporarily) become intertwine d and develop in parallel.

Not much is known about the way interests develop in relation to each other, although case-studies have provided some initial conjectures. For instance, Az evedo (2011) investigates amateur model rocketeers and observes links with other objects of interest. Amat eur rocketeer Bill, in this study, is also a movie fanatic and often specifically seeks out movies about rockets. His friend George has a computer engineering background and consequently experiments with incorporating electric machinery in his rockets. Similar examples can be found in work of Ito and colleagues (2019), e.g. a girl makes crochet designs associated with her interest in Harry Potter. Next to such intertwined interest pursuits, Slot and colleagues (2020) report on alternation of intere st based on their investigation of how school is reflected in students’ interest experiences. They find that students sometimes deliberately alternate school-related interests with leisure interests (e.g., gaming or watching serie s) in order to process their school work or rejuvenate after a lon g day. These articles together show some initial ex amples of intertwined developments, and demonstrate the need for a more systematic investigation of intertwined development of interest pursuits over time. As such, the present study aims to extend understanding of  interest development as not just the deepening of a single i nterest, but also the broadening and connecting of multiple interests. By explicating the ways in which adolescents relate their interest pursuits in daily life, we hope to aid educators in recognizing and amplifying existing co nnections between objects of (potential) interest f or adolescents within classroom settings and beyond.

In the current study we describe interest developme nt from a comprehensive person-objects-contexts perspective such as proposed in earlier work by the  last author (i.e., P-O-Cs perspective, Akkerman & Bakker, 2019), a perspective that assumes persons to continuously pursue and shape multiple idiosyncratic interests across multiple social contexts throughout their everyday lives. We explore the development of interest pursuits in daily life by answering the following research question: What intertwined interest development do adolescents report? A better understanding of the developmental process es of multiple interest pursuits can extend interes t theories beyond a single interest and support theorizing multiplicity. This in turn can add to the practical understanding of interest developments which may seem unexpected when just looking at a single interest. In addition, explicating the intertwined development of adolescents’ interes ts can aid approaches that aim to connect education  to adolescents’ daily life experiences (e.g., Ito et al., 2019), by demonstrating how these connections naturally take shape and develop in daily life.

## Methods

In order to explore the multidirectional developments in parallel interests, we employed a qualitative longitudinal approach using interviews informed by qualitative E SM (Experience Sampling Method) data collected usin g a smartphone application called inTin. The timeline of this study is outlined in Figure 1. The participating students were interviewed three times, with one year in between each interview. Preceding the interviews, students reported on their interests in inTin during three intermittent weeks (three to four months in between each week). Data was collected between March 2018 and December 2020, the  last two inTin weeks and the last interview took p lace during the COVID-19 pandemic.

## Participants

Thirty students participated in the interviews and ESM study. Participants were enrolled in diverse ed ucational tracks in the Netherlands, including (pre-)vocation al education, general (professional) and (pre-) uni versity education (in Dutch: mavo, havo, vwo, mbo, hbo, wo; five participants per track) and were aged 14-25 ye ars at the start of the study, with 63% female. This diver se sample was chosen to capture a large variety of complex interest experiences across different educational settings and life-wide situations. The first intervi ew took place in the final year of the students’ study program. Respondents volunteered to participate and were offered financial compensation for taking part in the study. All participants signed an informed consent form and for participants who were younger than 16, a parent or caregiver signed an additional permission form. Ethical approval for this study was given by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Social Sciences. Materials The methods employed in this study concern interviews informed by qualitative ESM data. An ESM application was used to capture (changes in) daily-life interests of the participating students. Students used this application to log their existing and novel (incidental) interests that they experienced within a week. Following from our personcentered perspective, participants were free to give any name to their objects of interest instead of choosing from pre-existing categories, doing justice to the idiosyncratic nature of interest (Akkerman & Bakker, 2019). Each participant was interviewed three times, with one year in between each interview. The interview scheme contained semi-structured questions that were adapted to the interests that the participants reported in the ESM application during the three weeks of using the  application preceding the interview. In the first interview, participants were asked about the history, value and forms of engagement for several of their interests, aiming at a variety of different interests (e.g., asking them to talk about an interest they had pursued for a long time and an interest that was rather new). These questions were aimed at one interest at a time, but participants often mentioned other interests that they considered related. In ad dition, participants were asked to think about next  year, and to predict which of their objects of interest they would still be interested in and why, which probed them to consider any foreseen trade-offs between interests. In the s econd and third interview, the students were asked about any changes in their objects of interests that occurred  since the preceding interview. Each experienced ch ange was discussed in more detail using semi-structured questions, asking about the experienced origin of the change, and about the current value and form(s) of engaging. In answering these, participants regularly related these changes to (changes in) their other interests.

Analysis The second interview is the main focus of analysis.  In this interview we detected relations between in terests by marking passages where the student, when talking about one interest, mentions one of their other objects of interest. For example, when asked about their interest in hiking, a participant describes how they also listen to music while hiking, which is one of the other objects identifie d as interest. For each interest-relation that is i dentified in this way, passages about the associated interests are se lected in all three interviews. When reading these interview ICLS2022 Proceedings 982 © ISLS excerpts, we selected for further analysis only those relations where a) the relation between the interests is objectbased and b) the interests are involved in each other’s development. This means we did not include relations where the described connection was generic (e.g., when reading a book is mentioned in relation to soccer because they both take up leisure time), nor relations without indication of intertwined development. For each selected interest relation, we made short descriptions of all involved interests and their relation at each interview timepoint. These descriptions were further synthesized in one summary per relation that reflected the core of the intertwined development. We then co ntrasted and grouped together all interest relation s that showed comparable intertwined development, until exhaustive and meaningful categories were reached. Half of the sample is used for initial explorative analysis, and after the categories were determined we examined whether these were supported by data of the remaining fifteen adolescents.

## Results

Results demonstrate notable inter-and intraindividual varieties in development of associated interests. Whereas some adolescents experience many connections between their parallel interests, others seem to experience or talk about their interests in more isolated ways and mention only few notable relations between their various interest pursuits. Below we describe the three intertwined developments that we observed in the data. Object genesis Firstly, adolescents described how novel objects of  interests arose from engagement with their longsta nding objects of interests. For example, Ronald has been interested in “playing videogames” with friends for a long time. In the interview, when asked how his interest in “p rogramming” started, he mentions: “My brother bough t an Xbox when I was six, or eight, the very first one and then we started gaming together. So that's how the interest in those devices started actually. It was quite a l ogical step for me, it's just fun.” In this case it  seems that the content of playing videogames and programming are c lose enough together that simply playing games spar ked Ronald’s interest in programming. Ronald pursued a study program and career in IT, which shows that this interest that initially arose from a leisure interest becomes a long-lasting theme in his educational and professional life. Further examples show the mediating role that other people can play in this process. This is evident for Steve, who was interested in “badminton” for a long  time, training multiple times a week and being ver y competitive. In the first interview he says he defi nitely wants to continue playing badminton, but pre ceding the second interview two of his teammates quit the spor t. His remaining teammate then said he was going to  play tennis instead, and Steve tells us: "So then I said: ‘great, I'll do that too’.” Despite his initial p lans to continue playing badminton, he switches to tennis rather imp ulsively. During the third interview he still becam e increasingly more skilled at and enthusiastic about  his new interest in “tennis”. Other people within a certain interest-related practice can thus suggest ideas th at become identified as new objects of interest, wh ich can subsequently last for a long time.

Parallel engagement A second intertwined development regards parallel engagement of interests. The most predominant way in which we observed this to happen is co-occurrence, wherein an adolescent recognizes and appreciates multiple objects of interest within the same moment. For example, Piper is interested in “music”, and enjoys listening to all kinds ranging from classical music to heavy metal. She describes how she sometimes recognizes and appreciates music when watching movies, which for her is another interest: “If I hear something in a movie, … if I hear something somewhere that really grabs my attention, then I im mediately turn on Shazam and then I link it right t o Spotify and I have it right there and then, yeah, I can stu dy it a little bit, listen to it a little bit more often.” She also describes how, when watching certain movies, she ca n really feel the music inside and it makes her fee l strong emotions. In Piper’s case, these objects of interest co-occur because they are combined in societal structures (i.e., films generally contain music), which she then both recognizes within one situation. In some cases the objects of interest even seem to be nested, meaning they canno t be separated but co-occur by default of the socia l activity (e.g., “science” and “Medicine”, “judo” and “giving  judo training”). This is contrasted with cases whe re cooccurrence is not imposed by (societal) structures but emerges from daily life or from personal initiatives. Notably, we also found instances where participants described parallel engagement without the interests co-occurring in a situation. These engagements were connected through skills, knowledge or resources by which engagement of both interests developed in a similar  way, which we term mediated engagement. This can be illustrated by Nicky, who is interested in the “new s”, which she reads daily and discusses with her pa rents. She feels it is important to know what is going on in the world and it makes her realize how good her own life is. After completing secondary school she starts studying “Medicine” and there learns to read scientific articles critically. She observes that this has changed the way she read s the daily news as well, looking at news articles more ICLS2022 Proceedings 983 © ISLS critically (especially when this concerns scientifi c findings). In this way, the adolescent describes how the development of these interests is intertwined by sk ills that are used in both practices, or transporte d from one practice to another.

Related meaning-making A third intertwined development concerns adolescent s relating the meaning they ascribe to their intere st, to another interest. A first exhibit of this concerns interests pursuits whose meaning is similar to each  other, i.e. comparable meaning. Nicky talks about this in the context of her inte rest in “childcare”, her side job, which decreased when she started studying Medicine: “I have always liked taking care of people, I can't explain it, but I used to get that satisfaction from childcare. But n ow I'm pursuing a study where actually caring for p eople is central, so that's where I kind of get that satisfaction now”. Although she still likes to work at the daycare, it has become less important to her and she talks about looking for another job closer to her university. In this way, the comparable meaning of both interests has consequenc es for the continuity of her interest in childcare:  it seems less necessary than before. It should be remarked t hat comparable meanings were often observed for mul tiple sport-related interests, which often had a similar meaning in an adolescents life (e.g., maintaining a  healthy lifestyle) and thus were seen as interchangeable.

Whereas the above describes a stable relation betwe en the interests, we also observed many instances where an interest gains a different meaning in rela tion to another interest pursuit, which we call transformed meaning. For example, Hilbert becomes increasingly interested in what he calls “personal development”: learning new things in general and setting goals for his fut ure. In light of this interest and his desire to do  useful things with his time, he reconsiders the value of other in terests, such as “Netflix” and “gaming”: “If you lo ok at what gaming brings me, then it is not as much as three hours invested in watching YouTube videos on how I can achieve my goal or … whether I want to become smarter in a way or whatever. So that's why certain traits have been toned down or left out altogether, like gaming and Netfli x.” In other words, Hilbert looks at his interests in “gaming” and “Netflix” through the lens of his interest in “personal development”, and increasingly regards playing games and watching series on Netflix as a waste of time c ompared to activities that allow him to develop as a person. Hence, Hilbert compares and contrasts his interests, which leads to one of them decreasing in value and possibly disappearing altogether.

## Discussion

The aim of the present research was to explore inte rtwined development of interest pursuits. By studyi ng what connections adolescents themselves make between the ir interests, we have demonstrated how intertwined development can be described in terms of object gen esis, parallel engagement and related meaning-makin g. Whereas interest theory typically speaks of interes t development as a process of deepening of a single  interest (e.g., Hidi & Renninger, 2006; Renninger & Hidi, 2015), the results of the current study demonstrate how interest development can also reflect broadening and connecting of multiple interests through these three developments. Processes of object genesis, parallel engagement and related meaning-making were observed for objects of interest that can be considered close in terms of domain (e.g., food/health and cycling), but also for objects that are societally further apart (e.g., food and music). Hence, even when connections seem unlikely given societal distance, in daily life different objects of interests can connect. This goes not only go for leisure interests, but also for school related interests. Hence, educators that want to connect their learning materials to students’ daily life, may start by being open to the connections that students themselves already naturally see.
