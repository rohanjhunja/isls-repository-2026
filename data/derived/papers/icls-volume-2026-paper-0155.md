# Patterns of Student-AI Collaboration in Problem-Solving: An Ordered Network Analysis

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research investigates interaction dynamics between graduate students and AI agents during collaborative problem-solving activities within a curriculum design context. Analyzing 1,569 conversational exchanges, we applied ordered network analysis to ma p behavioral sequences and identify emergent patterns across problem-solving phases. Three dominant interaction pathways emerged: students soliciting AI perspectives, followed by resource provision; students seeking clarification with AI, which responds to refine their understanding; and students prompting elaboration that triggers extended cognitive exchanges. These patterns reveal AI functioning beyond a passive information source, instead operating as an active collaborator that reciprocally shapes problem-solving discourse. Findings indicate the formation of hybrid intelligence environments where human-AI partnerships distribute cognitive work. This research contributes theoretical insights into human-AI collaboration, demonstrates ordered network analysis as a methodology for examining conversational patterns, and offers practical implications for designing educationally productive AI-mediated learning experiences.

Collaborative problem-solving (CPS) represents an essential competency requiring coordinated social and cognitive processes among participants (O'Neil et al., 2024). Effective collaboration historically centered on human-to-human exchange, yet recent devel opments position AI as an active partner rather than merely a tool. Students now engage AI agents for ideation, planning, and critical inquiry across various academic contexts (Kim et al., 2024), including design-oriented tasks traditionally viewed as exclusively human endeavors. Student-AI collaboration may yield cognitive, metacognitive, and affective benefits (Kim et al., 2025); however, the socio-cognitive complexity of these interactions requires further investigation. Recent scholarship has shifted to examining how interaction patterns unfold chronologically and focusing on overall interaction features (Kim et al., 2024). However, as CPS is multi-phase and process-oriented, gaps remain regarding behavioral dynamics across CPS phases and their relationship to performance outcomes. This study examines interaction patterns during multi-stage curriculum design activities, addressing the following research questions: RQ1: What sequential student-AI interaction (SAI) patterns emerge across collaborative problem-solving stages? RQ2: How does SAI influence task performance?

## Literature review

Student-AI collaborative problem solving CPS draws from constructivist theory, emphasizing social interaction's role in knowledge construction (Feng, 2025). Effective CPS requires participants to jointly construct shared problem understanding and co-develop solutions, fostering critical thinking, reasoning, and metacognitive skills through collective engagement with complex challenges (Law et al., 2025). Design thinking (DT) provides one structured approach for CPS, guiding learners through iterative phases of problem analysis, stakeholder consideration, ideation, prototyping, and evaluation (Avci & Durak, 2025).

Contemporary CPS extends beyond human-human collaboration to include AI agents. Research shows that generative AI enables human-like interactions (Kim & Cho, 2023) and supports problem decomposition and perspective-taking (Kim et al., 2025). Moreover, CPS unfolds as a dynamic, sequential process among collaborators (Ashwin et al., 2023). However, empirical studies still tend to treat AI as a static tool or outcome predictor, overlooking the dynamic nature and stages of interactions in CPS. Given that AI can provide prompts, 1257 feedback, and solution suggestions at different task points, examining the sequential patterns of student-AI collaboration is crucial for understanding how AI shapes the CPS process. Student-AI interaction and learning outcomes Emerging research has begun to explore how AI-supported interactions affect learning outcomes and task performance. Evidence suggests that AI can foster CPS skills, such as perspective-taking and task regulation. For example, students using generative AI in digital storytelling showed greater improvements than those using conventional digital tools (Wei et al., 2025). However, findings remain mixed, as uncritical reliance on AI may impede higher-order thinking necessary for effective collaboration (Guo et al., 2025). Few studies have compared student performance across multiple dimensions between AI-supported and traditional learning conditions, leaving the relationship between student-AI collaboration and task outcomes underexplored.

## Participants

This research occurred within an MSc Digital Education module (EDS431) at a Sino-British international university employing blended, design-centered pedagogy with integrated AI tools. Forty-three master's students participated: 95.3% Chinese (n=41), 4.6% international (n=2, Canadian and Namibian); 90.7% female (n=39), 9.3% male (n=4) —a distribution typical of Education programs. Average age was 26 (range: 23-41), including recent graduates and experienced professionals.

Participants' undergraduate disciplines demonstrated exceptional diversity, spanning humanities (Calligraphy, French, Media Studies), sciences (Biology, Data Science), and engineering (Unmanned Aircraft Systems). This disciplinary heterogeneity, combined with demographic homogeneity, created optimal conditions for ordered network analysis within standardized design processes. While the predominantly Chinese, female cohort might appear to limit generalizability, this demographic consistency effectively controls for confounding variables in CPS, enabling clearer focus on interaction behaviors themselves. Task design and procedures The module centered on designing and developing a digital education curriculum. This was a complex, iterative problem-solving task structured around a seven-week DT framework (Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test) based on fishbone digital learning design principles (Li et al., 2024). Students defined learning objectives, analyzed learner needs, synthesized theoretical models, and integrated technology to address educational gaps. Projects varied widely: online sustainability courses, blended scientific writing modules, or mobile coding apps for primary students. Complexity was scaffolded through sequential development, first designing overall course architecture, then fully implementing specific components such as AI tutors or interactive games. AI tools were strategically mapped to DT stages  and accessed via participants' personal devices and accounts. Interaction duration and frequency were unrestricted, reflecting authentic course engagement. During Empathize (Week 1), students used a custom conversational agent ("EDS431 AI TA," XPU AI/Kimi) for simulated user interviews. The Define stage employed vi sualization tools (Miro AI, Xmind AI) for empathy mapping and problem articulation. Ideate (Week 2) utilized Miro AI for SCAMPER-based brainstorming. Prototype and Test phases (Weeks 3-7) incorporated Canva AI, Moodle+H5P, HTML, and XPU AI/Kimi for component development. This sequential deployment of conversational and task-specific AI tools generated rich data on interaction pattern adaptation across cognitive demands.

## Data analysis

To address RQ1, SAI data were extracted from multiple sources: XPU AI chat histories (provided by the university), and student-submitted chatlogs and screen recordings from Miro AI, Xmind AI, Canva AI, and Kimi AI aligned with DT phases in summative assessments. Screen recordings captured chatlogs, and were transcribed using AI-assisted tools (ChatGPT-4) and cross-verified by two researchers.  From 2,146 collected threads, we retained only those relevant to CPS, excluding small talk and incomplete exchanges. After filtering, 1,569 threads (2,382 utterances) remained.

Data underwent Ordered Network Analysis (ONA). Utterances were segmented using sentences as minimal meaning units and coded according to Zhang et al.'s (2022) CPS framework across three categories: sharing resources/ideas (S1-S3: sharing information, providing retrieval cues, responding with task-relevant resources), negotiating ideas (N1-N6: expressing agreement/disagreement, requesting responses, seeking clarification, identifying deficiencies, elaborating statements), and regulating problem-solving activities (R1-R9: identifying objectives, suggesting next steps, expressing confusion/progress, reflecting on achievements, checking 1258 understanding, assessing contributions). Non-text AI outputs were coded according to their functional role in the CPS. For example, when a student requested an image and the AI generated one, it was coded as responding to a request (S3). Two researchers established interrater reliability through iterative coding (initial Cohen's kappa = 0.703; final = 0.920) before independently coding all threads. The 1,569 threads were grouped by DT stage (average 239 threads per stage). A thread was defined as a student input followed by AI responses.  ONA was conducted using R packages (Saqr & López -Pernas, 2024) with a four-utterance moving window reflecting typical topic completion patterns. Singular value decomposition reduced dimensionality, with visualizations showing node size (code frequency), i nner circles (self-loops), directed arrows (transitions), and two-dimensional positioning (co-occurrence relationships). For RQ2, students completed an independent work and a subsequent AI-collaborative work, both based on identical requirements. The AI-collaborative task was built on prior independent work and followed it in a natural learning progression. Three education experts evaluated student curriculum designs using a sixdimensional rubric (learning goals clarity, coherence/alignment, innovation/creativity, inclusivity/responsiveness, CPS integration evidence, feasibility/practicality) on a 5-point Likert scale. Performance differences between the two works were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests in SPSS 27.0. Student-AI collaborative problem solving Across all DT phases, codes N3 (requesting responses), N4 (seeking clarification), N6 (elaborating ideas), and S3 (providing task-relevant information) exhibited the highest frequencies. Statistically significant sequences N3 → N6, N3 → N4, and N3 → S3 per sisted throughout, indicating response requests serve as core triggers for collaborative dynamics. This pattern demonstrates AI providing instrumental and scaffolding support within student-AI collaboration (Kim et al., 2024).

Empathy phase N3 dominated, followed by S3, N6, and N4, reflecting information gathering and perspective-taking essential to understanding user needs (Razzouk & Shute, 2012). This phase activated the most nodes, indicating comprehensive interaction strategies. Prominent  sequence N3 → S3 showed AI providing evidence in response to student requests. Self-transitions in N3 and S3 revealed continuous asking and responding cycles. Node positioning indicated sharing resources/ideas rarely co-occurred with regulating problem-solving activities (Figure 1).

Define phase S3 became most prominent as focus shifted from broad insights to precise problem articulation requiring structured information. Sequence S1 → N4 showed proactive information sharing prompting clarification requests. Pattern R1 → S3 revealed cognitive labor  division: students framed problems while AI handled information retrieval (Gandhi et al., 2023). Overlap of N3 and S3 indicated responses frequently triggered deeper cognitive processing (Figure 1).

Ideate phase N6 dominated, showing active idea elaboration with AI support. Strongest edge N3 → N6 demonstrated AI elaborating detailed responses to student requests. N6's large self-loop reflected iterative refinement consistent with DT principles (Razzouk & Shute, 2012). The proximity of N6 and S3 indicated mutual interdependence between elaboration and responsive contributions (Figure 1). Prototype phase S3 was most prominent. Sequences R1 → S3 and R1 → N3 showed AI providing resources or triggering clarification after students articulated prototype goals, illustrating AI's dual role as collaborator and scaffolding tool (Chiu, 2025). S3's self-loop emphasized AI's sustained support during iterative prototyping (Figure 1). Test phase N3, S3, N6, and N4 remained prominent with fewest total nodes. Sequences S1 → R2 and N4 → R2 showed resource sharing and clarification requests triggering next-step suggestions. The self-loop in N3 reflects repeated questioning (Figure 1).

This study revealed distinct SAI patterns during CPS within a DT framework, analyzing 1,569 conversation threads from graduate curriculum design tasks. Ordered network analysis identified consistent sequences across all phases, demonstrating AI's role as an active support resource. Phase-specific patterns emerged: empathy emphasized information gathering, define focused on structured articulation, ideate highlighted iterative refinement, while prototype and test maintained resource provision and elaboration cycles. Student task performance improved significantly overall (Z = 7.04, p ≤ 0.001), particularly in CPS integration, feasibility, and goal clarity. However, innovation/creativity showed no significant gains, potentially reflecting limited critical feedback (N2, N5) and regulatory behaviors (R1-R9) in observed interactions. Theoretically, findings provide an empirical foundation for human-AI collaboration models in education.
