# Distant Collaboration at University: Anticipatory and Reactive Emotion Regulation of Argumentation-Based Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We study emotion regulation in a distant Collaborative Argumentation Based-Learning (CABLe) setting at university. We analyze how students achieve the group task of synthesizing the literature on a topic through scientific argumentation on the institutional Moodle’s forum. Distinguishing anticipatory from reactive emotion regulation shows how essential it is to establish and maintain a constructive working climate in order to make the best out of disagreement both on social and cognitive planes. We operationalize the analysis of anticipatory emotion regulation through an analytical grid applied to the data of two groups facing similar disagreement. Thanks to sharp anticipatory regulation, group 1 solved the conflict while group 2 had to call out for teacher regulation. The observed complementary use of synchronous tools for emotion regulation led us to discuss the affordances of the technical environment studied and to draw recommendations for fostering distant CABLe at university.

## Introduction

Many university teachers design Collaborative Argumentation Based-Learning (C ABLe) tasks to foster distant socio-cognitive conflict (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995) for disciplinary improvement. N evertheless, engaging productively in such tasks requires appropriate group emotions to solve the socio-c ognitive conflict on the cognitive plane (Polo et al., 2016). From an ecological perspective on affordance, we study how students successfully establish and preserve a positive working climate, managing conf lict in a constructive way. Our argument is illustrated by an in depth case study investigating how students in a F rench university structure and regulate a group literature review about adult education making use of different tools in their environment.

## Theoretical background: Affording emotion regulation in ecological setting

Emotion regulation of CABLe as both reactive and anticipatory Teacher-led, self-and group emotion regulations Traditionally, learning regulation falls into teacher’s competency, responsible for ensuring that each member of the classroom group is in an emotional state appropriate for learning. Complementa ry to this key role of the teacher, self-regulated learning theory has pointed students’ own work to plan, monitor and evaluate not only the cognitive but also the affective aspects of their learning activity (e. g. Zimmerman, 2000). Learners, through such regulation process, may even modify their emotions (occurrence, intensity, duration), with a positive effect on their motivation and achievement (Wolters, 2003).

In CSCL settings, regulation at the group level was studied through multiple term s: co-regulation, collective or collaborative regulation, shared regulation, socially shared regulation, socially shared metacognitive regulation, socially shared metacognition, (Hadwin et al., 2018). Here we use the term ‘group regulation’ to refer to students’ regulating activity at the group level, without any a priori judgement  about the extent to which such activity is truly collaborative or shared among the group members. Järvenoja  & Järvelä (2009) have shown that students may specifically regulate emotions in a CSCL context. Using a linguistic approach, our study encompasses any communicative act regulating individual or group activity on the affective plane. The role of face-work and self-identity footing in argumentation Such communicative approach recognizes the specificity of argumentative interactions regarding the preservation of participants’ face. In CABLe, the students have both to ensure that th e collaboration keeps on going through usual face-work (Brown & Levinson, 1988), and to explore the socio-cognitive confli ct by showing overt disagreement and justifying it with arguments, a face-threatening, disprefer red attitude in daily conversations (Pomeranz, 1984). Displaying emotions regarding their faces, the members of a  group may figure out the contextually relevant in-between politeness system and align on a self-identity footin g allowing them to engage collectively into argumentation (Polo et al., 2017). Revealing the social functions of emotions in reasoning, such interactional alignment contributes to group emotion regulation. What is at stake is notably to avoid the socioCSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 12 cognitive conflict, which is a priori beneficial for learning (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995), to be solved on the social plane only, either avoiding cognitive disagreement by developing cumulative talk or turning to personal fights of opinions in disputational talk (Polo et al., 2016). Even though conflict is needed and might be fruitful in CABLe, it would only be so if appropriate regulation of emotions prevent it to turn into a dispute. A need for acknowledgement of anticipatory emotion regulation Research on emotion regulation has little studied this type of phenomenon so far, m ostly focusing on the metadiscursive level, involving explicit monitoring of affective arousals. For instance, some studies consider the benefits of talking about emotions related to the task or the collaboration for group emotional awareness and regulation (Järvenoja & Järvelä, 2013; Näykki, et al., 2017). The literature on emotio n regulation typically deals with identifying the socio-emotional challenges and the strategies that the teacher or the students, individually or interacting, develop to cope with them (e. g. Järvenoja and Järvelä, 2009). In this sense, regulation is apprehended as a reactive process to ‘negative’ emotions that threaten learning. Besides, part of this literature also mentions the display of ‘positive’ em otions, associated with higher-quality group regulation. Such positive ‘socio-emotional interactions’ are considered as spontaneous phenomenon, although clear indicators of active monitoring, evaluation and adaptation ar e identified to describe them, such as encouragement (Bakhtiar et al., 2018, Kwon et al., 2014), complimenting (Lajoie et al., 2015), motivating (Bakhtiar et al., 2018), socio-emotional support (Isohätälä, Näykki & Järvelä, 2019) and consideration of divergent views (Isohätälä et al., 2018). Some aspects are also stressed out at the discursive level, consisting of more implicit regulation: attentive listening and openness to divergent ideas (Ucan & Webb, 2015); signs of joint listening and respect, inclusion, and group cohesion (Rogat & Linnenbrink-Garcia, 2011); displaying a sense of community (Kwon et al., 2014), joint participation (Isohätälä, Näykki & Järvelä, 2019), tent ativeness of claims, and moderate tension relaxation (Isohätälä et al., 2018). But to date, no conceptual frame work clearly integrates into regulation theory such interactional work of creating a positive working climate. Occurring at the foreground even before socio-emotional challenges arise, we call for the recognition of such anticipatory emotion regulation. Ecological approach to affordance Distant teaching frames CABLe interactions and its emotional regulation by the  use of specific communicative tools. Understanding how they prove affording argumentation and affective metacognitive  regulating moves is necessary. Here we use the term ‘affordance’ as a concept comin g from the ecological approach of phenomenology. In this perspective, perception depends on certain properties of the envi ronment, apprehended as components of the environment to meet a vital or psychological need. Affordanc e is thus a process of "perceiving one's capacity to act", even before the action itself, which can thus be understood as the result of such perception (e. g. Stoffregen, 2003; Simonian et al. 2019). In CSCL, the pedagogical script and global orchestration play a great role on the affordance of the digital instruments provided to the students, the degree of prescription being a determining variable: a script that specifies the meaning of a tool related to the task promotes homogeneous affordances (Simonian et al., 2019). If the properties of the tools perceived by the students to perform the task me et the prescription, the instrumented environment affords for CABLe and its regulation.

## Pedagogical setting: Collaborative literature review through forum debate

We study an asynchronous online CABLe setting, as part of a distant master programme in Educational Sciences offered by a French University (2019-2020). The forum used is Moodle. As part of a specializing course in adult education, the teacher asked students in their 1st year of master to work collaboratively on a research question through asynchronous discussions, producing a literature review on the issue. The ass ignment’s instructions are the following:  1. work in groups of three to five, 2. choose a topic relate d to adult education, 3. conduct a desk research about it, 4. decide collectively on a research question, 5. explore it  through hypotheses and provide a synthesis. Each of these steps must be carried out through argumentation (supported by bibliographic research) on the Moodle forum, the teacher prescribing its use. At the end of the six-week course, each gr oup must upload their collective report, as well as an individual one (including personal summ ary, feedback on the teaching method, and 3 reading notes). The marks for the studied year (2019-2020) reflec t that all students passed the course succeeding ‘well’ or ‘very well’. However, one group had to calle out for teacher regulation. Multimodal dataset CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 13 The dataset consists of all the forum discussion of the 6 groups (25 students), their final collective and individual reports, and the email exchanges some had with the teacher. In this paper, we focus on the data related to 2 groups, putting forum discussion in perspective with final reflexive written narratives on the collaborative work. We aim at analyzing what happened within the group asking for teacher regulation (group 2), in comparison with another one where similar cognitivo-emotional tensions were self-regulated (group 1).

## Research questions & hypotheses

In order to study how these two groups self-regulated emotions related to the tas k and understand why group 2 finally asked for teacher regulation, we structured our investigation around 2 research questions and 3 hypotheses.

• RQ1-How do the two groups regulate the disagreement when conflict arises-which is to say, what are

their reactive emotion regulation practices?

• RQ2-Can considering anticipatory emotion regulation help better understand what di ffers between the

socio-cognitive processes that the two groups are engaged in?

• H1-Conflict self-regulation differs among the two groups, explaining that one fin ally needs extra

external regulation by the teacher.

• H2-Such differences relate to distinct previous working climate among each group achieved through

distinct anticipatory emotion regulation practices.

• H3-A group fruitfully articulating challenge on the cognitive plane and collaboration on the social plane

through displaying appropriate emotions is more likely to self-regulate the task. We also designed a coding scheme, specifically to address RQ2 regarding antic ipatory emotion regulation.

A coding scheme for anticipatory emotion regulation of CABLe By definition, anticipatory emotion regulation may occur at any time during group work, before conflict arises, at expressing disagreement or after a dispute, to prevent it from starting aga in. Theoretically, high quality anticipatory emotion regulation should frame collaboration on the long term, establ ishing a socioemotional climate beneficial for CABLe. After a first draft based on the literatur e, we refined the coding scheme for anticipatory emotion regulation inductively, on the basis of our data (cf. Table 1 & Table 2). ## Quantitative overview of the two forum discussions

Group 2 (n = 3, 3 girls) produced more content than Group 1 (n = 3, 1 girl, 2 boys) (Group 1: 51 messages, 10266 words; Group 2: 126 messages, 18889 words), which indicates that its difficulty in self-regulating conflict cannot be explained by low engagement. We hypothesize that such need for external regulation relates to the anticipatory emotion regulation practices developed in this group before the conflict (H2). To e xplore such hypothesis (H2), we compare the activity of this group with the one of group 1, because it also comprises 3 students and faced similar disagreement, occurring at the same stage of the assignment: the elaboration of a research question. Such disagreement, in messages 3 and 4, gi ves birth to well-structured and respectful argumentation. Clémence is the student displaying the more self-regulation practices (the 2 own work planning utterances so far and most of the following ones), specifically in terms of self-efficacy (valuing her work 3 times). She also proves reflective (1 utterance) and once capable of insisting when not considered, doing so at the first person, emotionally expressing her “staying convinced” that the research question shou ld not be limited to school. Djamel only shows perseverance embodied in repeating twice his challenged opinion (focusing on school). At the group level, politeness is very developed, either through institutionalized practices (3 by Djamel, 11 by Clemence), systematic name signatures, and by warmly calling each other. Articulating collaboration and challenge in CABLe (h3) At the end of the day, practices of anticipatory emotion regulation are obser ved in the two groups, either before conflict arises or once it seems overcome (group 2). At the individual level, the degree and nature of self-regulation differs from one student to another. If group members trust each other enough to  clearly express their feelings related to the ongoing task, it makes it possible for others to motivate them or give them feedbacks. Here, the two groups rather succeed in self-regulating by referring to their activity. But discursive markers show that they unequally manage to articulate the collaborative and challenging dimensions of the argumentative task. The discussion trajectory surrounding the conflict, regarding the display of collabor ative attitude, challenging attitude and hybrid attitude (linguistic features mixing collaboration and c hallenge) differs between the two groups. In group 1, students spend a great effort from the beginning in displaying pol iteness and respect to each other’s work by referring to what was previously argued, and by motivating each other. When conflict arises, they build on these existing resources to reaffirm their willingness to keep on collaborating. Such positive working climate does not prevent them from expressing disagreement or negative emotions, but they do so without falling into personal attacks. Most of the messages are hybrid, involving both the display of a collaborative and an challenging attitude. In contrast, in group 2, disagreement is directly associated with relational issues. The three students do an extensive interactional work to display a collaborative attitude, but mostly after the conflict arised, only then using politeness terms. The girls succeed in decreasing the emotio n tension this way, but they do so avoiding the cognitive issue. From time to time a student tries to discuss it again, displaying a hybrid attitude, but emotional tension unceasingly reappear, with very offensive contributions, present ing own ideas as unquestionable truth and strongly criticizing others’. It seems that the anticipatory emotion regulation remains superficial in group 2, the collaborative features just serving to hide or limit  the expression of an unceasingly resurfacing dispute. At the end of the day, this case study suggests that the f orm of displaying collaboration and challenge should be consistent through time in order to build and maintain trust within the group in CSCL. Discussion: Affording for anticipatory & reactive emotion regulation in CABLe CABLe supposes the students to be able to collaborate arguing together, henc e to let emerge a socio-cognitive conflict (Roschelle and Teasley, 1995) and regulate it fruitfully. In a French dis tant university course of adult education, comparing how 2 groups, the first reaching self-regulation while the second had to call out for teacher regulation, reveals that they do not only differ in their reaction to the conflict, but also in their effort in creating a positive working climate. Actually, handling disagreement constructively supposes to actively built and maintain an appropriate socioaffective climate is throughout the interaction, thanks to antic ipatory and reactive emotion regulation. In this regards, our research addresses a literature gap providing a fruitful conceptual distinction between anticipatory and reactive emotion regulation processes. It also contribut es to making such theoretical framework operational for educational interaction analysis thanks to a grid des cribing anticipatory emotion regulation at the individual and group levels (cf. tables 1 & 2). On the research plane, future work may investigate the relation between re adiness (Frijda et al., 1989) and anticipatory and reactive regulation practices. In particular, future studies could not only scale up the present case study, but also enlarge its s cope by apprehending university students’ “anticipatory and reactive emotion regulation readiness”, as it is actualized in CABLe settings. On the pra ctice plane, the Moodle forum proved CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 18 globally affordant for task completion and associated anticipatory emotion regulation. However, our study reveals that even if it was not sufficient, in itself, to afford for decision-making regarding group work organization, and reactive emotion regulation, which needed a mediation by synchronous communication. Actually, the two groups spontaneously started their work with such informal tools (WhatsApp for group 1, Discord for group 2). At the end of the day, our interpretative hypothesis is that the discussion forum, e-mail and synchronous non-institutional tools altogether constitute a technical whole affording both CABLe and group emotio n regulation. This is consistent with the literature: if asynchronous modality provides a fruitful space for collaborative writing, it also gives “fewer opportunities for informal team building” (Mabrito, 2006: 105) than synchr onous tools. Hence, it could then be relevant to ask the students explicitly, in the initial instructions, to use both the forum and the social media of their choice. Designers could even script specific synchronous phases of group work addressing matters of emotion regulation at each step of the collaboration (e. g. Näykki et al., 2017).
