# Learning to Engage in Climate Activism in High School Classrooms towards Collective and Systemic Changes

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This qualitative ethnographic case study examines how two school students learn to take collective, informed actions toward systemic change in a year-long climate change course. The data include artifacts produced from students’ climate civic action projects and the course activities, 10 video-taped lessons, and interviews. The analysis is guided by two questions: (1) How did the students respond to deliberately designed curricular activities that support their climate activism throughout the year? (2) How did the two students narrate their experiences? Using the notion of ‘collective care,’ the analysis reveals the processes in which the students connected their personal experiences with the climate issues discussed in the class; turned their anxieties into materialized doings with others to create a more just and sustainable world while navigating politics associated with proposed changes. The implications for supporting students’ climate civic actions through formal education are discussed.

## Introduction

Youths around the world are increasingly participating in climate activism (Tattersall et al., 2022; Verlie & Flynn, 2022). Engaging in complex civic dilemmas, such as ‘how can we center justice as we adapt and mitigate  the climate crisis?’ requires founda tional competencies and dispositions, such as being  able to consider multiple points of view, to grapple with complexity, and to exhibit empathy and a deep respect towards impacted  people (Lee et al., 2021). In the context of climate civic activism, it is critical to take collective and well-informed actions for systemic change, beyond increasing individual awareness (IPCC, 2022; Trott et al., 2023). Despite the important role of education in adapting and mitigating the climate crisis, currently, we know very little about how youths learn to engage in climate civic actions in formal school settings. It is only through public schooling that a society can provide a basel ine preparation to build those foundational compete ncies and dispositions (Lee at al., 2021). Currently, there  are few studies that provide insights into the proc esses of youths' learning to engage in climate activism along with curricular and pedagogical supports. In this long-term partnership project, high school science teachers and educational researchers worked together to support students to engage in collective civic actions in a year-long climate change course. Students were guided to explore various climate change related issues while engaging in deliberately designed pedagogical activities. Employing a qualitative longitudinal ethnographic case study approach (Angers & Machtmes, 2005), we explore how two case study students, who bring different cultures, identities, and historical relationships with climate change and schools, come to develop their agency and take collective, informed actions. Th e research questions are: 1) How did the students respond to  deliberately designed curricular activities  that support their climate activism throughout the year?  2) How did the two students narrate their experiences? Youth climate civic action and collective care Youth climate civic action refers to the actions and initiatives undertaken by young people to call out and change the business-as-usual practices that exacerbate climate change thro ugh political participation, volunteer work, or grassroots organizing in their communities (Ekman &  Amnå, 2012; O’Brien, 2018). In this study, w e use ‘collective care’ as our analytical lens to examine youths’ participation and engagement in justice-centered climate civic action guided by school curricula.

Grounded i n feminists’ perspectives, we view that care consists of three interrelated dimensions: maintenance doings and work, affective engagement, and ethico-political involvement (Fisher & Tronto, 1990; Gunkle & Tolbert, 2018; Puig de la Bellacasa, 2011,  2017). In our conception, material doing is a central aspect of care. Even though a student cares about the  climate crisis or the impacted people, without materialized doing for people, affectivity does not make it up to care. In addition, the work of care is fundamentally poli tical. Everything that people do to maintain, continue, and repair the world as well as possible can have harmful effects on local human or more-than-human communities. For whom is care given and what are its consequences? In the issue of food loss and waste issue, for example, students might propose removing a free and reduced meal policy in schools as an act of care for reducing food waste  in schools. This act of care for the environment might result in increasing the burden of under-resourced families who are the least responsible for the food waste problem. As Puig de la Bellacasa notes  (2011), care is  “a concrete work of maintenance, with ethical and a ffective implications,” and “a vital politics in interdependent worlds” (p. 5). In the context of youths’ climate activism, we posit that caring is a key practice of justice--“an ethically and politically charged practice” (Puig de la Bellacasa, 2011). To enact a justice-oriented agency while engaging in climate activism, students must come to understand complex non-technocratic dimensions of climate change related problems. In addition, students need opportunities to engage both empathetically and politically within the problem space. We think it is particularly important to support students to engage in collective caring. It involves feeling with, rather than a feeling for, others, and doing with, rather thandoing for others with political clarity and attention to power dynamics. To support youth to engage in climate civic actions and exercise agency, adults have duties to attend to young people's climate emotions, engage in collective caring by sharing power and preventing tokenization, and creating a support network with community members (Wrigley et al., 2024). We use the concept of collective caring to examine students’ gradual engagement in climate civic actions  in a deliberately designed learning environment.

## Methods

School and pedagogical contexts The study setting is a 12th grade elective climate change course at a pu blic high school in the Pacific Southwest of the United States. During the 2023-24 school year, students engaged in three units --climate psychology, food waste, and environmental injustice--while completing two civic action projects. The first one was the Food Action Project that took place in the middle of the year. Students  worked in groups of 4-6 with their choice of members and designed a solution of their choice to improve the school fo od system. The other was the Action for Climate Empowerment (ACE) project, an independent culminating project at the end of the year. In both projects, students were guided to identify the problem of their concerns, propose and implement a solution, and evaluate and reflect on their impacts.

## Participants and data sources

Two case study students, Linda and Andy, were selected for this study because of their trajectories of participation in their climate civic actions throughout the year. None of them had any experience of participating in  climate civic activism prior to this course. Linda, who initially described herself as a ‘shy’ or ‘in trovert’ person, showed a dramatic increase with her agency. In the first project she created an awareness poster and had little interaction with people. Later in her ACE project, Linda led her team while actively interacting with over 400 people. Andy was one of the few students who continuously took a ction for lasting, system-level changes across the two projects. The other reason for selecting these cases has to d o with the richness of the data. The cases presented rich opportunities for researchers to learn about h ow student identities and personal backgrounds inte racted with pedagogical supports, resulting in growing agency and evolving understanding of complexities. Multiple sources of data were generated through artifact collection, classroom observation, and interviews throughout the year.

## Data analysis

Employing a qualitative ethnographic case study approach, three researchers and two undergraduate assistants conducted the analysis for about eight months. During the first stage, we created memos, video logs, and vignettes that described students’ participation in the cours e activities as well as activism. Two case profiles were coconstructed through weekly meetings focusing on the  following questions: a) Who is this student? b) H ow did each student participate in various learning activi ties? c) How did they narrate their experiences in this course in general, their civic action projects in particular?  d) Which experiences did they perceive as impactfu l in shaping their climate activism? Next, guided by the collective cari ng framework, we conducted in-depth analyses of the cases focusing on: a) what aspects of a climate change related issue did students attend to and how did the student reason about? b) what emotion did students express, when, and with whom? c) what did they do with whom, and towards what end?  d) what were the ethical and political tensions that  students  navigated with and how? Employing constant comparison method (Glaser, 1965), cross-case analysis was conducted to theorize how and why the students engaged in climate civic action in a particular way in contexts.

## Findings

The Case of Linda: “Crawl out of my shell”--A “shy” girl becoming an active climate advocate who took collective action.

Introduction of Linda. Linda was a 12th grade Vietnamese girl from a singl e family and had a part-time job at a restaurant at the time of this study. Linda shared that her parents experienced food insecurity and “g ave her a sense of anxiety about wasting food.” Linda described herself as “shy” and “introverted.” She decided to join this course because she hoped to gain “better social ski lls and confidence in taking  actions on my own for causes I want to support.” Linda also aspired to go into environmental policies in college. “Taking caring for it [the earth] as it cares for us.” At the beginning of the year, many students in the course, including Linda, expressed her climate anxiety. In her “My Climate Story”, Lin da referred to the term 'Solastalgia’, stating “how climate change has sign ificantly impacted my mental state.” Throughout the  year, students shared their feeling sculpture s, climate stories, and food stories with each other. It appeared that openly sharing her anxiety with her peers helped Linda and other students “realizing the severity of the predicament and being part of a community,” which in turn enabled her to invest herself more deeply in climate action. Despite her growing awareness, it was still no easy task for Linda to interact with people to change their thoughts and actions. Being part of a community of like-minded peers helped Linda to “crawl out of her shel l,” empowering her to take risks and see herself as part of a larger movement. While implementing the ACE project, Linda felt nervous speaking in front of teachers and students she never met before during the homeroom presentation—an experience she described as “not an introvert’s dream because all eyes were on me.” However, she found encouragement in working with her group and noted t hat “seeing lots of students actually listen to us, pay attention, and ask questions was pretty cool.” Although nerve-wracking, Linda pushed through by focusing on the shared goal, finding the experience “fun to do with a group of people.” Reflecting on the ACE p roject, Linda felt proud of her growth and her ability to engage so many students in a short amount of time. The course activities presented opportunities for students to engage in  multiple ways of knowing and being in the world. It appeared that these cumulative experiences helped Linda to develop deep care for the earth while deepening her understanding of the interconnectedness between people and the environment. Linda pointed out reading Braiding Sweetgrass by Indigenous scholar R obin Wall Kimmerer as one impactful experience. She stated, “It is the responsibility of humans to care for natur e in turn...we build a symbiotic relationship with the Earth, taking caring for it as it cares for us. ” After interviewing a local farmer, Linda stated that we must live in balance with the land since “we rely on the land and must understand it to care for it.” While engaging in civic action projects, Linda had to navigate ethical and political tensions. For example, while exploring ways to reduce school lunch waste, she shared that “By removing free lunches, we may mitigate food waste, but we place the burden of it on people  who don’t cause the problem. This can be related t o the way lower class people suffer most of the environmental  deficit while causing the least damage.” Later in the year, Linda crawled out of her shell and actively and collectively interacted with people towards a more just solution. The Case of Andy: Persistently engaging in activism for lasting, system level changes Introduction of Andy. Andy was a 12 th grade Chinese-Jewish boy from a middle-class family who lived with his parents, grandparents, and siblings. He described himself as an outgoing boy and a soccer player. Andy chose to enroll in the focal course because he hoped to “gai n a new understanding of climate change” to pursue this field in college.

"We truly doneed to make changes.”  While engaging in two civic action projects through out the year, Andy consistently took actions for lasting, system-level changes. Notably, Andy shifted his roles from a member to a leader, suggesting his increasing agency. It appeared that Andy’s personal experiences as well as interacting with multiple adults  through course activities (e.g., teacher, corporation managers, district’s nu trition director) helped him to see the complexity of the problem  and politics. The success of his first civic action project fuel ed Andy to continue to engage in activism for a greater and lasting impact. Growing up, Andy worked in his grandma’s Chinese restaurant. He helped to buy ingredients that were out of stock in the middle of the day. Andy stated,  “my parents instilled in me the significance of no t wasting food. In our household, the emphasis was not just o n avoiding food waste but also on actively contribu ting to sustainable practices.” Andy expressed his frustration of seeing food waste  at schools, saying “It frustrates me when there is a lot of food on the conveyor belt.” In his first project (the Food Action Project), Andy and his team interviewed restaurant staff, cafeteria supervisor,  and the district nutrition director. Andy and his peers noticed how politics played out in dealing with food waste issues. While sharing their interviews, they noted, “corporations have to consider publicity when talking  about topics like this, so interviews were anonymo us and there were some limits as to what could actually be  mentioned...we are positive there are way more w ays companies are handling food waste.”  The activities increased students’  awareness of the complexity of the problem intersecting with the economic, cultural, and political dimensions. At the same time, Andy and his team members were e mpowered by multiple adults who invited them to work together. The cafeteria supervisor, for example, encouraged that “we [adults and students] need to create like a team where we can work together share ideas with each other and see how we can come up with solution.” Inspired by this, Andy and his peers advocated for a shift to sustainable packaging  in the first civic action project, which resulted in a real change at the school. The school transitioned from plastic to paper salad containers. In his second civic action project, Andy worked with less enthusiastic peers than himself. Assuming the role of the leader, Andy explored ways to make a lasting and bigger impact on the problem of plastic pollution on the beach. Leveraging his prior experience about sustainable packaging, Andy reache d out to a plastic bag company to advocate for sustainable material. Andy reflected, “the most important thing I got out of [this course], just knowing that like, these things are happening, and we truly doneed to make changes.”

## Discussion and conclusion

This study intends to better understand how high sc hool students learn to take informed actions toward s lasting, systemic change through formal education in schools. The findings shed light on the processes in which the two students engaged in collective car ing guided by the course activities. Both Linda and And y connected their personal experiences with the climate issues discussed in the class. They turned their anxieties and emotions into materialized doings with others  while navigating politics associated with proposed changes. The analysis points to three key features of the learning process, providing ins ights into the needed pedagogical supports. First, learning to engage in productive climate activism requires cumulative and guided opportunities to engage in civic action for an extended period of time in a supportive learning community. As demonstrated in the case of Linda, students need multiple experiences to expand identi ties and increase agency. Second, both cases highlight the importance of engaging students in collectively reflecting on their climate emotions in order to transform climate anxiety into active hope. Notably, formulating hope  was mediated by students’ experience of making pos itive small impacts, as illustrated in both cases. Third, learning activities should foster students’ complex understanding about the system while adults share decision-making power and work with the students. By engaging youths in climate justice issues empathetically and political ly with a caring community network, students candeliberately work towards a more just world while reflecting on the scope of the impact that their action can make.
