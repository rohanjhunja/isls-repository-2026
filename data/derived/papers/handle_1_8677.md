# Dynamic Modelling of Collaborative Sensemaking: A Case Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
How do explanations emerge and evolve in collaborative sensemaking? We present a preliminary attempt to model the evolution of collaborative sensemaking over a short timespan with high temporal resolution. We operationalized Sherin et al.’s (2012) node-mode framework of individual knowledge systems to a distributed knowledge system and examined its dynamics in high temporal resolution.

## Introduction

In an attempt to examine why, and under what condit ions, students generate coherent explanations in cl inical interviews, Sherin, Krakowski, and Lee (2012) examined a large corpus of interviews with students about seasons and climate. Their analysis was situated within the  Knowledge in Pieces (KiP) epistemological perspect ive (diSessa, 1993). Sherin et al. (2012) suggested tha t students’ reasoning draws on a set of interconnec ted knowledge elements. They used the term nodes to describe elementary pieces of knowledge; and modes, to describe interconnected collections of nodes that a re stable and are activated as a whole. They descri bed sensemaking and reasoning as a process in which ind ividuals create dynamic mental constructs-unstable and temporary knowledge structures comprised of nodes and modes that are activated and ad-hoc connected for the purpose of constructing an explanation.

The current study focuses on collaborative sensemak ing, in which collaborators create and use a joint problem space (JPS) that provides a platform for me aningful communication in support of the sensemakin g process. To construct their JPS, collaborators need ways to introduce and display knowledge in the JPS as well as methods to monitor differences in meaning and to re solve obstacles to the collaboration (Roschelle, 19 92; Roschelle & Teasley, 1995). Inspired by studies of distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995), we aim to m odel collaborative construction of explanations as drawi ng on a distributed knowledge system, and we sugges t an operationalization of Sherin et al.’s mode-node fra mework for a distributed cognition, for example, a pair of students working in collaboration. We examine the d ynamics of the collaborative knowledge construction with high temporal resolution. We use the term shared nodes to describe nodes introduced and accepted into the JPS, and the term shared modes to describe shared knowledge structures that are re-used in the JPS. The term shared dynamic constructs (SDCs) is used to describe the unstable and temporary knowledge structures that are connected for the ad-hoc purpose of constructing an explanation created in collaboration.

## Methods

The data is drawn from a group interview with a pair of eleven-grade students who study advanced-level physics. The students knew each other and collaborated on pr evious occasions. The students were asked to discus s and explain why is it warmer in the summer than in the winter. This question was selected because it can be tackled using naïve knowledge, but it requires making sense of several natural phenomena and the connections between them, and thus answering it is far from trivial. An other reason for this particular choice was its use  by several research groups in the past (Lelliott & Rollnick, 2010; B. Sherin et al., 2012) which allowed comparisons. The interview was video recorded. The recordings we re transcribed and specific episodes which were rich in shared knowledge construction were selected for analysis. The analysis involved the identification of nodes (Sherin et al., 2012), shared nodes, SDCs and shared modes. Relational expressions (such as “because”, like, etc.) were identified and used to code attempts at knowledge construction and successful connections between shared nodes, both resulting in SDCs, as well as the creation of shared modes. A temporal representation of the collaborative knowledge system was created for each episode (here we present one).

## Results

The temporal representation of the collaborative knowledge system in one episode (3:50 minutes) is presented in Fig. 1. The Y-axis of the graph shows the nodes that are used in this episode. The X-axis reflects the conversational turns, and colored dots identify the speakers using the respective node in their utterance. The episode starts with the collaborators setting the stage for the process by introducing nodes into the JPS. Both partners contributed nodes and engaged in attempts at assembly, using nodes that they introduced into the JPS as well as nodes introduced by their partner. This symmetry in participation is an important feature of a ICLS2022 Proceedings 1988 © ISLS collaborative interaction (Dillenbourg, 1999)and validates the construct of shared nodes. Following are attempts to combine the shared nodes into SDCs. Successful attempts are characterized by well-de fined connections between the shared nodes in the utterance, via expressions like “because”, “and then” or “is”. They are marked in green in the graph. Conversational turns which i ncluded shared nodes  without a meaningful connection between them were coded as “attempted connection”, depicted in the graph as yellow connections between nodes. Those included for instance sentences in which there was an “if node A” phrase, but the “then” part was missing, or “let’s say that node A and let’s say that node B”. Attempted connections were sometimes scientifically wrong. Interestingly, both attempted connections and succe ssful ones resulted in the generation of shared modes identified as the re-use of previously developed structures in subsequent conversational turns. Some SDCs were created in collaboration and thus span more than one conversational turn (see turns 18-19 as well as 22-23-24), in line with our assumption that shared dynamic constructs are generated by a distributed cognition, similarly to the way an individual creates dynamic mental constructs (Sherin et al., 2012). This type of collaboratively-created SDCs was coded when the participants continued each other’s ideas. In this episode, a repetition of the question aske d at the beginning of the interview (turn 27), intended to check if the shared knowledge structure provides an acceptable explanation, led to its collapse, but shared nodes that were created in the process were used in further turns in this episode and along the rest of the interview. ## Discussion

We operationalized Sherin et al.’s (2012) node-mode framework of individual knowledge systems to a distributed knowledge system and examined its dynamics in high temporal resolution. The analysis captures how the distributed knowledge system evolves in collaborati ve sensemaking and guides this process. While this is a preliminary attempt, we believe that it provides in sights into the emergence of explanations in collaborative inquiry. Further investigation will aim to refine t he model by specific attention to available materia ls and tools, and specific attention to the nature of the interaction.
