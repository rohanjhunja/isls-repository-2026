# Unpacking the Complex Relationship Between Epistemic Curiosity and Uncertainty in Uncertainty-Driven Science Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines the relationship between epistemic curiosity (EC) and perceived uncertainty, which is posited to form an inverted U-shape, in the context of uncertainty-driven science learning (UDSL). Using path analysis, survey data from 181 middle school students collected at multiple timepoints across a three-week photosynthesis unit designed to facilitate UDSL were analyzed. Results show that the inverted U-shaped relationship between EC and uncertainty does not consistently hold over time. Instead, prior uncertainty negatively affects this relationship unless mediated by EC or sustained uncertainty in subsequent activities. At the trait level, the study highlights the mediating role of epistemic orientation toward uncertainty in fostering trait EC in UDSL. The findings underscore the importance of supporting students' productive uncertainty navigation and signify the positive impacts of UDSL in fostering EC.

Epistemic curiosity and uncertainty Epistemic curiosity (EC)—the desire to acquire knowledge—drives students' scientific inquiries as they recognize and navigate uncertainty (Lamnina & Chase, 2019). E C arises from cognitive sources of uncertainty, suc h as insufficiency, ambiguity, incoherence, or conflict (Lowenstein, 1994), and is viewed as a process of u ncertainty reduction (e.g., Berlyne, 1962; Van Lieshout et al., 2018). Recognizing uncertainty’s role in fosterin g curiosity, recent literature emphasizes the importance of unce rtainty-driven science learning (UDSL), where students are supported in navigating uncertainties during inquiry (Jirout & Matthews, 2022). The relationship between EC and uncertainty is prop osed to be nonlinear, forming an inverted U-shape (Kang et al., 2009), rather than a simple linear pattern. Experimental studies support this: students show the highest EC under moderate uncertainty, with lower EC under minimal or extreme uncertainty (Dubey & Griffiths, 2020; Spitzer et al., 2023). Recent work has explored or challenged this curve by identifying moderating var iables or deviations. For instance, Lewry et al. (2023) found that impossibility, as an uncertainty source, raises the curve’s peak higher than improbability. Spitzer et al. (202 3) identified cases where higher-than-moderate uncertainty elicits the greatest EC, depending on the perceived value of information. While studies on moderating variables offer insight  into designing tasks that foster EC (e.g., using impossibility or increasing task relevance), they o ffer limited guidance on student characteristics th at might reverse the expected pattern and enhance EC.  More critically, these findings are difficult to tr anslate into classrooms like UDSL, where perceived uncertainty f luctuates as students raise, maintain, reduce, or p ostpone uncertainty over time (e.g., Heal et al., 2025). In UDSL, the relationship between EC and uncertainty at one point depends on later moments, unlike the independence a ssumed in controlled settings. The inverted U-shape may not consistently hold, as it depends on how students navigate uncertainty and respond to their EC. The current study This study aims to investigate how the inverted-U shaped relationship translates to classroom science learning like UDSL, and which student characteristics can contribute to fostering curiosity in such contexts. Specifically, it explores students' epistemic orientation toward uncertainty as a potential mediating variable, which is assumed to affect how students navigate uncertainty and, co nsequently, how they respond to EC. As discussed in Spitzer et al. (2023), the extent to which students evaluate the value of information they can obtain influences EC levels. This can be translated to the UDSL context, suggest ing that the extent to which students evaluate the potential value of uncertainty in developing their knowledge will impact their EC levels. If students accumulate experiences of EC in UDSL contexts, they are likely to cultivate trait-level epistemic curiosity, a stable disposition reflecting a tendency to seek new information (Boyle, 1989; Ma rkey & Loewenstein, 2014), mediated by an advanced epistemic orientation toward uncertainty (Park et al., 2024). To achieve this aim, a path analysis is conducted using student survey data on EC (collected at three time points, T1-T3) and uncertainty (collected at two time points, T2-T3) over a three-week middle school photosynthesis unit designed to facilitate UDSL (see Figure 1). Specifically, the study investigates how students' perceived uncertainty affects their EC (Paths 1a –1c) and how both evolve—examining how prior EC (Paths 2a – 2c) and uncertainty (Path 3) influence current and future states. Lastly, the study examines how accumulated experiences of EC in UDSL contribute to cultivating  students’ trait EC (Paths 4a–4c). Another survey dataset on these trait-level variables, collected after the unit's completion (T4), is included in the analysis.

## Methods

The study was conducted in a middle school science classroom with a teacher trained to design and impl ement UDSL through a professional development (PD) progra m provided by the authors at a university in the southwestern United States. The PD aimed to enhance  teachers’ ability to support students in productiv ely navigating uncertainty, thereby fostering EC (Park et al., 2022), which, in turn, enhances students’ epistemic orientation toward uncertainty. Following the PD, the first author collaborated with the teacher to ensure that her lessons facilitated UDSL throughout a three-week photosynthesis unit. A total of 181 students in this teacher’s classes p articipated in at least one survey (three time poin ts for state-level measures of uncertainty and EC, and a post-unit trait-level measure of epistemic orientation toward uncertainty and EC). Previously validated surveys w ere adapted to measure study variables: State EC us ing a four-point Likert scale (Litman, 2005; Cronbach’s α were.76 or more); P erceived Uncertainty using a six-point Likert scale (Glogger-Frey et al., 2015; α were.83 or more); Trait EC u sing a seven-point Likert scale (Litman, 2008, α was.82); and Epistemic Orientation toward Uncertainty using a six-point Likert scale (Park et al., 2024; α was.84). Average scores were used for each measure. Missing data analysis, comparing complete (n = 99) and incomplete (n = 82) groups, supported Missing at Random (MAR), allowing the use of the Full Information Maximum Likelihood estimator for path an alysis in Mplus ver. 8.8. A measurement invariance test, comparing the three-and two-time point measures of EC and uncertainty, supported invariance of the measures.

## Implications for science education

The results of this study offer two key implication s for science education. First, theoretically, the inverted Ushaped relationship between EC and uncertainty cannot be directly translated into classroom setting; inconsistent mediation effect of uncertainty between prior activity uncertainty and current EC highlights the deviating pattern of the assumed relationship between EC and uncertainty. The negative effect of prior un certainty on subsequent EC (Path 1b) could be mitigated by increasing or su staining uncertainty in the next activity. Second, t his underscores a practical implication: the critical role of uncertainty navigation and the importance of implementing UDSL. Navigating uncertainty can be overwhelming for many students, necessitating instructional support (Chen, Jordan et al., 2024; Chen, Park et al., 2024; Manz & Suárez, 2018; Watkins & Manz, 2022). When uncertainties are productively navigated through UDSL, students’ state EC is sustained, ultimately fostering the dev elopment of epistemic orientation toward uncertainty and trait EC.
