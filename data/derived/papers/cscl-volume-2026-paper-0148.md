# Using Generative AI as an Epistemic Catalyst for Collaborative Lesson Planning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines the integration of generative AI to cultivate critical AI literacy and epistemic understanding in pre-service teachers. The AI-generated lesson plan is conceptualized as a boundary object, compelling collaborative groups tonegotiate and externalize their disciplinary knowledge and reasoning, considering the 5E inquiry model and discourse structure. A multi-modal analysis is utilized to quantify and qualify shifts in epistemic engagement, serving as a robust testbed for necessary design principles in critical collaboration with intelligent systems.

The rapid and ubiquitous integration of generative artificial intelligence (e.g., ChatGPT) fundamentally alters prevailing educational praxis, thereby necessitating investigative research that moves beyond the mere adoption of instrumental tools (Holmes & Tuomi, 2022; Cress & Kimmerle, 2023). This transformation holds particular salience within teacher education, as the preparation of pre-service teachers (PSTs) must necessitate the critical evaluation and integration of AI outputs in a manner deemed pedagogically sound (Long & Magerko, 2020; Guan et al., 2025). Current scholarly discourse frequently positions artificial intelligence as a utility for the attainment of automation or efficiency, often neglecting its capacity to reshape the dynamics of collaborative learning and the evolution of professional epistemic understanding (Chen 2025; Wu et al., 2025). The growing influence of generative AI reshapes the locus of epistemic authority in education, challenging traditional grounds for knowledge and underscoring the need to reaffirm human-centered agency (Peters & Dobber, 2025). Despite the substantial promise of artificial intelligence, its theoretical function in the domain of collaborative learning remains nascent. Much existing research positions AI as a tool for optimizing learning efficiency and performance, rather than as an active participant in the social and epistemic processes of collaboration. However, it remains unclear how AI not only simply support existing collaborative practices but also actively reshape the social and cognitive dimensions of group work (Järvelä et al., 2023). Two gaps remain in the literature on AI in collaborative learning. First, although AI literacy encompasses both technical skills and the critical capacities to evaluate and responsibly use AI outputs (Long & Magerko, 2020), there is limited empirical research on how AI is mediating learners' epistemic understanding in CSCL contexts (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023; Cheung et al., 2025). Second, more empirical research is required to inform how to leverage AI for future educators by designing and examining the collaborative interaction in teacher-AI collaboration (Holstein et al., 2019; Kim, 2024). This study presents the design of an intervention aimed at fostering critical AI literacy and deeper epistemic understanding among PSTs in a collaborative lesson planning context. The intervention is grounded in constructivist theory, which posits that knowledge is actively co-constructed through structured interaction. The pedagogical core employs the 5E instructional model (Duran & Duran, 2004), an inquiry-based framework central to science education, integrated with Accountable Talk strategies (Michaels et al., 2008), which promote scientific discourse among teachers. This integrated pedagogical framework structures collaborative lesson planning to promote the co-construction of knowledge, generate accountable talk discourse moves, and advance group regulatory skills. This design reconceptualizes the role of artificial intelligence in collaborative learning as a socioepistemic agent whose outputs are to be examined, debated, and reworked through group dialogue. By inviting participants tonegotiate rather  than accept AI-generated suggestions, the study foregrounds the relational and epistemic dimensions of human–AI co-construction, specifically addressing two research questions (RQ): RQ1. How does AI-assisted collaboration influence PSTs' epistemic understanding of lesson planning RQ2. How do PSTs interpret and critique AI suggestions during discussions?

## Research design

The intervention employs a within-subject, comparative design implemented in a university-level science teacher education course. This structure enables the systematic observation of epistemic change by examining identical groups across two distinct conditions. In Phase 1 (Lesson Plan 1; LP1), groups collaboratively revise lesson plans without the use of generative AI.  The focus is solely on applying the 5E instructional model and Accountable 764 Talk strategies to establish a pedagogical baseline. This phase provides essential diagnostic insight into participants’ existing pedagogical reasoning and discourse patterns. In Phase 2 (Lesson Plan 2; LP2), the same groups are required to engage with generative AI (ChatGPT). Participants use the AI to generate lesson components or full drafts, which are then collectively critiqued, negotiated, and refined in alignment with the 5E and Accountable Talk frameworks. Within this design, AI-generated content in LP2 functions as a boundary object, an artifact situated at the intersection of human pedagogical reasoning and machine-generated content (Star & Griesemer, 1989). Recent research recognizes generative AI as a cognitive collaborator that enhances boundary objects by mediating shared understanding (Naganuma et al., 2024). Specifically, the AI output functions as a Partially Shared Object (PSO) that allows collaborators to coordinate their work while maintaining distinct interpretive perspectives (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011; Naganuma et al., 2024). By encouraging participants to articulate and justify their acceptance, rejection, or transformation of AI contributions, the design externalizes pedagogical knowledge and strengthens PSTs’ epistemic commitments. To capture the multi-layered nature of AI-enhanced collaboration, this study employs a multi-modal mixedphysical artifacts. Two primary instruments were utilized to assess PST’s epistemic understanding and socially shared regulation. A pre –post test measured conceptual growth regarding inquiry-based learning, the 5E instructional model, and scientific discourse, combining scenario-based reasoning and concept-mapping tasks to capture how participants articulate, justify, and apply 5E cycles and AT principles (Crawford & Capps, 2018). Complementing this, the Adaptive Instrument for Regulation of Emotions (AIRE) was adapted to the teacher education context to assess socially shared regulation during collaborative lesson planning (Järvenoja et al., 2013). Situated within this context, the survey captures how PSTs monitor, express, and co-regulate affective and motivational states within their groups, providing insight into both individual experiences and group-level regulation. Together, these instruments address RQ1, illuminating how PSTs develop epistemic understanding and socially shared regulation in AI-supported collaboration.

To address RQ2 (Critical Engagement with AI), analysis focuses on the socio-technical dynamics emerging across individual and group levels. Individual ChatGPT trace data are examined through Lag Sequential behavioral patterns such as prompting, critique, and reformulation (Bakeman & Gottman, 1997). This sequential modeling reveals how PSTs critically engage with and regulate AI input during lesson planning. Complementarily, Interaction Analysis (IA) is applied to the  group discussion videos,  focusing on how participants collectively evaluate, challenge, or integrate AI-generated content within collaborative dialogue (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). ## Significance

This AI-enhanced collaborative lesson planning design positions PSTs as active agents in evaluating, integrating, and adapting AI suggestions, fostering ownership, collaborative reasoning, and deeper epistemic understanding. By centering PSTs in the learni ng process, the intervention promotes reflective and socially shared regulation rather than simple task completion. AI output is treated as a boundary object, mediating interactions and supporting the articulation and refinement of disciplinary knowledge and reasoning. The design also provides a methodological model for examining AI-mediated collaboration, combining Interaction Analysis and Lag Sequential Analysis to capture both individual and group-level engagement. Future research can investigate impacts on lesson plan quality, group discourse, and AI literacy development, as well as explore diverse tools, scaffolds, and collaborative formats to optimize AI integration in teacher education. 765 Akkerman, S.
