# How Learners Detect Revision Occasions in Texts Labeled as Peer-Written or AI-Generated

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Although the potential of large language models in education has been widely recognized, research on how learners revise AI-generated content remains limited. The aim of this study was to determine whether learners detect different revision occasions when revising texts labeled as written by different authors (peer vs. AI). In a controlled study, learners (N = 152) revised a text that was labeled as either peer-written or AI-generated, while the con-tent of this text was in fact identical in both conditions. We found that learners descrip-tively detected fewer surface-level revision occasions in a text labeled as AI-generated, although this difference was not statistically significant. No notable difference was found for semantic (i.e., meaning-changing) revision occasions.

Revision occasions in peer-written and AI-generated texts A substantial body of research has examined how lea rners revise their own academic texts and those wri tten by their peers (e.g., Rijlaarsdam et al., 2004). Recently, initial empirical evidence on learners’ behaviors when revising AI-generated texts has bee n presented (e.g., Radtke & Rummel, 2025). However,  further research is needed to examine learners’ approaches to revising AI-generated content, particularly with regard to whet her learners exhibit specific foci and deficits in their revision that should be considered in educational practice and research. Revision of peer-written and AI-generated texts Revision is considered a crucial stage in the writing process. In addition to improving the quality of a text, revision can serve as a ‘learning tool’ (Rijlaarsdam et al.,  2004, p. 199): While revising a text, learners can engage in a comprehensive evaluation and internalization of its  content, recall and apply previously learned rules, or even acquire new revision skills through practice. Moreo ver, when learners revise texts that they ha ve not written in the first place, they can gain insight intonew fac ts and arguments developed by their peers (Fitzgerald, 1987; Rijlaarsdam et al., 2004). Furthermore, empirical evidence suggests that revis ing peer-written texts can also enhance learners’ individual writing and revision skills (e.g., Wichmann & Rummel, 2013). The advent of generative artificial intelligence (AI), particularly large language models (LLMs) such as GPT or BLOOM, in the field of education has major implications for learning practices (e.g., Kasneci et al., 2023). Given the capacity of LLMs to generate texts of a h igh quality that closely resemble the style of huma n writers, students worldwide are keen to use this technology, especially in academic writing (e.g., Albayati, 2024). Nevertheless, despite the initial impression of polished and coherent content, a closer examination often reveals that AIgenerated texts require substantial revision.  For instance, there is a risk that AI-generated texts contain incorrect information (e.g., hallucinations; Marcus & Davis, 2020) or biased content (OpenAI, 2024). Additionally, it is not implausible that AI-generated texts may contain grammatical mistakes, particularly in non-English texts (Advertext, 2023). However, given the relative novelty  of the phenomenon of AI-assisted writing, there is currently a lack of empirical evidence examining how learners engage in the revision of AI-generated academic texts. The processes of revising peer-written and AI-generated texts appear similar in that in both cases, learners are engaged in revising a text that they have n ot written in the first place. A further rationale for comparing these processes is the growing body of research that draws parallels between collaborative writing and AI-assisted writing in general, with revision being an essential component of both of these learning si tuations (e.g., Cress & Kimmerle, 2023). In this study, we provided participants with the same text to be revised, varying only the author label (peer vs. AI) between the experimental condit ions. This allowed us to determine whether learners  exhibit different patterns of detecting revision occasions when revising purportedly peer-written or AI-generated texts. Classification of revision occasions When revising an academic text, learners need to employ two main revision skills: (1) detecting areas that require improvement (revision occasions) and (2) applying the most appropriate improvement strategie s (Hayes, 2004).

In this paper, we deliberately use the term revisio n occasions in order to emphasize the following: The revision of a text usually goes beyond correcting obvious er rors, such as grammatical mistakes or factual discr epancies. Some revision activities are aimed at improving the quality of error-free content, for example, enriching the writing style of already well-written passages (Hayes, 2004). Thus, the term revision  occasions points out that the detection of revision occasions is a product of learners’ subjective perception and assessment. This means that besides some apparent errors, there are also passages in a text that different learners may perceive as either already being perfectly fine or as revision occasions, ‘stimulated by the discovery of new connections, new ideas, or new arguments seem intrinsically more interesting’ (Hayes, 2004, p. 20). Revision occasions and the resulting revisions can be classified as either surface-level or semantic (i.e., meaning-changing; Faigley & Witte, 1981; Fitzgerald, 1987): Whereas surface-level revisions change the text without affecting its meaning, semantic revisions focus on critical reflection and profound  modification of the content. In this study, we used the Classification of revision occasions  (an adjusted version of Edit Intent Classification developed by Ruan et al., 2024; see Table 1), to categorize the revision occasions detected by learners. This classification was developed with reference to central theories and empirical research on text re vision (e.g., Faigley & Witte, 1981). Effects of revision occasions on the learning processes As surface-level and semantic revisions are associated with different kinds of text modification, it seems reasonable that the detection of different revision occasions may initiate differ ent learning processes. This can be illustrated by the ICAP framework  (I–interactive, C–constructive, A–active, and P–passive; Chi & Wylie, 2014). Learners’ detection of surface-level revision occasions indicates that they are engaging  in active learning mode (A), as they are not merely passive recipients of a text but are instead proactively improving it. Such engagement with a text allows learners to gain a superficial u nderstanding of the content, which they candraw up on to reinforce their existing prior knowledge or strategies (Chi & Wylie, 2014). However, it is only through the detection of semantic revision occasions that learners can become co-creators of a text by producing, integrating, or excluding ideas and facts, or establishing a deeper level  of coherence between the arguments. Such in-depth evaluation and synthesizing of content (i.e., constructive learning, C) encourages learners to engage more meaningfully with the text, which sustainably facilitates knowledge a cquisition and transfer (Chi & Wylie, 2014). Thus,  while detecting surface-level revision occasions is fundamental in the initial stages of revision, the detection of semantic revision occasions has  greater positive effects, not only on the quality of the te xt, but also on internal learning processes.

The present study aimed to answer the following research question: Do learners detect different revision occasions in texts labeled as peer-written or AI-generated?

## Methods

We analyzed a subset of data from a larger study (R adtke & Rummel, 2025), which was conducted to exami ne the fundamental differences between learners’ revision behaviors when revising texts labeled as written by either a peer or by an AI. While in the larger study, participants revised two texts, in this paper, we limited our analyses to a subset of the sample that revised a text on th e topic of sleep (N = 152). We classified the revision operations performed by the participants as either detected surface-level or semantic revision occasions and compared the data for the texts labeled as written by different authors (peer vs. AI).

## Sample, design, and procedure

The sample consisted of N = 152 participants (76.3% male, 23.0% female, and 0.7% diverse), with n = 76 in each experimental condition (text labeled as peer-written vs. AI-generated). The mean age of the sample was M = 25.89 years (SD = 4.95). As the highest educational degree, 42,1% reported a high school diploma, 30.9% a Bachelor’s degree, 18.4% a Master’s degree, and 18.6% another type of academic qualification. Most participants were students (72.4%). Prior to their participation in the study, 95.4% of the participants had previously heard of AI-based tools for automated text generation, while 65.1% had previously used them. In an experimental session, participants revised a text (approx. 3.5 p. long)  that, according to the randomly assigned experimental condition, was labeled either as peer-written or as AI-generated. The content of the text was identical in both conditions and addressed the topic ‘Relationship between sleep and life quality.’ Participants in both conditions reported similar levels of  familiarity with the topic of the text to be revis ed, measured on a scale from 1 (completely unfamiliar) to 5 (very familiar; in the peer-condition: M = 3.36, SD = 0.91 and in the AI-condition: M = 3.26, SD = 0.96). The average duration of the experiment was  2 hours and 5 minutes, and participants were compensated with a 20 Euro voucher.

Dependent variables In this study, detected revision occasions served as dependent variables (see Table 1). Two re search assistants analyzed the revised texts to determine the revision occasions detected by participants applying the Classification of revision occasions (adjusted from Ruan et al., 2024). To ensure the consistency between the rates, we conducted inter-rater reliability analyses. The Cohen’s Kappa (κ) for both rater s (based on a randomly selected 30% of the documents with a total of N = 1016 analyzed revision operations) was excellent (κ =.835; Fleis s et al., 2003, p. 604). In addition, we run the analyses for rater s’ agreement in each class of revision occasions se parately. For each class, the agreement was excellent (.808 ≤ κ ≤.957), except for Claim, where Kappa was fair to good (κ =.413). Detailed data analysis revealed a particular dis crepancy between the two raters regarding the assig nments for Claim and Fact/Evidence (both of which belong to the semantic revisions occasions). Given the lack of clear delineation between these two classes, subseq uent analyses focused primarily on the overarching classes (surface-level and semantic revision occasions).

## Results

The descriptive analyses for the revision occasions (see Table 2) indicated that learners detected more surfacelevel revision occasions (in total) in the text labeled as written by a peer (M = 34.83, SD = 21.85) than in the text labeled as written by an AI (M = 29.32, SD = 18.22). This difference was particularly evident for the class Clarity (peer-condition: M = 18.57, SD = 16.69; AI-condition: M = 15.16, SD = 13.15). However, no appreciable difference was observed for the semantic revision occasions. In both conditions, detected surface and semantic revision occasions accounted for roughly 70% and 30% of all revisions, respectively. To test whether the observed differences are statistically significant, we computed a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) with the labeled author as a factor and surface-level revision occasions (in total) and semantic revision occasions (in total) as two dependent variables. The results indicated n o significant effect of the labeled author on the combination of both variables (Wilk’s Λ =.973, F(2,149) = 2.058, p =.131, ηp² =.027). The separate univariate tests also indicated no significant effects – neither on the surface-level revision occasions (in total), F (1,150) = 2.854, p =.093, η p² =.019), nor on the semantic revision occasions  (in total), F(1,150) = 0.033, p =.857, η p² =.000). Both separately performed MANOVA for surface-level and semantic revision occasions were also not significant. In summary, we observed a descriptive, but statistically non-significant, trend indicating that learners who were informed that a text was generated by an AI detected fewer surfacelevel revision occasions (in total) than learners who revised a text labeled as written by a peer. ## Discussion

The aim of this study was to gain a deeper understa nding of learners’ approaches to revising AI-generated academic texts, and particularly their detection of re vision occasions in these texts. To this end, we co nducted an experimental study in which participants revised a text labeled as either peer-written or AI-generated. The actual content of the text was identical. We analyzed the revision occasions that learners detected in their revisions. We found that, descriptively, learners detected fewer surface-level revision occasions in a text labeled as AI-generated as compared to a text labeled as peer-written. However, these differences were not statistically significant. Nevertheless, this non-significant tendency for learners to underestimate surface-level revision occasions is concerning in light of evidence that AI-generated content often contains linguistic inaccur acies (Advertext, 2023). For the semantic revision occasions, we found no descriptive and statistically significant differences. However, it is also debatable whether such learners’ r evision strategy is optimal when revising AI-generated content. Even though there is no consensus on the extent to  which learners need to revise an AI-generated text to claim it as their own intellectual work, it seems certain th at their personal extensive contribution to the text’s content is essential for it (e.g., Salden & Leschke, 2023). In collaborative writing, learners are usually expected to contribute equally to writing and revising the text, with no peer expe cted to revise disproportionately more than the oth ers. This prompts the question of whether learners should engage in even more extensive and in-depth revision when they consider the text to be the originated by an AI – i.e., detect even more semantic revision occasions. Thus, on the one hand, it is crucial to foster learners’ awareness that surface-level revision occasions in AI-generated content are more prevalent than they may realize. On the other hand, learners should be made aware that revising AIgenerated texts is a learning situation, in which detection of semantic revision occasions is particularly important. Considering the potential of learners’ detection of semantic revision to activate constructive learning (C; Chi & Wylie, 2014), this aspect of revision in AI-assisted writing seems even more relevant. Neverthe less, as these conclusions are based on descriptive yet non-significant differences, further studies on this topic are necessary. With respect to methodology, this study has certain  limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, when applying the framework developed by Ruan et al. (2024), we did not achieve sufficient differentiation between the classes Fact/Evidence and Claim. This was not entirely unexpected, as claims are o ften phrased in a fact-like manner and are frequently intertwined with the  facts within a single sentence, which complicates the identification of the precise fine-grained distinction between these classes  (Ruan et al., 2024). Thus, in future research, raters should undergo more exte nsive training to achieve sufficient results in ter ms of agreement. Secondly, the design of this study did not al low for an analysis of whether a particular way of interacting with the originator of the text (peer vs. AI) could impact learners’ detection of revision occasions by prior activation of the interactive learning mode  (I; Chi & Wylie, 2014). Although the ICAP-framework was developed a decade ago, the authors have already indicated that  the interactive learning can take place with ‘another person who can be a peer, a teacher, a parent, or computer agent’ (Chi & Wylie, 2014, p. 223). Indeed, LLM technology enables learners to iteratively engage in argumentative dialogues with the tool while writing and revising, providing this process with more dynamic analogues to peer interactions (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023; Nguyen et al., 2024). Thus, we believe that this aspect of the revision o f AI-generated texts should be further investigated in f uture research.
