# Adolescent Identity as a Complex Adaptive System: Implications for Informal Pre-college STEM Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This theoretical poster conceptualizes adolescent identity formation as a system of representational systems, using the terminology of semiotics to distinguish between signifiers (identity-relevant cues and how they are interpreted) and their referents. This approach facilitates the use of complex adaptive systems to model the dynamic and reciprocal interactions that constantly shape overall identity formation and that are shaped by overall identity. Theoretical frameworks of self-determination theory, social cognitive career theory, and interest-driven creator theory furnish considerations for informal science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) intervention designs while addressing what drives the dynamics of the complex identity formation systems. The poster further suggests that finding congruence between the semiotic and the referent is a motivational force fueling identity formation. While conceptual in nature, the poster treats identity formation as operationally relevant to the design of informal STEM interventions and furnishes interview excerpts from two such interventions to illustrate selected constructs.

## Introduction

This poster suggests that adolescent identity formation may prove a powerful lens in designing interventions to address deficiencies in pre-college science, technology, engineering, and mathe matics (STEM) interest and subsequent participation in tertiary STEM education  and entry into STEM-rich professions. It does so by proposing the viability of complex adaptive systems  (CAS) theory to identity development and then appl ying CAS to three theoretical frameworks especially pert inent to identity development – Social Cognitive Care er Theory (Lent et al., 2002), Self-Determination Theory (Reeve, 2002), and Interest-Driven Creator Theory (Roschelle & Burke, 2019). Indeed, emphasis on helping adolescents navigate the task of identity development in a healthy and integrated manner is essential to creating successful STEM intervention experiences (Olszewski‐ Kubilius, 2023). Yet with  degree credentials in various STEM fields, most principal investigators or leaders of such interventions hold primary expertise outside of the social psychology of identity formation. What might be involved in more explicit grounding of intervention  design in such factors?  And how or why might such factors be germane to communities such as the International Society for Learning Sciences (ISLS)? This poster addresses the intersection of learning science and adolescent identity theory as they pertain to informal STEM interventions. While primarily theoretical in advocating the tools of complex adaptive thinking, the poster illustrates constructs with pertinent subject interviews from two projects f unded by the US National Science Foundation (NSF). One project is a retrospective impact study of currentl y middle-aged professionals who participated in the 1990s er a Young Scholars Program for middle and high school s tudents (Hamilton, 2021). A quarter-century later, how do they reflect on the ways that participation in proj ects shaped their eventual career pathways? The sec ond project (Hamilton et al., 2022)  is part of NSF’s current Advancing Informal Science  Learning (AISL) portfolio (NSF, 2023). The AISL project emphasizes Global South – Global North collaboration and entails creating project-based learning activities by which teenagers from differe nt countries collaborate over virtual spaces. Data from both projects appear in the poster; they lend themselves to exploring how individuals view personal life pathways and how they situate themselves from the vantage of STEM pathways. Theoretical frameworks involving STEM interest, workforce pathways, personal identity, and complex adaptive systems (CAS) Research in the continuum of childhood and adolescent interest in STEM fields up to and through eventual entry into STEM careers has led to multiple theoretical f rameworks relevant to the design of the types that NSF and counterpart agencies in other nations support. One such framework, Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT), has evolved since its introduction in the early 2000s (Lent et al., 2002). SCCT focuses indirectly on topics of identity, with emphasis on self-efficacy, perceived family and other external support, and goal setting. Self-Determination Theory (SDT) (Reeve, 2002) promotes constructs of autonomy, connection, and co mpetence as three critical human needs. originates in personality and humandevelopment theory but relates directly issues at play in STEM interventions. Popular notions such as “I am a math  person” or “she is a nerd” speak in a limited way to how teenagers see themselves and others in ways that po tentially disclose interest and intention as they n avigate the transition to adulthood. SCCT and SDT constructs en compass a larger and more holistic approach to iden tity.

Using those constructs, this poster suggests that a  complex adaptive systems (CAS) approach (Carmichael & Hadžikadić, 2019) to adolescent identity formation may prove especial ly fruitful in the design of STEM interventions or more broadly in any field that pro motes the well-being and growth of adolescents. Kaplan’s Dynamic Systems Model of Role Identity (DSMRI) (Kap lan & Garner, 2017) is one CAS approach that seeks to encompass multiple interpretations or frameworks to  such an  explanatory tool for identity development. It employs the metaphors and tools of complexity theory to give greater analytic and unifying traction to i dentity research, and perhaps a more inviting canvas for learning science researchers to situate their investigations. CAS theory provides both descriptive and prescriptive i nterpretations of a wide spectrum of phenomena. Kap lan’s approach, as the DSMRI acronym suggests, emphasizes  dynamic nature of identity. The approach taken in this poster focuses more on adaptation and equilibration in identity formation. Semiotic structures and referents Our approach highlights and relies specifically on epistemological distinctions between a) identity-relevant cues and how they are interpreted to build an aggregate self-concept; and b) the underlying phenomena to which those identity-relevant cues refer. An identity-relevant cue, coupled with an individuals interpretation of it, represents a semiotic structure (Bell, 2002). One subject shared, “I feel like I am pretty strong  in math …it is probably the subject I like.. most.” The cues (grades, reinforcement, sense of satisfact ion and competence in these fields) combined with these self-assessing estimates to comprise the semiotic structure, with the underlyin g reality of actual student strength in each area as the referent. Another example from popular psychology includes the notion of “finding oneself” (Baumeister & Muraven, 1996) –intuiting an underlying reality about identity and one’s selfconcept, with “finding oneself” as a task of bringing congruence between underlying true identity and one’s selfconcept, a central construct of Oyserman’s theories of identity-based motivation (Oyserman, 2015).
