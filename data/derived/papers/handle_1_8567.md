# Increasing College STEM Instructors’ Equity-Oriented Teaching Competencies

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines what helps university STEM instructors learn and adopt inclusive teaching practices, such as Adaptive Equity-Oriented Pedagogy (AEP). AEP has been shown to improve student achievement by over a full-letter grade. This study randomly assigned 129 student-instructors to treatment and control pedagogy courses. While the control course taught student-instructors about AEP, the treatment course modeled AEP explicitly by using weekly student-instructor reflection data to continuously adjust course discussion and activities. Using multilevel-regression modeling, we found the treatment significantly improved studentinstructors’ AEP competencies over time compared to the control (p<0.001). Compared to the control, more student-instructors in the treatment used multiple data sources to adjust teaching and address equity barriers to advance student learning. Through the novel application of mixed-methods research and validated AEP measures, this study offers insights on what values, motivations, and practices characterize those who do and do not implement AEP.

## Introduction: Problem statement and research questions

Research shows that college teaching directly impacts student achievement and retention in STEM, especially for underrepresented students (URM; Sax et al., 2018). To improve STEM teaching, we studied adaptive equit yoriented pedagogy (AEP). Experimental research has found that compared to an active learning control c ourse, instructors applying AEP increased student achievement by over a letter grade for all students (Phuong & Nguyen, 2019; Phuong et al., 2017). This study examines how pedagogy courses teaching A EP principles can support student-instructors’ implementation of inclusive pe dagogies. The pedagogy courses primarily serve studentinstructors who teach large introductory STEM courses. For simplicity, we use the term “instructor” for graduate and undergraduate students who teach and are enrolled in the pedagogy courses. The term “facilitators” refers to the pedagogy course instructors. 129 instructors participated in a treatment or control AEP pedagogy course where they completed a teaching portfolio that included written reflections, evidence of student learning, and feedback on their teaching. The treatment course was adapted weekly based on instructors’ written reflections, whereas the control was not. Using mixed-methods, multilevel mo deling and AEP competency assessments, we examine whether pedagogy courses impact instructors’ learning progression of AEP. We explore 2 research questions: RQ1: Is there a greater increase in mean AEP competency scores across time in the treatment vs. control pedagogy courses, controlling for gender, URM statu s, years of teaching experience, years of tutoring experience, and number of students?

RQ2: How do low-and high-growth instructors on AEP competency differ with respect to their data use and responsibility for learning lenses?

AEP Pedagogy Course Design and Conceptual Framing The AEP pedagogy courses teach instructors about the key AEP principles. AEP is a framework for adapting teaching to address equity barriers to learning based on student data (e.g., formative assessment, observations, surveys) (Phuong et al., 2017). AEP practice is characterized by instructors’ use of 6 competency elements:

(1) Clarify learning outcomes, equitable course policies, and prerequisites; (2) Align formative assessments and

activities with outcomes; (3) Identify students’ competencies, interests, & needs; (4) Understand equity barriers and contextual challenges to meeting outcomes; (5) Adapt teaching practices based on these needs and barriers; and (6) Iterate: Reflect upon pedagogy to support continuous learning, adaptation, and growth. AEP builds on culturally responsive pedagogy (CRP) by offering a framework to adapt CRP practices: engaging learners’ experiences, critical consciousness, and cultural knowledge to make curricula more relevant and effective (Hammond, 2014). We draw on AEP as a conceptual framework to understand how pedagogy course facilitators adapt their practices to address instructors’ learning needs and perspectives in the treatment. Unique element of treatment: Facilitators modeled and applied the 6 AEP elements explicitly as a tool for instructor learning by using weekly instructor reflection data to continuously adjust pedagogy cou rse ICLS2022 Proceedings 1665 © ISLS discussion and activities. We hypothesize that this process would promote grea ter reflection in the treatment, which we expect to increase instructor learning over time in the treatment relative to the control as measured by AEPC assessments. Applying AEP, the facilitators adjusted the treatment based on instructors’ reflections of their cultural perspectives and backgrounds, which include their lived experiences, racial experiences, discourse, and STEM department culture (e.g., what is valued methodologically). Both conditions prompted instructors to reflect on how they may have experienced stereotype threat and microaggressions based on their identities. Lenses on instructional change. We are inspired by the notion of professional visio n (Sherin, 2014), which refers to developing ways of seeing phenomena that reflect growing levels of expertise. Accordingly, we identified lenses that seemed to serve  as important levers for instructi onal change. We conjecture that the treatment’s effectiveness may be related to the focus on data and responsibility for learning lenses that instructors may reveal in written reflections. These lenses allow us to understand instructors’ values, beliefs, and motivations for learning inclusive practices. The data lens is defined as the kinds of knowledge and data that instructors value to make pedagogical decisions. The responsibility for learning lens refers to how the instructor views their role and the student’s role in learning--e.g., whose res ponsibility is it if a student doesn’t learn a conc ept? This lens accounts for the systems, structures, and/or belief s that foreground individualistic notions of learni ng that have contributed to STEM students leaving the major beca use they felt under-supported and isolated (Seymour  & Hewitt, 1997). Identifying these lenses is beneficial for advancing instructors’ learning.

## Methodology: Experimental Design and Mixed Methods

STEM instructors at a R-1 public university participated in semester-long AEP pedagogy courses for first-time instructors where they completed a teaching portfolio. The pedagogy courses primarily served instructors who taught computer science, data science, math, and statistics. Instructors participated in either a treatment or control pedagogy course. In both conditions, instructors completed baseline, midterm, and final AEPC assessments. Each AEPC assessment elicits evidence about teaching philosophies, written reflections on teaching and assessment practices, evidence of student learning, student gain score visualizations, media, and peer/student feedback on their teaching. The AEPC assessment contained reflection questions that asked instructors to provide evidence of demonstrating AEP competencies. The scale was 0-22 with 3 items b eing scored 7 points each and 1 item being 1 point.  A detailed rubric (See table 3 for a summary) was app lied independently by two researchers to blindly sc ore assessments. Higher positions on the rubric imply that criteria of lower positions have been met. For research question 1, we use multilevel regression modeling to examine if there is a greater increase in instructors’ mean AEPC scores across time in the treatment than in the control, controlling for gender, URM status, teaching experience, tutoring experience, and number of students. We use multilevel modeling for longitudinal data with random intercepts to account for dependency in repeated AEPC measures. We examined growth in AEPC by estimating the increase from 1) pre-intervention AEPC scores to midsemester AEPC scores, and 2) pre-intervention to post-semester AEPC scores, and how much the growth differs between the treatment and control. Models were estimated by maximum likelihood with robust standard errors to protect against misspecifications of the model’s random part and residual covariance structure. For research question 2, we randomly sampled 10 high-growth and 10 low-growth instructors from the top 25% and the bottom 25% of AEPC growth from pre-to post-semester. We coded for the data lens and responsibility for learning lens that these instructors use, and were blind to the instructor's name, condition, and whether they were in the low-or high-growth group.  We analyzed the presence, frequency, and contingency of codes to determine similarities and differences in what characterize instructors who implement AEP.

## Discussion and Conclusion: Why the Treatment Worked

The treatment provided a culturally responsive and adaptive learning experience that strengthened the scaffolding and accelerated the learning progression relative to the control. Prior to intervention, the facilitators identified that many instructors in the treatment defined equity as equality and placed the responsibility for learning only on the student. Thus, the treatment had conversations on interrogating long-standing beliefs and inequitable ideologies that the “good students are the ones who teach them selves”. This approach expanded instructors’ critic al consciousness since it asked them to rethink common  cultural norms, values, meritocracy, and larger sy stems (e.g., structural racism) that reproduce inequities, where students from underprivileged backgrounds may not have equitable access to resources and opportunities tha t enable them to teach themselves. Proactively shar ing the learning responsibility shifts the pedagogical focu s from an individualistic “pull yourself up by the bootstraps” approach to one that is more social and culturally responsive. Furthermore, this study contributes insights on how to increase instructors’ adoption of inclusive prac tices, thereby addressing a long-standing barrier t o STEM pedagogical reform. This research offers strategies to support instructors on how to reflect on and us e multiple forms of data effectively-- rather than their assumptions-- to address equity barriers and increase student success.
