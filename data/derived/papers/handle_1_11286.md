# Experiencing Teaching in Artificial Intelligence-supported Virtual Reality Simulations: Unpacking Engagement

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Engagement is a multifaceted construct that can influence learning outcomes. In this ex post facto study, we examined the correlations between different engagement constructs, as well as the impact of engagement on preservice teachers’ teaching knowledge and skills. We collected and analyzed video data from fourteen individual sessions with a coding scheme for engagement in VR-based teaching practices using AI-powered virtual students, as well as a teaching knowledge and skills posttest. The preliminary findings suggested that preservice teachers’ VR movement may correlate with disengagement, while emotional engagement, particularly confusion, can support learning opportunities. Engagement observed in the current study significantly predicted teaching knowledge and skills development.

## Introduction and literature review

Engagement, a disposition of learners when actively participating in a learni ng activity or experience, is multifaceted and can influence learning outcomes (Dubovi, 2022; Finn & Zimmer, 2012; Wong & Liem, 2022). Engagement has been studie d extensively in classroom s (Bundick et al., 2014), in online learning  (Kahn et al., 2017), in computer-based or game-based learning (Chen, 2017; Ke et al., 2016), and across different education stages (i.e., in elementary, middle, high schools, and higher educa tion; Wong & Liem, 2022), but we still know relatively little about engagement in VR-supported simulation-based learning, especially for teacher learners. Engagement is a complex concept that can be defined from various perspectives and theoretical frameworks. It has been recognized as one of the most important elements in student learning (Finn & Zimmer, 2012). In the context of student learning, engagement  provides insight into understanding and enhancing learning outcomes (Finn & Zimmer, 2012). In technology-based learning environments, engagement has become a key construct that researchers strive to demystify, as it directly influences both l earning experiences and achievements (Dubovi, 2022; Wong & Liem, 2022). From the perspective of technology-based learning, Kearsley and Shneiderman

(1998) proposed an engagement theory grounded in constructivist theory. In general, engagement is often seen as

comprising several components, including academic, social, cognitive, a nd affective /emotional engagement (Akman & Çakır, 2023; Finn & Zimmer, 2012). While these models and propositions are in the broader education and school contexts, they have informed our approach to studying engagement in VR-supported simulation-based learning for preservice teachers.

Although research on VR-supported, simulation-based learning for engagement has been a focus in the literature, these studies have primarily concentrated on evaluating whether VR simulations are effective in promoting engagement within their target populations. For instance, Karnchanapayap (2023) examined activities in a VR experience and found that they can enhance audience engagement at an i nternational exhibition event. Similarly, Kersting et al. (2021) revealed that VR can boost visitor engagement at science festivals.  Although these studies have provided valuable insights, further research is neede d to explore the correlations between different components of engagem ent in VR-supported simulation-based learning environments and how these components affect preservice teachers' teaching knowledge and skills. These gaps have limited our understanding of the learning experiences in such environments, as well as the designs that could be improved to better support preservice teachers as they practice teaching in VR-supported simulations. The gaps in the literature underscore the need for the present study. Specifically, we asked the following research questions (RQs): RQ1. What are the correlations between teacher learners’ Cognitive Engagement, Emotional Engagement, and VR Engagement as a result of practicing teaching in AI-supported VR simulations?  RQ2. Is there an impact of teacher learners ’ Cognitive Engagement, Emotional Engagement, and VR Engagement on their teaching knowledge and skills when practicing in AI-supported VR simulations?

Engagement in VR In educational virtual reality studies, engagement often being clas sified as cognitive and social, as well as emotional (Akman & Çakır, 2023), while VR environment engagement has not yet been thoroughly studied. Some prior studies on engagement in VR have relied on self-report measures (Akman & Çakır, 2023; Wong & Liem, 2022), while other studies combining multimodal measures with sensors and subjective measures ha ve arisen (Dubovi, 2022). Previous studies (e.g., Huang et al., 2021; Ke et al., 2021) have briefly addressed the conce pt of VR engagement, specifically, Ke et al. (2021) explored the relation between VR environment exploration and engagement in teaching practices.  They found that the more participants explore the VR environment, the  less they are engaged in teaching practices. Despite these studies, the understanding and defining constructs of VR engagement is still limited and inconclusive.

AI-supported VR simulations in teacher education VR simulations have been recognized as a promising tool for learning a cross various disciplines, particularly in addressing complex decision-making and reasoning. Teacher education is one area that can greatly benefit from learning in VR simulations (Dai et al., 2024; Girod & Girod, 2006). However, the challenges of designing cost-effective VR simulations with adequate authenticity and fidelity ha ve posed significant limitations, especially when using unintentionally designed or ad hoc  VR simulations for pr actice-based learning in teacher education (Authors, 2024; Kavanagh et al., 2020). While integrating AI into VR simulat ions has helped address these challenges (Authors, 2024), further research is needed to refine the designs and explore how be st to support teachers in such authentic simulated environments for various outcomes such as engagement.

## Method

We adopted an ex post facto research design  (cf. Cohen et al., 2007), as part of a larger design-based research project, to explore the relations between different facets of engag ement as well as the relations between different engagement and learning outcomes (i.e., teacher learners’ teaching knowledge and skills). AI-supported VR simulations The AI-supported VR simulation learning environment we adopted is called Enactive Virtual Environment for Teaching Practices (EVETeach; Ke et al., 2021). EVETeach is designed with VR simulation software OpenSimulator, an open-source VR environment suitable for customizable and scalable designs. We leveraged a large language model trained on authentic classroom data to power the output of AI-driven virtual students. These virtual students interact with participants for pedagogical practices on topics such as math, biology, and gas laws. ## Data collection and analyses

We collected data from 22 preservice teachers (from various courses in colleges of education in the US)  with 42 two-hour sessions. We reported the preliminary analysis from the 16 sessi ons, accounting for 32 hours of data, from 14 participating sessions. The preservice teacher learners participated in VR simulation sessions to practice teaching with AI-powered virtual student agents. They completed a post-session teaching knowledge test (Dai, 2023;  K e  e t  a l.,  2 021) after the teaching practice in the VR simulation. The sessions were audio-and videorecorded. Two trained coders first browsed the collected video recordings with a particular focus on teacher participants’ engagement in teaching, with AI-powered virtual students, and in the VR environment. Via debriefing through theory-based (Akman & Çakır, 2023; Ke et al., 2016; Kearsley & Shneiderman, 1998; Watkins et al., 2020) and data-driven approaches, the trained coders and the researchers formulated the coding scheme that can address the research questions of the current study with th e collected dataset.  The coding scheme was iteratively refined to capture the constructs aligning with our re search aims, purposes, and questions. Based on the theoretical backgrounds, the literature, and our context, we used the coding framework with three major types of engagement: Emotional engagement, Cognitive and social engagement with AI-powered virtual students, and VR environment engagement. ## Findings

RQ1. Correlations between different dimensions of engagement For RQ1, we investigated the correlations between the indicators of teacher learners’ Cognitive Engagement, Emotional Engagement, and VR Engagement  with Pearson correlation analyses. The results suggested some significant covariate relations as the results of practicing teaching in AI-supported VR simulations. Interestingly, we found that VR engagement (“ In-world kinetic ”) significantly correlated with Emotional engagement (“Anything not engaged/bored ”) (r =.59, p =.02, 95% CI [.12,.85]), and Cognitive and social engagement (“Ignore students’ keywords or resources”)  (r =.53, p =.04, 95% CI [.02,.82]). VR engagement (“ Noticing pedagogical opportunities designed in VR”) significantly correlated with Emotional engagement––Excitement (r =.59, p =.02, 95% CI [.11,.85])  and Confusion/vexation (r =.65, p =.008, 95% CI [.21,.87]). Based on this finding, we aggregated “Noticing pedagogical opportunities designed in VR” and “Interacting with mix reality gadgets,” and found a positive significant correlation with “Cognitive and social engagement with AI-powered virtual students” (r =.90, p <.001). ## Discussion and conclusion

In this study, w e investigated  the relations between different engagement constructs and the  impacts of engagement on teaching knowledge and skills. Our preliminary findings offe red several emerging insights. First, our finding  suggested that preservice teachers’ movement in the VR simulation may correlate with desirable engagement outcome.  More specifically, the observed VR engagement behavior  (i.e., “In-world kinetic”) may contribute to distraction, or could be connected to “boredom” or “disengagement” of the designed VR activity. This finding aligned with a previous study (Ke et al., 2021) suggesting that VR environment exploration does not necessarily relate to pedagogical practices.

Second, our study builds on previous research by suggesting that specific VR engagement —such as noticing pedagogical opportunities designed in VR —may be related to emotional engagement, including both excitement and confusion /vexation. Notably, confusion /vexation can serve as a valuable opportunity for supporting the development of teaching knowledge and skills in AI-supported VR simulations. This adds to the dispositions discussed by Loderer et al. (2020), who suggested that negat ive emotions like confusion, anger, or boredom can benefit learning under certain circumstances, particularly  when they promote deeper engagement with content and can be successfully  resolved. Our findings provide preliminary empirical evidence within the context of VR-supported simulations for teacher learning, highlighting how confusion ca n support preservice teachers in noticing pedagogical opportunities embedded in VR activit ies, contributing to the speculative discussions noted in the previous literature (Loderer et al., 2020).  To this end, finally, our results reveal ed that engagement can significantly predict teaching knowledge and skills develo pment. Based on the findings, the design of VR environments could consider how engaging learners with pedagogical opportunities in AI-supported simulations and interactions with virtual agents and tools can enhance  constructive pedagogy. These findings contribute to the underexplored area of teacher engagement w ithin AI-supported VR simulations and shed light on the relations between VR engagement and emotions. They provide insights to guide the design of VR learning environments, using evidence-based indicators to support informed design decisions.
