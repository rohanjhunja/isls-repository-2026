# Co-design as a Catalyst for Becoming: Identity, Practice, and Learning Trajectories in Undergraduate Science

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Co-design is a collaborative methodology for creating educational innovations involving researchers, educators, and developers. While research-practice partnerships in the Learning Sciences have historically co-designed with teachers, recent developments have begun involving students. However, research on students’ experiences of co-design remains limited. This study explores what students learn from engaging in co-design through a situated learning framework linking learning to identity formation. Using a multiple case study methodology, the relationships between the identities, learning, and participation of six undergraduate science students were examined in a co-design project for creating educational virtual reality simulations. Findings reveal that identity, practice, and learning exist in dynamic and reciprocal relationships within co-design contexts. Students’ existing identities informed their co-design engagement. Through practice, students developed collaboration skills and learned about themselves which led to diverse identity development trajectories. These findings suggest engaging students as co-design partners creates unique opportunities for integrated learning and identity development.

With its roots in participatory design, co-design has emerged as an innovative method for creating educational advancements through collaboration between researchers, educators, and developers (Hoadley, 2017; Penuel et al., 2007). Co-design has shown to be particularly effective for engaging teachers in shaping technology-enhanced learning tools (Kali et al., 2015), with benefits including better student learning outcomes (Gerard et al., 2011), greater teacher ownership (Cviko et al., 2015), and a method of  professional development (PD) for educators, influencing their pedagogical beliefs and collaborative practices (Voogt et al., 2015). Scholars have recognized the need to extend co-design to include students as active partners, highlighting the value of student voice and agency in shaping educational experiences (Cook-Sather, 2003; DiSalvo et al., 2017), yet research to date has focused primarily on design processes and outcomes, with limited attention to students’ lived experiences and what they learn through participation (Woods & Homer, 2022). Although early little research deliberately centers student learning in co-design or examines how participation shapes their identities.

This gap is significant given that identity fundamentally shapes students’ motivation and engagement in post-secondary education (Destin & Williams, 2020). For science students specifically, identity predicts persistence and retention in the field (Jiang & Wei, 2023). Given the value of co-design as a professional learning opportunity for teachers, there is strong potential for it to serve as an analogous opportunity for students. Understanding how participation in co-design relates to students’ science identities could provide insights into leveraging design-based collaboration as a transformative learning opportunity. This study seeks to address this gap by examining how participation in the co-design of educational technologies influences undergraduate students’ science identities. Drawing on Lave and Wenger’s (1991) theory of situated learning and Wenger’s (1998) communities of practice, this research explores the connections between learning, identity, and the practice of co-design. Specifically, the study investigates the experiences of undergraduate science students who engaged in co-design with educators and resear chers to create multi-user virtual reality (VR) learning activities. The central research question guiding this study is: What is the relationship between a student’s identity, how they participate in the practice of co-design, and what they learn from the experience? The relationship between these three elements is examined in this study, namely (1) the relationship between identity and practice (2) practice and learning, and (3) learning and identity.

## Theoretical framework

Situated learning theory posits learning as legitimate peripheral participation within a community of practice, asserting that to learn “implies becoming a different person with respect to the possibilities enabled by these 1902 systems of relations” (Lave & Wenger, 1991, p. 53). Wenger (1998) uses the term identities-in-practice to describe identities that emerge in participation through four interconnected processes: (1) Identity forms through negotiated experience, reified through social discourse and artifact creation; (2) it functions as community membership, involving personal competence and recognition by others; (3) identity is a learning trajectory, continuously renegotiated as individuals encounter new experiences and roles; and (4) each person is a nexus of multiple community memberships, requiring ongoing reconciliation into a coherent self. Holland et al.’s (1998) concept of authoring the self  complements Wenger’s idea of reconciliation by emphasizing the role of agency and imagination in identity formation. While identity is shaped through socialization into existing community practices, individuals also improvise with available cultural resou rces, roles, and narratives to interpret their experiences. Like learning trajectories, authorship is ongoing: individuals continually reinterpret, resist, and reconfigure their self-narratives in response to the worlds they inhabit. Wenger (1998) synthesizes these ideas in his social theory of learning, which frames learning through four mutually constitutive dimensions: meaning (making sense of participation), practice (developing competence through shared tools and activities), community (building relationships and belonging), and identity (learning as becoming). Together, these dimensions describe learning as the transformation of one’s identity-in-practice.

## Context and participants

This case study is embedded within a larger design-based research project co-designing multi-user VR learning experiences for undergraduate science education (Lui et al., 2026). Eighteen participants were grouped into six design teams of 2– 4 members, compr ising either mixed student-faculty teams or student-only teams, recruited from The University of Toronto. Teams were formed based on content expertise and availability, and each completed three consecutive 3-hour co-design sessions over the span of five months, working with a three-member research team to design a multi-user VR simulation. Six students from four teams were selected for the current study (see Table 1).

## Data analysis

All interviews and voice memos were audio recorded, transcribed, and analyzed inductively using in vivo followed by axial coding (Saldaña, 2021). Codes were organized under the categories of identity, practice, and learning, and thematic analysis (Auerbach & Silverstein, 2003; Boyatzis, 1998) identified patterns within each case. A cross-case analysis (Creswell & Poth, 2025) then surfaced broader patterns across participants. Students’ identity, learning, and their participation in the practice of co-design were found to exist in dynamic and reciprocal relationships. As depicted in Figure 1, (1) students’ existing identities informed how they engaged in co-design practices and interpreted their experience; (2) co-design practices facilitated student learning while also generating new understandings of collaboration; and (3) participation in co-design created opportunities for identity development and transformation.

Three modes emerged across cases. First, John and Pepper leveraged personal learning preferences to guide their design contributions. John’s advocacy for interactive, game-based experiences reflected his belief that “learning should be interactive … I just thought of everything as like, you really have to be engaged into this game in order to learn” (Post-session interview, 2025).  Similarly, Pepper’s contributions focused on designing learning activities that were actionoriented and cognitively engaging, noting that these were the learning methods that worked for him. Second, Bane and Sunny drew from their memberships in other communities of practice outside of school, exemplifying co-design as a boundary practice where diverse expertise enriches collaborative work (Penuel et al., 2015). Bane’s medical professional identity shaped his concern for clinical realism: “interacting with physicians and nurses and admin staff, it played a big impact as to what we thought was important for the simulation” (Post-session interview, 2025). Relationship between practice and learning Engaging in co-design generated learning about collaborative practice, with three areas of growth emerging across cases. First, Sarah and Sunny both developed greater confidence in sharing ideas. Sarah overcame self-doubt by 1904 reframing brainstorming as collective rather than evaluative: “other people might think my idea is good. So it’s better to just brainstorm … if they don’t like it, at least I contributed” (Post-session interview, 2025). Sunny, whose VR expertise made him overly self-critical, learned to voice ideas regardless of perceived feasibility: “I’ve been trying to note down my ideas, no matter if I think it’s gonna work out or not … people might be able to build up on those ideas” (Post-session interview, 2025). Second, Veronica and Bane developed nuanced understandings of group decision-making. Bane learned to distinguish when to advocate for his position and when to yield, describing this as learning to “pick your battles” (Post-session interview, 2025), reframing disagreement as a resource for joint work rather than an obstacle (Penuel et al., 2015). Third, multiple students came to appreciate diverse perspectives. John’s assumption that shared gaming experience was a prerequisite for good game design was challenged through collaboration with an educator: “the difference actually is helpful. Because it gives different perspectives for the game to be more well-rounded” (Post-session interview, 2025). Relationship between learning and identity Participation in co-design produced three distinct identity development trajectories, highlighting both co-design’s transformative potential as a learning context and the central role of human agency. John and Sunny reconciled previously compartmentalized identities: John bridged his gaming and academic selves, resolving to experiment with gamification as a study strategy: “maybe if I study for something I would try to make it into a small game … or at least make it entertaining” (Post-session interview, 2025). Similarly, Sunny moved from VR consumer to creator, expressing a desire to “be someone who cando both … designing stuff … but also conducting research” (Post-session interview, 2025). For Bane, co-design strengthened his existing healthcare identity.  Experiencing research from the participant’s perspective helped him recognize that “the reason why participants aren’t responding right away … is because they have other things going on” (Post-session interview, 2025) which deepened his empathy and reinforced his commitments to participating in the medical community. For Sarah and Veronica, co-design opened new possibilities. Sarah revised her narrative about her lack of creativity, discovering that scientific expertise could be a resource for creative exp ression: “I’ve always thought that I’m not a creative person … but if it’s designing something related to my field, I have a lot of ideas” (Post-session interview, 2025). Veronica’s exposure to educational technology expanded her sense of professional poss ibility: “this co-design session just shows me that there are so many other fields outside of what I do … there’s so many other things I can pursue” (Post-session interview, 2025).

The findings of this study revealed an iterative relationship between identity, practice, and learning that operates at multiple levels. Students’ existing identities informed their practice, and through practice, they transformed their identities, potentially altering future practice. Within this larger cycle, a tighter feedback loop exists between practice and learning, as students developed skills and perspectives that immediately informed their collaborative approaches. The study demonstrates that co-design as a site of identity construction can reinforce existing notions of self as well as unlock new ways of being. Future trajectories are informed by past experiences without being predetermined, emphasizing the creative and agentic dimensions of identity authorship. The power of co-design as a boundary practice (Penuel et al., 2015) lies in its capacity to bring together participants from different communities of practice, creating fertile ground for transformation. When students with diverse expertise collaborate on authentic design challenges, the intersection of different ways of knowing generates rich learning opportunities that extend beyond technical skill development to encompass fundamental questions of identity and belonging. This study presents a compelling case for engaging students as partners in co-design, not merely as beneficiaries of educational innovation but as active contributors whose diverse identities and perspectives strengthen the design process while creating powerful contexts for their own learning and development. The findings suggest that when we invite students to participate authentically in shaping their learning experiences, we create new opportunities for integrated identity, practice, and learning development. ## Ecological and temporal perspectives on agency

Teacher agency is essential for understanding how educational reform becomes practice. According to Biesta and Tedder (2007), from an ecological perspective, agency is not a fixed personal trait but an achievement that emerges through teachers’ engagement with their environment. It arises from the interaction between teachers’ past experiences, professional orientations, and the opportunities and constraints present in the local context. This 1907 means that teachers do not simply implement reform; they actively interpret, adapt, and sometimes transform it in response to the conditions they encounter.

In this sense, agency is not something teachers possess but something they accomplish through ongoing interaction with contextual conditions. While this perspective conceptualizes agency as emerging through engagement with context, this paper extends this view by focusing more explicitly on how teachers continuously adjust and reinterpret pedagogical reforms in response to changing classroom realities. Building on this perspective, this paper introduces the concept of adaptive agency. Priestley et al. (2015) conceptualize agency as emerging through the interaction of three interrelated temporal dimensions. The first is the iterational dimension, involving teachers’ past experiences —their professional histories, beliefs, and routines that shape how they understand and interpret reform. The second is the projective dimension, referring to teachers’ future orientations, goals, and aspirations that motivate them to innovate and pursue change. The third is the practical-evaluative dimension, which reflects teachers’ ability to make judgments, adapt, and take action in response to the cultural and structural conditions of their present context.

Together, these dimensions help explain how teachers’ actions are shaped by what they have learned, what they aim to achieve, and what their environment currently allows. However, while this model accounts for temporally embedded action, it is less explicit in explaining how teachers continuously adapt and reconfigure pedagogical reforms over time in response to evolving classroom conditions. Priestley et al. (2012) further argue that agency is exercised within structural and cultural constraints that define teachers’ “space for maneuver”—the extent to which they can act, adapt, and make decisions within institutional settings. Eteläpelto et al. (2014) similarly emphasize that agency is closely tied to teachers’ professional identity and subjectivity, highlighting how agency is shaped through ongoing learning and participation in professional practice.

Adaptive agency in PBL Teachers working with PBL need to be creative and flexible, as they guide inquiry, facilitate collaboration, and support students in investigating real-world problems (Krajcik & Blumenfeld, 2006; Bell, 2010). They must guide group work, facilitate inquiry, and evaluate open-ended projects—making decisions that cannot be fully planned. Throughout the process, teachers constantly balance curriculum goals, time limitations, and students’ needs, adjusting their content knowledge and pedagogical methods as situations evolve. These demands highlight adaptive agency as a process through which teachers sustain pedagogical purpose while continuously responding to evolving learner needs, curriculum expectations, and institutional constraints. While this concept builds on Priestley et al.’s (2015) ecological model of teacher agency, adaptive agency differs from specific forms of agency described in the literature. For example, the practical-evaluative dimension refers to teachers’ ability to mak e situated judgments in response to present contextual conditions. Adaptive agency expands this dimension by highlighting teachers’ continuous reinterpretation of reform-oriented pedagogies as they manage the conflicts between policy expectations and class room realities. This distinction is important because it highlights that adaptive agency is not limited to isolated decisions but reflects how teachers sustain and rework reform over time.

Moral agency represents teachers' ethical dedication to meaningful learning, while relational agency emphasizes collaboration and coordination with others. In contrast, adaptive agency encompasses the overarching process that integrates these orientations into action. In this sense, adaptive agency can be understood as the dynamic enactment through which different forms of teacher agency—including moral, relational, epistemic, and practical-evaluative orientations—are mobilized together as teachers adapt pedagogical reforms such as project-based learning to their local contexts. These forms of agency are not treated as separate categories but as interconnected dimensions that become visible through the enactment of adaptive agency in practice. In Intykbekov (2017), teachers described how they modified PBL tasks to fit limited time and resources while still prioritizing student engagement—an example that can be interpreted as adaptive agency in action. Teachers working with PBL in Kazakhstan often face challenges such as limited time and uneven student participation within exam-oriented and structured school systems. For example, when group work leads to unequal contributions among students, teachers may adjust their facilitation strategies by redistributing roles and providing more structured guidance to support active participation from all group members. This illustrates how teachers balance the goals of collaborative learning with the realities of classroom dynamics, adapting their pedagogical approach while maintaining the core principles of PBL. Similarly, a teacher working within a highly exam-1908 oriented curriculum may shorten a long-term PBL project into a series of smaller inquiry activities that still promote collaboration and problem-solving. While practical-evaluative agency helps explain teachers’ immediate decisions in response to classroom  conditions, adaptive agency captures the broader process through which teachers reinterpret reform-oriented pedagogies to sustain meaningful learning under institutional constraints. Adaptive agency becomes visible across various stages and dimensions of PBL implementation, as teachers respond to the complex and context-dependent nature of PBL (Condliffe et al., 2017). For example, when it comes to student motivation and engagement, te achers demonstrate moral agency under exam pressure by focusing on meaningful learning rather than test preparation. For instance, a teacher might create a community-based or ecological project to keep students interested in both schoolwork and real-world problems. In the collaborative dimension of PBL, teachers exercise relational agency by fostering cooperation, trust, and fair participation among students. Such practices illustrate how adaptive agency enables teachers to balance pedagogical ideals with everyday realities in their classrooms. These examples show the most common forms of agency in some PBL areas, but they can also occur in other teaching contexts. ## Implications and conclusion

This paper expands the ecological model of teacher agency to the context of educational reform in Kazakhstan, enhancing comprehension of the practical functioning of agency. According to Biesta and Tedder (2007), agency emerges through engagement with contextual affordances; the concept of adaptive agency broadens this view by showing how teachers interpret and reshape reform goals within institutional constraints. Integrating Priestley et al.’s (2015) temporal model positions adaptive agency as a process of professional judgment enacted through everyday teaching. In this way, adaptive agency connects theory and practice, demonstrating that reform is not simply implemented but continuously co-constructed through teachers’ situated decisions and reflections.

## Practical implications

Professional programs should shift their focus from fixed methods or ready-made techniques to supporting teachers' adaptability—the ability to learn, evolve, and strengthen their capacity to interpret and adapt reforms within their contexts. Peer mentoring, collaborative lesson planning, and reflective dialogue can help teachers discuss their problems and share their ideas. These kinds of activities help teachers develop their epistemic and relational agency, which means they can learn from each other while trying out new ways of teaching. Ultimately, supporting teachers as reflective professionals rather than merely following orders makes pedagogical change more meaningful and enduring.

## Policy implications

Educational policy in Kazakhstan continues to operate in a centralized and assessment-driven way, leaving little room for teachers to act creatively or take initiative (OECD, 2018). The concept of adaptive agency emphasizes that reforms become meaningful to the extent that teachers are given space to interpret and shape them rather than simply follow directives. Policymakers should therefore view teachers not as policy executors but as agents of educational change. Creating supportive conditions—such as flexible assessment frameworks, sufficient time for collaboration, and genuine trust in teachers’ professional judgment —can help teachers enact innovation more authentically. Recognizing and developing adaptive agency at the policy level would contribute to building a reform environment that is both responsive to local realities and sustainable over time. This paper examines how teacher agency is enacted within project-based learning. The concept of adaptive agency highlights how teachers evolve, adapt, and navigate the gap between global reform ideals and local classroom realities. This framework links theoretical understanding with the actual practice of teachers in reform-oriented systems like Kazakhstan's by combining ecological and temporal perspectives (Biesta & Tedder, 2007; Priestley et al., 2015).

When teachers are recognized as adaptive agents, educational change becomes more meaningful. It comes not just from policy but also from teachers’ everyday acts of creativity, reflection, and professional judgment. In this sense, educational change is understood not as policy implementation but as a situated and evolving process shaped through teachers’ professional judgment.  To build educational systems that are both responsive to their contexts and able to support meaningful change over time, it is important to support adaptive agency.
