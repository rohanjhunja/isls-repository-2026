# Reframing resistance: How teachers reclaim judgment in professional development on controversial issues

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teacher resistance is frequently framed in educational research as an obstacle to change, yet this view obscures the potential advantages of resistance as a resource for professional learning. We reconceptualize resistance as a professional stance in which teachers assert their judgment vis-à-vis educational purposes. Using micro-ethnographic discourse analysis, we examined how resistance emerged and functioned in teacher-facilitator interaction across 47 hours of video-recorded sessions from two year-long professional development programs addressing controversial social and political classroom issues. We identified 14 resistance segments, categorized into classroom-related and program-related topics. In most cases, facilitators swiftly closed resistance, thereby hindering deliberation and reinforcing bureaucratic logic. In some cases, facilitators' legitimized resistance open ed up exploration of educational goals and assumptions. These interactional patterns carried political weight: closure reinscribed hierarchical authority, while opening positioned teachers as agentive professionals. The study contributes a framework for understanding and leveraging resistance as a potential tool for professional learning.

In contexts of controversial political and social issues, teachers face complex professional demands in classroom life, which are shaped by public polarization and competing civic values such as free expression, inclusion, and equality. Engaging such issues, as Hess & McAvoy (2015) argue, requires clarity about the purposes of schooling in a democratic society. Teachers need to navigate competing obligations: deciding whether and how to intervene in classroom discussions, balancing open inquiry and upholding inclusivity, all within the sensitivities of their communities and the expectations of their institutions.  These conditions expose tensions at the heart of teachers’ professional judgment. Professional development (PD) programs can provide spaces to address these challenges, offering structured opportunities for reflection and collective deliberation  (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017; Lefstein et al., 2020). Yet, the structure and goals of these programs often clash with teachers' lived classroom realities, raising questions about what counts as good teaching and who defines it. When teachers challenge these misalignments, acts of resistance can create openi ngs to examine educational purposes and reaffirm their professional judgment.

In educational research and in studies of PD, resistance is often framed as individual teacher behavior (Supovitz & May, 2003), and, in practitioner discourse, is often viewed as an impediment to learning rather than a potential resource. Limited attention has been given to the interactional nature of resistance: how it emerges through discourse, how facilitator roles and interactional norms shape it, and how it in turn reshapes the interaction (Gitlin & Margonis, 1995; Oreg, 2018).

We argue that when resistance is supported in teacher-facilitator interaction in PD, it can function as a learning resource. Resistance can serve as an opportunity to articulate assumptions, deliberate over purposes, and reexamine practices, thereby strengthening teachers’ professional judgment. In this framing, resistance is not a deviation from learning (Humă et al., 2023), but an interactive process with professional significance: an act through which teachers reclaim judgment and assert their role in shaping educational purposes. To understand why resistance has so often been dismissed as obstruction, it must be situated within its political struggles over the role of teacher judgment in education.

Interpreting resistance: Bureaucratic and professional logics When teachers engage with controversial social and political issues, their professional judgment is often tested. Such engagement can challenge established norms of “good teaching” and generate conflicts in teachers’ perceptions that teachers must confront  and resolve (Kolikant et al., 2020). For example, teachers must decide how to balance the norm of nonpartisanship with the ethical responsibility to help students deliberate on questions of how we should live together (Hess & McAvoy, 2015). These conflicts test teachers’ instructional choices and also challenge their underlying assumptions about their professional roles. As Brookfield (1995)  argues, when 1362 assumptions tied to professional values and norms are questioned, there is a tendency to instinctively resist such challenges, a reaction rooted in fear of what such questioning might reveal. While these psychological accounts highlight how people instinctively resist when their assumptions are challenged (Clark, 1993), such explanations do not fully explain why, at the broader macro level, resistance has so often been labeled as obstruction. Labeling resistance as obstruction frames it as interfering with a legitimate course of action, implying that teaching goals have already been defined  and positioning teachers who challenge those goals as deviating from a predetermined course. In PD contexts, these goals are often set by facilitators or program designers. This, in turn,  raises a question of authority: who define s teaching goals and what role teachers’ judgment plays in relation to those goals. Th is question of authority can be understood th rough Freidson's (2001)  competing occupational logics. Freidson identifies three such logics, bureaucratic, professional, and market, of which the first two are relevant to this study. A bureaucratic logic organizes teaching around hierarchical control and efficiency, and a prof essional logic views teachers as autonomous practitioners exercising judgment over educational ends and means (see also Labaree, 2010).

From this perspective, resistance is defined by how it is interpreted within these competing logics. Within a bureaucratic logic, resistance appears as an obstruction to the implementation of predetermined goals. Within a professional logic, the same acts can be understood as legitimate claims of professional judgment, and as efforts to preserve education’s broader purposes. Seen in this light, contemporary acts of resistance in professional development can be understood as bids to reclaim professional judgment, creating openings for teachers’ learning.

## Toward a new conceptual framework: Resistance as a professional stance in

teacher-facilitator interaction Educational reform literature often frames resistance as a problem of implementation: a gap between policy design and teacher practice (Supovitz & May, 2003). This framing is often associated with teachers’ reluctance to adopt reforms and signals noncompliance rather than professional deliberation. Others, however, suggested that reality is more complicated, specifically that resistance often reflects structural mismatches between reform agendas and classroom realities (Darling-Hammond, 2004), and teachers’ “good-sense” resistance reflects the failure of reform itself (Gitlin & Margonis, 1995) or divergent interpretations of instructional goals (Philip et al., 2024). Despite recent rethinking, much of the literature still portrays resistance primarily as obstruction (LombaPortela et al., 2022). Kennedy (2016) notes that professional development often prescribes solutions without addressing the realities of teachers’ contexts. In such cases, resistance reveals what is absent in professional development design (overlooked challenges or unacknowledged assumptions), but it is rarely examined as a productive resource to teacher learning.

Building on this critique, we reconceptualize resistance as part of the professional life of education. We view resistance as an interactional act of participation in which teachers claim a right to co-determine the conditions, meanings, or aims of their professional work. In PD, teachers’ and facilitators’ responses reveal how the competing logics of bureaucracy  and professionalism are negotiated in practice through resistance acts. Accordingly, we ask: how resistance unfolds in teacher– facilitator interaction, how it is managed and taken up, and under what conditions it fosters professional learning or is dismissed as obstruction? Conceptualizing resistance as a professional stance contributes to existing research in two ways. First, it connects how authority over teaching is organized between bureaucracy and professionalism to the micro-level of teacher–facilitator interaction, showing how those  logics are enacted in the interaction here and now. Second, it offers an analytic lens for examining how professional learning unfolds in the context of discussions of controversial issues by investigating how responses to resistance shape whether teachers’ judgments are silenced or legitimized.

## Research design

We used a micro-ethnographic approach to discourse analysis  (Bloome et al., 2005)  to examine how resistance emerged and functioned in teacher-facilitator interaction over time. Our object of analysis was segments of interaction in which teachers challenged assumptions or authority within professional development sessions, and the responses that shaped whether such challenges were hindered or developed into resources for teacher learning. This approach enabled us to capture resistance as situated in moment-to-moment interaction, while also interpreting its broader context for understanding teachers’ learning. Data were collected from two year-long PD programs in Israel (August 2024– June 2025), involving 50 teachers and four experienced facilitators. We transcribed 47 hours of video-recorded sessions, where teachers planned and reflected on classroom experiences in addressing controversial social and political issues  (Hess & McAvoy, 2015). The PD programs offered teachers a collective space to air and deliberate on these questions. 1363 This context placed teachers and facilitators on the emotional edge of their professional practice, where tensions were heightened, and assumptions unsettled. Such conditions are precisely those in which acts of resistance are likely to arise, making this setting suited for examination. Our analysis process included six steps: (1) Segmenting the data. We segmented the transcripts by topic. We then limited the analysis to whole-group discussions and excluded small-group interactions, as our interest lay in the collective negotiation of ideas within the teacher –facilitator group. (2) Identifying resistance. Within each segment, we classified resistance events (Bloome et al., 2005, p. 8) by looking for acts that (a) challenged the premise of the activity, and (b) addressed the authority being contested. We considered both verbal and nonverbal cues (e.g., vowel lengthening), drawing on repeated listening and detailed transcription. (3) For each event of resistance, we asked: Who resisted? To what? To whom was it addressed? What happened next? How did teachers and facilitators respond, and why? What was the duration of the event? (4) Categorizing resistance topics. Comparing resistance events across sessions revealed two recurring topics: classroom-related issues and professional development programs. (5) Analyzing uptake. We examined how facilitators and peers responded to resistance and how their responses shaped the ongoing discussion. We distinguished between closing responses (e.g., agenda redirection) and opening responses (e.g., legitimizing, inviting different perspectives). (6) Interpreting broader logic. We analyzed how these responses reflected broader ideological logics (bureaucratic and professional).

Ethical approval was obtained from the University Ethics Committee and the Chief Scientist of the Israeli Ministry of Education. At the outset of the program, teachers and facilitators were informed of the study’s aims, consequence. Participation was entirely voluntary, and confidentiality was assured. Occurrence and forms of resistance Across the 47 hours of data, we identified 134 topical segments, of which 14 involved resistance events (10.4%). Although relatively infrequent, these events recurred across sessions, indicating that resistance was an  ongoing interactional feature. These segments clustered around two main types of topics: Classroom-related resistance (6 events) – contesting the framings of teachers' classroom experience; and program-related resistance (8 events) – questioning the goals, structures, or authority of the professional development program itself. Both types illustrate teachers' professional judgment by raising challenges that differed from the program’s pre-planned agenda. What happened next: Closure and opening responses Across the dataset, two categories of responses were identified: closing responses, which curtailed resistance and limited opportunities for teacher learning, and opening responses, which extended resistance and created space for reflection and judgment. Both response categories occurred within each topic type: program-related resistance included 6 closing and 2 opening responses; classroom-related resistance included 4 closing and 2 opening. The trajectory of resistance, therefore, depended on how participants responded, rather than on the topic itself. This varied by role: 10 of 14 closing responses were enacted by facilitators, indicating their primary role in determining when resistance was dismissed. Opening responses, by contrast, were enacted by  facilitators and peers (2 each), showing that extending resistance to collective inquiry was not tied to formal authority.  The following two cases illustrate each response type: one program-related and one classroom-related. Resistance dismissed: A typical case of the closure response happened in Session 4. The program manager, Ori, invited the group to discuss how to define their role in leading controversial issues in school. After two teachers shared examples of classroom incidents, Lili offer ed another story and then resisted the way the program pushed for tackling controversy in the classroom: “We deal with explosive issues… we’re still working inside our education teams… and this is also a conflict that we should be taught how to manage ourselves within it”. Lili’s resistance challenged the assumption that program decisions, such as initiating role boundaries and authority, can move forward without addressing the emotional and practical complexities educators face. In doing so, she suggested a new participation frame: that PD should directly equip teachers to manage such conflicts as part of their learning when discussing controversial topics. Then facilitator Bob responded:  “With your permission, [I want] to respond briefly to the three stories. Each one of them—you could hold a whole seminar on it. I’ll start [my explanation] from the end. On February 10th, we’ve already planned and set a seminar for our staff”. By redirecting the discussion to a future seminar, Bob deferred discussion of the issues raised rather than inviting collective examination at the current time. This move functioned as a closure response, limiting opportunities for shared deliberation in the moment, preventing the group from deliberating about whether the program’s goals aligned with teachers’ immediate needs. Instead, 1364 teachers were positioned as implementers of predetermined agendas, with authority over agenda-setting shifted back to program structures. In this way, the facilitator reproduced bureaucratic logic: compliance with preplanned solutions was prioritized over exercising professional judgment. Resistance as a resource: In session 7, the facilitator Bob invited teacher Dalit to share her school’s plans for the next year. Instead, Dalit raised concern about the program losing focus: “I sometimes feel we drift from the program’s goals… We need to sharpen what makes it unique” (line 87). Teacher Yahel resisted Dalit’s concern: “If the program is the high school, then it isn’t a question—everything must shift. We’re making a school-wide shift from exam-oriented learning to values and students’ agency” (line 88). Yahel broadened the frame: the program should not be treated as a bounded set of activities but as a lever for whole-school cultural change. In doing so, Yahel contested both Dalit’s authority and the assumption that the program could be ringfenced. This resistance surfaced a deeper question: whether the PD should define the program boundaries or serve as a space for examination for broader cultural change. Dalit refined her position, reframing the program as an opportunity to articulate the school’s values and identity: "Yes. This program is our chance to clarify who we are as a school—our values, our identity" (line 89). She acknowledged both the potential and fragility of the effort, surfacing the challenge of collective ownership. Here, peer resistance pushed the discussion from program boundaries to surfacing the systemic challenge of institutionalizing cultural change. Another teacher, Lital, then translated these challenges into practice: “We gave teachers free rein to address issues close to their hearts and relevant now” (line 94). Anchoring the program in weekly homeroom lessons, she described how her team slowed the  pace and supported teachers in leading authentic discussions. Heated discussions revealed the need to support homeroom teachers, and by midyear, her team slowed the pace and granted teachers autonomy in decision-making to select issues “close to their hearts”. Teacher Ori validated this move, affirming: “That’s the starting point… even if the issue is right-wing, left-wing, explosive —that’s where the secret lies” (line 104). Ori emphasized teacher decision-making autonomy as the foundation for authentic cultural change. She framed this as the starting point for authentic cultural change, arguing that even when discussions are politically charged (“right-wing, left-wing, explosive”), authenticity and openness are the real “secret". She emphasized the need for teachers’ professional judgment: what might appear risky by granting autonomy, tolerating mistakes, or allowing politically charged views into the classroom should instead be recognized as essential conditions for value-driven instruction. She reframed risk not as something to minimize but as integral to modeling value-driven discourse for students. Her move validated Lital’s account, consolidating peer resistance into a shared vision of teacher-led, authentic practice. This example illustrates how resistance can function as a resource when taken up by peers and facilitators as a basis for collective inquiry. Through such exchanges, participants redefined controversial discussions not as external implementations to be followed, but as professional practices requiring judgment and negotiation. This study demonstrates that acts of resistance, when taken up in interaction, can create openings for strengthening teachers professionally and engaging them actively with change. Our analysis shows that the impact of resistance depended on how it was managed, that is, how facilitators and peers address ed resistance -- as a problem to be contained or as a contribution to be explored. We demonstrated both: in Session 4, Lili’s challenge to the program’s goals was redirected to a future seminar, implicitly framing the issue as deferred rather than open to collective examination. This response reflected a bureaucratic logic that prioritizes adherence to predetermined plans and procedural control, closing in-the-moment collective examination and constraining teachers’ professional judgment. In Session 7, in contrast, Dalit’s concern about program focus was expanded by peers, refined into questions of school identity, and validated by the facilitator. Here, bureaucratic logic was balanced with recognition of teachers’ authority, enabling resistance to generate inquiry and collective ownership. These cases demonstrate how the political struggle was enacted turn by turn: closure reinscribed bureaucratic  logic, while opening legitimized teachers’ professional judgment. These interactional patterns also carried political weight. When facilitators closed resistance by redirecting to preplanned agendas, teachers’ claims to authority were curtailed; when facilitators or peers elaborated resistance, teachers’ voices shaped the direction of examination. In this sense, resistance was not only an interactional move but a political act: teachers asserted their authority to define educational purposes in contexts where bureaucratic logics positioned them as implementers. Facilitator responses were likewise political: closure reinscribed hierarchical authority, while opening legitimized teachers’ contributions as central to the professional development process.
