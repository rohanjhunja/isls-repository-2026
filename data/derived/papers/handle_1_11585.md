# Weaving Knowledges: Digital Technology Pedagogy as Situated Community Practices

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This ethnographic study explores how Indigenous teachers and communities in Oaxaca, Mexico, design digital pedagogies that are grounded in local social, cultural, and linguistic knowledge systems. These approaches challenge dominant educational technology frameworks by reimagining digital pedagogies as situated, relational, and community-centered practices, challenging assumptions that frame Indigeneity as incompatible with technological innovation. This research contributes to conversations on the politics of learning with digital technologies by advancing place-based, community-grounded pedagogical designs.

## Background and research goals

The rapid development and institutionalization of digital technologies has profound implications for e ducation, both by offering new possibilities and posing new c hallenges for teaching and learning. As digital tec hnologies become increasingly ubiquitous in classrooms worldwide, a deeper understanding of alternative teaching practices with digital technologies is urgently needed (Garci a & Philip, 2018). This urgency is heightened as ne wer technologies, including digital learning platforms,  artificial intell igence (AI), and surveillance technologies, become part of dominant educational technology fram eworks and carceral deployments that reinforce ineq uities and erase local knowledge systems (de Roock, 2021).  These deployments prioritize efficiency, complianc e, and control, shaping everyday teaching practices in way s that contribute to the disproportionate policing and surveillance of marginalized students, teachers, and their everyday curricular and pedagogical decisions (Aguilera & Salazar, 2023; LeBlanc et al., 2023).

As a former substitute teacher in the San Francisco  Bay Area, I frequently encountered teaching practices that centered digitized forms of schooling, such as relying on personalized language learnin g platforms to teach English Language Learners, which  often failed to address students’ educational needs  (Watkins, 2018). In contrast, Indigenous teachers in Oaxaca, Mexico, take a different approach to digital technology pedagogy, one grounded in local contexts and community needs. Indigenous teachers in Oaxaca leverage digital technologies to create localized community-oriented educational materials, such as digital sto ries in local languages, that reflect the cultural and linguistic practices of their stud ents' communities. Here, digital technology pedago gies are designed to meet community needs, encourage interge nerational learning, and sustain cultural and lingu istic practices. These forms of using, learning about, and designing teaching practices with digital technol ogies not only challenge dominant educational technology frameworks but also advance technological imaginaries that are rooted in Indigenous perspectives, centering community values and needs. This study addresses the underexplored intersection  of Indigenous knowledge systems and digital technology, offering alternative possibilities for teaching and learning with digital tools. It shows how Indigenous teachers and communities in Oaxaca reshape digital technologies in ways that reflect and uphol d local cultural, linguistic, and social contexts. This approach reim agines the potential of digital technology pedagogy  to include relational, intergenerational,  and community-centered practices that aim toward more just and culturally thriving futures (Bang, 2020). Through ethnographic research in Indigenous educational settings, this work explores how these teachers use digital tools toward liberatory ends, challenging dominant educational technology frameworks and design processes that prioritize efficiency and control.

## Conceptual framework

Over the last 20 years, digital technologies have b ecome deeply embedded in our day-to-day realities; however, they are often perceived as neutral tools, lacking cultural or ecological implications (de Roock, 2021). This perception overlooks the complex networks that enta ngle these technologies with the people, environmen ts, and histories that enable their existence (Hornborg, 20 22). Indigenous knowledge systems challenge this tr aditional view by recognizing the mutual dependence of all elements of life, including technology (Kimmerer, 2013). This perspective allows us to reimagine digital technolo gies as living entities embedded within local conte xts: lands, communities, and cultural practices. Thus, I concep tualize digital technology pedagogies  (see Figure 1) as practices that align with and draw from community k nowledge systems by foregrounding ethical, relation al, and future-oriented approaches to teaching and learning (Bang,  2020). This framework highlights the daily practic es and decisions of Indigenous  peoples and allies, who actively draw upon and exte nd Indigenous knowledge systems to design pedagogies while navigating the c onstraints of digital technologies. Design, in this  context, is a process of continuous negotiation involving multi ple actors, human and non-human, in learning, sensemaking, and decision-making, developed through deep engagement with dive rse ways of understanding the world (Escobar, 2018). This approach enables teachers to make principled decisions in response to the challe nges and opportunities they encounter. It acknowledges and affirms multiple ways of knowing and being, each carrying its own legitimacy and significance. Rather than offering corrective solutions to issues within dominant educational technology frameworks, these designs create spaces where diverse ontologies and epistemologies can coexist and influence one another (Vossoughi et al., 2023; Warr en et al., 2020). This approach offers a new way of  telling stories, emphasizing the importance of designing po litical, ethical, and relational digital technology  pedagogies in teacher education that stand in solidarity with all communities. ## Study design

The analysis presented in this poster is part of my  ongoing dissertation research, which is currently in the data collection phase. This study draws on ethnographic and co-design methods to look at how five teachers and fiv e community members in Oaxaca design, learn, and make  sense of these tools in both classroom and communi ty contexts. Ethnography enables in-depth observation of pedagogical practice, while co-design ensures participants actively shape the research process and its interpr etation. Grounded in decolonial and solidarity-driven research practices (Smith, 2021; Philip et al., 2022), this study centers mutual learning through ongoing, iter ative conversations, co-analysis sessions, and sustained engagement with pa rticipants. Participants were sele cted for their daily use of digital technologies and work ac ross both rural and urban communities, to reflect O axaca’s cultural, linguistic, and educational diversity. Wh ile some teachers work in their home communities, m ost work in communities where cultural and linguistic practices may differ. Data collection includes weekly semi-structured interviews, video-recorded classroom observations, and informal conve rsations that document how participants design, use, and make sense of digital tools.

## Preliminary findings and future directions

This study contributes to the learning sciences by examining how digital technologies are used in ways that situate these tools within local social, cultural, and ling uistic contexts. It offers a framework for rethinki ng digital technology pedagogy by demonstrating how participants use digital tools to engage with, sustain, and extend local knowledge systems through culturally grounded, relational, and intergenerational practices. Preliminary findings suggest that when digital technology pedagogies emerge from community contexts, they create more meaningful opportunities for students to learn, create, and pa rticipate in intergenerational learning, local lang uage revitalization, and everyday community life. For ex ample, teachers in Oaxaca have collaborated wi th youth and elders to transcribe oral stories into written form  using offline computers, resulting in digital read ing materials and computer classes in local Indigenous languages (e.g., Díaz Robles, 2021). These pedagogical design s reflect place-based, re lational, and storied approaches to digital technol ogy, positioning teachers, students, and community members as co-designers and co-theorists of digital pedagogies that draw on local social, cultural, linguistic, and epistemic practices. While these in sights highlight the potential of digital technology p edagogies as situated, community-grounded practices, ongoing analysis will examine how these approaches may challenge, or at times unintentionally reproduce, dominant educational technology frameworks.
