# Inquiry Script Modeling: A Formalism for Scripting and Orchestration of Collective Inquiry

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper introduces Inquiry Script Modeling (ISM), a formalism designed to represent and operationalize inquiry-oriented learning workflows in classrooms that adopt a learning community approach. Drawing on prior CSCL research, including process modeling and orchestration frameworks, ISM supports interpretability, temporal sequencing, and adaptability. This paper presents ISM using a use case focused on AI and disinformation, to show how such formalisms can help teachers to design, communicate, and dynamically manage complex collaborative learning scripts.

Keywords: Scripting, Orchestration, Collective Inquiry, Formalism Standardized formalisms such as chemical notation or calculus, accelerate scientific progress by enabling clear theory-building and collaborative discourse (Abe, 2012). The learning sciences, however, lack such universal representations for pedagogical designs. While CSCL "scripts" define learner interactions and roles (Kollar et al., 2006), and frameworks like IMS-LD attempted to standardize these processes across contexts (Koper & Olivier, 2004), a gap remains in modeling guidance and orchestration across  diverse activity types (Fischer & Slotta, 2013).

The quest to formalize learning processes has consistently struggled to balance computational rigor with conceptual clarity. Early standards like IMS-LD attempted platform-agnostic modeling but were often critiqued for technical complexity and an inability  to capture the dynamic, social nature of collaboration (Koper & Tattersall, 2005; Hernández -Leo et al., 2005). This highlights a persistent challenge: opaque formalisms offer limited utility if they cannot be interpreted by the stakeholders they are meant to serve. While CSCL frameworks-from micro-level interaction moves to macro-level orchestration-have defined essential building blocks like

roles and resources (Harrer et al., 2007; Dillenbourg, 2013), these representations are never truly "agnostic." Each carries epistemic commitments that prioritize specific pedagogical values, such as rigid task sequencing over adaptive roles. Even breakthroughs like Orchestration Graphs often remain too coarse for complex activity designs. Shifting toward a Learning Communities framework, our work develops granular, machine-readable scripts that explicitly reflect the theoretical commitments of collective inquiry. This structured approach aims to enhance the shareability and execution of designs while safeguarding human agency and addressing the orchestrational demands of technology-enhanced environments. The present work begins with the premise that educators, including researchers and practitioners, will benefit from representational tools that are both formal and usable: formal enough to be machine-readable and support computational orchestration, yet usable enough to be meaningful and adaptable for educators in practice. In response to this fundamental tension, we have developed Inquiry Script Modeling (ISM) as a formalism that enables educators (including researchers, who are an important user group for ISM) to specify inquiry-oriented learning designs, communicate them effectively to other practitioners, researchers or system designers, and adapt them dialogically during the design process. Our guiding research question is: What elements should be included within a scripting formalism to enable educational researchers and practitioners to communicate about, and co-develop curricular designs, relating to inquiry-oriented pedagogy? Methodological approach Using a Design-Based Research (DBR) methodology, this study details the iterative development of the Inquiry Script Modeling (ISM) as both a theoretical outcome and a functional tool for computational orchestration (Barab & Squire, 2004). A multidisciplinary team of learning scientists, AI researchers, and software engineers conducted two primary design cycles. The first cycle involved a comparative analysis of CSCL scripting, Petri Nets, and Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) to identify core modeling primitives (e.g., control flow, transitions, and temporal 534 sequencing). In the second cycle, these constructs were operationalized through an authentic inquiry use case regarding misinformation, leading to the refined node taxonomies and AI-supported orchestration principles presented in Figure 1 and Table 2. This  DBR phase establishes ISM’s conceptual foundation, derived from systematic analysis and iterative refinement.

An analysis of core functions across formalisms To inform the development of ISM, we analyzed five formalisms across computer science, business, and the learning sciences: Process Modeling, Petri Nets, Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs), Orchestration Graphs, and CSCL scripting models.  As shown in Table 1, these frameworks offer complementary strengths for modeling complex workflows but vary significantly in their pedagogical and computational utility. Petri Nets and DAGs provide mathematical rigor for formalizing control flow, concurrency, and dependencies, making them ideal for scalable, machine-executable workflows. However, they lack intrinsic support for pedagogical semantics, such as social roles, adaptive feedback, or learner agency. Conversely, Orchestration Graphs and CSCL scripts are highly expressive of pedagogical intent and social-plane interactions (individual, group, and class levels) but often lack the structural constraints required for direct computational execution (Dillenbourg, 2013; Harrer et al., 2007). Our analysis suggests that while the CSCL community has proposed tailored languages (e.g., Miao et al., 2005), limited uptake persists due to the difficulty of balancing human readability with machine interpretability.  ISM is designed to bridge this gap, synthesizing the structural clarity of Petri Nets and DAGs with the social coordination and instructional richness of Orchestration Graphs. 536 ISM: design considerations The Inquiry Script Modeling (ISM) formalism is built upon five foundational design pillars that balance computational rigor with pedagogical usability. Balancing Simplicity and Complexity  navigates the trade-off where detailed models reveal essential workflows but risk overwhelming users while overly simple diagrams may miss key teaching or timing relationships. ISM addresses this through selective abstraction and visual diversity, leveraging Colored Petri Nets and Business Process Model notation with varied shapes and colors to distinguish actors and activities. Purple ovals represent students, pink ovals represent teachers, and yellow circles represent AI agents. Solid lines denote control flow while dashed lines denote data flow. Vertical ordering establi shes temporal sequencing and labels clarify logic for actions and transitions, ensuring the visualization captures complex processes while remaining interpretable and actionable (Möller et al., 2025). Temporal Sequencing and Flow Distinction is critical for effective orchestration, requiring a clear separation of control flow (the sequence and timing of actions) from data flow (the movement and transformation of learning artifacts into shared knowledge). Because educational workflows involve complex dependencies between human and computational actors, ISM provides teachers a "mastery" of timing by seeing real-time statuses of student devices and ongoing activities (Dillenbourg & Jermann, 2010). This ensures interventions occur at the right moment to support engagement. ISM utilizes AI agents to automate high-load tasks such as analyzing submitted examples to generate thematic categories, tagging individual data points, and updating central tables with a "theme ID." This automation reduces teacher cognitive load and ensures timely distribution of content without creating bottlenecks (Bergmann & Gil, 2014). Flexibility and Adaptability requires balancing dynamic orchestration with pedagogical coherence (Dillenbourg & Jermann, 2010). Our design minimizes extrinsic constraints originating from technical limitations while preserving intrinsic constraints or core pedagogical rules to ensure instructional intent is maintained (Dillenbourg & Tchounikine, 2007). In the ISM framework, AI agents handle repetitive and computationally intensive tasks such as data clustering and content delivery while teachers retain "driver" status, controlling sequencing and timing to avoid the pitfalls of overscripting (Harrer & Lingnau, 2016). This hybrid approach ensures adaptability, allowing teachers to take ownership of the script and adjust it based on evolving classroom contexts (Tchounikine, 2011). For instance, AI agents notify teachers of student progress but the human instructor decides when to advance the activity. Making Pedagogical Topologies Visible  addresses the sociomaterial connections among users, devices, displays, and content (Castañeda et al., 2024). ISM clarifies this ontology by differentiating between personal student devices and shared group displays while labeling the specific content such as a "form" or "page" on each. This dual-layer visibility allows teachers to detect participation bottlenecks or synchronization mismatches such as a group display showing outdated content before a result appears. This enhances teacher agency by enabling targeted adjustments like redistributing content or shifting activities between device types (Liu et al., 2023). Integrating Physical, Social, and Curricular Spaces leverages the HCI concept of the ambient interface to extend the u ser interface to the whole environment. By orchestrating across individual learner devices, group collaboration at tables, and whole-class activities mediated by large shared displays, ISM captures how the curricular space (learning objectives and inquiry tasks) intersects with physical devices and social interactions. This layered representation provides a coherent framework for embodied, situated interaction within a collective inquiry context.

This paper has examined the challenges and opportunities of formalizing inquiry-oriented learning designs, with a focus on learning communities frameworks. By reviewing prior formalisms from learning sciences, computer science, and business process managem ent, we highlighted the trade-offs between formal rigor, interpretability, and pedagogical usability. Our proposed ISM formalism demonstrates how complex learning workflows can be made visible, interpretable, and actionable for both educators and computati onal systems. A key contribution of this work is its theoretical saliency: ISM makes learning communities elements, knowledge building, inquiry scaffolding, and shared knowledge artifacts, visible, allowing designers and teachers to reason about and reflec t on how these elements are enacted in practice (Peters, 2011; Acosta & Slotta, 2018). It is important to note that at this stage, ISM is still in the design and development phase. We do not yet offer a fully machine-readable implementation; the formalism remains under active research, focusing on interpretability, usability, and the visualization of learning communities elements.
