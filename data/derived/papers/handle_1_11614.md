# Balancing Infrastructural Constraints and Learning Goals during the Redesign of Games

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite the potential benefits of game-based learning, infrastructural constraints often prevent educators from introducing it in classrooms. We were interested in understanding how student teachers balance learning objectives and infrastructural constraints when redesigning games for secondary mathematics. Preliminary findings suggest that student teachers might sometimes lead their redesign efforts with those constraints, rather than with learning goals. This has implications for how teacher training programs can empower and support student teachers’ capacity for instructional innovation.

## Background

Game-based learning has been of interest to the learning sciences commu nity for many years because of the flexibility, creativity, and opportunities it can afford student s (Gee, 2005). In historically challenging academic topics such as science or mathematics, game-based learning (GBL) has been shown to increase student motivation, support learning, and cultivate communication (Barab et al., 2009; Dishon & Kafai, 2020; Divjak & Tomić, 2011). Despite these promising findings, widespread adopti on of GBL remains a challenge, particularly in secondary mathematics classrooms  (Matic et al., 2023). Implementing GBL requires an often radical shift in how most educational systems are currently organized, and such a shift is costly; not only financially, but also in terms of the time investment it would require of various stakeholders (Gee, 2005). When implementing GBL in classrooms, many teachers engage in similar types of teaching activities pre-, during, and post-gameplay. These activities often include gameplay training, content scaffolding, providing students with technical assistance, and debr iefing sessions (Bado, 2022). However, how exactly each of these activities might look like in a classroom can vary a lot. Class schedules, along with constraints in i nfrastructure, are two important factors that can influence the wa y different teachers choose to incorporate GBL acti vities in their classrooms-for example when making choices about how to struct ure and organize student groupwork, or whether to create stand-alone or consecutive lessons (Marklund & Taylor, 2016). Some of the challenges teachers often mention regarding  GBL implementation a re: (i) effective implementation of games, (ii) using technology, (ii i) obtaining games, and (iv) the current educationa l system (Razak et al., 2012; Watson et al., 2016). In particular, challenges with effective implementa tion of GBL were more often a concern of secondary school teachers and teachers who had no prior experience using games in their classrooms (Watson et al., 2016). Pre-service and early career teachers were also more li kely to cite a lack of supporting materials and the fixed class schedules as barriers to GBL classroom implementation, while more experienced teachers were more likely to perceive inflexibility of a curriculum as a barrier to implementing GBL (Baek, 2008). These findings suggest that teachers with more teaching experience  might feel more confident in their abilities to implement GBL in their classroom s, but less empowered due to long-established systemic constraints. On the other hand, teachers with few y ears of teaching experience might be more open to t rying to make the established educational system work for GBL in their (future) classrooms, as long as they feel like they have adequate time and access to sufficient GBL teaching materials. Prior research on  teacher training initiatives that tried to support teachers with the enactment of GBL activities in their classrooms showed that such initiatives can enhance teachers’ competencies to select, evaluate, and utilize GBL as an instructional tool (Meletiou-Mavrotheris & Prodromou, 2016). However, Kenny and McDaniel (2011)  found that teachers often preferred games without steep learning curves, as t hey considered those games easier to implement. Given the time and  resource constraints teachers are typically working under, their preference for shorter, simpler games is not surprising. Still, deprioritizing the use of complex games in classrooms could result in missed opportunities for student engagement and lea rning (Foster & Shah, 2020). We were interested in better understanding how student  teachers, who might be more open to experimenting with GBL, might try to balance learning goals and infrastructural constraints when redesigning a game for use in their classrooms. Our study was guided by the question: How might student teachers  balance learning goals and infrastructural constraints  when collaboratively redesigning a game  for use in secondary mathematics classrooms?

## The current study and preliminary findings

Given our interests in the work of a group and in the ways infrastructu ral constraints might influence that work, we employed the “learning in activity” framework (Greeno & Engeström, 2014). This allowed us to study the complex systems in which activities of (i) redesigning and (ii) using a game took place, while considering how components of the systems might interact with each other. In particular, we looked at a subset of the group’s object in the first activity system (i.e., the learning goals they wanted to support  with their game), and (some of) the anticipated rules (e.g., timetables, budgets) in the system in which they envisioned their games being used.

The study was conducted in the context of an undergraduate cour se on GBL, as part of a mathematics teacher training program in a university in central Europe. In the course, students played some tabletop and digital games (Carcassonne, Logifaces, The Sims 4, Kahoot! Algebra 12+, Hyperbolica, Prodigy Math, Escape from the aliens in outer space), reflected on their experiences, a nd discussed the learning potential of the games. In-class gameplay sessions were followed by short readings that further discussed, or expanded  upon topics covered in class (e.g. Avraamidou, 2015; Capaldi & Kolba, 2017; Gierus et al., 2024). During the second half of the course, students worked in groups (3-4 students per group) to redesign a game and create a lesson plan for teaching a topic of their choice. Students presented their projects during a poster session at the end of the semester. We invited all e nrolled students and 21 consented to participate in the research study. We collected (i) survey responses examining participants’ views on games and learning, (ii) written reflections on their experiences learning about GBL, their readiness to implement GBL in their classrooms and goals for doing so, (iii) exit tickets regarding early work on their final project, (iv) lesson plans and game materials, and (v) final poster s. We also invited participants for interview s, to discuss competencies development and decision-making during collaborative project work; three participants agreed to be interviewed and interviews lasted about one hour. To analyze how student teachers in our study balanced infrastructural constraints with learning goals for their students, we looked at participants’ lesson plans, exit tickets, final posters, and interview data. Informed by the literature (Baek, 2008; de Freitas, 2006; Razak et al., 2012; Watson et al., 2016), we first developed a coding rubric that focused on infrastructural constraints (i.e., curriculum constraints, time constraints, lack of GBL resources, financial cost, and technical infrastructure). We employed simultaneous and structural coding (Saldaña, 2015) at the sentence level to code interview transcripts and exit tickets for presence of such constraints. Both authors coded the full transcript independently first, and then met and collaboratively decided on final codes. Then, the first author used open coding and examined the lesson plans and final poster for explicitly stated learning goals. We share some preliminary findings based on data from one interview (David, pseudonym) and his group. David shared that his group was in alignment from t he very beginning. One of their biggest concerns was time constraints (mentioned n=12 in the interview), and choosing which game to modify was also influenced by a concern for a lack of GBL resources. Even though the group had initially discussed games they had played in the course, they chose to work with a game one of them had previously used in their classroom: David:  We wanted something in the direction of a practice game, and just, orienting yourself to something that has been used before, you can better estimate how long it will take, and that was a big issue for us. Additionally, one of the reasons for choosing to design a practice game was curriculum constraints: David:  All three of us had the feeling that this [i.e. exploratory games] was more suitable for an elective subject or afternoon care or something. His group developed “Mathingo,” a practice game inspired by Bingo that invited students to firs t create and then simplify algebraic expressions.  The accompanying lesson plan was for a single class period, and the game was meant to support students with (i) term simplification, (ii) backward thinking, (i ii) understanding the function of neutral elements (0 and 1), and (iv) understanding additive, commutative and distributed properties. Data analysis is ongoing, and preliminary findings from one group suggest that student teachers may sometimes lead a game redesign process for math lea rning based primarily on infrastructural constraint s rather than target learning goals-especially if one of those constraints is a major concern for all members of a group. As we continue to examine more groups, we expect our findings to shed light on different decision-making processes mathematics student teachers might follow when redesigning games for le arning. Better understanding these processes has implications for how we design teacher training programs that not only empower student teachers to experiment with instructional innovations, but equip them with the necessary skills, confide nce and resources to do so within the constraints of formal learning environments.
