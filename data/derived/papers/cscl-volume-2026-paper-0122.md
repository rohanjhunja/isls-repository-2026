# Calling, Collecting, and Transformation: Playful Artifacts as Pivots for Joint Activity and Game Design in a Collaborative VR Design Studio

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines how artifacts and environmental features mediate collaborative design in immersive VR. In a six-week VR collaborative studio, six designers co-created an LGBTQ+ mini-golf game. By triangulating video recordings with reflective journals, we found a stimulus-to-transformation design process that emerged from the perceptual noticing of collectibles, evolving into shared ideation and transforming game mechanics. Situated within activity theory, the study contributes process-oriented methods for researching collaborative VR design.

Design work often begins with exploration, involving the gathering of references, observation of environments, and examination of materials that can spark inspiration and guide ideation (Chan et al., 2015). Virtual reality (VR) expands this exploratory dimension by enabling designers to step into shared, three-dimensional spaces, where they can co-experience environments and examine design ideas in real time (Xia et al., 2018). Where screen-based collaboration confines teams to mouse clicks and keyboard input, VR environments enable full-body movement, direct object manipulation, and shared spatial presence, which fundamentally transform how teams generate and evolve ideas together (Dorta et al., 2016; Freeman et al., 2022; Paré et al., 2019). These affordances position VR not only as a technical tool but as a unique medium for collaborative design inquiry. However, previous VR studies predominantly centered on learning outcomes (e.g., knowledge gains), and we have a limited understanding of how environmental  features and artifacts mediate early-stage design ideation and collective sensemaking (Suthers, 2006).

To unpack the dynamics of learners' interactions with different elements in the collaborative VR space, we draw on activity theory (Engeström, 1987). Within activity theory, we conceptualize the “subject” as the individual player within the VR collaborative studio community, while “tools” are the virtual manipulable artifacts and environmental cues (e.g., virtual collectables) present in the space. In addition, the “object” refers to players’ actions of collecting these virtual items. For the “division of labor”, players searched for the collectibles and called out their locations to other players to direct everyone to the found item(s), thus organizing the group’s activity. At the end of their VR field trip, players could exchange their collected items for rewards based on their choices, which aligns with the “rule” element in the activity system. Moreover, to understand how meaning evolves within the system, we integrate the concept of “expansive learning,” where systemic contradictions drive the transformation of the activity system intonew forms of practice (Engeström & Sannino, 2010). Furthermore, we draw on the concept of “pivots” (Vygotsky, 1967) to conceptualize how learners separate abstract meaning from immediate physical objects during this transformation. Guided by these lenses, we then ask (1) How do artifacts and environmental features in a collaborative VR design studio shape players’ transformation of game design mechanics to a future game design context?

This case study (Yin, 2011) was embedded in a large six-week participatory design project from July to August 2025, exploring how six participants (aged 25– 42; a mix of graduate students and VR professionals) collaboratively designed a U.S. LGBTQ+ mini-golf game. Across nine three-hour sessions, participants engaged in VR field trips (e.g., VRChat, Walkabout Mini Golf) to explore pre-designed virtual environments and VR prototyping tools like ShapesXR for design. Data sources included video recordings and 44 individual reflective journals documenting their learning and design trajectories. We then adopted two rounds of qualitative analytic coding (Emerson et al., 2011). First, we coded participants’ journals line-by-line to identify initial codes based on participants’ ideas or issues they suggested. Second, we conducted a fine-grained analysis, narrowing the codes toward categories we identified as of specific interest, i.e., environmental cuing (e.g., spatial features, game mechanics, and design idea generation (e.g., naming an idea out loud, drawing a design). Through these inductive, iterative processes, we identified recurring patterns (e.g., stimulus, transformation) that supported players’ design 686 ideas, revealing how players’ interactions with artifacts and environmental features led to coordinated activity and evolving future design concepts.

Our analysis reveals how salient environmental features triggered an expansive learning cycle, transforming an abstract stimulus into a design mechanism. Triangulating video data with reflective journals, here, we showcase a representative episode involving two participants, Jazlyn and Skye, during a VR world exploration (i.e., Spirit of the Sea) (see Figure 1). For example, when Skye navigated toward a hidden collectible object (crab coins) in the VR space, she called out her discovery to direct the whole group’s joint attention. This spontaneous division of labor transformed individual perception into a shared group action (collect coins). As Jazlyn corroborated this scenario in her journal, she noted that “we all searched for glowing or hidden crab coins together,” highlighting the visual salience of the coins acted as a mediated tool. Within this activity system, a contradiction emerged between the initial perception of the coins as mere decorative “environmental stimuli” and their “potential functional value.” This contradiction drove the system’s expansive transformation when the participants engaged with the mini-golf game’s rules. Jazlyn reflected that the coins “could be traded in for actions like jumping abilities (in the game design), which added meaning and fun to collecting them.” This game transformed the virtual objects from decorative collectibles in the environment to consequential, playful objects with exchange value. The play process of transforming an object allowed for the object to be conceptualized abstractly as a game mechanic. This builds on earlier work by Paré et al. (2019), showing how playful interactions with virtual artifacts in VR can serve as pivots for learners between lived experience and future, imagined designs. In this case, the virtual coin served as a “pivot” (Vygotsky, 1967, p.13), supporting participants to separate the abstract concept of a reward mechanism from the specific virtual object. Participants then successfully transformed this newly negotiated meaning into a cooperative progression-andreward system for prototyping their LGBTQ+ mini-golf design in ShapeXR. This illustrates how environmental affordances and interactions in shared spaces facilitate participants’ thinking with design mechanics. The coordination of joint activity in a community (calling out, finding coins) around the game rules (collect coins to gain rewards) then leads to shared meaning-making as participants reflect on this activity system to transform into their design of the mini-golf game.

Our findings reveal that meaning-making in VR emerges as a cyclical ecology of perception, action, and reflection, me diated by the activity system. The stimulus-to-transformation pathway aligned with the concept of expansive learning, driven by systemic contradictions regarding the utility of virtual objects. Participants did not simply consume content. They moved from noticing an environmental stimulus (a glowing coin) to ideating on the game mechanic (the exchange value of collectibles) and transforming it into a future game design context (the LGBTQ+ mini-golf game). These VR artifacts and environment designs thus also served as Vygotskian pivots that reconfigured the group’s division of labor (calling out to the group) and game rules (exchange value), thereby scaffolding collective creativity in the activity system.
