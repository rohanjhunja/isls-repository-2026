# Using Participatory Software Design to Reconfigure Traditional Power Asymmetries Between Dominant School Systems and Nondominant Families

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We describe an iterative participatory development of a software application based on Ishimaru’s (2019) model of equitable collaboration between nondominant families and dominant school systems. We share (1) findings from our qualitative analysis and collaborative software design with two family focus groups, (2) the design process that led us to the development of a family notebook to balance traditional power asymmetries, and (3) the current version of the software design.

## Introduction

In this paper we present the current results of an iterative participatory-design research project (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) and social design experiment (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016). Our collaborative design centered nondominant families as experts (Barajas-López & Ishimaru, 2020) in the design of a software application aimed at facilitating communication between families and early childhood settings during their children’s behavior support process. Given the historical inequities between nondominant families and dominant school systems, our design was grounded in Ishimaru’s (2019ab) work on equitable collaborations in an attempt to address the power asymmetries between nondominant families and dominant school systems.

## Participants

We collaborated on the software design with 10 fami lies. The family participants spoke English and Spa nish. They identified as white, Black, Latino, and Hispanic. Nearly all of their children had an individualized behavior support plan and several had already been suspended or expelled from preschool or other early learning settings. The final design of the software application was grounded in the expertise of Black and Latino families.

## Methods

As part of our participatory design research proces s, we conducted and recorded data from two differen t focus groups (FG1 and FG2 hereafter); those involved qual itative, survey, and informal methodologies as part  of our initial data collection. We recorded, transcribed, and coded conversations between family members as w ell as collected design artifacts. As part of our design p rocess, we asked families to respond in a semi-stru ctured interview and discussion format to questions about their partnerships in early childhood programs. The families made it clear to us early on that given their inequ itable experiences with school systems, the softwar e had to facilitate communication and position families as equal participators in their children’s support process. Talking about their past and present experiences families e xpressed feelings of shame, fear, and guilt when he aring negative and judgmental communication about their c hild. Thus, they emphasized the importance of posit ive, reciprocal, and human centered communication, i.e.,  communication that is compassionate, respectful, a nd strengths-based. Families also expressed a clear de sire to learn about their child’s behavior and the behavior support process from professionals in their child’s early learning program, while also being able to provide their own expert feedback to program staff.  Lastly, fami lies wanted to contribute to and be acknowledged as  part of their child’s support process. When asked questions  about technology, families requested a practical communication system allowing for transparency and quick access to their child’s process. After analyzing the initial data from FG1, we set out to translate our findings into several graphic versions. We arrived at a family-school notebook as a design which addressed reciprocity, family contribution as well as access and transparency. To confirm whether we corr ectly interpreted the family voices, we brought the  first family notebook design a few months later back to the families from FG1 for member check as well as to  some new families. We asked our FG2 to further help clar ify and improve on the design. For our design activ ity with families in FG2, we printed out the first family notebook design and then asked families to write, mark, and place stickers indicating questions, approval, or disapproval. ## Results

Nondominant families in the focus groups were much more likely to share negative experiences about the ir relationships with their children’s early learning programs. Their experiences had a profound impact on our design thinking. Two questions that arose in response were, (1) how can software design help ameliorate the trauma and stress of nondominant families in dominant school s ystems? And (2) how and what aspects of the traditi onal power asymmetries could we shift (or balance) through collaborative software design and architecture? In response to our first question, the main aspect of the family notebook that attempted to ameliorate stress was an intentional slowing down of the communication process. Unlike common formats of communication made available through messaging apps (i.e., immediate, instant, and dialogic), the design of the note book asks families and teachers to leave notes for each other asynchronously (family members can also communicate with each other). Once logged in, for a family member to see teacher notes and vice versa, family members must leave their side of the notebook by toggling a switch to the teacher’s side of the notebook.

To address the notion of power in our design, we an alyzed our qualitative data through the lens of Ishimaru’s (2019a) framework of equitable collaboration. A major feature that addresses the main dimensions of equitable collaboration strategies allows family members to invite other family or co mmunity members e.g., advocates, translators, or community elders to be part of the family notebook with access to all documentation as well as the ability to communicate with the teacher s and each other. The ability of families to add as  many members of their family, friends, advocates, or community members to the notebook as needed is directly linked to existent power asymmetries. This design move inh erently shifts (1) the intervention level from individual/parent/student to a collective expertise, family, and community level. (2) Moreover, the shared space created by the notebook shifts the unilateral power  held traditionally by school systems towards share d and relational power by making transparent the documentation of the child’s support process to the families and those members of the notebook that families choose to add. In the same context, (3) the notebook design shif ts the directionality of possible partnerships between sch ools and families from a historically unidirectiona l to a reciprocal mode of interaction.

## Conclusions

The family notebook design is currently in its next phase of user testing. There were two main design components that we translated from the input by our family exp erts into a core feature of the notebook which addr esses equitable collaborations. The one feature we wanted to focus on in this short paper was the ability of families to add as many collaborators to their notebook as they wanted. It is a subtle feature, not explicit, yet one that directly engages with the equally subtle violent ideologies present in learning settings. Equally subtle, the concept of place as understood by cultural and human geographers (e.g., Cresswell, 2014; Tuan, 1977), i.e., “produced by practice that adheres to (ideological) beliefs about what is  the appropriate thing to do” (Cresswell, 1996). Th e notion of place was an everpresent heuristic in the transformation of power asymmetries in an intermediate space between families and learning settings, i.e., the notebook, connected to a dominant system of education, yet with its own specific set of rules. In the case of our social de sign experiment, those rules were (re)defined by a software architecture designed by those who occupy nondominant positions on the power spectrum.
