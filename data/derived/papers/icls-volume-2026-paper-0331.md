# Wordless Books as Tools for Language, Identity, and Voice

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper is led by a dual language kindergarten teacher and presents insights on a multi-year participatory research study on wordless books. We discuss how wordless books can support emergent dual language learners by fostering literacy through storyte lling and creative expression. The paper highlights a case study on how wordless books can empower children to see themselves as competent readers. We argue for a broader definition of literacy that values and includes students’ linguistic and cultural resources, and position wordless books as a tool for equitable access to reading for all children. Objectives of our practice-oriented paper Over the past three years, Arlen and Christine, a dual language kindergarten teacher and a university researcher, have collaborated on a participatory design-based research study (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) examining the potential of wordless books for young dual language learners. Our paper examines how wordless books can serve as a powerful tool for dual language literacy, empowering young learners to take agency in their oral storytelling and reading. When we think of literacy instruction in primary grade levels, it tends to focus solely on phonics, accuracy, and decoding words. This experience can provide a narrow view of literacy, and it views children as passive recipients rather than active meaning makers (Freire & Slover, 1983). It also doesn’t leave ro om for student-driven creative expression, like storytelling. For emergent dual language learners, the act of learning to read in a new language can present an extra level of pressure, setting expectations that they have to somehow quickly master both languages. Wordless picture books offer a different kind of entry point into literacy. It can remove the pressure to perform and instead invite children to build their own narratives using a variety of resources like language, tone, gestures, and imagination. Our paper shows wordless books as a resource to support children’s identities as lifelong readers and empower them by giving children agency in their storytelling. We argue that we need to move away from deficit thinking by highlighting the immense skills children already bring to the world of reading. This means that we must ultimately expand the definition of literacy by exploring ways of being a lifelong literate human being, and help children form a relationship with literature that extends beyond merely decoding words. In this paper, we present a case study that shares how a student with limited Spanish, in a dual language classroom, can communicate powerfully to tell stories when given the tools and space to do so. We follow a kindergarten student (5 year old) who used wordless books to tell stories in both English and Spanish over time. Her storytelling showed how young children can express meaning in complex and personal ways, even when their vocabulary in a second language is limited and still dev eloping. Therefore, we wonder, how can wordless picture books support student autonomy in storytelling for emergent dual language learners? Wordless books Wordless books are picture books with little to no written text. Thus, readers rely on the illustrations and images to read and tell stories. These visual stories invite readers to interpret meaning and construct narratives using their own imagination, lived experiences, and cultural knowledge (Lee, et al., 2025; Razfar & Gutierrez, 2003; Vygotsky, 1978). Our case study draws on literature that positions literacy as a meaning-making social activity. Freire and Slover (1983) argue that literacy is not a mechanical skill to be mastered, but a process of consciousness formation where learners first “read the world” before they “read the word.” His conception of reading was that it was a critical, experiential, and transformative act (Crawford & Hade, 2000). Therefore, reading is inherently social, reflective, and tied to identity, power, and context (Vygotsky, 1978). We argue wordless books can support storytelling, language development, and creative expression, and offer young learners the opportunity to reflec t on their own experiences and prior knowledge (Arif & Hashim, 2008). Wordless books open up storytelling for all children, regardless of their reading level or language proficiency. They invite students to navigate meaning using gesture, tone, facial expression, and oral language across multiple languages (Alvermann, 2002; Dehghani et al., 2013). In doing so, wordless books position students as active participants in the meaning-making process (Freire & Slover, 1983), rather than passive recipients of a fixed and scripted narrative. Wordless books also offer space for children still developing language to engage with complex ideas and express personal insights through their own narrative decisions (Arif & Hashim, 2008; Goriot et al., 2024). For emergent dual language learners, wordless picture books offer inclusive, flexible, and linguistically accessible 2147 entry points into literacy. Children may even feel freer to communicate authentically by lifting the pressure of correctness and allowing for fluid, spontaneous storytelling. Because the narrative is not fixed by written words, students are free to interpr et and reframe the story in ways that reflect their multilingual and multicultural experiences (Crawford & Hade, 2000). This makes wordless books especially powerful in culturally and linguistically diverse classrooms, where children can exercise autonomy and agency in meaning-making, without being confined by rigid, traditional models of literacy instruction that often feel limited at a young age. Each child, regardless of language or fluency, is encouraged by the pictures in wordless books to construct their own meaning of the story, offering beneficial possibilities for use in all-inclusive learning settings. Designing wordless books into literacy curriculum The design and implementation of wordless books into our literacy curriculum came from a multi-year collaboration between Arlen and Christine. We began the work by searching for and reading research on wordless books in early literacy. We then introduced wordless books to our kindergarten (5-6 years) classroom, and immediately realized the potential these books had for emergent readers, especial ly as a way to equitably engage and provide access to literacy regardless of the child’s background. Our initial observation of students exploring wordless books was that these books can be a powerful tool for voice and agency. This is important for dual language classrooms and multi-lingual learners because many children in a dual language classroom can often be positioned as “being academically behind” in their literacy or language skills. We took these preliminary observations and focused on designing a learning experience for children with wordless books where they can see themselves as creative meaning-makers, where they can author their own stories, and come to form a solid identity as a reader early on. Our focal student, Camila (pseudonym), was a five year old emergent bilingual learner who primarily spoke English at home and was newly acquiring Spanish through the school's dual language program. Together, we analyzed the two transcripts and found two emerging themes that we present in this paper (Braun & Clarke, 2022). Theme 1: Emotional expression as a storytelling strategy Across our transcripts, Camila demonstrated that emotional expression can be a powerful tool for storytelling. Throughout the Spanish storytelling transcript, Camila relied on her vocal tone, pauses, facial gestures, and nonverbal sounds (like dramatic sighs) to convey a character's or narrator's feelings. The transcript of her Spanish reading of the wordless book was rich with these emotional expressions and was a crucial part of her storytelling. Camila expressed deep understanding of the story even when her Spanish vocabulary was limited, showing how emotional gestures, prosody, and tone can support meaning-making when verbal language is still developing. In her English narration of Good Dog, Carl, Camila primarily used sentences with consistent pacing and a neutral tone. For example, she said, “Carl looks out the window. The baby sneaks out, and the dog comes by”. While this sentence is descriptively accurate of the story, her delivery and oral t elling of it resembled the cadence of classroom read-alouds, where students are taught to prioritize clarity and linear sequencing rather than emotional interpretation and creative storytelling. In contrast, her Spanish narration of The Girl and the Bicycle had nonverbal elements like tone, gesture, and diverse pacing to convey emotional depth. Earlier in the story, she asked, “¿En dónde está mi dinero? ¿Aquí? ¿Aquí?” with a rising intonation while using her whole body to mimic the action of searching for something. Her rising intonation and repetition of “¿Aquí?” created a sense of urgency and concern, as she embodied the character's anxious search for money to buy a bicycle. Later, Camila captured the character’s disappointment by using a different tone to interpret and communicate the emotional arc of the story. She paused and sighed dramatically before saying “Poquito triste,” meaning “a little sad” as she embodied the character’s emotions when she found out the bicycle was gone. Camila effectively communicated the sadness of the moment through her tone shift, softly stating “Poquito triste”, dramatic pause, and pacing. These emotional expressions added depth to the statement, expressing a feeling that extended beyond the image itself and making listeners understand the heavy moment in the story. Therefore, Camila’s use of semiotic resources effectively communicated the feelings and emotional depth of the story despite speaking fewer words – illustrating the true power of embodiment and emotional connections in rich storytelling. Theme 2: Narrative perspectives Camila's storytelling across the two transcripts also revealed a shift in narrative perspectives. When she read Good Dog, Carl in English, her point of view was in the third person, and when she read The Girl and the Bicycle in Spanish, it was in both the third person and first person perspectives. For example, in Good Dog, Carl, she said, “They open the door, and went to the Mommy and Daddy's room, and they played with makeup”. Camila stayed in the third person, narrative perspective throughout her entire reading of the story as she orally described the events of the story. 2149 In contrast, Camila's narration in Spanish showed more fluidity. Rather than sticking to a single point of view, she moved between third person and first person to tell the story. For example, on page 8 of The Girl and the Bicycle, Camila said “No tiene más money, no tiene más monedas,” meaning “She doesn’t have any more money, she doesn’t have any more coins,”. But later, on page 13, she exclaimed, “Ven con yo! No! ¡No quiero, no gracias!” meaning “Come with me! No! I don’t want to, no thank you!”, steppi ng into the character’s voice and embodying what the character would excitedly say in the story. Camila exercised narrative autonomy, shaping the story in deeper ways that felt meaningful to her in the moment. Her inventive dialogue and expressive stance reflect how learners construct meaning by using their voice and lived experience. Instead of simply retelling the events, Camila authored a story that reflected her understanding, feelings, and agency. Concluding remarks One of the most powerful lessons learned from observing students engage with wordless books is how quickly they are able to see themselves as competent readers. Even at a young age, children can step into stories with confidence and agency before they’ve mastered decoding text. They also don’t need to rely on others to access these books. This accessibility gives them the opportunity to take ownership of their reading. As a dual language teacher, this was powerful for students because it encouraged them to take risks and play with language. It was wonderful to see young children feel empowered with their reading, as if they could grab a book and tell a story all on their own. As a result of this work, a parent thanked Arlen for our work with wordless books because her son had a hard time feeling confident while decoding text. However, she mentioned that with wordless books, he was never discouraged and always saw himself as a storyteller and reader. Recently, Arlen saw this parent on campus again, and she shared that her son now reads books and loves being a reader. She attributed his journey and growth as a reader to wordless books because they made him feel like a competent reader as he learned to decode text. As a dual language teacher, A rlen also learned that wordless books encourage emerging dual language students to take risks. While there is clearly a story to tell, wordless books have no script; thus, there is no strict expectation of what students must say. Instead, students are able to freely use the words they already know or play with new and unfamiliar words in their storytelling. Through their experimentation with storytelling, they are able to stretch themselves in a low-stakes and playful way. This feeling of agency and creative freedom is what creates space for incredible growth. We also realized that wordless books served as a tool for equity because the stories welcome every child, no matter their background knowledge. Regardless of the child’s dominant or target language, wordless books provide everyone an opportunity for conversation, making literacy accessible across all learners. The books create a space of inclusivity and allow students to connect, share, and learn from each other. Thus, every child’s unique voice is honored through expression filled with emotion, because although children are reading the author’s art and images, they are ultimately telling their own stories. When children are able to bring aspects of themselves into the classroom, such as their home language or family stories, they naturally create a space f or cultural representation through wordless books. This affirms their identities as competent and agentic storytellers and makes them visible within the classroom setting. Wordless books become a universal resource for students. Institutionalized education often lacks opportunities for every child to be heard and valued. Wordless books remove these barriers, allowing every child to be seen. Throughout our research partnership, we came to realize how intertwined research, teaching, and learning are. Before becoming a teacher, Arlen studied with Fred Erickson, whose work with teachers showed Arlen the power in positioning educators as co-constructors of knowledge. In our work, we made decisions about how wordless books would be integrated into the curriculum and how to collect and analyze our data together. We respected and made space for our intellectual insights and curiosities in this work. Not only has this made our research stronger, but it has also given Arlen the confidence to speak to other educators to reform literacy programs, especially for elementary dual language classrooms. The collaborative participation in research gave Arlen the data and the tools to communicate and share the importance of wordless books in this paper, which invites other teachers to engage in this work as a way to transform and grow our teaching practices. Objectives of our practice-oriented paper Over the past three years, Arlen and Christine, a dual language kindergarten teacher and a university researcher, have collaborated on a participatory design-based research study (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) examining the potential of wordless books for young dual language learners. Our paper examines how wordless books can serve as a powerful tool for dual language literacy, empowering young learners to take agency in their oral storytelling and reading. When we think of literacy instruction in primary grade levels, it tends to focus solely on phonics, accuracy, and decoding words. This experience can provide a narrow view of literacy, and it views children as passive recipients rather than active meaning makers (Freire & Slover, 1983). It also doesn’t leave ro om for student-driven creative expression, like storytelling. For emergent dual language learners, the act of learning to read in a new language can present an extra level of pressure, setting expectations that they have to somehow quickly master both languages. Wordless picture books offer a different kind of entry point into literacy. It can remove the pressure to perform and instead invite children to build their own narratives using a variety of resources like language, tone, gestures, and imagination. Our paper shows wordless books as a resource to support children’s identities as lifelong readers and empower them by giving children agency in their storytelling.  We argue that we need to move away from deficit thinking by highlighting the immense skills children already bring to the world of reading. This means that we must ultimately expand the definition of literacy by exploring ways of being a lifelong literate human being, and help children form a relationship with literature that extends beyond merely decoding words. In this paper, we present a case study that shares how a student with limited Spanish, in a dual language classroom, can communicate powerfully to tell stories when given the tools and space to do so. We follow a kindergarten student (5 year old) who used wordless books to tell stories in both English and Spanish over time. Her storytelling showed how young children can express meaning in complex and personal ways, even when their vocabulary in a second language is limited and still dev eloping. Therefore, we wonder, how can wordless picture books support student autonomy in storytelling for emergent dual language learners? Wordless books Wordless books are picture books with little to no written text. Thus, readers rely on the illustrations and images to read and tell stories. These visual stories invite readers to interpret meaning and construct narratives using their own imagination, lived experiences, and cultural knowledge (Lee, et al., 2025; Razfar & Gutierrez, 2003; Vygotsky, 1978). Our case study draws on literature that positions literacy as a meaning-making social activity. Freire and Slover (1983) argue that literacy is not a mechanical skill to be mastered, but a process of consciousness formation where learners first “read the world” before they “read the word.” His conception of reading was that it was a critical, experiential, and transformative act (Crawford & Hade, 2000). Therefore, reading is inherently social, reflective, and tied to identity, power, and context (Vygotsky, 1978). We argue wordless books can support storytelling, language development, and creative expression, and offer young learners the opportunity to reflec t on their own experiences and prior knowledge (Arif & Hashim, 2008). Wordless books open up storytelling for all children, regardless of their reading level or language proficiency. They invite students to navigate meaning using gesture, tone, facial expression, and oral language across multiple languages (Alvermann, 2002; Dehghani et al., 2013). In doing so, wordless books position students as active participants in the meaning-making process (Freire & Slover, 1983), rather than passive recipients of a fixed and scripted narrative. Wordless books also offer space for children still developing language to engage with complex ideas and express personal insights through their own narrative decisions (Arif & Hashim, 2008; Goriot et al., 2024). For emergent dual language learners, wordless picture books offer inclusive, flexible, and linguistically accessible 2147 entry points into literacy. Children may even feel freer to communicate authentically by lifting the pressure of correctness and allowing for fluid, spontaneous storytelling. Because the narrative is not fixed by written words, students are free to interpr et and reframe the story in ways that reflect their multilingual and multicultural experiences (Crawford & Hade, 2000). This makes wordless books especially powerful in culturally and linguistically diverse classrooms, where children can exercise autonomy and agency in meaning-making, without being confined by rigid, traditional models of literacy instruction that often feel limited at a young age. Each child, regardless of language or fluency, is encouraged by the pictures in wordless books to construct their own meaning of the story, offering beneficial possibilities for use in all-inclusive learning settings. We began the work by searching for and reading research on wordless books in early literacy. We then introduced wordless books to our kindergarten (5-6 years) classroom, and immediately realized the potential these books had for emergent readers, especial ly as a way to equitably engage and provide access to literacy regardless of the child’s background. Our initial observation of students exploring wordless books was that these books can be a powerful tool for voice and agency. This is important for dual language classrooms and multi-lingual learners because many children in a dual language classroom can often be positioned as “being academically behind” in their literacy or language skills. We took these preliminary observations and focused on designing a learning experience for children with wordless books where they can see themselves as creative meaning-makers, where they can author their own stories, and come to form a solid identity as a reader early on. Theme 1: Emotional expression as a storytelling strategy Across our transcripts, Camila demonstrated that emotional expression can be a powerful tool for storytelling. Throughout the Spanish storytelling transcript, Camila relied on her vocal tone, pauses, facial gestures, and nonverbal sounds (like dramatic sighs) to convey a character's or narrator's feelings. The transcript of her Spanish reading of the wordless book was rich with these emotional expressions and was a crucial part of her storytelling. Camila expressed deep understanding of the story even when her Spanish vocabulary was limited, showing how emotional gestures, prosody, and tone can support meaning-making when verbal language is still developing. In her English narration of Good Dog, Carl, Camila primarily used sentences with consistent pacing and a neutral tone. For example, she said,  “Carl looks out the window. The baby sneaks out, and the dog comes by”. While this sentence is descriptively accurate of the story, her delivery and oral t elling of it resembled the cadence of classroom read-alouds, where students are taught to prioritize clarity and linear sequencing rather than emotional interpretation and creative storytelling. In contrast, her Spanish narration of The Girl and the Bicycle had nonverbal elements like tone, gesture, and diverse pacing to convey emotional depth. Earlier in the story, she asked, “¿En dónde está mi dinero? ¿Aquí? ¿Aquí?” with a rising intonation while using her whole body to mimic the action of searching for something. Her rising intonation and repetition of “¿Aquí?” created a sense of urgency and concern, as she embodied the character's anxious search for money to buy a bicycle. Later, Camila captured the character’s disappointment by using a different tone to interpret and communicate the emotional arc of the story. She paused and sighed dramatically before saying “Poquito triste,” meaning “a little sad” as she embodied the character’s emotions when she found out the bicycle was gone. Camila effectively communicated the sadness of the moment through her tone shift, softly stating “Poquito triste”, dramatic pause, and pacing. These emotional expressions added depth to the statement, expressing a feeling that extended beyond the image itself and making listeners understand the heavy moment in the story. Therefore, Camila’s use of semiotic resources effectively communicated the feelings and emotional depth of the story despite speaking fewer words – illustrating the true power of embodiment and emotional connections in rich storytelling. Theme 2: Narrative perspectives Camila's storytelling across the two transcripts also revealed a shift in narrative perspectives. When she read Good Dog, Carl in English, her point of view was in the third person, and when she read The Girl and the Bicycle in Spanish, it was in both the third person and first person perspectives. For example, in Good Dog, Carl,  she said, “They open the door, and went to the Mommy and Daddy's room, and they played with makeup”. Camila stayed in the third person, narrative perspective throughout her entire reading of the story as she orally described the events of the story. 2149 In contrast, Camila's narration in Spanish showed more fluidity. Rather than sticking to a single point of view, she moved between third person and first person to tell the story. For example, on page 8 of The Girl and the Bicycle, Camila said “No tiene más money, no tiene más monedas,” meaning “She doesn’t have any more money, she doesn’t have any more coins,”. But later, on page 13,  she exclaimed, “Ven con yo! No! ¡No quiero, no gracias!” meaning “Come with me! No! I don’t want to, no thank you!”, steppi ng into the character’s voice and embodying what the character would excitedly say in the story. Camila exercised narrative autonomy, shaping the story in deeper ways that felt meaningful to her in the moment. Her inventive dialogue and expressive stance reflect how learners construct meaning by using their voice and lived experience. Instead of simply retelling the events, Camila authored a story that reflected her understanding, feelings, and agency. Concluding remarks One of the most powerful lessons learned from observing students engage with wordless books is how quickly they are able to see themselves as competent readers. Even at a young age, children can step into stories with confidence and agency before they’ve mastered decoding text. They also don’t need to rely on others to access these books. This accessibility gives them the opportunity to take ownership of their reading. As a dual language teacher, this was powerful for students because it encouraged them to take risks and play with language. It was wonderful to see young children feel empowered with their reading, as if they could grab a book and tell a story all on their own. As a result of this work, a parent thanked Arlen for our work with wordless books because her son had a hard time feeling confident while decoding text. However, she mentioned that with wordless books, he was never discouraged and always saw himself as a storyteller and reader. Recently, Arlen  saw this parent on campus again, and she shared that her son now reads books and loves being a reader. She attributed his journey and growth as a reader to wordless books because they made him feel like a competent reader as he learned to decode text. As a dual language teacher, A rlen also learned that wordless books encourage emerging dual language students to take risks. While there is clearly a story to tell, wordless books have no script; thus, there is no strict expectation of what students must say. Instead, students are able to freely use the words they already know or play with new and unfamiliar words in their storytelling. Through their experimentation with storytelling, they are able to stretch themselves in a low-stakes and playful way. This feeling of agency and creative freedom is what creates space for incredible growth. We also realized that wordless books served as a tool for equity because the stories welcome every child, no matter their background knowledge. Regardless of the child’s dominant or target language, wordless books provide everyone an opportunity for conversation, making literacy accessible across all learners. The books create a space of inclusivity and allow students to connect, share, and learn from each other.
