# Exploring the Influence of Affective Factors on Cognitive Factors in Primary School Students' Scientific Literacy

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examined the relationships among affective and cognitive factors of scientific literacy using data from 5,957 4th and 6th graders in China. The SEM results revealed that: (1) a significant internal chain exists within the affective dimension, progressing from curiosity to scientific interest and then to science identity; (2) cognitive factors follow a sequential path from content knowledge to scientific reasoning, and further to inquiry; (3) the direct effects of affective factors on cognitive outcomes are limited, with a more prominent role played by a complete mediation pathway: "curiosity to interest to identity to content knowledge to reasoning/inquiry". Multi-group path analysis further indicated that the overall model structure differs significantly between 4th and 6th graders, with sixth graders showing higher efficiency in transforming curiosity into interest and converting knowledge into reasoning and inquiry. The study provides an integrated model and evidence for stage-specific instructional strategies in K12 science education.

Scientific literacy is central to equipping students with the ability to engage with science-related issues as informed and reflective citizens (OECD, 2023). Recognizing its significance, numerous countries and international organizations have incorporated scientific literacy into core educational policies. For example, Saskatchewan Ministry of Education (2009) in Canada underscores scientific literacy as a key goal, focusing on students’ ability to reason scientifically and solve problems through investiga tion. In the United States, the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS)  (2013) highlights integrating disciplinary core ideas, science and engineering practices, and crosscutting concepts across K-12 education as performance expectation, to evaluate students’ scientific literacy. China’s  Compulsory Education Science Curriculum Standards (2022 Edition) (Ministry of Education of the People’s Republic of China, 2022) emphasizes developing students’ scientific literacy for all students. The PISA 2025 framework  continues to emphasize scientific literacy as a "fundamental competence for modern citizens," expanding its connotation to include cognitive abilities, practical skills, and attitudinal dimensions (OECD, 2023).

Beyond policy alignment, fostering scientific literacy is essential for addressing complex global challenges, such as climate change, public health, and sustainable development, which require citizens to critically evaluate scientific information, engage in evidence-based reasoning, and participate responsibly in societal discourse (National Research Council, 2012; Osborne et al., 2003). Moreover, scientific literacy supports personal decision-making, from health-related behaviors to environmental actions, thereby enhancing individual and collective well-being (Feinstein, 2011; Hodson, 2003).

A comprehensive understanding of scientific literacy necessitates attention to both its cognitive and affective foundations. Cognitive factors, such as content knowledge, reasoning, and inquiry, form the essential architecture for scientific thinking and p roblem-solving (Díaz et al., 2023; NGSS Lead States, 2013). Simultaneously, affective factors, including curiosity, interest, and science identity, are pivotal in motivating engagement, sustaining inquiry, and fostering a sense of belonging in science (Archer et al., 2015; Potvin & Hasni, 2014). Given that cognitive and affective dimensions are each vital to the development of scientific literacy, understanding how they interact and jointly contribute to student learning becomes a compelling and necessary area of research. Examining these dimensions together can provide a more holistic view of scientific literacy and uncover how emotional dispositions support and enhance cognitive growth. Furthermore, exploring whether these relationships vary across educational stages can offer critical insights into the developmental dynamics of scientific literacy. Therefore, grounded in the framework of China’s Compulsory Education Science Curriculum Standards (2022 Edition) and informed by the indicators mentioned in NGSS (2013) and PISA (OECD, 2023), this study investigates the relationships between affective factors (curiosity, scientific interest, science identity) and cognitive factors (content knowledge, scientific reasoning, and inquiry), and compares these pathways across 4 th and 6th grade students.

## Literature review

Affective dimension: Curiosity, scientific interest and scientific identity The affective dimension encompasses curiosity, scientific interest, and scientific identity —three interrelated constructs that drive engagement in science. Curiosity is defined as a multifaceted disposition comprising both interest-based curiosity (driven by intrinsic interest) and depriving curiosity (motivated by resolving knowledge gaps) (Cui et al., 2022). Scientific interest refers to a sustained and positive affective orientation toward scientific topics and activities (Hidi & Renninger, 2006). Scient ific identity is conceptualized as the extent to which individuals view themselves as competent in and belonging to the scientific community, shaped through both personal internalization and social recognition (Carlone & Johnson, 2007). Research demonstrates a developmental progression within these affective factors. Curiosity serves as an initial trigger that can evolve into stable scientific interest when supported by appropriate contextual factors (Gottfried et al., 2016). Cui et al. (2022) demonstrated that both forms of curiosity positively predict interest in STEM careers, with depriving curiosity showing a stronger predictive effect. Furthermore, curiosity contributes to scientific identity development by motivating exploratory behaviors that provide opportunities for mastery and recognition (Gottfried et al., 2016; Huffmyer et al., 2022). Scientific interest, in turn, strengthens scientific identity through multiple pathways. Carlone and Johnson (2007) illustrated that strong scientific interest motivates individuals to pursue recognition and achievement, which bolsters identity. Alhadabi (2021) quantitatively confirmed that scientific interest directly enhances scientific identity and also operates indirectly through science self-efficacy. Contextual factors such as peer and teacher recognition can moderate this relationship, helping transf orm personal interest into a socially grounded identity (Rodriguez, 2015; Vincent-Ruz & Schunn, 2018). Cognitive dimension: Content knowledge, reasoning, and inquiry The cognitive dimension of scientific literacy comprises three integral components: content knowledge, scientific reasoning, and scientific inquiry, as outlined in China's Compulsory Education Science Curriculum Standards (2022 Edition). Content knowledge refers to students' understanding of fundamental scientific facts, concepts, and theories across domains such as Material Science, Life Science, and Earth and Space Sciences. Scientific reasoning encompasses the internal cognitive processes and skills for thinking scientifically, while scientific inquiry denotes the practical ability to conduct systematic investigations, including skills such as experimental design, data analysis, and conclusion derivation.

These cognitive components form a dynamic, interdependent relationship. Content knowledge serves as the foundation that enables reasoning and inquiry. Hiğde and Aktamış (2022) demonstrated that activities organized around specific scientific content allowed students to conduct meaningful reasoning and inquiry only after mastering relevant concepts, with the depth of content understanding correlating positively with inquiry proficiency. Scientific reasoning provides the cognitive framework that guides scient ific inquiry. Díaz and colleagues (2023) identified that core aspects of scientific reasoning, including hypothesis generation and evidence evaluation, are essential cognitive skills for the scientific inquiry process. This relationship is reinforced by empirical evidence showing that tasks requiring scientific reasoning significantly enhance students' scientific inquiry capabilities (Hiğde & Aktamış, 2022).

The relationship between affective and cognitive dimension The affective and cognitive dimensions of scientific literacy interact through multiple pathways. Curiosity serves as a key driver of cognitive engagement, motivating students to actively acquire and deepen their content knowledge through autonomous exploration (Singh & Manjaly, 2022). It also fosters scientific reasoning by driving hypothesis generation and logical inference (Jirout, 2020) and acts as a catalyst for scientific inquiry by promoting cognitive and emotional investment in investigative practices (Wu & Wu, 2020). Scientific interest builds upon this foundation by providing a more sustained motivational force. Its positive correlation with the mastery of content knowledge is well-established across different age groups, operating through both direct pathways and mediators like engagement and self-efficacy (Leibham et al., 2013). Interest also enhances scientific reasoning by fostering reflective thinking and enabling knowledge integration across disciplines (Guo et al., 2022), while comprehensively promoting scientific inquiry by increasing learners' initiative, persistence, and rigor in investigative activities (Toli & Kallery, 2021). Science identity represents a deeper internalization of the affective connection to science. It acts as a significant mediator, translating scientific interest and self-efficacy into improved mastery of content knowledge, particularly for underrepresented groups (Alhadabi, 2021). A strong science identity directly predicts greater 585 participation in inquiry activities, which in turn strengthens specific scientific inquiry skills such as experimental design and data interpretation (Vincent-Ruz & Schunn, 2018; Chen et al., 2020). Collectively, these findings suggest that affective dimensions form a progressive relationship where curiosity sparks interest, which in turn contributes to science identity formation. Together, they exert significant direct and indirect influences on the cognitive dimensions of scientific literacy, underscoring the necessity for an integrated theoretical model that can simultaneously capture these relationships.

## Research questions and hypotheses

While prior research has extensively explored the relationships among affective and cognitive factors in scientific literacy, two significant limitations persist. First, few studies adopt a holistic view that simultaneously examines the relationships encom passing all three core affective factors (curiosity, interest, identity) and all three core cognitive factors (content knowledge, reasoning, inquiry). Second, there is insufficient investigation into how these structural relationships might differ across key educational stages, such as between upper elementary grades.

To address these gaps, this study proposes an integrated model grounded in social cognitive theory and seeks to answer the following research questions: (1)What are the direct influence relationships among the affective factors (curiosity, scientific interest, science identity) and cognitive factors (content knowledge, scientific reasoning, scientific inquiry) within the hypothesized model?

(2)Do the hypothesized structural paths within the model differ significantly between 4th-grade and 6thgrade students? Based on the theoretical framework and the proposed model (Figure 1), the following hypotheses are formulated.

H1–H5 propose that curiosity has a significant positive direct effect on scientific interest, science identity, content knowledge, scientific reasoning, and scientific inquiry, respectively. H6–H9 propose that scientific interest has a significant positive direct effect on science identity, content knowledge, scientific reasoning, and scientific inquiry, respectively. H10 proposes that science identity has a significant positive direct effect on content knowledge. H11–H12 propose that content knowledge has a significant positive direct effect on scientific reasoning and scientific inquiry, respectively.

H13 proposes that scientific reasoning has a significant positive direct effect on scientific inquiry. H14–H15 propose that curiosity has a significant positive effect on scientific reasoning and scientific inquiry, respectively, through the chain mediating path of scientific interest, science identity, and content knowledge.

H16 proposes that the structural paths in the model differ significantly between fourth graders and sixth graders.

## Method

This study employed a cluster sampling approach, encompassing all fourth-and sixth-grade students from a representative district in Central China. In April 2024, these students participated in a scientific achievement assessment aligned with the core lite racy-oriented framework of science education. The assessment consisted of two complementary components: a standardized science performance test and a affective dimension questionnaire, which correspond to the cognitive and affective dimensions examined in this study, respectively. The development of the science performance test was rigorously guided by the Compulsory Education Science Curriculum Standards (2022 Version) of China, with a focus on evaluating students' mastery of scientific content, scientific inquiry, and scientific r easoning. The entire assessment process from test design and instrument validation to scoring and data entry was conducted in strict accordance with standardized research protocols. The monitoring framework and assessment tools underwent multiple rounds of  review and refinement, and the administration procedures were verified through practical implementation, ensuring the reliability and validity of the collected data.

## Participants

A total of 3,767 fourth-grade students participated in the science assessment, among whom 2,527 students (including 1,237 boys, accounting for 48.9%, with an average age of 9.94 ± 0.53 years) took part in the subsequent survey on science affective dimensions. For the sixth grade, 3,970 students participated in the assessment, and 3,070 of them (including 1,502 boys, accounting for 48.9%, with an average age of 11.65 ± 0.50 years) participated in the follow-up survey. Therefore, a total of 5,957 valid samples were obtained in this study. 586 Instrument Affective questionnaire: Curiosity, scientific interests and scientific identity The questionnaire consists of three dimensions: scientific interest, curiosity and scientific identity. The curiosity scale adopted in this study is adapted from the Scientific Curiosity in Learning Environments (SCILE) scale, with reliability of 0.91 (Weible, & Zimmerman, 2016). The adapted instrument consisted of 8 questions, including "I like to solve problems or difficulties that have more than one answer", using a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from 1 "I always do this" to 5 "I never do this") and reverse coding of the scores. The reliability of the curiosity scale in this study was 0.87 in terms of Cronbach's alpha.

The scientific interest scale used in this study was adapted from the School Scientific Interest Questionnaire, Science Career Interest Questionnaire, and Extracricular Scientific Interest Questionnaire (Van Griethuijsen et al., 2015; original Cronbach's alpha = 0.502/0.673/0.852). The adapted instrument consisted of 26 items, including "I like all science-related courses offered by the school," rated on a 3-point scale (1 = "No," 2 = "Uncertain," 3 = "Yes"). Higher scores indicated stronger scientific interest. The reliability of the adapted scale in this study was 0.88 in terms of Cronbach's alpha.

The scientific identity scale was adapted from the Scientific Identity Scale (Vincon-Ruz & Schunn, 2018; original Cronbach's alpha = 0.84). The adapted instrument consisted of 9 items, including "I am a person with scientific literacy," rated on a 3-point scale (1 = "No," 2 = "Uncertain," 3 = "Yes"). Higher scores indicated stronger scientific identity. The reliability of the adapted scale in this study was 0.82 in terms of Cronbach's alpha. Cognitive test Standardized tests measuring content knowledge, scientific reasoning, and scientific inquiry, adapted from international assessments. Cronbach's alpha for 4th/6th grade: content knowledge (0.749/0.782), inquiry (0.672/0.653), reasoning (0.861/0.836).

## Data analysis

The data analysis process consisted of two main steps. First, SPSS 30 software was used to conduct descriptive statistics for each variable. Second, structural equation modeling was performed using SmartPLS 4 to answer research question 1 to 3. Thirdly, MPLUS 8 was used to conduct a group analysis of the path models between 4th and 6th graders.

The specific results can be divided into the following three categories of characteristics: First, all internal paths within the affective dimension are significant, with strong effect sizes. Second, all internal paths within the cognitive dimension are significant, showing a progressive relationship of "knowledge → reasoning → skills", and most paths have high effect sizes. Scientific content knowledge has a significant positive impact on both scientific reasoning (H11, β = 0.540, p < 0.001) and scientific inquiry (H12, β = 0.433, p < 0.001), which indicates that scientific knowledge is an important foundation for 588 reasoning ability and practical skills. Scientific reasoning also has a significant direct impact on scientific inquiry (H13, β = 0.269, p < 0.001), suggesting that logical thinking helps to improve the standardization and effectiveness of scientific inquiry.

Third, the cross-dimensional (affective → cognitive) impacts are partially significant. Curiosity has a significant direct impact on scientific content knowledge (H3, β = 0.022, p = 0.028) and scientific inquiry (H5, β = 0.034, p < 0.001), but its impact on scientific reasoning is not significant (H4, β = 0.015, p = 0.127). Scientific interest has a significant impact on scientific reasoning (H8, β = 0.035, p < 0.001) and scientific inquiry (H9, β = 0.025, p = 0.004), but its impact on scientific content knowledge is not significant (H7, β = 0.010, p = 0.394). Scientific identity has a significant positive impact on scientific content knowledge (H10, β = 0.066, p < 0.001). Overall, the impact of affective variables on cognitive variables shows a certain degree of heterogeneity, and some paths need to rely on mediating mechanisms to be realized. To further examine the indirect impact of affective variables on scientific abilities through cognitive variables, this study used the Bootstrap sampling method (with 5,000 repeated samplings) to test the proposed chain mediation paths. Both chain mediation paths reached a statistically significant level (p < 0.001), indicating that affective variables mainly exert an indirect impact on higher-order cognitive abilities through multi-step mediation paths. Specifically, the indirect effect value of the path "Curiosity → Scientific Interest → Scientific Identity → Scientific Content Knowledge → Scientific Reasoning" (H14) was 0.015 (p < 0.001), while the indirect effect value of the path "Curiosity → Scientific Interest → Scientific Identity → Scientific Conten t Knowledge → Scientific inquiry" (H15) was 0.016 (p < 0.001). This result further supports the existence of an orderly multi-step mediation mechanism between affective and cognitive variables: affective factors primarily promote the accumulation of knowledge by stimulating interest and identity recognition, and ultim ately drive the development of scientific reasoning and inquiry abilities. Additionally, the chain mediation results of the sixth-grade students were similar to those of the fourth-grade students.

Group analysis of the path models The multi-group path analysis revealed significant differences in the model structure between 4th and 6th graders, illustrating a developmental model where "efficiency and driving force waver and wane". Sixth graders demonstrated higher transformation efficiency in key pathways: (1) Curiosity → Scientific Interest: They were more effective at converting curiosity into stable interest. (2)Content Knowledge → Scientific Reasoning/Inquiry: They more efficiently applied acquired knowledge to reasoning and inquiry practices. Fourth graders relied more on initial driving forces: (1) Curiosity exerted a stronger direct effect on scientific inquiry. (2) Scientific identity played a more central mediating role in connecting affect to content knowledge.

In summary, as students progress to higher grades, their affective-cognitive pathways become more refined and efficient, while the direct driving force of initial curiosity diminishes in relative importance. The following discussion is organized around the research questions, analyzing the consistencies and differences between the current findings and prior research.

The relationship between affective and cognitive dimensions of scientific literacy for 4th graders The findings confirm a stable positive chain within the affective dimension "Curiosity → Scientific Interest → Scientific Identity" among fourth graders. The strong path coefficients for "Curiosity → Scientific Interest" (β=0.597-0.604) align with Cui et al. (2022), who identified curiosity as an "igniter" of scientific interest. Similarly, the significant effect of "Scientific Interest → Scientific Identity" (β=0.552-0.591) supports Alhadabi's (2021) finding that interest strengthens identity, and echoes Carlone and Johnson's (2007) proposition that "interest is a core component of identity".

Regarding cognitive dimensions, the progressive relationship "Scientific Content Knowledge→ Scientific Reasoning → Scientific inquiry" was strongly supported. The substantial effect of content knowledge on reasoning (β=0.376-0.481) and inquiry (β=0.276-0.478) confirms Díaz et al.'s (2023) "knowledge-thinking589 skills" cycle, while the effect of reasoning on inquiry (β=0.380-0.409) aligns with Hiğde and Aktamış's (2022) For cross-dimensional relationships, most direct effects from affective to cognitive variables were non-significant in fourth grade. This finding is consistent with Leibham et al. (2013), who found that early interest had limited direct impact on cognitive  outcomes. The exception was curiosity's weak but significant direct effect on inquiry (β=0.043-0.101), partially supporting Hunaepi et al.'s (2024) proposition that curiosity directly promotes inquiry, though the effect size was modest.

The relationship between affective and cognitive dimensions of scientific literacy for 6th graders The affective chain remained robust in sixth grade, with notably stronger "Curiosity → Scientific Interest" path coefficients (β=0.649-0.650) compared to fourth graders. This developmental trend, not explicitly reported in Gottfried et al.'s (2016) longitudinal study, suggests that older students become more efficient at converting curiosity into stable interest, possibly due to accumulated scientific learning experiences. Within the cognitive dimension, all pathways remained significant, with notably stronger effects from content knowledge to reasoning (β=0.445-0.540) and inquiry (β=0.343-0.433) compared to fourth graders. This supports Yildiz and Guler Yildiz's (2021) general conclusion about cognitive development with age, while specifically identifying improved efficiency in converting knowledge into thinking and skills. For cross-dimensional effects, sixth graders showed more significant direct paths from affect to cognition. Scientific interest demonstrated small but significant effects on reasoning (β=0.035-0.059) and inquiry (β=0.0250.048), consistent with Toli and Kallery's (2021) finding that interest improves abstract reasoning. Curiosity's direct effect on inquiry, while still significant, was slightly weaker than in fourth grade (β=0.034-0.090), suggesting a shift toward more mediated influence pathways in older students. The comparison between 4th and 6th graders Multi-group analysis revealed significant differences in structural paths between grades (Δχ²=53.756, Δdf=12, p<0.001), supporting the hypothesis of grade-level moderation. Sixth graders showed significantly stronger pathways in "Curiosity → Scientific Interest" (difference=0.056, p=0.001), "Scientific Content Knowledge → Scientific Reasoning" (difference=0.065, p=0.042), and "Scientific Content Knowledge→ Scientific Inquiry" (difference=0.103, p<0.001). This indicates that older students not only convert c uriosity to interest more efficiently, but also apply knowledge to thinking and skills more effectively. Conversely, fourth graders relied more heavily on curiosity as a direct driver of inquiry (β=0.120 vs. 0.101) and on science identity as a mediator to content knowledge (β=0.145 vs. 0.106). This pattern represents a "trade-off between efficiency and drivin g force": while sixth graders exhibit more efficient cognitive pathways, fourth graders depend more on the initial affective drive of curiosity and identity reinforcement. The chain mediation "Curiosity → Interest → Identity → Content Knowledge→ Reasoning/Inquiry" was significant in both grades, extending Alhadabi's (2021) serial mediation model. Notably, the mediation proportion was higher among sixth graders for both reasoning (44.4% vs. 34.0%) and inquiry (13.5% vs. 8.7%), indicating that as students advance, curiosity's influence becomes more channeled through sequential psychological processes rather than acting directly. This developmental shift suggests that older students' science learning relies more on integrated affective-cognitive architectures. These findings collectively highlight the developmental trajectory of scientific literacy, where lower grades benefit from curiosity protection and identity building, while higher grades capitalize on interest-driven knowledge application. This provides empirical basis for differentiated pedagogical strategies across grade levels. Theoretical contribution This study offers three key theoretical contributions. First, it moves beyond the unsystematic examination of isolated scientific literacy components by constructing an integrated affect-cognition model. Grounded in social cognitive theory, this model incorporates three affective factors (curiosity, interest, identity) and three cognitive factors (content knowledge, reasoning, inquiry), and systematically validates a key chain mediation pathway: "Curiosity → Interest → Identity → Content Knowledge → Reasoning → inquiry" (e.g., Alhadabi, 2021; Zhu & Luo, 2024). This demonstrates that affective factors are not merely ancillary but act as core drivers of cognitive development through a progressive mechanism.

Second, the study identifies a grade-level moderating effect, revealing a "trade-off between efficiency and driving force" across developmental stages. For fourth graders, curiosity provides a direct drive and identity serves as the core mediator (Vincent-Ruz & Schunn, 2018). For sixth graders, conversion efficiency increases significantly in pathways such as "Curiosity → Interest" and "Content Knowledge → inquiry," and the direct 590 influence of interest expands (Gottfried et al., 2016; Toli & Kallery, 2021). This refines the developmental theory of scientific literacy and supports stage-targeted educational interventions. Third, the study innovates methodologically by combining self-reported affective scales with a standardized cognitive test aligned with curriculum standards, while applying both PLS-SEM and multi-group path analysis (Coppi et al., 2023; Thomas, 2023). This  integrated approach enhances ecological validity and provides a replicable paradigm for multi-dimensional scientific literacy research. Despite the significant theoretical contributions and important findings of this study, there are several areas for improvement. First, the sample was from one Chinese district, limiting generalizability. Future studies should include broader regions, school types, and grade levels. Second, contextual variables (family, school) were not included. Future work could use hierarchical models to analyze multi-level influences (Crowley et al., 2015). Third, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. Longitudinal tracking and cross-lagged modeling are recommended (Gottfried et al., 2016). Fourthly, field-specific differences (e.g., physics vs. biology) were not examined. Discipline-specific models could yield more precise insights (Zhu & Luo, 2024). Finally, stage-specific interventions should be designed and tested, such as curiosity-stimulating activities for lower grades and interestintegrated projects for higher grades. Alhadabi, A. (2021).
