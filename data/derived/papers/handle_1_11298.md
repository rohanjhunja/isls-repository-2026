# Exploring Students’ Emotions in An Extended Reality (XR) with Generative AI Learning Environment

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This case study explored students’ emotions while learning in an environment that incorporated extended reality and generative AI (GAI). In this study, twelve fourth graders participated in the eight-day technology-supported STEAM instructional program. They were required to create, set up, and arrange a virtual ecology corner in their classrooms using extended reality (XR), which combines immersive virtual reality (IVR) and mixed reality (MR). We evaluated students’ emotions while using different technologies during the learning process. The results showed that students experienced positive emotions, such as enjoyment and hope, throughout the learning activities, regardless of the technologies they used. However, the functional complexity of the technologies increased students’ anxiety levels. Furthermore, girls exhibited marginally higher levels of anxiety compared to boys.

## Introduction

There is a growing interest in exploring emotions in learning, which has been historically marginalized compared to cognitive aspects (Dukes et al., 2021; Hayat et al., 2020; Richards, 2022). Students’ emotions can impact their learning through  attention, memory, cognition, and meta-cognition processes (Obergriesser & Stoeger, 2020; Taub et al., 2021), which can further impact students’ learning outcomes (Chang et al., 2023; Hayat et al., 2020). Therefore, understanding emotions is crucial for gaining insights into human learning. As the learning environment can significantly influ ence the emotions of learners  (Li et al., 2020; Taub et al., 2021), it is important to investigate the potential impact of technology-supported learning environments on students’ emotions. Recently, there has been increasing attention on the potential of innovative technologies such as extended reality (XR) and generative AI (GAI) that could revolutionize classrooms (Baidoo-Anu & Ansah, 2023; Logeswaran et al., 2021). Despite the beneficial features of XR and GAI in enhancing learning, few studies have examined students’ emotions in learning environments that incorporate both technologies. Therefore, this study examined students’ emotions w hile engaging in a learning environment that incorporated extended reality and generative AI (XRGAILE). This study has two research questions:

1. What emotions did students experience when using different technologies, i.e., immersive virtual

reality (IVR), mixed reality (MR), XR (in this case, a combination of IVR and MR) and GAI?

2. What factors influence students’ emotions within an XRGAILE?

Research background Academic emotions and learning Pekrun’s Control-Value Theory (CVT) is a key theory in educational research (Pekrun, 2006). Based on the CVT theory, academic emotions are tied directly to learning activities or learning outcomes (Pekrun, 2006). These emotions can be categorized as positive and negative, activating and deactivating,  and activity-oriented and outcome-oriented (Lewis et al., 2010). From the CVT perspective, emotions are linked to motivations  based on how learners perceive their control and value in their practice (Pekrun, 2017). Academic emotions can impact students’ learning outcomes through attention, motivation, us e of learning strategies, and self-regulation of learning  (Pekrun, 2017). Many studies have shown that positive emotions such as enjoyment can positively predict s tudents’ academic performanc e (e.g., Putwain et al., 2021), cognitive strategies use (Obergriesser & Stoeger, 2 020), and motivation  during the learning process (Pekrun, 2017). However, negative emotions have mixed effect s on students’ learning. For example, while boredom and anxiety can negatively predict subsequent achieveme nt (Putwain et al., 2021), frustration can be a beneficial emotion for predicting individuals’ cognitive performance in positive ways (Taub et al., 2021). Emotions in technology-supported learning environments Regarding the development of emotions, it was found  that factors such as learning environment, prior achievement, gender, and socioeconomic status can i mpact students’ emotions (Pekrun, 2017). Hence, technology-supported learning environments can influence students’ emotions. In this study, we incorporated XR and GAI into the learning environment. XR includes virtual reality (VR), mixed reality (MR), and augmented reality (AR). Specifically, as a type of VR, IVR ca n provide students with a 360-degree immersive experience through IVR devices; MR encompasses the integration  of virtual and physical environments, allowing for interactive experiences with in both realms; and AR superimposes digital data and objects onto the real world (Logeswaran et al., 2021; Maas & Hughes, 2020). Previous studies indicate that while learning wit hin IVR (Liu et al., 2022), AR (López-Faican & Jaen, 2020), and MR (Beyoglu et  al., 2020), primary school students experienced higher levels of positive emotions (e.g., enjoyment and curiosity) and lower levels of neg ative emotions (e.g., anxiety), suggesting XR’s benefits for learning. Another technology used in this study, GAI, is an i nnovative AI technology based on large language models. It can generate continuous, multimodal, personalized respo nses and feedback based on students’ input, potentially supporting learning activities  (Baidoo-Anu & Ansah, 2023). This capability makes GAI a promising tool for enhancing educational experiences.  Up till now, few studies have explored students’ em otions when learning with GAI. Understanding these emotional experiences is crucial for optimizing the use of GAI in educational settings.

## Methods

Learning environment: XRGAILE The XRGAILE involved IVR, MR, and GAI. For IVR technologies, we used an XR application called MultiBrush. This application allows students to view 360-degree panoramic pictures (see Figure 1a) and draw 3D objects collaboratively (see Figure 1b) via the IVR devices. Students can scale and rotate the object, which allows them to draw paintings from different scales, angles, and perspectives. This can afford students an expanded opportunity for imagination and creativity. Regarding MR technologies, we used an XR application developed based on an open-source VR application called OpenBrush. Students can use OpenBrush  to interact with their VR paintings within a real-world setting (see Figure 1c for an example). We used two G AI applications called SkyBox AI  to generate panoramic pictures and Midjourney to generate general pictures according to the prompts. Figure 1a showed an AI-generated panoramic picture with the prompt “ realism, ecological wetland, flowers, birds, fish, i nsects, forest, iceberg.”  Students also used  a GAI application called ChatGPT to generate texts during their creative activities. When using the GAI appli cation, students wrote the prompts, and the teacher or research assistants then helped them send these prompts to the GAI. Learning activities and procedures We developed a series of STEAM activities based on the design thinking approach (Henriksen et al., 201 7). The STEAM activities are aimed at developing students’ creativity, understanding of ecological concepts, a nd appreciation of art. The main task for students is to design an ecologic al corner for their classroom,  which is a complex, ill-structured problem. Here, the “ecological corner ” refers to a designated space within a regular classroom where students design and set up a combin ation of small animals,  plants, and microorganisms. Each setup is required to be based on a theme chosen by the students, such as a succulent garden or a miniature Jurassic Park, and can range from a few potted plants to mor e elaborate arrangements.  Students need to define, ideate, prototype, and assess their designs, and finally de velop their creativity through hands-on practices (Amabile & Pratt, 2016; Huang et al., 2024). The main focus of the STEAM activities is art, but they also incorporate science, as students are encouraged to consider how to keep the organisms alive by applying their scientific kn owledge and thinking. Technology is also integrated, as students created and refined their design within a XRGAILE. The learning activities began with a pretest follow ed by an eight-day instructional program. Students were divided into two groups based on gender. Each group was then split into sub-groups of three students. Each session was 60 minutes long, during which the students collaborated on daily t asks. The program was facilitated by an art teacher. After completing all eight sessions, the post-test was administered to all students. Following the post-test, the students participated in focus groups.

On Day 1, students followed the teacher’s instructi ons to explore VR painting using IVR devices. On Day 2, students received their main task: to design an ecological corner for the classroom. To spark their creativity, students used Skybox AI to generate panoramic pictures and viewed these pictures in the IVR-based environment. On Day 3, students began creating the draft of thei r design in MultiBrush. Students were asked to use Midjourney to generate general pictures to support their design. Students viewed these AI-generated pictures through a tablet. After finishing the draft, the teacher helped students set up their draft prototype in the designated space in the classroom through OpenBrush. On Day 4, the teacher provided instructions and feedback on the drafts and taught students relevant knowledge and skills. After that, all groups revised their drafts solely using paper and pencils. On Days 5 and 6, students continued to refine and finalize their designs under the teachers’ guidance. On Day 7, students wrote a brief introduction to describe their work. To stimulate their creative thinking, students used ChatGPT to generate a draft description for their work. The teacher again assisted in setting up their VR paintings in the classroom through OpenBrush. Fi nally, on Day 8, each group presented their design to the whole class. The teacher and all the students discussed and evaluated each design. Students completed their daily worksheets and questionnaires during the learning activities. Data This case study involved 12 Year 4 students (six bo ys and six girls) from a public primary school in S hanghai. Most participants had no prior experience with XR o r GAI, except for two students who reported owning VR devices at home. The data was collected during the fall semester of 2023. We collected students’ (a) demographic information, (b) pre-and post-tests to evaluate their creativity, (c) process data that recorded academic emotions during learning, and (d) a focus group interview after completing all sessions. Students’ emotions were assessed at the end of the daily session except on Day 4 and Day 8, when no technologies were used. We evaluated students’ emotions while using IVR, MR, XR, and GAI  based on the Achievement Emotions Questionnaire (AEQ) with a five-point Likert scale (Pekrun et al., 2011). The mean Cronbach’s α of the AEQ is 0.8.

## Results

RQ1: What emotions did students experience when using different technologies? As shown in Table 1, students experienced high levels of positive emotions (Mpositive = 4.43, SDpositive = 0.90) and low levels of negative emotions (M negative = 1.16, SDnegasitive = 0.53) throughout the learning activities.  To further explore the emotions students experienced in the XR GAILE and the factors that triggered them, we creat ed a Bayesian generalized linear model (GLM) with emotional intensity as the dependent variable. The Bayesian GLM was chosen for its flexibility in modeling data that deviate from norm ality and its suitability for small sample sizes. Independent variables include the type of emotions,  the type of technology, gender, and some of their interactions, while learning duration (as the ordinal variable “Day”) was treated as a random effect to account for variability across learning days. Anger and IVR were set as the baseline levels. Regarding the type of technology, the results indic ated that students experienced marginally lower emotional intensity when feeling anxious (β = -2.61, 95% CI [-6.28, 0.95]) while using GAI compared to IVR. Furthermore, girls experienced marginally higher levels of anxiety (β = -2.59, 95% CI [-6.09, 0.64]) and shame (β = -2.54, 95% CI [-6.11, 0.87]) than boys. In the focus group, three students mentioned feelin g anxious while doing VR painting due to the complexity of the functions. I had to draw over and over again.” Figure 2 Students’ Emotions over the Daily Learning Activities Scientific and educational importance of the study The preliminary findings of our study reveal ed several important insights and suggestions. First, students experienced positive emotions throughout the learni ng activities, with a low degree of negative emotio ns, suggesting that the XRGAILE provides a positive learning experience. Se cond, half of the students reported feeling more anxious when using IVR, likely due to the challenges of mastering the complex functions o f VR painting. However, anxiety and shame decreased over time, suggesting the importance of designing XR and GAI learning activities wit h sufficient duration to allow students to adapt to  novel technologies.  Fourth, students’ emotions, such as pride, fluctuated during the learning activities, even when using similar technologies, suggesting that the daily tasks might impact their emotions, warranting  further exploration in future analysis. Finally, g irls appeared slightly more anxious than boys, indicating a need for additional emotional support for female students when engaging with innovative technologies like XR and GAI. Nevertheless, as a preliminary study with only 12 participants, generalizing these findings to a larger population needs to be cautious. Overall, this study reveals that an XR and GAI-supported learning environment has the potential to facilitate positive learning experiences. The desig n of learning environments and activities of this s tudy offers a model for supporting students’ positive emotions in learning with innovative technologies.
