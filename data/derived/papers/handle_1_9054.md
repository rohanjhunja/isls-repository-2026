# Lessons From Manoomin: Toward Acknowledging the Sovereignty of Indigenous Making and Sharing

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While multiple systematic reviews on constructionist learning activities have been conducted over the past decade, very few acknowledge the unique contributions of Indigenous learners and their communities as part of the maker movement. The purpose of this paper is to provide a review of existing literature on Indigenous approaches to making and sharing with a goal of identifying common technologies utilized when designing learning-by-making experiences for Indigenous youth. We summarize current findings and categorize them in alignment with principles of Indigenous making and sharing practices. These findings promote a comprehensive perspective on ways to enact Indigenous making and sharing approaches while working with Indigenous young learners and their communities. We conclude with recommendations for furthering current understandings of Indigenous making and sharing skills and pedagogical practices as well as weaving in lessons we have learned from manoomin.

## Introduction

Manoomin (Zizania palustris) is a type of grass that grows on the lakes, and i t is one of the only grains native to North America. The Anishinaabe people’s companionship with manoomin goes back centuries (e.g., Vennum, 1988). Often referred  to as the “seed of life,” manoomin is culturally v ital and sacred to the Anishinaabeg. The Anishinaabeg believ e we owe our lives to manoomin since it provides nourishment throughout each season for human and more-than-human relatives alike, especially when food is not always plentiful. For generations, wild rice camps like these have engaged Indigenous youth, families, and cultural leaders alike in the sharing of traditional teachings and creation stories, demonstrating companionship with wild rice and other more-than-human relatives, and constructing their own rice tools for harvest (e.g., Vennum, 1988). As a scholar of Anishinaabe (Ojibwe) and Europeand escent, Author 1 was struck by how deeply engaging and transformative her manoominike experie nce was. Especially in how it embodied the “traditi onal forms of making as a living legacy of Indigenous scientific and technological activity” (Barajas-López & Bang, 2018, p. 18). Moreover, having participated in the design and research of multiple learning-by-making experiences, the authors were further perplexed as to why Indigenous making and sharing is not more widely represented across the maker movement and maker spaces. The dissonance  between such a rich “maker” activity and the limit ed representation of such knowledge in the maker movement scholarship prompted us to take a deeper look at what we can learn from Indigenous making and sharing. While the scholarship is still largely rooted in west ern ways of knowing and being, we also encountered rich cultural experiences that enacted principles of Indigenous making and sharing and (re)centered Indigenous ways of knowing and being (Barajas-López & Bang, 2018). In this paper, we look across this work on Indigenous making and sharing with youth to offer expanded understandings through a systematic review woven together with lessons we learned from sacred manoomin.

## Overview of Indigenous Making & Sharing Principles

The principles of Indigenous making and sharing hav e been enacted in Indigenous communities since time immemorial. The Anishinaabeg, for example, have stewarded manoomin ever since it was gifted to them by the Creator (Gitche Manitou) for generations prior to settler colonial expansion. While conducting a summer Science, Technology, Engineering, Art, Mathematics (STEAM) c amp with Indigenous youth, Barajas-López and Bang (2018, p. 17) observed the principles of Indigenous  making and sharing practices to include: (1) Enact s and ICLS2022 Proceedings 417 © ISLS communicates stories in a multiplicity of forms in and across temporalities. Knowing, being, and doing  are simultaneously unfolding and are essential/defining elements in the practice of material storytelling; (2) Reflects the patterns and processes that cultivate life and draw on deeply interrelated socioecological phenomena requiring interdisciplinary forms of sense-making; (3) Enacts  relationships with animate materials that have lif e courses toward the fulfillment of communal responsibilities; and (4) Enables continual renewal of family and community to assert contemporary presences and living Indigenous nature-culture relations. Similarly, Author 1 observed Indigenous making and sharing principles during her family’s wild rice camp experiences that unfolded near the waters of L ittle Elbow Lake on Gaa-waabaabiganikaag (White Ear th Nation). In our findings, we further discuss Author 1’s observations of these principles being enacted at wild rice camp as well as the lessons from manoomin we have learned over the course of this literature review.

## Need for Literature Review

Over the past decade, multiple systematic reviews h ave been conducted on constructionist learning acti vities (Papavlasopoulou et al., 2017; Timotheou & Ioannou, 2019). While findings from these literature reviews help us further our understanding of making, few acknowledge the unique contributions of Indigenous learners and their communities as part of the maker movement. As a result, they do little to address tensions surrounding equity and pedagogical practices of the broader maker movement (Vossoughi & Bevan, 2014). Therefore, in this paper, we examined studies incorporating Indigenous notions of making and sharing while maintaining and acknowledging Indigenous ways of knowing and being. We focused so lely on studies that described any kind of learning-bymaking experience with Indigenous youth. The purpose of this review was to examine the current state of scholarship in the maker movement and how it intersects with Indigenous making and sharin g practices. For this systematic review, we examine d empirical studies grounded in Indigenous epistemologies that attempt further exploration of the making practices specific to Indigenous communities. Guided by Baraj as-López’s and Bang’s (2018) definition of Indigeno us making and sharing, we address the following resear ch question: How does scholarship on Indigenous making and sharing define and enact making and sharing principles?

## Procedures & Methodology/Methods Literature Search

We conducted a systematic literature review (Gough et al., 2012) by examining scholarship related to Indigenous making and sharing with youth. We explored article databases in the fields of learning sciences and In digenous education including Education Source, PsycINFO, and JSTOR as well as journal databases such as Associa tion of Computing Machinery (ACM) and the Journal of American Indian Education (JAIE). Keywords used to search the databases included the following and similar te rms: “Indigenous”, “making”, “youth”, “science”, and “technology”. We found a total of 333 articles.

Inclusions Criteria We included empirical articles and conference proce edings published in the last twenty years and appli ed the following criteria:(1) peer reviewed article, (2) f ull text available, (3) written and/or available in  the English language, (4) having Indigenous or Aboriginal young learners in their participant population, and (5) published in an academic journal or conference proceedings. F irst, we evaluated the titles and abstracts of each  article according to the inclusion/exclusion criteria, whic h resulted in 99 articles. Next, we screened for inclusion/exclusion based upon whether each article  was related to learning experiences where Indigeno us learners are engaging in learning-by-making as a result. This resulted in 20 articles included in this review. Classification Criteria We modified and collapsed principles of Indigenous making and sharing identified by Barajas-López and Bang (2018, p. 17) to classify the articles in our liter ature search. Specifically, we classified literature based on these four major categories: (1) enacts and communicates storytelling (2) walking and reflections of sense-making and interrelated socioecological phenomena (3) enacts m aking (including both forms of Indigenous and/or we stern forms of making) and (4) demonstrates renewal of family and community relations. Category classifications were not mutually exclusive.

First, we identified the types of technologies used to create learning artifacts across each of the 20 studies. We classified the articles into learning experiences that utilized original technologies (e.g., making of moccasins, claywork, building of canoes, etc.) versus digital technologies (e.g., digital storytelling, software, etc.). Similar to Barajas-López and Bang (2018), we refer to Indigenous technologies as “original technologies” in order to reclaim old and new Indigenous making and sharing practices  and acknowledge them as more than just “artistic expressions” (pp. 9-10), but rather legitimate productions of the knowledge-making process. Next, we classified ICLS2022 Proceedings 418 © ISLS studies based on what categories of Indigenous making and sharing they utilized (Barajas-López & Bang, 2018). To be classified in the “enacts and communicates st orytelling” category, youth had to engage in either  oral storytelling or digital storytelling practices as a  means to “communicate meaning, feelings, and ways of being” (Barajas-López & Bang, 2018, p. 9). For many of the studies, oral narratives were shared through some form of digital media. Therefore, we utilized the following definition of digital storytelling: “Digital storytelling combines the art of telling stories with a mixture of digital media, including text, pictures, recorded audio narration, music and video” (Robin, 2016) to inform our categories. And lastly, we examined studies that specified the renewal of family and community relations. Studies that utilized this principle had to demonstrate some form of collaboration with Indigenous community members in order to be assigned to this category.

## Findings

We use principles of Indigenous making and sharing (Barajas-López & Bang, 2018) to frame our findings.  We first provide a summary of findings. Then for each principle we provide (1) definitional grounding, (2) discuss related findings from our review, and (3) reflect upon lessons we have learned from manoomin as they relate to each principle.

Studies noted a range of learning environments that  included informal after-school settings, summer camps, inschool spaces, and libraries. Because these st udies demonstrated a single category of “enacted making,” it is a matter of debate as to whether Indigenous making and sharing was truly demonstrated across such studies. Central to In digenous ways of learning and research, the practic e of storytelling “sustains communities and validates th e experiences and epistemologies of Indigenous peop les” (Iseke, 2013, p. 559). Through storytelling, community members can impart cultural and traditional Indigenous knowledge to young learners (Davis, 2014; Iseke, 2013). Learning scientists are starting to examine storytelling practices in their research with Indigenous learners (e.g., Barajas-López & Bang, 2018; Searle et al., 2017; Tzou et al., 2019). For example, Barajas-López and Bang (2018) youths’ participation in storytelling was not only found to allow for Indigenous young people to practice their own forms of storytelling, but it also allowed for them to participate in relationship-building activities with members from their Indigenous communities. Throughout our review, we found storytelling to take on various digital and original forms. Of the studies we found to utilize storytelling (n = 11), nine studies incorporated digital tools (e.g., film, robotics, video games, digital stories) to assist young learners in their story creation process while two studies used oral technologies (e.g., oral traditions) as part of their learning experience. As an example, Carlson and colleagues (2011) illustrated storytelling practices when they asked Elders to te ach part of their science curriculum and share Indi genous perspectives of science with Native students. As part of their study, Elders engaged in ceremonial practices with their students prior to harvesting materials to build heritage crafts (e.g., snowshoes). The practice of storytelling also emerged as a framework for centering “family-making” practices where family members share stories and memories while creating and learning intergenerationally. As an example, Tzou and colleagues (2019) designed a family-based robotics program, called TechTales, grounded in storytelling that was implemented over the course of multiple weekly workshops. Two Indigenous families used both person al and traditional stories to inform the design of dioramas they co-created using robotics as well as other technologies. Tzou and colleagues (2019) argued that engaging in family-making and storytelling helped the families to deepen their own personal and cultural identities while al so participating in acts of “renewal and resurgence ” of Indigenous ways of knowing (Tzou et al., p. 324).

Walking & Sense-Making with Interrelated Socioecological Phenomena For many Indigenous peoples, knowledge and culture are heavily influenced by relationships with the la nd (Brayboy, 2005; Barajas-López & Bang, 2018b). Land-based pedagogies (e.g., Benton, 2017) are commonly used approaches for cultivating environmental awareness among Indigenous learners. Land-based and place-bas ed pedagogies (Feld & Basso, 1996) are similar learning practices due to how deeply situated within the concepts of time and space. Land-based pedagogies also incorpor ate Indigenous ways of knowing by including Traditi onal Knowledge perspectives (Tuck et al., 2014). As an example, Barajas-López & Bang (2018) utilized the principle of “walking and sense-making” with youth as they pa rticipated an I-STEAM program where learners were encouraged to walk while engaging in storytelling involving their surrounding local ecosystems. Youth engaging in sense-making with socioecological  phenomena occurred in less than half of the studies we examined (n = 6). There were a variety of land-based and space-based pedagogies utilized among the studies examined. For instance, Searle and colleagues (2017) employed space-based pedagogies with Native youth during visits to community sites. The sites youth visited then became incorporated into the designs of their games for future visitors to play. Other land-based pedagogies invited youth to manipulate aspects of their physical world. As an example, Carlson and colleagues (2011) provided Ojibwe students direct interactions with physical space by teaching them to harvest maple syrup. This experience encouraged young learners to engage in their people’s ancient traditions while also gaining knowledge in key features of their local food sources. Given the  deep connections between many Indigenous peoples and the ir traditional land and ecologies (e.g., Barnhardt & ICLS2022 Proceedings 420 © ISLS Kawagley, 2005), it is concerning that this way of being is not more represented in current work with Indigenous youth around making and sharing.

Enacts Making Learning through the process of making has taken pl ace for generations of Indigenous learners. In his study of Yupiaq culture, Kawagley (1995) describes how membe rs of his tribe have long engaged in scientific and technological practices while being guided by the “ knowledge of their ancestors” (p. 56). For example,  he describes the science and technology incorporated i nto the design of Yupiaq’s local fish camps. Each Y upiaq family owns a fish camp, which serves as a space for living, fish preparation, and storage during the fishing season. While taking a Yupiaq worldview, Kawagley (1995) proposed an educational program to teach Indigenous youth about scientific phenomena including weather patterns and local fauna and flora. The program also teaches how to use solar energy while creating culturally centered products such as a fish rack for their fish camp. His proposed educational program illustrates the making of cultu rally related artifacts (e.g., fish racks) that cou ld help create authentic learning experiences for students from traditional societies. All studies we examined (n = 20) enacted making throughout a prescribed learning experience (including forms of both digital and original technologies). We observed original technologies (e.g., making of moccasins, claywork, building of birch bark canoes) to reflect Indigenous forms of making versus digital technologies (e.g., digital storytelling, software, etc.) that reflected more western instantiations of making. We classified only nine studies as including forms of original technologies, while 15 studies utilized contemporary western di gital technologies. Three studies utilized both types of technologies and were classified in both categories. For example, Searle and Kafai (2015b) used e-textile constructio n kits combined with traditional sewing practices f or their learning experience with American Indian boys. The medium uniquely pulled in a “strong-community based craft tradition linked to heritage cultural practices and Indigenous Knowledge Systems” (Searle & Kafai, 2015b, p. 31). The nine studies including original technologies sh owcased technologies used for developing land restoration practices (e.g., Bang et al., 2013), claymaking (e.g., Barajas-López & Bang, 2018), making moccasins, harvesting maple syrup, building birch bark canoes (e.g., Carlson et al., 2011) and designing fish racks for drying salmon (e.g., Rickard, 2005). Digital technologies, though, were the most common technologies used in current Indigenous making and sharing experiences with Indigenous youth. Of the 20 studies, we classified 15 learningby-making experiences that utilized some form of di gital and/or digital fabrication technologies. This  included technologies for digital storytelling (e.g., Beltrán & Begun, 2014; Coenraad, 2019; Iseke & Moore, 201 1; Mills et al., 2015; Riecken et al., 2006; Searle et al., 2017; Weston & Biin, 2015; Wexler et al., 2013), game design and development (e.g., Lameman et al., 2010; Webb et al., 2012), and heritage craft design and development  (e.g., Eglash et al., 2013; Kafai et al., 2014; Searle & Kafai, 2015a; Searle & Kafai, 2015b). The majority (n = 8) of the learning experiences included digital technologies that specifically supported digital storytelling. T he types of digital storytelling media ranged from film or video production to augmented reality and interactive storytelling platforms. In sum, there is currently an overrepres entation of western technologies in the learning-by-making workshops with Indigenous youth.

Demonstrates Renewal of Family and Community Relations Including family and community members in the desig n and implementation of learning experiences is a distinguishing feature of Indigenous knowledge syst ems (e.g., Barnhardt & Kawagley, 2005) and Indigeno us making and sharing practices (Barajas-López & Bang, 2018). Members of Indigenous committees can also serve as cultural practitioners, so-called “cultural gatekeepers,” who have critical insights into the cultural practices of Indigenous peoples (Bequette, 2007). Throughout the various forms of these storytelling processes, we reviewed a range from within-family interactions (e.g., Tzou  et al., 2019) to larger across community interacti ons (e.g., Iseke & Moore, 2011). Many of the studies engaged c ommunity members as cultural experts. For example, Riecken and colleagues (2006) conducted a participatory research project where Indigenous youth developed their research questions and conducted interviews with members of their family and community to create a video that they would share with their community.

The renewal of family and community relations invol ves both living and more-than-human relatives (Barajas-López & Bang, 2018). From the perspectives  of Barajas-López and Bang (2018), Indigenous makin g and sharing allow participants to employ their own agency while engaging in the histories and technolo gies of their ancestors and subsequently maintaining such practices for future generations. One of the participants of their I-STEAM project, Miguel, describes his first-time experience in the use of claywork by stating that he “was able to connect with those that came before me” throughout the experience (Barajas-López & Bang, 2018, p. 7). However, this was the only examined review that dem onstrated the renewal of family and community relationships with more-than-human relatives.

To date, many studies that demonstrate aspects of Indigenous making and sharing also involve different forms of community engagement. Of the 20 studies re viewed, 13 studies showed the renewal of family and community relationships in some form during the lea rning-by-making process. Demonstration of renewal o f family and community relationships varied across experiences, but a common thread among the studies was that community members were utilized as cultural experts at some point in time during the making process. Sometimes this took place on a broader scale across communiti es or in a smaller family setting. Researchers like  Tzou and colleagues (2019) conducted their intergenerational, robotics-based storytelling intervention with two  Native American families. Other scholars like Wexler and c olleagues (2013), who implemented multiple digital storytelling workshops across Alaska, took place across multiple communities. Seven articles did not mention any form of family or community involvement. Further, while the studies we reviewed included aspects of family and community engagement, the extent to which families and communities were engaged throughout the entirety of the learning experience, beyond cultural experts, was limited. ## Discussion, Implications, and Limitations

Our analysis helped us to identify ways Indigenous making and sharing approaches have been enacted whi le working with Indigenous young learners and their co mmunities. In the following discussion, we discuss the limitations of our review as well as turn to ways in which manoomin has taught us and continues to teach us. This review seeks to acknowledge the unique contributions of Indigenous peoples to the field of maker education and by further revealing non-normative in stantiations of learning-by-making experiences. The major limitation of our review is that our extensive search yielded only 20 articles. While the amount of scholarship on the maker movement is increasing, there remain few projects that further the principles of Indigenous making and sharing. We found limited literature addressing wal king & sense-making with interrelated socioecologic al phenomena (n = 6) as well as literature demonstrating renewal of family and community relations (n = 13). Even more, of the 20 articles included in this review, four studies only include a single category of Indigenous making and sharing principles, specifically “enacting making,” in their work with Indigenous youth. Taking this together with the fact that most studies also solely utilize d western digital technologies, there is great need  for deeper integration of Indigenous and western making and sharing, especially when working with Indigenous youth and their communities. To this knowledge, as we further explore such integrations, scholars like Barajas-López and Bang (2018) warn us to not “fit or impose Indigenous knowledge systems into Western conceptions of knowledge means maintaining asymmetrical power relations and reproducing inequities and dominance” (p. 20). Afte r all, Indigenous knowledge systems and practices are not assets to be appropriated nor exploited but rather embraced. Throughout our search, we did not identify any guidelines for conducting Indigenous making and sharing principles with Indigenous families and communities. As such, as we continue such scholarship, we must begin to acknowledge our responsibilities toward protecti ng Indigenous making and sharing from appropriation  and exploitation. Sovereignty and stewardship of these practices require the highest levels of protection as we work to provide and sustain reciprocal relations (e.g., Kimmerer, 2015). Like manoomin, wild rice camp and other forms of Indigenous making and sharing are spiritua l entities with their own agencies that require rec iprocal relations. Therefore, as we reflect upon the lessons that manoomin provides us, we also question how and to what extent we as scholars should move to protect the sovereignty of Indigenous making and sharing as its own entity.
