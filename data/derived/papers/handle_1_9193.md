# Investigating Relationship Development Processes Between 3D Conversational Agents and Learners in Collaborative Discussions

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Social agents (SA) like robots or conversational agents can be great catalysts for fostering co-regulation of collaborative discussions as learning peers who help learners maintain apt participation in group discussions. However, prior CSCL studies have rarely addressed SA’s design of sociability, which should be a key for SA to become actual learning peers. This paper describes a qualitative analysis of face-to-face group discussions to investigate the relationship development between learners and a three-dimensional holographic SA. The analysis revealed that the learners’ initial impressions of the SA varied between positive and negative; sometimes the learners even ignored or refuted the SA’s prompt at first. However, the relationship between the SA and the learners improved as the learners recognized the SA’s abilities and potential, so they engaged in the discussion according to the SA’s prompt. The authors discussed the need for research in designing SA and its circumstances to achieve effective facilitation.

## Introduction

Active and equal participation is important for regulation of collaboration (Isohätälä e t al., 2017). An approach for improving such regulation in collaborative learning is providing prompts or scripts from software or an agent to regulate or guide the group members’ activity (e.g., Borge et al., 2018; Vogel, et al., 2016, for a review). Conversational agents are widely used for prompting and scripting computer-me diated discussions, and prior studies showed a range of their effectiveness in supporting collaborative learning (e.g., Dyke et al., 2013). Social agents such as robots and virtual agents are typically applied to regulate face-to-face collaborative learning (Okita & Clarke, 2021). Such social agents for collaborative learning are expected to support interactive learning through dialogues among learners by providing appropriate feedback with less authority than that when teachers do so, thus the learners can be aware of and internalize such scripts in their learning processes. As such, a social agent is expected to work with students as “a learning peer” who helps them enjoy their classroom or small group settings, and thus the students remain engaged in the learning process and maintain apt participation in the discussion (Miyake & Okita, 2012).

However, the relationship development between a social agent and learners has ra rely been considered in the CSCL, even though the literature on peer learning has identified that the quality of the relationship impacts the quality of peer learning (Riese et al., 2011). Even though users may consid er conversational agents as more goal-oriented tools rather than social actors for establishing communication (Rehu et al., 2020), there is a need to investigate how learners perceive an agent and how they establish a relationship with the agent so that they have a quality socio-emotional state with the agent that leads to improve and regulate their collaborative discussion. The aim of the present study is to examine how learners develop relationships with conversational agents in collaborative discussions. We applied critical discourse analysis (CDA) to examine power relations in collective activities so that we could reveal the developmental trajectories of the relationships between them.

## 3D holographic conversational agent supporting regulation in discussion

Mochizuki et al. (2020) developed a three-dimensional holographic embodied conversational agent that can work as a facilitator for a student group discussion, providing suitable collaboration prompts (Strauß & Rummel, 2021) with some verbal and non-verbal reactions (nodding, pointing, turning its face sideways, etc.). The agent system collects all voice data of each member from a headset fitted with a directional microphone and calculates a variety of variables such as how long each student utters within every 20 seconds, a group’s ratio of silence durations for every 20 seconds, each student’s frequency of utterances, the turn-taking frequency and directions, etc. The system calculates such variables in real-time, and the agent provides prompts as  configured when the threshold matches the corresponding pattern.

## Method

The study was conducted in one class hour session of a socio-informatics course at a private univers ity in Japan. Thirty-six undergraduates (Female 25.0%) majoring in informatics attended the session. At the beginning of the session, the instructor told the students that the goal of the session was learning strategies for promoting effective regulation for participation in group discussions through three discussions with or without the agent, followed by explaining the strategies to regulate discus sions (Table 1). Then, the students were randomly assigned to two different conditions: One condition was discussing the  first two topics in a triad with the agent and discussing the third topic in a group of four without the age nt; the other condition was discussing the first two topics in a group of four without the agent and discuss ing the third topic in a triad with the agent.

The students discussed three topics for 12 minutes: dream, happiness, and being an adult. These topics were taken from junior high school textbooks of the Japanese language for learning wa ys of discussions. All the triads and groups were restructured in the third discussion in order to adjust the number of members in accordance with the changes of the discussion forms, i.e., with or without the agent. The discussions were video-recorded and transcribed. This study addresses only the triad discussions with the agent. All the triads had one agent at their table and an operator monitoring and operating th e agent according to signals given by the system, as a preliminary Wizard of Oz experiment (Figure 1). Each operato r also worked as a timekeeper, asking their triad to b egin and end the triad’s discussions. The students knew that the operator was assigned to each group to monitor whether the agent worked appropriately. CDA is not exactly a procedural method for analysis but an approach to understanding power relations in collective activities (Wodak & Meyer, 2015). We believed CDA could be useful in identifying how the relationship between the agent and the students would be realized and transformed during the discussion.

## Results

We identified a variety of learners’ impressions as their recognition of the agent when the first prompt was provided by the agent, such as “This guy is great,” “This guy looks strong,” “Is this  guy talking?,” “Wow, I was asked for my opinion,” “That's interesting.” All these utterances indicate the positive beginnings of relationships and show two aspects of the students’ recognition: their appraisal of the agent and their equal or lower positioning of the agent. “Great” or “strong” indicate recognition of the ability of the agent which has the potential to lead to developing trust toward the agent. The agent’s first reaction was also the s tudents’ first experience to receive reactions from a social agent, therefore this reaction seems to be natura l. “Strong” seemed to indicate fear of the ability of the agent in addition to recognition. However, “this guy” (koitsu in Japanese) is usually used for either belittling or being friendly to the person talked about. This indicates that the stude nts neither fear the agent nor feel its authoritative power.

In contrast, a few triads initially had difficulty establishing good relationships wit h the agent: for instance, they ignored its prompts. Such triads showed their development of a re lationship with the agent after being exposed to several prompts from the agent that made the students recognize the its ability. ## Discussion and implications

This paper describes a qualitative analysis of discussions to examine the  relationship development between learners and a 3D holographic conversational agent designed to help regulate their participation and encourage their co-regulation in a face-to-face discussion. Importantly, there were student groups that attempted to ignore or resist such interventions in the discussion. However, the excerpts from s uch groups showed that an opportunity in which they recognized the agent’s ability and potential could b e a trigger to develop a better relationship between the students and the agent. Consequently, the students engaged in the discussion according to the agent’s prompts.

These findings contribute the research in designing learning environments with social conversational agents in CSCL. To ensure the effectiveness of the scaffolds, the agent designer must conjecture moments in which students can trust the agent when they se e the potential for benefits from the agent’s scaffolds. In addition, the designer should consider learning environments around the agent during its designing b ecause the relationship between learners and the agent is contextualized by the instructor’s ins tructions, the course design, the expected outcome for the students, etc. (cf. Rheu et al., 2020). Describing such factors around the agent is key for enhancing the relationship development to achieve a state where the agent becomes an apt learning peer.
