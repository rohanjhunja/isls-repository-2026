# A Framework for Supporting the Development of Students’ Personal Data Competencies

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research shows that children and adolescents have limited awareness of big data practices and do not understand how and why their personal data are being used. Several scholars call for the need to increase children’s critical literacy competencies in relation to their personal data; however, there is limited published literature on how such kind of literacy can be conceptualized, and what kind of competencies students need to develop. Existing frameworks predominantly focus on adult populations, which do not encompass the unique needs, perspectives, and challenges faced by students in comprehending and managing their personal data. This research, following a systematic literature review and situated in a design-based research larger project, contributes to bridging this gap by introducing the PeDaL framework, a pedagogical tool demonstrating fundamental competencies to support the development of students’ personal data literacy.

## Introduction and motivation for this study

Children, through their online activity and the use of smart technologies, are transformed into data producers of a vast amount of data. Children’s data are collected and analyzed to inform data profiling and algorithmic decisionmaking processes, to offer  targeted products and services, but also to predict  children’s choices and their future behaviors. Children’s  data profil ing may negative ly impact their future opportunities, such as employme nt, banking, health, travel, and access to information, products, or services (Yates et al., 2021). Therefore, it is important to investigate how to help children devel op critical literacy competencies in relation to th eir personal data, especially since children are reported to have difficulties in identifying and understanding the well-hidden techniques of data mining (Livingstone, Stoilova & Nandagiri, 2019). In addition, evidence shows that if they understand these issues to some extent, they do not  know how, or are not willing to employ strategies to protect their personal digital data (Selwyn & Pangrazio, 2018). In some cases, children choose to disclose rather than to protect their personal data to gain incentives and prizes (Desimpelaere, Hudders & Vande Sompel, 2020; Heirman, Walrave and Ponnet, 2013). Scholars call for the development of children’s critical awareness to address the privacy-related risks of the digital era and increase their agency in the data economy (Livingstone et al., 2019). Children should be supported to recognize data minin g practices and to choose whether there is a need t o effectively respond to them, thus increasing their personal data literacy. However, there is limited published work on how to foster such kind of literacy, what kind of literacy efforts work best, or how to address children’s limited understanding and lack of knowledge on the matter. Even though several theoretical and pedagogical frameworks have been proposed, the theoretical ones have been developed with adults in mind, targeting citiz ens rather than children (e.g., Pangrazio & Selwyn, 2019; Yates et al., 2021), while the pedagogi cal frameworks  have not specifically provided explanations  on how designers and practitioners could use them when designing and implementing programs aiming to foster personal dat a literacy (e.g., Kumar & Byrne, 2022; Lee, Wilkerson & Lanouette, 2021; Philip, Schuler-Brown & Way, 2013). Even though  these approaches are useful in framing personal data literacy, they are not focusing on th e issue of children’s data profiling, neither are t hey developed based on empirical research on students’ stances about data privacy and their attitudes on data disclosure requests. Pangrazio and Sefton-Green (2020) discuss how data literacy approaches reported in literature have been implemented in educational settings, noting  that research on data literacy pedagogy is limited.  They state that current data literacy efforts are unable to capture the range of skills and dispositions needed to tackle datafication and that the term needs more theorization to address a more critical and practical approach (Pangrazio & SeftonGreen, 2020). This work contributes towards addressing this issue, by proposing the PeDaL framework to support design efforts and educational programs aiming to increase students’ personal data literacy. The framework was developed through a systematic literature review, which aimed at answering: (1) How can students’ personal data literacy be defined? (2) What competencies does a student need to have to be considered person al data literate?

(3) What educational guidelines are discussed in the li terature as effective pathways to foster personal d ata

literacy?

## Methodology

The papers included in the systematic literature review encompass theoretical and empirical research discussing critical approaches to data literacy. The Scopus database was queried using the following keywords: “Critical data literacy”, “Critical big data literacy”, “Personal data literacy”, “Data privacy literacy”, “Big data literacy”, “Online privacy literacy”, “Privacy literacy”, and “Social media literacy”. The search results were analyzed  using the PRISMA standards and followed three sequential stages: identification, screening, and eligibility. To be included in this systematic literature review, a paper  ought to meet one of the following criteria: It should (1) includ e a definition and/or take a critical approach to data literacy, and/or (2) indicate the necessary competencies a person should have to be considered personal data literate, and/or (3) provide insights on how to foster students’ personal data literacy and approach the matter pedagogically. Thirty publications were finally included in this systematic literature review. The publications were coded to identify competencies discussed as required for a personal data literate student. The coded information was synthesized to formulate a list of initial categories, which were then organized under larger dimensions and aspects.  T his paper focuses on presenting an overview of  the PeDaL framework; further findings from the systematic literature review can be found in Agesilaou (2024). Becoming personal data literate, as presented in the PeDaL framework, is a graded process which begins from the acquisition of mere skills and knowledge, leading to the development of more nuanced understa nding and competencies. However, becoming personal data literate is also a dynamic process, and students may follow different routes for the acquisition of the required competencies. For e xample, a student might need to develop a comprehensive understanding of all the issues repor ted in the Understanding dimension before deciding if and how to act, while for another student an increased awareness on the matter (Knowing dimension) might be enough to prompt skepticism and action as reflected on the Questioning and Doing dimensions. The PeDaL framework The PeDaL framework presents a spectrum of competencies required for students to effectivel y manage their personal data, understand and act in respect to their data profiling and the commercial use of their data. It consists of three aspects (data proficiency, data reflection, and data agency), seven dimensions (Recognizing, Managing, Knowing, Understanding, Questioning, Doing, and Enacting), and encompasses 29 competencies. The Recognizing dimension, under the Data proficiency (practical competencies)  aspect, includes competencies highlighting the importance of recognizing different types of data and being able to  read and interpret different forms of data. If students have difficulties in understanding data visualizations, then they will also face difficulties in  interpreting their own personal data presented in the form of graphs. The Recognizing dimension includes competencies reflecting the skills to read, visualize, interpret, and under stand different types of data (be it qualitative or quantitative). The PeDaL framework highlights the importance of  the Recognizing dimension which can lead to students’ motivation to manage and protect their own personal data. If students do not understand what information is revealed about t hem, they will have no interest in protecting their  data. The ability to recognize and read different types of da ta is mentioned in the literature as an important a spect of data literacy (Yates et al., 2021). In addition, in previous work (Agesilaou & Kyza, 2021) it was found that it is important that students can read and interpret the visualizations of their data, otherwise they feel disengaged and less motivated to participate in relevant discussions.

The Managing dimension, under the  Data proficiency (practical competencies)  aspect, includes competencies reflecting the ability  to work with data, interact with digital environments to locate personal data (for example, retrieve the outcomes from physical activity using fitness trackers), or use data as evidence to support an argument and related claims. The competencies included in the Managing dimension also reflect the technical ability to modify privacy settings, choosing options other than the default settings, understand terms and conditions to choose only necessary options, and im plement data protection strategies to prevent access to personal data that can lead to identification.

The Knowing and Understanding dimensions, which are included under the Data proficiency aspect, focus on conceptual competencies. Agesilaou and Kyza (2022) reported that students exhibit different levels of awareness and understanding  about the uses of their personal data. For example,  they found that students were aware that other entities can have access or are interested in their data, but only few students could explain why or what these entities might do with the data. Students who acknowledge that others can access their data, or can explain data uses, can be seen as  more personal data literate than students who do not recognize that others can access their data. The PeDaL framework recognizes the existence of  such differences and suggests developing students’ competencies gradually, especially due to the complexity of the digital infrastructure and data practices. Knowing includes competencies on raising students’ awareness on several issues, such as, among others, what are exactly personal data, the importance of online pri vacy and data protection strategies, the basics of how online environments function, and knowledge about data rig hts. It also involves awareness about big data, algorithms, artificial intelligence, online and data disclosure risks. Students should have an initial awareness that others have access to their personal data, and even if they do not understand or cannot fully explain who has a ccess to their data and for what purposes, being aware that their online behavior leads to personal data disclosure to other parties, could trigger students to care about their data privacy and, thus, employ data dis closure mitigation strategies. Agesilaou and Kyza (2022) found that students’ misc onceptions on the matter was because they lack know ledge of the basis on which platforms and online applicat ions operate, and do not acknowledge the shared ownership aspect they agree to when accepting the terms and conditio ns. We argue that helping students understand the shared ownership aspect, becoming aware of the cont ent of a privacy notice or cookies policies, and developing the ability to recognize when they concede ownership of data to others, is of vital importance to increase students’ agency on the matter and empower them to take control of their data. Understanding is about students’ ability to start unpacking the u nderlying principles of the data ecosystem: how, where, why, and by whom their data are generated, collected, pr ocessed and stored; how their data flow on the internet; how artificial intelligence and algor ithms work, what data profiling is and for what purposes it is utilized, including potential benefits for the user (such as, targeted advertisements, personalized online experiences, and free access to content). Students should be able to understand the value of their own data and their commercial use by other parties, who own s the data, who can sell them, and who can use them. The Understanding dimension targets a holistic understanding of the data ecosystem, involving issues of data bia ses, data manipulation, and data commodification.

Questioning, under the Data reflection aspect, is about a deeper, critical reflection. Having developed a deeper understanding of the data mining practices, students should start to question themselves about how their online experience has been shaped through their dat a disclosure decisions and how their online behavio r created their digital identity. It also involves the competence to assess one’s fee lings about data disclosure: Now that I know how my personal data are being used, am I feeling comfortable to continue disclosing them? Do I feel more worried about some of my data, but I am comfortable to disclose other data? Shall I proceed to change my privacy settings? Students should begin questioning  the longer-term implications of their online behavior and big data practices. However, we anticipate that this personal reflection will be affected by students’ privacy concerns, their feelings of data ownership, and their disclosure practices. Students who prefer disclosing their personal data  in favor of receiving benefits and rewards,  despite the potential privacy risks in data disclosure situations, are less probable to experience this inner dispute.

Doing, under the Data agency aspect, includes competencies reflecting students’ ability to weigh privacy risks or disclosure request s and making informed decisions. Agesilaou and Kyza (2022) examined students’ disclosure practices to commercial requests of self-tracking data and reported that students’ practices reflected the following specific disclosure criteria: what is the nature of the personal data being shared, how they will be used, by whom and for what purposes. Employing these criteria should help studen ts decide how to respond to d ata disclosure requests from commercial parties or other entities, while at the same time also honoring their personal values. Students should consciously choose to employ spec ific data privacy practices, that correspond to the ir preferences and priorities. For example, students may decide they will continue their daily online activities, such as using social media, purchasing products and serv ices, playing online games, creating and sharing mu ltimedia content, etc., but they will do it with the minimum privacy cost, based on their own priorities and preferences, by adjusting privacy settings, using incognito windows, blocking ad vertisements, using alternative software and search engines that do not collect personal data, and so on. The last dimension, Enacting, under the Data agency aspect, includes competencies reflecting students’ ability, but also willingness, to disseminate their knowledge and skills to their broader community, family, peers, school, friends, etc., to improve other peoples’ lives and help them with their data literacy.

## Concluding remarks

The PeDaL framework aims to promote a comprehensive understanding  towards the socio-technical practices associated with  big data. The intended outcome is to help students develop the ability  to recognize big data practices, comprehend the power dynamics that have been created through the emergence of the se systems, consider the future implications that may arise, make informed decisions regarding the necessity for  adapting online behavior, and having the skills, and motivat ion to do so. The framework lists a spectrum of competencies which, to be fostered, require sustained and consistent efforts, likely on a longi tudinal basis, conceivably implemented through a spiral approach spanning across various age groups. It is envisaged that the framework will stimulate discourse and offer guidance in desi gn and instructional efforts aimed at advancing stu dents' personal data literacy.  The systematic literature review, which informed the development of the framework, encompasses both theoretical and empirical research, as empirical research on the subject remains relatively limited. Future research efforts  can provide avenues for updating and refining the PeDaL framework. Furthermore, additional research is required to examine the comprehensibility and applicability of these competencies across diverse student populations, and various educational settings and cultural contexts.
