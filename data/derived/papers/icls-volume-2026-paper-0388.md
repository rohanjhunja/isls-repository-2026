# Children’s Augmented Reality Narratives: Exploring Forest Entanglements and More-than-Human Storytelling

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how children’s augmented stories compose multispecies relationality through narrative, spatial, and material engagements with the forest. Grounded in posthuman and sociomaterial perspectives, we analyzed how children, environments, and digital technologies participate in storytelling. Findings show that nonhuman beings act ed as co-narrators, the forest shaped narrative development, and augmented reality intra-acted in situ composition, enabling children to construct hybrid storyworlds through embodied and collaborative engagement with place.

This study examines how children’s augmented stories compose multispecies relationality through narrative, spatial, and material engagements with the forest environment. As digital technologies increasingly participate in children’s meaning-making, it is important to understand how such practices can support relational engagements with more-than-human worlds. Augmented storying—the blending of storytelling with augmented reality (AR) — offers a generative site for this inquiry. While prior research has explored digital storytelling and creativity  (e.g., Burnett & Merchant, 2020; Leander & Boldt, 2013; Wohlwend, Buchholz, & Medina, 2018), less attention has been given to how augmented storying unfolds in situ and how environments participate in narrative composition (Kumpulainen et al., 2023). Grounded in posthuman and new materialist perspectives, this study understands storytelling as an emergent, relational process in which children, materials, environments, and technologies intraact. Stories are thus not about the world but composed with it, as children engage with the forest and coordinate digital and physical elements in meaning-making. The study is guided by three interrelated questions:

1. How do humans and nonhumans intra-act and become entangled in children’s AR stories?

2. How do the forest and its more-than-human elements shape and become shaped by children’s AR stories?

3. How does augmented reality mediate the spatial and material composition of children’s stories?

## Theoretical framework

Posthuman, new materialist, and sociomaterial perspectives challenge human-centred accounts of learning by foregrounding the agency of human and more-than-human actors (Barad, 2003, 2007; Bennett, 2010; Haraway, 2016). From this perspective, children’s augmented storying is understood as a situated process in which meaning emerges through embodied engagements among children, environments, materials, and technologies (Kumpulainen et al., 2023). Rather than treating stories as representations, we approach the m as composed with place, as children, story characters, forest elements, and AR technology participate in shaping narrative activity (Fenwick & Landri, 2012; Tanggaard, 2013). To deepen this relational view, we draw on Lenters et al. (2025), who conceptualize children’s storying as mycelial: distributed, nonlinear, and partly in/visible. This perspective highlights how meaning-making unfolds across visible and subtle processes of participation and attention, advancing and re-emerging across time and contexts, challenging linear and outcome-driven models of learning (Burnett & Merchant, 2020; Harwood & Collier, 2017; Jukes, Stewart, & Morse, 2023; Wohlwend, Buchholz, & Medina, 2018).

This study draws on creative and multimodal research strategies to examine the material-discursive intra-actions that account for  children’s augmented storying (Hull & Nelson, 2005; Scott-Stevenson, 2020). Aligned with posthuman and sociomaterial perspectives, the methodology attends to how meaning-making emerges through embodied, relational, and often unpredictable engagements among children, materials, environments, and technologies. The study involved 21 children (aged 6–7) at an elementary school in Western Canada and a nearby forest. Over three months, a project co-designed with the classroom teacher integrated classroom and forest-based activities. Children designed characters, explored narrative ideas, and learned to use the AR application (AR Makr), which enabled them to place and animate story elements within the forest environment. Story development 2590 was iterative, moving between planning and in situ composition across multiple sessions. Children worked in flexible groups of two to four or individually, resulting in 15 AR stories. Teachers and researchers facilitated the process by introducing the AR tool, supporting technical use, and inviting children’s storying and reflection, while primarily adopting an observational and responsive stance. Primary data consisted of the children’s AR stories, complemented by ethnographic fieldnotes, video recordings, and interviews with children and the teacher. Data were analyzed through ethnographic and visual narrative approaches (Moore, 2020; Pink, 2014),  focusing on how digital and physical elements, story characters, and environmental features were composed and related within the stories. Visual analysis involved layered examination of digital characters and physical forest elements to trace their roles and entanglements within children’s narratives.

The forest itself acted as a participatory agent: its roots, branches, and terrain shaped narrative possibilities and influenced movement, power, and plot, blurring boundaries between environment and story. Augmented reality participated in these processes by enabling in situ composition, where digital and material elements were coordinated through embodied collaborative engagement. Together, these dynamics reflect a mycelial form of storying (Lenters et al.,  2025) in which meaning emerges through nonlinear and overlapping relations among children, environments, and technologies.

## Scholarly significance

This study contributes to research on children’s storytelling and digital literacies by showing how augmented storying functions as a relational practice through which multispecies narrative worlds are composed. The findings demonstrate how nonhuman beings, forest environments, and digital elements participate in shaping narrative activity, extending sociomaterial perspectives on learning as distributed and situated. By examining how stories unfold in direct engagement with place, the study highlights the importance of in situ composition for understanding children’s meaning-making and relational attunement with the more-than-human world (see also Renshaw, 2021; Vossoughi, Marin, & Bang, 2024). Methodologically, it advances multimodal and ethnographic approaches for tracing how digital and material elements are coordinated in storytelling. Together, these findings position augmented storying with the living world as a site where relations with the more-than-human world are actively composed rather than represented.
