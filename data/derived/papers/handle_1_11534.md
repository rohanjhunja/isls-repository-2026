# Potions: Caring in Showing with an Asynchronous Partner

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We conceptualize showings as interactions when someone shares and contextualizes their perspectives, experience, practice, and/or interests and provides contextualization. Showings are opportunities for demonstrating care for children and their experience. We analyze how one child demonstrated that she trusted an asynchronous adult interactional partner would care about her and her telling across multiple instances of video-recording her potion making practice. This work contributes to understanding of learning in COVID-competent relations and co-research partnerships.

Framework When a child makes an interactional bid for attendi ng to a particular idea, object, or practice,  adults can share children’s attention and thereby demonstrate care (Keifert & Team Poof, accepted). —“regard for another to enhance the welfare or well-being of the party cared for” (Goodwin & Ceakite, 2 018, p. 121). We focus on showing as a key mechanism by which children share their perspectives, experience, practice, and inter ests and adults demonstrate care. A showing occurs when something is shared and contextualized. Contextualization may include telling of the artifact’s history (e.g., wh en it was made, how it was made/remade), how it ope rates (e.g., how it works, what parts do what), and its purpose (e.g., its job, how it relates to other artifacts, people, or beings). In the present poster, we explore asynchronous show ing. Here we use showing as a part of our framework, but showing emerged out of our work with families. When  asked “What do we do together,” children replied m ost often with “showing” (see Gentry et al., 2024). Giv en the importance of showing to our child co-researchers, we have chosen to explore the interactional mechanisms of showing to honor their leadership.

## Methods

Data are from the Learning, Being, and Doing (LBD) in Families study. LBD developed co-observation, an emergent (Keifert et al., 2024) method merging multiple research traditions with everyday practices (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016) to examine children’s lived  experience (Rogoff et al., 2018). This centered fam ilies’ existing storying practices —documenting, reflecting, theorizing (Ochs et al., 1 992)—with traditions of video-based research (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; DeLiema et al.,  2023). Through COVID-competent virtual visits, families were involved in agenda-setting, data production/collection (e.g., virtual visits, asynchronous digital sharing via Google Photos, texts, videos recordings), data interpretation, and authoring. Further description of the LBD study and methods are available in Keifert & Team Poof (t his volume). In the present manuscript, we focus on  Team Neuron to Something, including Cheetah Owl Unicorn (COU, focal child), Daddy Poppins (DP, Dad), and Dr. S/Tiger Wing (Mom). DP serves as co-analyst and co-author. DP helped to choose these clips as exemplars of showing, as DP and Teo discussed COU’s leadership i n our co-research team. DP provide critical contextualization of COU’s practice within their fa mily history, cultural practices, and geopolitical events (e.g., COVID). In this poster, we focus on COU’s practice of making potions in order to understand how showings were accomplished through asynchronous means in Team Neurons as COU recorded videos for Teo (LBD PI). ## Discussion: A Case of (Asynchronous) showing

COU’s showings above illustrate that she was accust omed to Teo’s habits of attending during synchronou s showings including asking questions about COU’s pot ion making, materials, their uses/functions, and ot her understandings of COU’s magical worlds. Through syn chronous showings, Teo demonstrate d routine care, building COU’s trust in Teo-as-(interactional)partner for showings. This trust was on full display as COU interacted with the Teo as a non-synchronous interactional partner. DP noted that when COU was filming for Teo, her time was respected both by parents and by COU herself, attending for longer periods of time in ways than was typical of other virtual calls (e.g., with aunts). We suggest here that this extended-time “with Teo” built on Teo’s history of questions and curiosities about COU’s pr actice. DP described this as trust that Teo would a ttend to COU’s interests, curiosities, stories, and ongoing activity with care. Moreover, DP reflected that after a few years working together, he has become “sort of addicted t o seeing [curiosity and perspectives] in my child w hen we have interactions” shaping forms of care in their f amily interactions.  The learning that happened was COU learning to trust Teo, and the learning that became possible as a result was what adults could learn from a child.

## Significance

The emergent marginalized COVID-competent community is inadequately studied. Contrary to deficit perspectives of this community, LBD data illustrate the multitude of ways that ca re and trust have developed between children/families and uni-based team members. COU’s asynchronous showings ill ustrate her trust that adults are caring interactional partners. As Learni ng Sciences resear ch comes to better understand COVIDcompetent community, LBD can serve as a guide for understandi ng the development of care and trust in co-research via virtual visits and how synchronous routines can support asynchronous components of collaboration. This approach and understanding might also be relevant for understanding international learning for members of the diaspora  (Madianou, 2019).  Furthermore, LBD and COU’s asynchronous showing sup porting expanding “what we know about human possibilities” (Philip et al., 2018, p. 83) by understanding learning at the intersection of local interactions, cultural repertoires, histor ies of participation, and geopolitical events (Dani el et al., 2023). This is critical, ongoing work in our field, particularly in relation to the emergent COVID-competent community.
