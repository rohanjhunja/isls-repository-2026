# Validating a Code-Free Computational Thinking Assessment for Elementary School Students: Preliminary Results

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Computational thinking (CT) assessments are increasingly available, but it is still challenging to measure the impact of CT interventions. Assessments employing the same coding context as the intervention confer an advantage to treatment students, and possibly also to comparison group students with prior coding experience. Here we report initial findings of our effort to validate a CT assessment without a coding context and to account for prior coding experience in the validation process.

## Introduction

The increasing number of computing-related careers has expanded the essential skills students need to develop throughout their school years. Such skills include computational thinking (CT), which Shute et al. (2017) defined as “the conceptual foundation required to solve problems effectively and efficiently (i.e., algorithmically, with or without the assistance of computers) with solutions  that are reusable in different contexts” (p. 151).  Due to its roots in computer science (CS), CT has been nurture d through engaging students in computer programming  or coding-infused activities, such as scientific computational modeling where students build simulations of scientific phenomena in a coding environment. Therefore, many available CT assessments use coding languages as th e context of questions (e.g., Román-González et al., 2017; Wiebe et al., 2019). This practice does not a lign completely with Shute et al.’s CT definition, which  includes contexts beyond programming. In addition,  using coding contexts in a CT assessment gives students w ith prior coding experience an advantage because th ey are more familiar with such representations compared to  students with no experience. Hence, such an assess ment might lead to biased interpretations and difficulty measuring the impact of CT activities. Relatedly, code-centric CT assessments might not be usable when conducting a study comparing computational modeling activity t o a ‘business as usual’ condition in which students hav e not been exposed to coding, as the assessment wou ld be overly aligned with the intervention. We encountere d such a situation in the project from which this v alidation study stems. In response, we assembled a coding-free CT assessment for elementary school students to minimize pre-existing differences in students’ prior coding experiences. The questions in this CT assessment measure five out of six CT facets proposed by Shute et al. (2017): decomposition, abstraction, algorithms, debuggin g, and generalization. This paper attempted to answer one research question: is this CT assessment valid and reliable?

## Context and Participants

This study is part of a larger project focused on exploring the impact of learning mathematics through computer programming on fifth graders’ CT skills. The study is still ongoing; the data we used in this paper were from the pilot study. A total of 127 fifth-grade students (age M = 10.52, SD = 0.66) participated in this pilot study and took the CT assessment. These students were from rural a reas in a central US state and predominantly White (72%), 2% Black/African American, 1% Latinx, 6% mixed-race, and the remaining 19% did not provide their racia l group information. Regarding gender, 46% of student s identified as female, 48% male, and 6% opted not to answer the question. The number of students who reported never having any experience with coding (31%) was almost equal to the number of students who had some coding experience (at least a one-time experience learning how to code; 64%), and 5% of students did not report their prior coding experience. The Code-Free CT Assessment The pilot assessment was comprised of 16 questions. The questions were drawn mainly from three CT instruments for elementary school students: Bebras CT Challenge s, the National Assessment of Educational Progress’ s (NAEP) mathematical problem-solving questions, and Math and Computational Thinking Through 3D Making (MPACT) assessment. Among the 16 questions, nine were from Bebras Challenges, five were from NAEP, and two were from MPACT. These questions were selected based on their concordance with Shute et al.’s (2017) CT facets. Each question measures multiple CT facets. Hence, we conceptualized that this assessment is unidimensional – measuring one construct, which is CT.

## Analysis and Validation Procedure

Rasch modeling was performed to gather evidence of the assessment’s construct validity. Infit and outfit meansquare (MNSQ) values generated through Rasch modeli ng were used to evaluate whether each question coul d differentiate students with low CT scores from ones  with higher CT scores. The MNSQ cutoff of 0.7 – 1. 3 proposed by Wright and Linacre (1994) was used in t his study. In addition, differential item functioni ng (DIF) analysis was run to identify any questions exhibiti ng bias based on students’ prior coding experience.  We used the cutoff of p <.01 for X213 to identify significant DIF as suggested by Choi et  al. (2011). The questions either flagged as biased in the DIF analysis and/or having  infit and outfit MNSQ values outside the acceptabl e range were removed. Reliabilities were computed using Rasch item and person reliabilities and Cronbach’s alpha.

## Initial Findings and Future Directions

We found that almost all questions had MNSQ values within the acceptable range and were not flagged as  DIF prior experience, except Questions 13 and 16. Quest ion 13 particularly had both MNSQ values outside th e acceptable range and were flagged as DIF item, and Question 16 was flagged as a DIF item. We then remo ved these two questions and re-ran the analysis. The fo urteen-question assessment (Table 1) had acceptable  person and separation reliabilities as well as Cronbach’s alpha values which were.64,.61, and.66, respecti vely. Furthermore, even though the findings do not yet have high interpretative power due to the limited sample size, we believe that the results are promising, especial ly toward establishing a more generalizable CT asse ssment. Hence, we plan to collect more data to strengthen the current findings and to have a more valid and re liable CT assessment for elementary school students.
