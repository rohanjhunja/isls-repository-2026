# The Tensions of Making Space: How Teachers Work Toward Heterogeneous Science Sense-Making

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As science education navigates the tensions between convergence-seeking and heterogeneity-seeking images of science, this paper investigates how elementary science teachers work within and around these tensions as they make space for student sense-making. We focused on two teachers who participated in a multi-year professional development and analyzed moments when they engaged in both responsive and culturally responsive teaching practices. The first teacher used distributed scaffolds (i.e., zooms) to expand participation in science activities and foreground students’ prior knowledge about the phenomenon, but often toward canonical or dominant cultural knowledge. Our second teacher showed a shift in her practice by restructuring a categorization activity from a pre-determined worksheet to having students determine the categories. We argue that making space practices that align more with culturally responsive and sustaining pedagogies could help teachers pursue students’ ideas towards heterogeneity-seeking images of science.

## Motivation and research questions

According to one of our teacher participants: “The process of [science] is really organic, not as static as we make it...we just have this really narrow vision of what science has been.” The “we” refers to teachers and stakeholders with power (e.g., district leaders, policy makers) to enforce standards that K-12 science teachers fee l immense pressure to cover. The most recent science standard reforms represent an important paradigm shift from focusing on knowledge attainment to authentic practices (NGS S Lead States, 2013). This paradigm shift is often implemented in convergence-seeking ways: students w ork to collaboratively build shared understandings and knowledge, drawing from their diverse initial ideas towards an accepted set of understandings represented in state standards (e.g., “storylines;” Reiser et al., 2021). This image of science is fundamental to making sc ientific progress, though it can often overlook the messines s, uncertainty, and multiple existing theories abou t science phenomena common in professional science practice (Pickering, 1995). Therefore, a heterogeneity-seeking image of science, where divergent explanations and processes for pursuing ideas about scientific phenomena are valued, is a lens used to validate students’ multiple ways of knowing (Warren et al., 2020) moving towards more equitable sense-making and science learning. As we re-imagine  how these two images of science (convergence-seeki ng, heterogeneity-seeking; Pierson et al., 2020) inform  science lesson planning and enactment, we ask: How  do science teachers navigate working within—or around— the tension between accountability to standards and  to equitable sense-making?

## Theoretical framework

In response to our teachers’ desire to broaden the vision of what science can be, we built on the work of Haverly et al. (2018) and asset-based pedagogies (Warren et al., 2020) to contribute to conceptualizations of how teachers can make space  for equitable sense-making—collaboratively constru cting knowledge with students’ diverse epistemic resources—in classrooms. Our PD design also built on responsive teaching (Robertson et al., 2015) and culturally responsive teaching (Gay, 2018). In our PD, we tried to support teache rs to see students’ disciplinary strengths from a responsive teaching lens, but this  pedagogical approach often assumes science is neut ral or objective and that the goal of science education is conceptual change, usually toward a specific learning objective. At the same time, we saw examples of our teachers e nacting culturally responsive and culturally sustaining pedagogies (Alim & Paris, 2017). As we continue to make space  for students’ diverse ideas to be added to collective scientific sense-making, we must also co nsider: “the ways in which young people are enactin g race, ethnicity, language, literacy, and their engagement with culture is always shifting and dynamic” (Alim  & Paris, p. 7).

To examine our research question (How do teachers navigate the tension between accountability to standards and to equitable sense-making?), we prese nt two cases that elucidate some ways teachers navi gated both within and around tensions between these two images of science.

## Methods

We focused on two of nine teachers who were all part of a larger, multi-year project in a Southeast city of the US. Our first case study (Yin, 2014) follows Anne, who taught first grade and was in her second year of te aching. Soren is our second case study teacher, who taught kindergarten, and was in her 14th year of teaching. We collected video data from two to three lesson observations pe r teacher and four video club professional developm ent meetings each academic year where teachers presente d a recorded clip from their classroom practice to collectively reflect on representations used in cross-grade-level groups (Sherin & van Es, 2005). We present data from classroom (observations and recorded clips) and video club data in our findings. Our research tea m then engaged in iterative video analysis using interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). We (1) time indexed and content logged videos (Derry et al., 2010); (2) wrote memos and timestamped episodes when teachers made space (e.g., validated student thinking, adding zooms on diagrams); and (3) collectively viewed episodes to refine our hypotheses (Engle et al., 2007) about how teach ers worked within or around the tensions between convergence-and heterogeneity-seeking images of science.

## Findings

Our first case study showed Anne used common distri buted scaffolding practices (Tabak, 2004; e.g., zoo ms on diagrammatic representations) to make space for broadened student participation. We argue space was made for students from historically minoritized backgrounds to contribute to the science storyline, but towards an image of science that was mostly convergence-seeking. Our se cond case study (Soren) represented an example of h ow a teacher’s making space and distributed scaffolding practices shifted from convergence-seeking to more heterogeneity-seeking images of science, presenting  more and different opportunities for sense-making.  These examples will show the variety of perspectives teachers may be operating under in respect to the paradigm shift we propose, and how there was still room for teachers to push forward toward authentic epistemic heterogeneity and validating students’ epistemic agency to author their own sense-making processes. Anne: A zoom that prompted a teacher reflection on epistemic authority In Anne’s classroom, she used diagrammatic represen tations to help first graders understand the relati onships between heat and energy. Specifically, the tool Anne used was a zoom. The zoom was introduced during PD as a learning scaffold that “lets us see something that is not really observable.” Using this tool as “microscope eyes,” students can make visible something that was not seen before. This analysis centers on Anne sharing a clip of her classroom teaching during a video club with other teachers. In the classroom video, Anne followed one student’s  idea to add a zoom coming from a stick figure’s foot. Then, the student suggested adding “red stuff” to the foot inside the zoom because “your feet will be red if they burn.” Anne used the color red as a convergenc e-seeking scaffold to highlight relationships betwe en heat transfer and the sun. Pivoting to the whole class, she took up the student’s comment about red burns asking, “Does anyone else know anything about burns? Anyone ever had a sunburn?” When only one student replied “yeah,” Anne responded, “Maybe? Maybe?” After a beat, Anne called on the student who replied and continued her line of questions about sunburns. “What happens to your skin when you get a sunburn?” Anne asked. “It—it tu rns red,” the student replied. From this affirmation, A nne turned to the representation on the board, adde d a zoom, and drew a foot inside it—toes and all. When the video was paused, Anne explained to the video club group that mentioning the sunburn “was an ‘uh-oh’ moment” because as “a white teacher when I think later, I was t alking about a sunburn… And, I was like, ‘Well that was a privileged moment of me.’” In this moment, Anne recognized that her assumption that all students could relate to having a red sunburn was based on the notion that everybody had experienced red sunburns, when “not all of [her] kids get a red sunburn.” From this “uh-oh moment,” Anne pressed on in the video. “What color do you think w e should color that foot?” Anne asked. In response, she got two answers from her students, “red” and “brown.” In this moment the tension became fully r ealized and Anne had to navigate between the lived reality of studen ts’ different races (of which a majority were non-w hite), and the color red that had surfaced as a scaffold for t he transfer of energy as heat. To reconcile this te nsion, Anne offered one student a skinny brown marker to “scribble-scrabble” in the color of the foot, while at the same time, Anne leaned over to color in spots on the foot with a skinny red marker to signify a “burn on [the] foot from [the] ICLS2022 Proceedings 1190 © ISLS sun.” Overall, this episode portrayed the tensions teachers navigate between convergence-and heteroge neityseeking images of classroom practices particularly as teachers attend to experiences different than their own. Soren: Evolution of an activity We now compare two instances of Soren teaching a similar lesson in Year 1 and Year 3 (Y1 and Y3). Activity Structure In the Y1 video, Soren designed an activity for students to record properties of materials at each of several stations; in the Y1 classroom video students were at the wood  station. Soren constrained students’ observations of wood objects by directing students on what part of the spatula to draw on their papers: “Draw the stick part, that’s the wood part.” In contrast, in the Y3 classroom video, Soren asked students to explore “different ways that we can sort” putting them in groups with bags including a mix of materials. In the Y3 video club, Alexis  (3rd) noted she liked how Soren gave “them the space to pick out what things they see…it’s like a mix of different reasons that they had to sort it.” Alexis emphasized opportunities Soren designed to allow students’ onto-epistemic authority (Warren et al., 2020) to define how to sort and wha t were reasonable sorting decisions. Indeed, Soren’ s design made space for students to sort by color, use, mate rial, and sound. In Y1, Soren worked to ensure that  students recorded information on their worksheet towards the convergence-seeking image of science where the standards correct answers were treated as the only right answers. In contrast, Soren in Y3 worked to create space for students’ onto-epistemic agency and drew upon their contributions as part of meaningful disciplinary sensemaking. Interactional Patterns There were significant shifts in interactional patterns from Y1 to Y3. In Y1, Soren emphasized a one-o n-one in series interactional pattern (Keifert, 2015) in which a teacher talked to one student, and then the next without other children talking (though they could observe). This is visible in Y1 when Ben wanted to connect the sha rp point on his pencil to the sharp points of the pinecone t hat another student had identified, Soren responded  “Oh, I’m asking [another student] but I like your connection. If you hold onto that connection, we’ll talk abou t it.” In contrast, in Y3, Soren emphasized collaborative discussion when one of the three students in the group described their decision making for their sorting, she responded, “Orange Tiger, do you agree with that?” and “Blue Cheetah, I like your thinking, but you need to share it with  your group,” to support students to learn how to e ngage with one another. In the Y3 video club, Regina (K) ackno wledged developing this practice as a challenge and  noted the practice of supporting shared sense-making between students could be difficult because they are still learning how to “verbalize their thinking.” Regina emphasized to Soren, “You checked in with each kid in the group. Like ‘You shared this, did you hear what he said? Do you agree? Do YOU agree?’” The Y3 shifts to encourage students to speak to each other was valued by teachers but also acknowledged as a challenge. Feeling Phenomena In Y1, Soren directed students towards what they should be noticing with their eyes, hands, and ears. For instance, when a child was struggling to describe the texture of a stick, Soren provided significant guidance when she said “Look. Is it smoo-oo-ooth (rubs hand slowly over su rface of the floor) or bumpy (lifts hand and moves it in a wave-like gesture)?” and then repeated the question  and gesture several more times before responding t o the student with, “ Good job! It’s bumpy! Excellent! That’s how it feels. You can write that down.” In this exchange, Soren pushed the student towards describing what he  felt to match her expected answer. In Y1, Soren al so supported a student in attending to sound as he tap ped a block of wood: “I saw you hitting it with you r hand (Soren hits her own hands together). What are you trying to discover?” and affirmed that the wood was hard. In Y3 when a child knocked two plastic items together, she asked: “What were you doing? Show us again what you were doing with them.” This time the student replie d, “Because they sound the same so I put them toget her.” Soren affirmed saying, “Oh, you were testing…how they sound and that helps you know what they were mad e of?” Even though she affirmed students’ reasoning in both cases, in Y1 Soren dictated what kinds of pr operties should be used while in Y3 Soren focused on revoicing student thinking. Thus, in Y1 Soren directed what to sense while in Y3 she followed students’ reasons for their ways of investigating the materials.

## Discussion and Implications

These case studies help answer the question of what  happens after space is made for student sense-maki ng, by showing how elementary science teachers navigated t ensions within and around the convergence-seeking a nd heterogeneity-seeking images of science education. With Anne, she embodied the attribute of culturally sustaining teaching as comprehensive and inclusive. Even thoug h her lesson used a collective model that our PD unknowingly communicated as a convergence-or consensus-seeking tool, Anne welcomed an unexpected piece ICLS2022 Proceedings 1191 © ISLS of cultural knowledge to the class model. Soren als o showed us how culturally sustaining teaching can be empowering: she redesigned her activity from Y1 to Y3 so that the children directed and decided the rules of the categorization activity, allowing them to use an expansive set of sense-making resources (e.g., matching colors, hitting materials and comparing the hardness or sou nds). As researchers, we are continually learning a longside our teachers as we pursue a heterogeneity-seeking image of science in our classrooms. While these examples are not an exhaustive list of ways to promote culturall y sustaining and heterogeneity-seeking pedagogies i n the classroom, we do want to highlight these moments as starting points to continue studying what making space and validating multiple ways of knowing look like in classrooms where there is still tension and immense pressure to meet predetermined convergence-sought standards. In  future PD meetings, we hope to design resources mo re intentionally to promote heterogeneity-seeking images of science.
