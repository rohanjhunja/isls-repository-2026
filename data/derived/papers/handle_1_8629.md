# Biology Isn’t Black and White: Deconstructing Biology Instructors’ Knowledge Networks of Biological Processes to Explore Nuance

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To fully appreciate the intricacies of biology, undergraduate students must understand how the components and processes of complex biological systems relate to each other over space and time. However, building normative knowledge networks and transferring across biological contexts is a challenge. In this study, we sought to identify the features of a model describing the relationships between three fundamental processes in biology: cell-cell communication, gene regulation, and phenotypic expression. Through interviews with six biology instructors, we found a diversity of knowledge networks relating the processes. Further investigation revealed that their knowledge networks are influenced by their definitions of phenotype, their biological disciplines, and accumulated professional experiences. Our data reinforce the idea of situated cognition and suggest that a single, acontextual knowledge network is difficult to transfer across biological contexts. We suggest educators invite students to critique models and to engage in explicit instruction about how and when to use them.

Understanding complex systems in biology A core concept in undergraduate biology education is for students to understand ‘Systems,’ which describes how organisms “integrate internal and external information, incorporate feedback control, and allow them to respond to changes in the environment” (Brownell et al., 2014, p. 208). Cell-cell communication (CCC), gene regulation (GR), and phenotypic expression (P) are three relev ant biological processes cells and organisms use to  interact with and respond to their environment. To explain h ow biological phenomena occur, it is important for biology students to understand the relationships between th ese three processes and how to apply them to unders tand a complex system. The theory of knowledge integration serves as a useful framework to investigate how individuals relate the three processes. During knowledge integration, learners process new information, organize these ideas or facts, and then integrate their thoughts into a network of knowledge (Clark & Linn, 2003). Ideally, a knowledge network built in one biology classroom can be transferred and utilized in another biology classroom. To facilitate the construction of knowledge networks and their transfer across contexts, educators can use simple frameworks as a tool to scaffold student learning (e.g., Hmelo-Silver & Pfeffer, 2004). Therefore, to aid s tudents in their learning of biological systems, we sought to derive a normative model regarding the relationships between CCC, GR, and P. We leveraged the theory of knowledge int egration to reveal and describe instructors’ knowle dge networks and answer an overarching research question: What are the features of biology instructors’ knowledge networks in regards to three foundational processes in biology (i.e., CCC, GR, and P)?

## Methodology

Population. We recruited 6 biology instructors from a large public Midwestern university with very high research activity who had previously taught introductory courses to biology major students. (IRB #1806020745) Procedure. These data come from a larger study aimed at reve aling knowledge of and the connections between the three biological processes: GR, CCC, and P. We used semi-structured, think-aloud interviews guided by the theory of knowledge integration to elicit and characterize knowledge networks such as definitions of the processes and what ideas they use to describe connections between the processes. In this paper, we present results from the question, “How are these three processes r elated?” in which instructors diagramed and explained their knowledge networks. The interviewer also asked follow-up questions to fully explore participant ideas. Analysis. We used knowledge integration as an analytical framework and built models (i.e., knowledge networks) to represent how the participants connected the three processes based on the participant-drawn diagrams and conversations. The diagrams drawn by each participant were used as the foundation of the relational models, and features of the models were described using fie ld notes and thematic coding (Creswell, 2013). Thro ugh an iterative process involving independent coding, con sensus discussions, and revision, a final codebook was developed to describe the knowledge networks. Finally, coding of the full dataset by the first author and an outside reader was conducted. All coding was discussed to complete agreement. ICLS2022 Proceedings 1896 © ISLS

## Variations in definitions and the importance of context

Most of the instructors (4/6) described phenotype as an effect or the outcome of the genotype or as an end product of gene regulation. In contrast, some instructors (2/6) loosely defined phenotype and viewed it as any physical or measurable characteristic of the cell, not strictly coming from gene regulation. We found that instructors with the loose definition described P as interconnected and intim ately involved with CCC and GR (Figure 1D) whereas instructors with the outcome definition did not (Figure 1A, B, and C). With fur ther probing of the 4 instructors who viewed phenotype as an outcome, 2 instructors declared that they could not concep tualize P as having any feedback to GR or CCC, but the other 2 instructors conjectured that there are certain contexts where P could affect GR or CCC. Indeed, most instructors (5/6) acknowledged that the relationships between the three processes can be drawn differently in other biological contexts, for different purposes, and depending on the indivi dual (e.g., personal definitions, expertise). To this point, we  found two instructors who chose to describe their knowledge networks in the context of their disciplinary backgrounds.

## Conclusions and implications: appreciating the nuance

In our study, we did not identify a single, normative relational model of the three biological processes. Although educators desire to give students common frameworks that they can transfer and apply to multiple subjects, it is clear that the relational model in our study is str ongly context-dependent and its transferability is limited. The issue is that transferability neglects the nature a nd circumstances in which the specific knowledge co ntent was built (Brown et al., 1989). While practicing application of the acontex utalized model across biological contexts could still be beneficial, a more useful approach would be inviting students to reflect and critique the model and engaging in explicit instruction about how and when  to use the framework (e.g., layering on detail, exceptions, uncertainty, nuance). Biology is a complex subject with few rules and boundless flexibility. This same flexibility needs to be applied when teaching and reasoning with foundational processes.
