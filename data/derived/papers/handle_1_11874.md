# The Role of Transactivity in Pre-service Teachers‘ Collaborative Diagnostic Reasoning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Diagnostic competences are vital for teachers, enabling them to interpret classroom situations and provide effective feedback. This exploratory study investigates transactivity patterns in collaborative diagnostic reasoning (CDR) among pre-service teachers from same-and multidisciplinary dyads. Transactivity is operationalized through the factors of novelty (introducing new ideas) and reference (connecting to prior contributions). Four dyads diagnosed the instructional quality of a lesson depicted in a short video. Their discourse was recorded and analyzed for sequential changes in novelty and reference. The results reveal that changes in novelty and reference are partly in parallel, partly opposite. While novelty generally decreases over time, the pattern for reference is more inconsistent. Notable differences emerge between same-and multidisciplinary groups, with the latter showing higher reference scores in later phases of the discussion. These findings highlight the dynamics of novelty and reference in collaborative problem-solving over time and inform scaffolding of video-based CDR.

Collaborative diagnostic reasoning Diagnostic competences are considered crucial in many professi ons, including the medical and teaching profession. Teachers‘  diagnostic competences are not only essential for s tudent achievement, classroom management, and instructional quality (Kramer et al., 2021), but they are also considered a prerequ isite for delivering effective feedback on teaching and learning (Prilop et al., 2024). In the teaching context, diagnosing is broadly describe d as „the goal-orientated collection and interpretation of case-specific or problem-specific information to reduce uncertainty in order to make […] educational decisions“ (Heitzmann et al., 2019, p. 4). According to the competence as a continuum model, diagnostic competences include situation-specific skills that mediate between the cognitive disposition (e.g. kno wledge) and the quality of the diagnosis. These sit uationspecific skills have often been referred to as Professional Vision, or as diagnostic activities (Kramer et al., 2021). The Collaborative Diagnostic Reasoning (CDR) model describes a process where two or more physicians diagnose a patient’s problem collaboratively by engaging in both diagnostic and social activities (Radkowitsch et al., 2021). The model builds on the scientific reasoning and  argumentation framework, outlining eight activitie s of diagnostic reasoning (Fischer et al., 2014). However, the CDR model only focuses the four activities generation and evaluation of evidence, generation of hypothese s and drawing concl usion as diagnostic activities. Accordingly, individuals create a shared evidence and hypotheses space by engaging in the collaborative problemsolving activities sharing, elicitation, negotiating, and coordination (Liu et al., 2015; Radkowitsch et al., 2021). While most research in the teaching context investigates individual diagnos is, recent studies also emphasized the benefits of collaborative learning for teachers ‘ diagnostic reasoning. For example, Pickal et al.

(2023) hint at the importance of collaborative and diagnostic activities  for the accuracy of the collaborative

diagnosis of students‘ skills. However, research in vestigating CDR in teacher education still remains scarce (Brandl et al., 2024). One particular research gap relates to the benefits  and challenges of teachers from same or different disciplines collaborating with each other. While a different disciplinary background may enr ich perspective and viewpoints to be discussed about (N oroozi et al., 2013), it may also be detrimental to  beneficial discussions due to a lack of group awareness and common ground (Buder & Bodemer, 2008) Transactivity in collaborative diagnostic reasoning Transactivity is defined as the extent to which lea rners in collaborative learning settings build on e ach other’s contributions (Teasley, 1997). Transactivity of learners’ discourse has been identified as a predic tor of collaborative learning and problem-solving solving outcomes (Vogel et al., 2016; Noroo zi et al., 2013). In research about effects of transactivity in social l earning processes on individual learning  outcomes, a level of transactivity is mostly conceptualized by the speci fic actions taken whi le being transactive and the amount of these actions emerging in a social learning process (Berkowitz & Gibbs, 1983). Categorizing these actions in high or low level transactivity mostly relates to the co gnitive elaborations needed to execute them. Straig htforward actions, such as paraphrasing or requesting feedbac k, are categorized as low level transactivity. More  complex actions, such as critiquing the other contributions  or integrating different opinions, are categorized  as high level transactivity (Teasley, 1997). While both levels involve building on previous contributions, the high level involves sharing more new insights and they demand a higher level of cognitive involvement related to individual learning (Chi & Wiley, 2014). A drawback of this conceptuali zation is that  it does not clearly specify how dimensions, such as referring to other contributions or the cognitive engagement level, contribute to transactivity and its effect on learning. Thus, Vogel et al. (2023) extended the  traditional view on transactivity suggesting novel ty and reference as defining factors to better capture the  dynamics of effective collabora tive learning. While novelty introduces new ideas to the discussion, reference e nsures that contributions remain interrelated. Thro ugh the alternation between novelty and reference, collabor ative learning and knowledge co-construction can be supported. This conceptualization addresses limitat ions of previous models and integrates the sequenti al nature of novelty and reference in learner’s discourse.

We assume that transactivity is also an important feat ure for understanding the underlying processes of CDR. Simulation-and video-based learning is considered an effective way to fo ster diagnostic competences among practitioners and teachers, both individually and collaboratively (Radkowitsch et al., 2021). However, little is known about the CDR processes in teacher educati on (Brandl et al., 2024). Using novelty and reference as defining factors of transactivity could inform meas urements and scaffolding in collaborative learning environments for CDR. To explore the interplay betw een novelty and reference in transactive exchange a nd the dynamic over time, we pose following research questions: RQ1: To what extent do transactivity patterns chang e over time and phases of CDR discourses of pre-service teachers?

RQ2: To what extent do CDR discourses of same and m ultidisciplinary pre-service teachers show similarities and differences in their patterns of transactivity?

At first, the participants watched and analyzed the video independently while being prompted to conduct three individual diagnostic activities  (evidence generation, e vidence evaluation and  d rawing conclusions). Evidence generation prompted the students to describe the teaching approach taken by the observed teacher. Evidence evaluation required them to asses s the effectiveness of the described approach and p rovide justification. Drawing conclusion involved presenti ng an al ternative teaching approach (Kramer et al., 2021). Subsequently, the two participants discussed their individual diagnosis online and formulated a joint feedback for the observed teacher. The conversations were recorded, transcribed and eventually coded for transactivity by two coders. Differences in coding were discussed until a consensus was reached leading to further improved  coding rules.

Coding rules Transactivity as dependent variable was operational ized by the level of each of the two factors novelt y and reference for each individual contribution during the collaborative process. Our operationalization is based on the shared thematic focus (Weinberger et al., 2013). For novelty, one point w as credited to each new overarching topic that was not mentioned before. Only 0.5 point s were assigned for each new thematic aspect added in the contribution that would not be extensive enough to count as an overarching topic. Furthermore, 0.5 points were assigned for each question or invitation to speak p osed to the peer but that did not mention topic rel ated aspects. For reference, one point was assigned for each over arching topic from the conversation the contributio n related to. Only 0,5 points were assigned for contributions  relating to earlier aspects or topics without talk ing about content (such as quick affirmative expressions). Also, 0.5 points were given when the author answered a question or reacted to an invitation to speak.

## Results

Participants discussed their responses from the individual activities, with evidence evaluation being the dominant diagnostic activity throughout the collaborative di scourse. This indicates that dyad s e ngaged in collaborative reasoning with a focus on evaluation of evidence (e.g.: “It was a very teacher-centered lesson.”). They engaged less often in other diagnostic activities, such as generation of hypotheses (e.g.: “Maybe this was just planned for this particular lesson, and in previous lessons, th ey had already worked a lot individually or in grou ps …”) or drawing conclusions (e.g.: “She could have given the students the opportunity to bring in their own examples.”). ## Discussion

The pattern of starting with high novelty scores and a decrease over time does not always occur. While gro up partners from same disciplines may be quicker to ex change novel information, in multidisciplinary grou ps more exchange may be needed over time to achieve a commo n ground. Likewise, reference scores increase for multidisciplinary more than for same disciplinary groups towards the end of the discussion. To implement future scaffolding in similar learning environments, this may be a hint towards strengthening group awareness  for multidisciplinary groups in the beginning (Buder & Bodemer, 2008). Whereas groups from same disciplines may benefit from a script prompting further uptake of p revious contributions particularly on the later pha se of the exchange (Vogel et al., 2017). Also, the decrease of novelty scores in thei r contributions, may also be a sign for not completely reaching the potential of social discourse in groups with learners from same disciplines, eventually leading to less knowledge co-construction and individual learning (Chi & Wiley, 2014). In previous studies, referential contributions ha ve been identified to be relevant for learning (Vogel  et al., 2016). However, the reference score can’t always be  on the highest level. Contributions with a high score in novelty may be important in between to add further content to be referred to. However, it is not yet clear to what e xtent high scores in novelty a re similarly relevant if they are combined with high or low scores in the referential aspect of the contribution. Due to the exploratory nature of this study and the  low number of participants the results are not generalizable. In particular, the difference between the same and multidisciplinary groups may be confounded by other aspects (e.g. age difference, level of expert ise). In future research we will replicate our stud y with a larger sample size and add measures for outcomes of the CDR process, such as diagnostic accuracy and feedback quality, to better understand which transactivity patterns lead to successful collaboration.
