# Teachers’ Perceptions Change of Scientific Uncertainty as a Pedagogical Resource over a Two-year Professional Development: A Longitudinal Study

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This qualitative longitudinal study examines how six in-service science teachers’ perceptions of student uncertainty evolved across two practice-based professional development (P-BPD) programs. Using a teacher uncertainty perceptions framework, we analyzed interviews collected at five stages to trace changes across five dimensions: roles, types, sources, comfort level, and desirability. Teachers showed their most consistent gains in re-positioning uncertainty as a driver of reasoning and in the ir emotional readiness to sustain it, with five reaching the highest level by study’s end. Desirability also improved as teachers increasingly named relevance, timing, and complexity as conditions for productive struggle. In contrast, integrating conceptual and epistemic uncertainty, and recognizing co-occurring sources (e.g., insufficiency with ambiguity), remained challenging for most participants. These results highlight the value of sustained, situated learning experiences and iterative reflection on cl assroom practice, and point to design needs for concrete tools that help teachers plan for multiple, interacting uncertainties.

Scientific uncertainty is a central feature of science education, emerging as students engage with real-world phenomena and grapple productively during inquiry-based instruction to foster sensemaking and deep learning (Metz, 2004; Kampourakis & McCain, 2019; Fries et al., 2021; Manz & Suárez, 2018). Recent reforms, such as the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) (NGSS Lead States, 2013), emphasize supporting students in navigating such uncertainty as a core component of scientific practice. However, su pporting student uncertainty in classrooms is challenging. Many teachers view uncertainty as a barrier to learning, often seeking to minimize it to prevent students from experiencing frustration, confusion, or discouragement (Anderson, 2006; Starrett et al., 2024). These perceptions can limit opportunities for students to explore complex scientific ideas and practices. Professional development (PD) programs (Starrett et al., 2025; Manz & Suárez, 2018) have begun to explore how teachers might reconceptualize uncertainty as a pedagogical resource.  This study addresses this gap by examining how teachers’ perceptions of student scientific uncertainty change across two practice-based PD programs and what these trajectories suggest about the design of teacher learning. Specifically, we ask: How do teachers’ perceptions of student scientific uncertainty evolve across multiple PD experiences, and what patterns in these changes can inform the design of practice-based PD?

## Literature review

Teachers’ perceptions of student uncertainty play a critical role in shaping how students engage with science learning. Although uncertainty is a fundamental aspect of authentic scientific practice, research suggests that teachers often reduce it by simplifying evidence or providing limited data to avoid student frustration (Berland & McNeill, 2010; Chin & Osborne, 2010). Similarly, Starrett et al.  (2024) found that teachers tended to eliminate student uncertainty quickly to sustain engagement and keep lessons on schedule. These practices reflect teachers’ underlying fear of introducing or prolonging students’ struggles with uncertainty, as they oft en associate it with confusion, disengagement, and loss of classroom control. Such perceptions are closely related to how teachers frame uncertainty (as a barrier or resource), and to their comfort with sustaining it during inquiry. Teachers’ discomfort of  maintaining uncertainty in their classrooms can lead them to early intervening, limiting opportunities for students to grapple with unknowns in ways that foster deeper understanding and sensemaking (Manz & Suárez, 2018). To support teachers in rethinking the role of uncertainty in science learning and developing greater comfort with facilitating it, professional development is essential. Teacher professional development, especially practice-based PD (P-BPD), has been widely recognized as an effective approach for supporting teacher learning. P-BPD situates teacher learning in the core tasks and activities of teaching rather than in abstract knowledge or theory (Ball & Cohen, 1999; Borko, 2004). Fishman et 1587 al. (2017) demonstrated its effectiveness, showing that students whose teachers participated in P-BPD outperformed their peers in discourse engagement compared to those whose teachers attended content-focused PD. While much of the literature highlights P-BPD’s impact on instructional practices, recent work shows its potential for fostering shifts in teachers’ conceptualizations. Starrett et al.  (2025) found that P-BPD supported teachers in developing a more nuanced understanding of using student uncertainty to foster student sense-making.

## Conceptual framework

Our PD design is grounded in situated cognition (Brown et al., 1989; Lave & Wenger, 1991), which positions teacher learning as participation in authentic practices where meaning is constructed through experience. In the context of science education, student scientific uncertainty is not merely an obstacle but a central driver of sensemaking, requiring teachers to interpret, sustain, and respond to moments of not knowing (Manz & Suárez, 2018; Kampourakis & McCain, 2019).

Prior research suggests that teachers’ engagement with uncertainty involves multiple interrelated dimensions. First, the role of uncertainty reflects whether it is framed as a barrier or as a resource for advancing reasoning (Ko & Luna, 2024). Second, types  of uncertainty distinguish between conceptual uncertainty, related to gaps in content knowledge, and epistemic uncertainty, related to how knowledge is constructed and validated (Manz, 2013). Third, sources of uncertainty capture the origins of uncertainty, including insufficiency, ambiguity, incoherence, and conflict (Radinsky, 2008; Watkins et al., 2018). Fourth, comfort level  reflects teachers’ emotional and psychological readiness to sustain uncertainty in classroom interactions (Helsing, 2007; Warshauer, 2015). Finally, desirability  concerns teachers’ ability to identify when and how uncertainty supports learning, such as in relation to task relevance, timing, and complexity (Chen et al., 2024).  Together, these dimensions provide an analytic lens for examining how teachers make sense of and respond to student scientific uncertainty over time.

## Design context

This study took place in the context of two week-long P-BPD designed to support science teachers in reconceptualizing student uncertainty as a pedagogical resource. The first PD (P-BPD1) introduced teachers to key concepts related to student uncertainty, including its roles in science learning, types of uncertainty, sources of uncertainty, and the desirability of fostering uncertainty in instructional settings. Teache rs participated in solar energy science lessons developed using the student-uncertainty-driven approach, reflected on their own experiences of uncertainty as learners, and collaboratively analyzed how uncertainty arose in instructional contexts. They also developed design principles for productively using uncertainty and considered strategies to raise, maintain, reduce, or postpone it during teaching. The second PD (P-BPD2) built on these foundations by revisiting and extending these core ideas. Teachers reflected on their classroom experiences implementing P-BPD1 concepts, engaged in new science lessons on semiconductors, circuits, electromagnets, and solar energy systems, and analyzed peer collaborative discourse to identify sources of uncertainty. They worked collaboratively to design transfer lessons incorporating principles of the student-uncertainty-driven approach, engaged in peer critique sessions, and shared their plans 1588 through teach-back activities. This iterative process encouraged teachers to reflect deeply on their evolving perceptions of student uncertainty and its role in science learning. Data source This qualitative study draws on five stages of interview data collected from six science teachers who participated in two PDs focused on using student uncertainty as a pedagogical resource.  Interview transcripts were analyzed using a rubric-based coding approach aligned with the five dimensions of teacher uncertainty perceptions. For each interview, segments were coded to identify teachers’ expressed views related to roles, types, sources, comfort level, and desirability of student scientific uncertainty, and assigned to levels (0 –3) based on the rubric criteria. We then conducted longitudinal comparisons across the five time points to trace changes in each teacher’s perceptions over time.

To enhance the trustworthiness of the analysis, multiple researchers engaged in iterative discussions to refine coding interpretations and resolve discrepancies. Coding decisions were revisited across time points to ensure consistency in how levels were applied. Interviews were conducted by members of the research team across all five stages. Data collection primarily occurred during PD sessions and follow-up periods during the school year, with no structured intervention between PDs beyond teachers’ own classroom implementation. This study examined how six science teachers’ perceptions of student uncertainty evolved across two P-BPDs, focusing on five dimensions: roles, types, sources, comfort level, and desirability. Table 2 provides visual support for this analysis by illustrating how each teacher’s perceptions progressed across dimensions over time. Teachers developed more sophisticated perceptions of the role of uncertainty after two PDs. Before PDs, teachers exhibited varying views of uncertainty.  For instance, Jazmine started at Level 1, perceiving uncertainty as a challenge to be minimized, while John and Portia already demonstrated Level 3 thinking, viewing uncertainty as integral to inquiry. By the end of P-BPD2, five of six teachers, including Jazmine and Clara, reached Level 3, recognizing uncertainty as a driver of scientific reasoning and designing instructional strategies to sustain it. This shift suggests that repeated opportunities to engage in and reflect on uncertainty-rich tasks during PD may have supported teachers in reframing uncertainty from a barrier to a productive resource. Beyond reconceptualizing the role of uncertainty, teachers also developed a more nuanced perception of its various types, though perceiving the intricate interplay between them remained a challenge for most of the teachers. Initially, most teachers identified only conceptual uncertainty, focusing on students’ content knowledge gaps. After P-BPD1 implementation, teachers like Kristina and May progressed to Level 2, distinguishing between conceptual and epistemic uncertainty. However, by the final stage, only Jazmine achieved Level 3, demonstrating how both types intertwine as students grapple with constructing and validating scientific knowledge. This pattern suggests that distinguishing and integrating conceptual and epistemic uncertainty may require more explicit support, as these forms of uncertainty are often intertwined in practice and less visible than role-based shifts. Similarly, teachers’ understanding of uncertainty sources  deepened slightly over time. At baseline, all but one teacher recognized only a single source, which is insufficiency (Level 1). By the end of P-BPD2, four teachers reached Level 2, identifying an additional source such as ambiguity, incoherence, or confl ict. However, only Clara demonstrated Level 3 awareness by recognizing two distinct sources (insufficiency and ambiguity) as co-occurring within the same learning context.  This limited progression may reflect the cognitive complexity of identifying multiple co-occurring sources of uncertainty within a single learning context, which was not always made explicit during PD activities.

The two PD generally had a positive impact on teachers’ comfort level  of student uncertainty. Initially, four teachers demonstrated moderate comfort: although they recognized the value of uncertainty, they often stepped in quickly to resolve students’ struggle. Over time, all four progressed to Level 3, exhibiting greater psychological and emotional readiness to let students grapple productively with extended periods of uncertainty. Teachers such as John and Portia consistently maintained Level 3 comfort throughout the study, reflecting stable confidence in managing uncertainty. In contrast, Kristina showed a decline in comfort after the PDs, increasingly prioritizing the pursuit of correct answers by the end of the unit.  One possible explanation is that, as a relatively novice teacher, sustaining extended uncertainty while managing classroom dynamics may have posed additional challenges, highlighting how teaching experience may mediate teachers’ ability to enact uncertaint y-oriented practices.

Teachers’ perceptions of desirability of uncertainty (i.e., when and how uncertainty supports learning) was the most variable dimension. At baseline, most teachers demonstrated little to no awareness (Level 0 –1). By the final stage, four teachers, including Jazmine and May, achieved Level 3, fully articulating at least one of the three key factors of desirable uncertainty (e.g., relevance, timing, and complexity) and describing how to integrate 1589 them into instructional planning. For example, May demonstrated a sophisticated understanding of complexity, recognizing that scientific uncertainty requires an appropriate level of challenge and that its perceived difficulty varies across students, while also emphasizing the importance of timing by providing just-in-time support.
