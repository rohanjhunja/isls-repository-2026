# Storytelling and Storylistening Towards Collective Learning and Relational Becoming

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Black, Brown, and Indigenous communities have long drawn upon storywork-the interrelated processes of storytelling and storylistening-as central strategies of wellness and thriving despite histories of dispossession and domination. This collection of papers theorizes with storywork and provides five contexts of storywork-in-praxis, where acts of creating, telling, and listening to stories feature centrally in critically re-designing spaces towards Black life, Indigenous futures, and the mutual flourishing of communities and lands. Through a syncretic approach, we bring together learning sciences research and perspectives from Black and Indigenous studies to further theorize the role that storywork plays within (1) sense-making activities, (2) the lives of communities that we have been a part of, and (3) our own lives as researchers-storylisteners. We consider the multiple roles of stories in research and design, the political/ethical nature of stories, and how storywork can re-organize power relations in ways that support individual and collective life.

Keywords: storywork, becoming collectives, Black and Indigenous studies, nature-culture relations, politics and ethics of learning Focus of symposium Throughout human history stories have been an organ izing technology in intergenerational strategies of resurgence and coming to know who we are and who we could become together across difference. Building with these histories, this symposium is grounded in the experiences, epistemologies, and story-theories of Black, Brown and Indigenous communities and collectives. W e choose to center our symposium on the interrelate d processes of storytelling and storylistening, or what Jo-Ann Archibald (2008) has called storywork. Reaffirming that stories are central to human learning and becoming (Marin et al., 2020; Brayboy, 2005), we extend lines of thought and forms of inquiry to more robustly under stand the forms, places, and processes of storying and restor(y)ing our relationships to each other and wh o we seek to become as educators, teachers, researc hers, relatives, and human people within this vast web of life. Which stories are told, by whom, and how we listen to stories matters and has consequences for how people experience the ethical, powered, and political dimensions of learning. In many schooling contexts, claims of objectivity permeate and control the permissible  bounds of sense-making within disciplines, shaping  what counts as science or knowing more broadly (e.g. Ban g., et al, 2012; Lee, 2001).  The act of living and  listening with stories can help with navigating the epistemological and axiological tensions that arise from engaging with settled expectations based in whiteness (Bang et al., 2012). Stories resonate realities and importantly ground-truth and make place for our felt theories (Million, 2008). In this way, stories and storytelling are methods for refusing ongoing ontological erasure and epistemic violence (Marker, 2006). They also provide a representationa l infrastructure (or conditions, Hall & Jurow, 2015) for continually enacting intergenerational healing, community wellness, and regeneration.

The papers within this symposium hold a broad defin ition of stories, including songs, platicas, dichos, proverbs, and other artistic traditions held by hum ans and more-than-humans. These story-forms engage the ICLS2022 Proceedings 1707 © ISLS multiple senses and ways humans come to know the world, our relations, and the peaceful social order, or what is known as omenàlà in Igbo society (Penfield & Duru, 1988; Kopano, 2002). As a collective, we theorize with storywork and riff on the personal, collective, ethical, and political possibilities that emerge from participating in storytelling and storylistening. Together, we pay a ttention to how stories might sensitize us to each other, the relations of others, and provide forms to bear witness to trauma. We also foreground efforts to reclaim stories that have often been stolen from many of our communities  and repackaged towards settler aims through hungry listening (Robinson, 2020).

As researchers of human learning and becoming, we are particularly interested in how stories may surface in the (re)membering of felt experiences in our own  lives within intergenerational and inter-ontologic al (e.g. across humans, waters, plants, animals, and other m ore-than-human beings) thought and ongoing activity. Through this dialogue, we are interested in how the  interrelated processes of storytelling and storyli stening connect our whole beings to other kin-relations-h uman, plants, animal, spirits, and ancestors across  multiple timescales where the act of listening is also an ac t of creation, health, and healing. Malian writer H ampaté Bâ

(1981) suggests that the health of a community’s social relations depends on how well their stories circulate and

continue. Given these responsibilities and possibilities, we continue to ask: ● How does storywork ethically guide our processes of  knowledge production (as teachers/learners/ researchers)?

● What does storylistening, an under-theorized dimension in the field of learning sciences, teach us about the design of learning environments in which relational knowledge can emerge? ● How are stories learned, taken up, circulated, list ened with, and brought forward across human and mor ethan-human interlocutors in learning environments-and towards what ends? ● What methodological approaches might help us to better understand storywork across human and more-thanhuman interlocutors? ● What kinds of visions of wellness, joint orientations and collective pursuits are possible with stories? Relevance and Contribution The collection of papers within this symposium spea ks to the multiplicity of ways that humans make mea ning, dream with, and become story-ready with each other and the rest of the living world. This session holds two main threads, theorizing with stories-as-theories and st ories-in-praxis. First, we theorize with stories and becoming story-ready as humans. Being story-ready refers to the ways we humans prepare our hearts and spirits to learn and listen with stories and find meaning from that story within our own lives (Archibald, 2008). It signals an openness and vulnerability to allow another being’s stories to guide what we ought to do or how we should act. For us, being story-ready is core to supporting relational becoming-the attuning of ourselves to the rhythm and resonance of others and their relations (Meixi, in press; Nzi nga, in press). Second, we turn to design and practice-based implications for the building and sustaining of rel ational or subject-subject focused designs within a nd across learning environments (e.g. Bang and Vossoughi, 2016; Booker, 2016; 2019; Marin & Bang, 2015). Across the papers, we make visible the work of stories within five distinct contexts: as professional and emerging artists that became community during the p andemic; as scholars and community members who recognize the capacity of Indigenous intellectual t raditions to shape and expand possible futures, par ticularly during widespread crisis; as mothers and community members on traditional lands and waters, within e-textiles quiltmaking and the strengthening of Black futures;  and as educators committed to offering more libera tory mathematical possibilities for children in elementa ry classrooms, imagining possible selves among elem entary mathematics educators through collective storying. Each of the papers comes from within particular contexts of power and historicity at multiple scales-histories of settler colonialism, systemic anti-Blackness, public schooling and institutionalized norms, and narrow notions of mathematics and science. As a field, we need more scholarship on the roles of storywork in critically re-designin g spaces of disequilibrium, negation, and harm for Black and Indigenous young people to ones that further our re lational responsibilities to each other and a more just and thriving living for all people and lands. We suggest that centering one of human’s oldest technologies-our stories as felt theories - (Brayboy, 2005; Million, 2008) c an facilitate the conditions necessary for collabor ation and educational innovation. This symposium offers a dee p dive into stories, with theoretical, methodologic al, analytical and practice-based implications for our designs and our relational becoming. Paper 1: Wellness Belongs to Us: Reimagining interg enerational knowledge and embodied wisdom through Hip-Hop arts storytelling Kalonji Nzinga and Marlene Palomar Researchers in the learning sciences have noted ways in which storytelling and storylistening in communities of color provide ways for youth to build counternarratives to resist mainstream narratives (Delgado, 1989; Solórzano & Yosso 2002). One such mainstream narrative is tha t wellness practices are lacking within marginalize d communities. The current design-based research study was constructed to analyze how a storytelling intervention introducing the essential elements of  hip-hop arts (Knowledge, Djing, Breakdancing, Graffiti, Emceeing) could provide space for adolescents to redefine wellness and articulate healing practices endogenous to thei r own communities. We were rooted in the following questi ons: (1) how do youth redefine wellness through art  and through their senses, and (2) where do narratives live and how do they circulate through the learning environment and in their communities.

Our intervention centered youth from Latinx, Africa n-American, AAPI, and Indigenous communities with deep histories in the Greater Denver area who are vulnerable to cultural displacement and gentrification. The communities we worked with are both underserved by mainstream mental health care institutions and have seen their cultural practices historically marginalized. The intervention took place within a hybrid (remote/in-person) Ethnic Studies classroom at North High School in th e midst of global pandemic. A design team consistin g of artists, activists, scholars, students, teachers, a nd emcees engaged in the creation of a hip-hop base d education learning environment that nurtured the exploration of wellness and resistance storytelling in digital form. Storytelling and storylistening was conceived of as  reactivating knowledge systems that have been rend ered useless by colonial and neoliberal conditions (Abebe, 2021). Our online learning environment weaved narrative, song, art, and intergenerational knowledge as a for m of cultural preservation through the circulation of stories (Lambert, 2010; Iseke & Moore, 2011; Cunsolo Willox  et al., 2013; Kitchenham, 2013) engaging multiple generations in meaningful engagement with storytelling/storylistening (Hausknecht et al., 2021). We take a case study approach, focusing on 8 learners and their stories (6 youth and 2 instructors).  Using multimodal discourse analysis we analyze a single artistic story produced by these 8 individuals (song, visual art, playlist, interactive website) as well as an artist  statement that they produced. Using open and close d coding techniques a team of artist-researchers investigated the ways in which each of these community members defined wellness, incorporated their senses, and also how their stories reflected relationships to community members. Our analysis produced various conclusions about the process by which stories circulated in the learning environment. First, the vulnerable artistic process  that the instructors went through (both while desi gning the curriculum and while presenting it to learners) modeled techniques of healing expression for the youth. There was evidence of storylistening by the youth, elements of the instructors wellness and artistic forms of expression were seen weaved through the artistic element and statem ent. In their art youth also reconceptualized welln ess as something that was already a part of who they were, and their commitment to transformational resistance in the context of their self, community, diaspora. Youth o ften reimagined everyday cultural practices that th ey were already engaged in as worthy, valuable, and connected to wellness. Paper 2: Stories of (Re)creation and (Re)emergence:  Engaging land-based approaches to education and wellbeing Emma Elliott, Sabrina Elliott, Meixi, and Alayna Eagle Shield Human survival is predicated upon ethical and sustainable relationships with the land and all living beings. The ethical foundations of our intellectual traditions are best understood as a reflection of our deep sen se of interconnectedness, characterized by relational reciprocities, both of which informs how we make sense and move about in the world. These axiological commitments s hape our identities, our reciprocal relations with plant and animal nations, and natural world relations, as well as our education, health, and political systems. By drawing on Indigenous stories as the crux of theory development and knowledge co-creation, as well as Indigenous concepts ICLS2022 Proceedings 1709 © ISLS of relationality, this study identifies social and psychological dimensions of wellbeing from a Cowich an perspective.

This study drew on principles of community-based de sign research (Bang, et al, 2015), and was a part of a larger study that took place within the context of Cowichan Tribes on Vancouver Island, British Columbia. To consider how Indigenous intellectual traditions and related ethical commitments can help illuminate strategies that facilitate overall wellbeing (e.g., education and health) of all peoples, this study engaged the power of Indigenous creation and land-based stories. With th e expertise of a community-based research assistant  and a graduate student researcher, we conducted 25 land-b ased interviews with elders and other cultural know ledge keepers. At each site, the researcher engaged each participant in Coast Salish storytelling and storyl istening (Archibald, 2008) to document the stories that emerged from each place, and recorded each interview using GoPro cameras to capture land-based methodologies including “walking, reading, and storying the land” (Marin & Bang, 2018). Four researchers, including the community-based research assistant, analyzed each interview u sing a constant comparative process consistent with grounded theory methodology (Glaser & Strauss, 2017) to identify local social and psychological dimensions of health and wellbeing. By engaging land-based and Indigenous approaches to research, we found that embedded within local, land-based stories are the frameworks to teach scie nce, technology, engineering, math, medicine, and environmental sustainability, as well as culture, l anguage, and arts. The stories gathered in this stu dy provided considerations about context and conditions, collec tive and intergenerational contributions, cross-cul tural relations, and directions forward toward a just and sustainable future for all. In particular, our study documented stories, both historical and contemporary, that pro vided Cowichan people with 1) connections to their land and territory, as well as to animal and plant nations; 2) land-based and Indigenous teaching and learning strategies, 3) a deep commitment to Indigenous relationality; and 4) theories and practices that included sustainable frameworks to teach all learners what it means to be a human being and how to “walk in a good way.” Throughout the course of this study, the world experienced (and continues to experience) multiple pandemics, including the novel coronavirus, police violence against Black people and anti-Black racism, and a widespread climate crisis (Ladson-Billings, 2021). As educators, we continue to imagine our contribution to education and wellbeing, and frankly to humanity, throughout this challenging time. As scholars, we recognize that through widespread crisis or chaos, creativity and imagination can emerge. We have the opportunity to recreate and re-imagine another way of educating and caring for our children. As Indigenous peoples, we recognize the strength of our relational onto-epistemologies and axiological commitments, and offer Indigenous relationality as a framework for addressing widespread crises across humans, plant and animal nations, and natural world entities. Indigenous intellectual traditions are rooted in our ancestral territories and expressed as “an ecology of relationships in the absence of coercion, hierarchy, or authoritarian power…, relationships based on deep reciprocity, respect, noninterference, self-determination, and freedom” (Simpson, 2017, p. 8). These systems of relationships have been put in place to ensure human collective survival. We must not only understand the theoretical underpinnings of our stories, we must also strive to engage these theories in the process of re-imagining our collective futures. The answers to the social and psychological predicaments faced by our communities are embedded within the intellectual traditions of each community, and more specifically, within land-based local stories that serve as theories of learning and wellbeing (Brayboy, 2005). Paper 3: Restorying STEM Learning Communities throu gh Etextiles Quiltmaking Mia S. Shaw The purpose of this study was to design an inclusive STEM learning community with minoritized youth from an informal STEM program at a local science museum. I conceptualize youth’s STEM learning through this fo uryear program as “legitimate peripheral participation in communities of practice” (Lave & Wenger, 1991, p. 31). In other words, youth deepen their knowledge of STEM practices and careers through deepening their membership into the STEM community in the form of hands-on wor kshops, field trips, and internships. However, dominant narratives about who or what are considered “legitimate” STEM identities, practices, and goals have resulted in the historical and institutional exclusion of communities across various social locations (i.e., race/ethnicity, gender, class, and ability). I ask: How do we design for cr eating inclusive STEM learning communities that rei magine dominant narratives surrounding what STEM is and wh o belongs? One of the ways the learning sciences ha ve considered reimagining or “restorying” narratives s urrounding STEM learning—particularly those endorsi ng “narrowly defined hegemonic science practices steep ed in material culture” (p. 409)—has been to frame the ICLS2022 Proceedings 1710 © ISLS heterogeneity of cultural practices and experiences  as central to learning and designing learning envi ronments (Rahm, 2019).

This study engages in heterogeneity through the sha ring of diverse STEM stories and experiences, for exchanging stories can create learning communities through providing a “cohering feeling of shared con cerns, values, and investigations” (hooks, 2010, p. 52). I n order to design for heterogeneous STEM identities  and practices through the sharing and listening to stories, I developed and facilitated a two-part, 45-hour workshop in which youth designed electronic textile (hereafter, e-textiles) quilt patches that “restor(y)ed” dominant narratives about computing technology. An analytical perspective used in narrative and literacy studies, restorying describes how minoritized youth “narrate the word and the wor ld, [analyze] their lived experiences and then [syn thesize] and [recontextualize] a multiplicity of stories in order to form new narratives” (Thomas & Stornaiuolo, 2016, p. 318).  E-textiles are fabrics embedded with microcomputers, lights, and sensors stitched together with conductive thread and programmed to perform various actions (Buechley, Peppler, Eisenberg, & Kafai, 2013). Furthermore, minoritized groups have used quilting as both an ar t form and sociopolitical tool to resist oppression  and collectively reconstruct their cultural histories through sharing stories of their lived experiences (Cash, 1995; Stull, 2001). Through the integration of historically-femi nized practices of crafting (and quilting) with his toricallymasculinized practices of engineering and design (B uechley, Peppler, Eisenberg, & Kafai, 2013), e-text iles quiltmaking serves as a novel approach for creating inclusive STEM learning communities. Through analyzing field notes and video observation s of community-building activities, participants’ exit tickets, and researcher memos, narratives surrounding the culture of STEM learning (particularly in formal schools) emerged. Through collaborating to design a  collective quilt including all their patches of re stor(y)ed narratives, youth and adults present were able to r eimagine and create a STEM learning community roote d in empathy-building and psychological safety, as well as play and authentic friendships. Implications for this study include the design of collective narrative-based ar tifacts as a context for developing inclusive STEM learning communities.

Paper 4: Co-constructed Anti-Deficit Storytelling in Mathematics Education Mallika Scott Justice-oriented teacher preparation programs aim to prepare novice teachers to create more humanizing learning communities in which each student can bring their f ull selves to learning (e.g. Andrews et al., 2019).  Creating these types of caring and rigorous communities in t he mathematics classroom requires grappling with and disrupting deficit discourses about students from currently and historically marginalized communities that remain prevalent in K-12 schools (Adiredja & Louie, 2020; McDermott et al., 2006). The stories teachers tell about children often function to reinforce narrow concept ions of ways of knowing and being in the mathematic s classroom and to reproduce existing hierarchies (Ho rn, 2007; Louie, 2019). However, stories also have the potential to support teachers toward more liberatory and expansive views of children and of mathematical learning (Battey & Franke, 2015). Here, I present an analysi s of a learning community of beginning teachers who  coconstructed a form of storywork – storytelling and storylistening about children that disrupted deficit discourses rampant in their school contexts.

This study was informed by social approaches to des ign that emphasize designing activity systems intended to afford new forms of activity (Gutiérrez  & Jurow, 2016; Gutiérrez & Vossoughi, 2010). The a uthor served as facilitator, co-designer, and researcher of a learning community of six first-year elementar y school teachers from the same preparation program. Teachers participated in monthly meetings in which they told stories of successes and dilemmas from their classroom teaching. Data sources include video recordings of meetings and audio recordings of interviews with participants. D ata were analyzed to examine how teacher stories fr om the classroom were co-constructed and functioned to rep roduce or disrupt deficit discourse about mathemati cs teaching and learning.

Findings indicate that participants co-constructed a new joint activity of telling stories about child ren that reinterpreted student behavior from an anti-deficit perspective. This activity grew out of an emergent need in the community for teachers to support each other to  expand their own limited views of the children in their classrooms by collectively investigating why student behavior might be considered sensible within the classroom learning ecology. As teachers listened to each other’s stories, examined classroom artifacts,  and thought together about the experiences of individual children in the ir classrooms, they supported each other to open th eir hearts and spirits to connect with children in the experie nce of learning mathematics. In describing how this  activity supported her learning, first-grade teacher Selina reflected, “I don’t think I would have gotten to th at point if I ICLS2022 Proceedings 1711 © ISLS didn’t have everybody looking at the same work with  me. Because I was such in a box. [...] So asking f or an outside perspective and not limiting yourself to a deficit view, like what are they not getting. It’s like what are they getting, and what is it about this that is confusing.” Through this activity, participants collectively constructed new stories of mathematical learning that pushed back on individual, deficit-oriented interpretations and honored children’s dignity as learners and as human beings (Espinoza et al., 2020; Espinoza & Vossoughi, 2014). Paper 5:  Becoming Maestras Matemáticas through Plá ticando Sandra Zuniga-Ruiz I grew up listening to my abuelita’s historias abou t being raised en el rancho and navigating the stru ggles of migrating to the United States. I bear witness to her memories, memories that would be intertwined with dichos and consejos of becoming buena gente. I begin with the stories of my abuelita because storytelling is interconnected with who we are in history, intergen erational knowledge and connetion that is passed on, and storytelling provides a groundwork for the future. These stories were of the form of pláticas, critical conversations. In this work, pláticas are a cultural practice of s torytelling embedded in the design of transformativ e learning communities (Gutierrez & Jurow, 2016) that I argue is central to learning and becoming. This paper cen ters pláticas as stories that create opportunities for prospecti ve teachers of color, Mexicanas, to engage with the complexity of issues related to social justice and mathematics. Becoming a mathematics social justice educator enta ils understanding and engaging with multiple literacies: racial, critical and mathematical liter acy (Freire, 1996; Sealy-Ruiz, 2011; Schoenfeld, 20 14). While such literacies are central and never an outcome, very little is known how one grapples and develops such literacies with mathematics while centering the experiences of  marginalized communities. One such way to center t he experiences of prospective teachers of color (PCTs)  is through the creation of a counterspace, a “safe  space” created by marginalized people in response to the d aily barrage of racial microaggressions (Solorzano, Ceja, & Yosso, 2000). This study merged the need to create spaces for us and with us while paying close attent ion to designing a transformative learning community.

The purpose of this project was to co-construct a learning community where PTCs had opportunities to grapple with issues of race, justice and mathematics. Such opportunities came from the use of tools and artifacts to spark conversation. The women had all taken the same math content course so they had a shared exper ience that allowed them to talk with one another about th eir math experiences. The co-participants in this s tudy are a researcher and 4 PTCs who self-identify as Mexicanas. Even though the participants are in the same ethnic group, there is a lot of diversity within the group. PTCs are all college graduates who attended a Hispanic S erving Institution in a predominantly immigrant Latinx community. At the time of the study, all co-participants had yet to begin a credential program and intended to continue to work in the community they grew up in. The participants were selected by their willingness to engage in conversations about social justice and mathematics. Data sources include video recordings of zoom meetings and interviews with co-participants. Through analyzing the video data, findings suggest that the stories that the mujeres shared about thei r families served to both construct new meanings with one another and with mathematics itself. For insta nce, the developmental stories of parents doing math in their everyday life pushed on the mujeres’ understandings of who does math and what is math. The storytelling about family helped build a collective community where they were vulnerable and were able to relate to struggles of their multifaceted identities as women of color. Fu rthermore, their experiences in the pláticas served as a foundation for the women to co-construct a vision of a mathematics learning community grounded on community, historici ty and dignity (Espinoza et. al, 2020; Espinoza & Vossoughi, 2014).
