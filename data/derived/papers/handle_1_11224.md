# Can We Trust Large Language Models for Video Analysis: An Exploration of Hallucination in Multimodal LLMs

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) show promise for facilitating qualitative video analysis, potentially reducing the manual effort required for video selection and coding. However, MLLMs are subject to hallucination, where models generate incorrect or unfounded descriptions and interpretations. Current frameworks for evaluating MLLM hallucinations primarily focus on object-centric tasks, with limited focus on behaviors and social interactions common in education data. Applying MLLMs to analyzing 36 video clips of collaborative learning activities, we identify three distinct types of hallucinations: description hallucination (factual inconsistency), interpretation hallucination (misinterpretation of behaviors), and instruction hallucination (deviation from analytical focus). Despite MLLMs’ ability to process educational videos, they exhibit gender misidentification and inaccurate interpretation of student roles and behaviors. This exploratory work develops a domain-specific framework to understand and evaluate MLLM hallucinations, laying the groundwork for more reliable implementation in educational research and calling for further investigations

## Introduction

Large language models like ChatGPT have shown potential in facilitating education research through supporting qualitative data analysis tasks such as coding transcripts and analyzing open-ended responses (Xiao et al., 2023; Zambrano et al., 2023; H. Zhang et al., 2024). Recent advances in multimodal large language mode ls (MLLMs) have extended these natural language processing capabilities to process both visual and textual information (Yin et al., 2024). MLLMs have demonstrated promising results in tasks such as image captioning, video understanding, and visual question answering (Wu et  al., 2023; D. Zhang et al., 2024). One potential application of MLLMs in educational research  is facilitating qualitative video analysis. Video a nalysis is an important but time-intensive method for understanding learning processes (Derry et al., 2010; Ramey et al., 2016), as it requires researchers to carefully code and interpret complex  behavioral patterns and interactions. Emerging educational research has explored machine learning classification to detect teaching and learning moves (Foster et al., 2024); however, these efforts rely on manually coded datasets that are time and resource intensive to develop.  MLLMs could potentially streamline this process by suppor ting video selection and behavior coding —tasks that traditionally require significant manual effort fro m researchers. For instance, these models could help identify relevant segments of classroom videos or provide in itial coding of student interactions, potentially r educing the time researchers spend on preliminary analysis.

However, while the potential is exciting, the implementation of MLLMs in educational research requires careful consideration of their reliability and limi tations. Educational video analysis requires not just accurate descriptions but nuanced interpretation s of learning behaviors and interactions within speci fic educational contexts. It is thus important to thoroughly evaluate MLLMs’ performance and limitations to ensure they generate reliable outputs. Otherwise, researchers risk relyi ng on models that generate inaccurate or misleading interpretations of student data.

This reliability concern becomes particularly impor tant when considering the issue of hallucination in MLLMs. Hallucination refers to the discrepancy between the generated text response and the provided visual content, i.e., cross-modal inconsistency (Bai et al., 2024). While hallucination has been studied in general MLLM applications, its implications for educational rese arch require specific attention. Current evaluation frameworks for MLLM hallucinations primarily rely on synthetic  datasets designed to measure general-purpose capabilities (Chang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). Moreover, these evaluation frameworks typically prioritize quantitative metrics like object recognition, counting, and math ematical capability (Fu et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024). While these metrics are valuable for general applications and computer visi on tasks, they do not adequately capture the complexities of educational video analysis. Video analysis in education often requires understanding subtle behavioral cues, interpreting social interactions, and making context-dependent judgments about learning processes in real-world settings.  This misalignment between current evaluation approa ches and educational research needs calls for the development of domain-specific frameworks that can better capture the com plexities of analyzing student behaviors in authentic learning environments. The current study addresses these challenges by examining hallucination in MLLM outputs when applied to educational video analysis. By grounding our inv estigation in real-world educational scenarios beyond synthetic benchmarks, we aim to examine how MLLMs perform in authentic contexts. Specifically, our research explores: What types of hallucinations emerge when MLLMs are applied to educational video analysis? Through this investigation, we aim to contribute to both the technical understanding of MLLMs and their practical application in educational research. While our analysis is exploratory and focuses on a specific learning context, the patterns of hallucination we identify offer initial insights into how MLLMs may fail when interpreting student interactions. These initial findings showcase issues that warrant further investigation and provide a starting point for researchers to systematically examine the  limitations of MLLMs and develop targeted strategi es to mitigate their impact. Ultimately, our goal is to contribute to the development of more reliable implementation of these models in educational research. The rest of this paper is structured as follows: Fi rst, we present a literature review on hallucination in LLMs and MLLMs, highligh ting the need for a domain-specific hallucination framework. Next, we detail our dataset, MLLM model,  and prompt design process. We then present our res ults, discussing the identified hallucinations and propos e a hallucination framework to evaluate the perform ance of MLLM in education tasks. Finally, we c onclude by examining the potential causes of each type of hallucination and suggesting future research directions.

## Literature review

Hallucination of LLM for educational applications To the best of our knowledge, no existing research has systematically examined hallucinations in MLLMs within educational contexts. Therefore, we review how hallucination manifests in text-based LLM applications  in educational contexts. In programming education, Hellas et al. (2023)  found that  48% of the LLM-generated feedback reported non-existent issues  in the student's code. This hallucination can lead students down a "debugging rabbit hole," wasting time and effort on  fixing non-existent problems. Similarly, i n teacher professional development, 20-30% of the responses were marked as unfaithful in i nterpreting the classroom transcript (Wang & Demszky, 2023). Meyer et al. (2023) highlight hallucination as a fundamental challenge for tasks like academic writing and grant proposal writing. These findings collectively suggest that hallucination may compromise educational applications.

Incorrect or unfounded content generation can significantly affect various downstream tasks, such as QA systems, information extraction, text summarization, dialogu e systems, and automated fact-checking (Chang et al., 2024). Despite these concerns, most reviews of LLMs in education either briefly mention hallucination s as a general issue or overlook it (Barany et al., 2024). Further, current educational research tends to treat all instances of hallucination as a uniform phenomenon, without distinguishing between different types of errors. For instance, hallucination in coding feedback (i.e., reporting non-existent code issues) is fundamentally different from the hallucination where LLM's interpretations disagree with teachers' intentions or actions, where no single true interpretation may exist. Understanding these nuances is crucial for developing targeted mitigation strategies and ensuring trustworthy LLM-generated content in education. The examples discussed above underscore the need fo r a clear framework that differentiates between specific types of hallucination in educational applications. To address this need, we examine existing hallucination frameworks in natural language processing and artificial intelligence domains in the next section. Hallucination frameworks of LLM and MLLM In the natural language processing community, which studies how computers understand and generate  human language, the hallucination problem is empirically categorize d into the intrinsic-extrinsic dichotomy. Intrinsic hallucination occurs when the outputs of LLMs direc tly conflict with the source content, potentially leading to confusion or misinformation. Extrinsic hallucination arises when the outputs of LLMs introduce information that cannot be verified or confirmed based on the provided content. Another commonly used framework, proposed by Huang et al. (2023), introduces the concept of faithfulness hallucination, which ta kes user instructions into account. Their framework distinguishes between factuality hallucination and faithfulness hallucination. Factuality hallucination emphasizes the discrepancy between generated content and verifiable real-world facts. Meanwhile, faithfulness hallucination refers to the divergence of generated content from user instructions or the provided context. This categorization emphasizes how hallucinations c an compromise task effectiveness even when factually accurate by producing irrelevant or off-topic content. The introduction of visual information processing c apabilities in MLLMs has necessitated new frameworks for understanding hallucination. Several survey s and research papers have discussed MLLM hallucination and proposed different taxonomies. Sun et al. (2023) have a broad category of hallucinations, including object attribute, adversarial object, com parison, counting, spatial relation, environment, h olistic description, and others. Bai et al. (2024)  specifically focus on object hallucination given th at objects are central to research in computer vision and artificial intel ligence domains. Their categorization thus focuses on category, attribute, and relation hallucination around objects.

However, these existing frameworks, while valuable for general applications, are primarily object-centric and rely on synthetic datasets. Effective application of MLLMs in education needs to go beyond object recognition and attribute classification, to understand and describe  nuanced and context-dependent learning behaviors. To address these challenges, a domain-specific framework for identifying hallucinations in educational video analysis should prioritize accurately interpreting and annotating student behaviors and interactions. This framework needs to focus on the types of hallucinations that are most relevant to key components of learning science research, such as action recognition, emotion attribution, and soc ial interaction dynamics  (Sharma & Giannakos, 2020). Furthermore, the framework should be grounded in real-world educational video data. This work aims to bridge this gap by proposing a hallucination framework specifically tailored to educational video analysis, grounded in authentic e ducational data rather than synthetic benchmarks. B y examining how MLLMs hallucinate in educational cont exts, we can better understand the unique challeng es of applying these models to educational research.

## Context and data source

Our study analyzed video recordings from students' collaborative activities through an immersive learning application called HoloOrbits. Developed in Unity  for both Microsoft HoloLens2 headsets (AR version) and laptops (WebGL version), HoloOrbits immerses students in planetary systems, allowing them to simulate and collect data to understand orbital motions and Kepler’s laws. Data collection involved six pairs of students (42% female) from a Midwestern university during pilot t esting of the AR version  of HoloOrbits.  The participants included 11 graduate students and one undergraduate  student, with eight from STEM fields and four from  nonSTEM fields. Each pair worked in a laboratory space  where they could freely move around to interact wi th HoloOrbits while wearing AR headsets.  Sessions lasted approximately one hour  and included a pre-survey, AR headset tutorial, structured collaborative problem-solving tasks, post-survey, and semi-structured interview. Our analysis focused specifically on the last task  of the HoloOrbits, a collaborative activity requiring pairs to connect theoretical understanding with hands-on measurement. During this task, students measured distances between celestial objects in the AR simul ation while documenting their findings in paper wor ksheets. Students were prompted to discuss with teammates and reach consensus while recording data. This task provided particularly rich opportunities for analyzing behav iors as students navigated b etween the paper worksheets and AR-mediated measurements, demonstrating peer interacti on and collaborative problem-solving. From the six participating pairs, we selected one whose collaborative interactions we re fully visible throughout the task (see Figure 1). This selection was determined by video c apture quality and completeness. While our camera s etup provided an overhead view of the workspace, most pairs moved frequently around the room while using their AR headsets, making their interactions only part ially visible. The selected pair maintained a seate d position throughout the task, allowing consistent capture of their full rang e of collaborative interactions, including both shared worksheet activities and coordinated AR-mediated behaviors. Model We employed LLaVA-Video-7B-Qwen2 (Y. Zhang et al., 2024), a state-of-the-art video MLLM. We chose this model for several reasons. First, this model was released and trained on recent datasets (October 2024), representing the latest advances in video MLLM. Second, it is open-source, without cost constraints associated with proprietary models. Third, this model has demonstrated robust performance across several benchmark tasks in video understanding and description  (Fu et al., 2024; Y. Zhang et al., 2024). We used the pre-trained model with temperature set to 0 to minimize random variations in output and ensure reproducible results across analyses. Prompt development We employed an iterative approach to refine the tex t prompts given to LLaVA, focusing on maximizing th e quality and relevance of the outputs for educationa l video analysis. While prompt optimization itself is not the primary goal of this study, developing prompts that reflect authentic educational research  scenarios was critical for meaningfully examining MLLM hallucinations in r eal-world applications. Thus, the focus on gestures and interactions in our prompts is grounded in establis hed educational research priorities. In AR-based learning environments, gestural analysis is particularly sig nificant as it reveals how students physically inte ract with and construct meaning from virtual objects  (Kang et al., 2024; Roth, 2001). Furthermore, in collaborative learning settings, documenting and analyzing student interactions prov ides crucial insights into learning processes and knowledge construction  (Puntambekar et al., 2011; Zhou & Kang, 2023). Given these research interests, we developed prompts focusing on two key aspects typic ally analyzed in videos of AR-based collaborative learning environment: peer interactions and gestures.

We began with an initial prompt that simply asked the model to “Describe the interactions and gestures exhibited by the students”. To provide more context and align better with educational research needs, we enhanced the prompt to “Describe the collaborative interactions and gesture s exhibited by the students in a collaborative learning activity”. However, we observed that the model often focused on describing irrelevant objects rather than student behaviors. This observation led to our fina l refinement:  “ Describe the collaborative interactions and gestures exhibited by the students in a collaborative learning activity, focus on their behaviors”. Throughout this iterative process, we maintained concise prompts to optimize token allocation in the model. MLLMs process both visual and textual inputs by converting them into t okens, and the computational complexity of these mo dels increases with the number of input tokens  (Wu et al., 2023). W e needed to ensure sufficient token capacity remained available for processing the rich visual information from video. Longer text prompts not only consume more of the limited token capacity but can also lead to attention dilution (Q. Huang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024), where the model's focus shifts away from visual ele ments. Model performance candegrade significantly with longer inputs, even before reaching technical token limits (Levy et al., 2024). Therefore, we designed our prompts to be concise while maintaining the necessary context, balancing the task specification and token allocation. This helped ensure the model could effectively process t he video input while generating outputs meaningful for our analysis of hallucinations in the context of educational video analysis. Video analysis For MLLMs to process video content, each video is first broken down into individual frames. These frames, along with text prompts, are converted into tokens. The model has a fixed context window that limits the total nu mber of tokens it can handle at once. When processing lengthy videos, MLLMs faces a fundamental trade-off between the number of tokens allocated per frame (affecting the detail of analysis) and the total number of frames (affecting video coverage). Each frame consumes tokens from th e model's fixed context window, necessitating a careful balance between temporal coverage and processing ca pacity. Given these constraints, we segmented the video recording of the pair’s last task into 10-second clips, resulting in a total of 36 clips, to allow more tokens per frame to capture more details. The 10-second duration was empirically chosen to optimize this balance, ensuring each clip provided sufficient temporal context to capture meaningful interaction sequences within the model's token limits. The resulting clips showcased a diverse range of behaviors, including using AR headsets for measurements, transitioning between AR activities and worksheet, and interacting with the facilitator. We used a sampling rate of 1 frame per second (fps). Our evaluation of different sampling rates  (1 fps vs. 2 fps) showed no meaningful differences in output quality, confirming that 1 fp s provided sufficient temporal granularity while maintaining computational efficiency. Then, all video clip s were processed through LLaVA using the refined prompt. Following the generation of model outputs,  the first author watched each video clip alongside its corresponding model output to identify any statements that did not align with the video content or researchers’ understanding. The second author independently examined 30% of the clips. This validation process confirmed all hallucinations identified by the first author while also revealing  additional cases. Through systematic examination of these discrepancies, we identified recurring patterns in how the model interpreted (or misinterpreted) the c omplex interplay between physical and AR-mediated learning activities.

## Result

Through an exploratory yet thorough  analysis of MLLM outputs in educational video proce ssing, we propose three distinct categories of hallucination, expanding on the existing frameworks: description hallucination, interpretation hallucination, and instruction hallu cination. Our analysis involved examining model responses, focusing particularly on how the model interpreted student behaviors, interactions, and learning proce sses. Each type of hallucination presents unique challenges fo r educational research a pplications. Table 1 summarizes the number of occurrences and descriptions for each hallucination type, with description hallucinations being the most common (13 out of 36 clips), followed by interpretation hallucinations (11), and instruction hallucinations (6). Several cases of description hallucination were found. The first one involves technology identification error  as shown in Figure 1, where the model confused distinct types of educational technology. Specifically, the model inc orrectly identified Augmented Reality  (AR) headsets as Virtual Reality (VR) headsets, demonstrating a basic misunderstanding of the educational technology being used. The second example relates to participant identification and counting errors. For example, the model described "three students each wearing VR headsets" when the video showed only two students wearing AR headsets and one facilitator without any headset.  Another case of error involves gender misidentifica tion, where the model incorrectly interpreted male facilitator with medium-length hair (sitting in the right) as female and described shorts as dresses, revealing potential biases in the model's visual processing. Interpretation hallucination  occurs when the model's description of physical eve nts and actions are correct, but its inference or interpretation is inc orrect. We consider an interpretation "incorrect" i f it either does not align with expert researchers' analyses or make  claims without sufficient observable evidence. For example, Figure 1 shows a behavioral inference error. When analyzing a scene where a facilitator was holding and reading paper materials without engaging in conversation, t he model incorrectly interpreted this as "The stude nt on the right is holding papers and appears to be explainin g or discussing something with the other two studen ts." Additionally, the model misinterpreted the AR manipulation gesture as explaining. These examples illustrate two issues: first, the model misidentified the facilita tor's role, and second, it fabricated an interactio n (explaining/discussing) that was not actually occur ring. Such interpretation errors are particularly problematic in educational research for several reasons. First, th ey can lead to incorrect conclusions about student engagement and learning processes. Second, the model may assume typical scenarios based on common patterns it has learned, such as students holding papers while participating  in discussions. This can result in the model overl ooking or misrepresenting unique or valuable instances that d eviate from the norm. These misinterpretations coul d significantly impact the validity of any automated analysis, leading to flawed or misleading conclusio ns if not carefully addressed.

Instruction hallucination manifests when the model's output diverges from the  specific analytical focus requested in the instruction prompt. For instance, when prompted specifically to analyze collaborative interactions and gestures, the model's responses often began app ropriately but then deviated into detailed descript ions of classroom furniture, room layout, or personal items  like water bottles on desks. This deviation occurr ed despite explicit prompting to focus on student behaviors and interactions. The problem became more pronounced in longer responses, suggesting a progressive dilution of analytical focus. This type of hallucination poses challenges for educational research applications where focused  analysis of behavioral or interactional patterns i s crucial. When researchers aim to understand specific aspects of learning interactions, such divergence from the analytical focus can significantly reduce the utility of autom ated analysis. The model's tendency to drift toward  irrelevant details suggests limitations in its ability  to keep sustained attention to educationally meanin gful patterns of behavior and interaction.

## Discussion

Our analysis revealed three distinct types of hallucinations in MLLM outputs when processing educational videos. Here we examine how these types relate to existing frameworks while highlighting the unique aspects that emerge in educational contexts. Description hallucination  conceptually aligns with  factual inconsistency in NLP frameworks (Huang et al., 2023) and object category hallucination in MLLM frameworks (Bai et al., 2024), where generated text contradicts the input content. However, while NLP frameworks focus on text-fact inconsistency and MLLM frameworks emphasize object misidentificat ion, our educational context reveals how descriptio n hallucination extends to technology identification and gender misidentification. Such errors carry uni que implications in educational research.  While misidentifying AR as VR technology might negligibly  impact analysis of student interactions, mis identifications of participant behaviors and  gender identity raise concerns about equity and inclusivity, particularly when ana lyzing diverse student populations. This observatio n suggests that not all description hallucinations carry equal  weight. While maintaining descripti on accuracy is important, minor inaccuracies in scene description may be less  detrimental to research validity than errors in describing student behaviors, gender, and learning processes. Interpretation hallucination represents a novel category that goes beyond existing frameworks' focus on factual accuracy or object attributes, capturing the model’s ability (or inability) to infer meaning from observed actions and events. This type of hallucination highlights a key difference between general MLLM applications and educational r esearch needs-the requirement to not just describe what is seen, but to accurately interpret behaviors with in specific learning contexts. Instruction hallucination, while conceptually related to faithfulness hallucination in NLP frameworks, specifically addresses the model's difficulty in maintaining consistent attention to educationall y relevant aspects. Unlike general adherence to prompts, this type of hallucination reflects the unique challenge  in educational research of sustaining analytical a ttention on learning behaviors of interest while filtering out irrelevant environmental details. Why hallucination may occur To better understand these hallucinations, we exami ne their underlying mechanisms and potential causes. Description hallucinations emerge from two fundamental mechanisms. The first i nvolves limitations in training data representation, leading to confusion such as educational technology. The second mechanism relates to how MLLMs process visual and textual information  together. MLLMs' reliance on pattern matching from isolated visual elements can cause overgeneralization  (Gao et al., 2024). This limitation was evident when the model incorrectly assumed all participants wore headsets after observing two students with headsets, illustr ating how models may overgeneralize from partial pattern matching and lead to factual inconsistencies. Interpretation hallucinations stem from the model's difficulty integrating observations with context. One reason is the overgeneralization from training data. This is exemplified when the model incorrectly interpreted paper-holding-reading behavior as explanation and leading discuss ion as these two behaviors often co-occur in training data. The model struggles particularly with context-dependent behaviors, where similar behaviors may carry different meanings, such as distinguishing between a facilitator's moni toring behavior and active participation in discussion. As Q. Huang et al.  (2024) note, when lacking sufficient contextual information, the models tend to make speculative interpretations rather than evidence-based analysis. Instruction hallucinations  stem from both training characteristics and architectura l constraints. These MLLMs were trained on detailed image captions that emphasize c omprehensive scene description rather than focused analysis (Yue et al., 2024). This leads to unnecessary emphasis on environmental details despite prompted for behavioral analysis. Moreover, the attention mechanism of these models i ntroduces the issue of attention dilution. As the model generates more text, the self-attention shifts toward previously generated textual content rather than maintaining focus on visual input  (Q. Huang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). Thus, the attention to the visual content becomes progressively diluted as it generates more text. This diluted visual attention explains why the model no longer focus es on the specific visual content (i.e., students’ int eractions), leading to off-topic and irrelevant contents in output.

## Implications and future directions

Our findings have significant implications for the development and application of MLLMs in educational research. First, they suggest a need to reconceptua lize how we implement automated support for video a nalysis in education. Rather than viewing MLLMs as automated analysis tools, they could be more effective  as initial coding assistants  (Lopez-Fierro & Nguyen, 2024; H. Zhang et al., 2024), with careful integration of human oversight particularly for role identification and interaction analysis. This approach acknowledges th e models' current limitations while leveraging their potential to str eamline initial analysis phases.  Second, our framework provides a foundation for developing education-specific evaluation criteria for MLLMs. Traditional  evaluation metrics focused on object recognition accuracy or g eneral language capability fail to capture the nuan ced requirements of educational research (Chang et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2 024). Our identification of distinct hallucination types suggests the need for multilayered evaluation approaches that consider not just factual accuracy, but also interpretative validity and anal ytical focus maintenance. Third, these findings poi nt toward specific technical developments needed for more reliable educational applications. The development of educationspecific training datasets could address some of th e basic recognition errors we observed. However, mo re fundamental architectural modifications ma y be necessary to maintain a consistent analytical focus on educationally relevant features. This might include  developing specialized attention mechanisms that b etter balance visual and textual information processing in educational contexts.

Our findings should be considered in light of limitations. First, our analysis is based on a relatively small sample of video data (36 video segments; one student pair). Future research should investigate the prevalence and type of hallucinations across a wider range of educational settings and participant s. Second, our study employed LLaVA-Video-7B-Qwen2. It is possible that different MLLM architectures or training approaches could result in different hallucination patterns. Comparative studi es using multiple MLLMs would help to decide the extent to which our findings are model-specific or reflect more general challenges in applying MLLMs. Finally, our study primarily focused on examining and categorizing hal lucinations, with limited exploration of solutions. Our work suggests several future research directions for detecting and mitigating hallucinations. For example, the identified hallucinations, particularly around gender bias and role misident ification, suggest a need for fine-tuned MLLMs that better meet the needs of educational research purposes. This might include developing training datasets tailored to educational settings that prioritize not just object recognition but also the complex behavioral patterns and interactions characteristics of learning environments. Additionally, architectural improvements should focus on maintaining sustained attention to visual input  throughout the analysis process, ensuring consisten t analytical focus especially in longer outputs. In conclusion, this work presents a  first step towards understanding how and why MLLMs may fail in educational applications. This work lays the foundation for more reliable implementation of these models in educational research. Our framework provides practical guidelines for current applications and outlines clear directions for future development. T his contributes to the broader goal of enhancing qu alitative research methods in education through the thoughtful integration of automated analysis tools.

## Conclusion

This exploratory study examined hallucinations in M ultimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) when applie d to educational video analysis, proposing a novel framework for categorizing hallucinations specific to educational contexts: description, interpretation, and instruction hallucinations. Our analysis revealed that while MLLMs offer significant potential for facilitating qualitative video analysis in education, their outputs exhibit inaccuracies, including misidentification of technology, particip ant gender, roles, and behaviors.  Our findings emphasize the importance of domain-specific evaluation frameworks that move beyond general-purpose metrics toward a more nuanced assessment of educationally relevant tasks.  By grounding our investigation in real-world educational scenarios rather than synthetic benchmarks, we highlight unique challenges such as the accurate interpretation of context-dependent interactions, behavioral nuances, and sus tained analytical focus. These challenges underscor e the need for careful integration of human oversight when employing MLLMs in qualitative educational research. Future research should investigate these hallucinat ion patterns across broader educational contexts and diverse student groups, incorporating comparative analyses of various MLLM architectures to ascertain generalizability.

In conclusion, while MLLMs offer  promising capabilities for streamlining qualitative  video analysis in educational research, careful attention to their limitations, particularly hallucinations, remains essential. This work provides a foundational step towards understanding these limitations and outlines clear pathways for f uture advancements to ensure more reliable and effective integration of MLLMs in educational research contexts.
