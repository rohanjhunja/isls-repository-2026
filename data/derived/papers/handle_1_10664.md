# How Pedagogical Hints Impact Embodied Geometric Reasoning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Actions relevant to mathematical ideas can benefit thinking and learning. However, actions alone are insufficient to generate generalizable mathematical reasoning. Pedagogical language that connects actions to mathematical ideas may promote generalizable mathematical reasoning. This study investigated the role of pedagogical language on geometric reasoning and gesture production. College students (N=94) were assigned to either receive “social” hints that linked actions to social norms or “math” hints that linked actions to geometry conjectures. Results indicated that students’ mathematical insights and proofs did not differ between conditions, but the speech and gestures of their explanations did. Students with valid proofs were more likely to use transformational speech that described cohesive situational models and showed a perspectival shift from egocentric to allocentric. Moreover, between conditions, students receiving math hints were more likely to produce dynamic depictive gestures as part of their justifications, which have been shown to contribute to superior generalizable geometric proofs.

## Introduction

Geometric proofs are generalized statements about p roperties and relations of space and shape that stu dents struggle to generate (Healy & Hoyles, 2000). While many scholars have researched the effectiveness of languagebased pedagogical  moves in geometry instruction (e.g., Chapin et al., 2009; Stylianides et al., 2017), r ecent research has revealed that experts’ proof practices involve embodied processes (Jacobson et al., 2000; Marghetis et al., 2014) and that students can learn generalized mathematical ideas through first person bodily actions (Roth & Thom, 2009; Nathan, 2014). For example, engaging students in bo dy movement to model angles with their bodies has been shown to improve their understandin g of angles (Shoval, 2011). Similarly, Smith, King,  and Hoyte (2014) tracked elementary students’ movements as they learned to identify different types of angles. They found that to benefit  from this action-based intervention, it is critical for students to connect arm movements to canonical geometric representations of angles via a  grounding metaphor ANGLES AS SPACE BETWEEN SIDES. Students who made multiple dynamic movements (i.e., changing the size of the angles) to explore the gr ounding metaphor and test their hypotheses benefitted the most. In addition to actions, mathematical knowledge also  emerged from the social construction mediated by language (Romberg & Kaput, 1999). While actions are  foundational to early math development (Dehaene et  al,

1993) and can affect intuitive and automatic pro cesses (Nathan & Walkington, 2017), actions alone a re

insufficient to foster complex reasoning and carefu l argumentation. One specific type of language that  may be especially important for making explicit conceptual connections and promoting complex reasoning is pedagogical language, which transmits semantic information through oste nsive and referential communication (Csibra & Gergely, 2009). Nathan and colleagues (e.g., Nathan  & Walkington, 2017; Nathan et al., 2014) found that performing task-relevant directed actions (i.e., body movements that learners are instructed to perform) facilitated the mathematical insight that was not well articula ted verbally. Moreover, adding pedagogical language that explicitly linked the actions to the tasks enhanced  the proof production that required a chain of verbal reasoning. While previous evidence supports that pedagogical language enhances the effects of actions on proof production, more evidence is needed to understand how pedagogical language affects embodied geometric reasoning. This study used a motion-capture video game to explore how students’  embodied geometric reasoning was affected when we varied the use of pedagogical language that accompanies the directed actions as r elevant to the geometric conjectures. Our goal was to understand how pedagogical and mathematical language that makes conceptual connections to relevant directed actions impacts students’ geometric reasoning and gesture production. This investigation offers insights into how languag e, along with action, can provide a system for grou nding geometric knowledge and supporting advanced mathematical reasoning.

## Theoretical framework

Embodied geometric thinking and proof production Mathematical proof “is a richly embodied practice that involves inscribing and manipulating notations, interacting with those notations through speech and gesture, and using the body to enact the meanings of mathematical ideas” (Marghetis et al., 2014, p. 243). For novice studen ts in classrooms, the multimodal nature of proof is  often evidenced by their spontaneous verbal and gestural explanations, as opposed to formal, written forms (Healy & Hoyles, 2000). Studies have demonstrated that components of transformational speech  and dynamic depictive gestures independently contributed to students’ constructions of mathematically valid proofs (Nathan et al, 2021; Pier et al., 2019). Transformational speech include s verbal explanations of goal-directed manipulations of mathematical objects (i.e., conditional statements and inferences), and dynamic depictive gestures (as opposed to static; see Garcia & Infante, 2012) are body-based enactments of motion-based transformations (e.g., rotating and dilating) of mathematical objects. These findings s uggest that gesture and speech are important embodi ed mechanisms that ground students’ expressions of proof-related reasoning. Reciprocity between directed action and cognitive process Evidence is mounting that mathematical ideas can be learned through action-based interventions (e.g., Nathan & Walkington, 2017; Walkington et al., 2022). Sensori motor activity can activate neural systems, which, in turn, induce and alter cognitive states (Thomas, 2013). This reciprocal relationship between bodily states and cognitive states is known as action-cognition transduction (ACT; Nathan, 2014). Empirical evidence from studies directing eye gaze (Thomas & Lleras, 2007) and arm movements (Nathan et al., 2014) to key insights for solving a problem has led to superior performance in general problem solving and mathematics. The ACT model allows us to explore two claims: (1) relevant actions induce cognitive s tates that benefit mathematical reasoning, and (2) actions may foster mathematical insights that are nonverbal (Nathan & Walkington, 2017). These two claims were tested empirically by Nathan and colleagues (2014) using directed actions to influence proof performance. They found that participants who performed task-relevant directed actions, despite being unaware of their relevance, were more likely to gen erate correct intuitions (i.e., snap judgments) and  key mathematical insights (i.e., gist) than those who p erformed task-irrelevant actions. However, they found that performing directed actions alone was ineffective i n producing valid proofs. Rather, it was the combin ation of directed actions with explanatory speech and gestur e that contributed to valid proof production, as ev idenced by participants’ gestures that replayed the directed a ctions during mathematical reasoning (Donovan et al., 2014). Walkington et al. (2022) corroborated that these gestural replays of directed actions were significant contributors to generating key mathematical insight and valid proof. Pedagogical language in mathematical reasoning and gesture production For a skill as complex as geometric proof, explicit  support for meaning making is needed (Lehrer & Chazan, 2012). Often, pedagogical language appears in the form of prompts (prospective statements) and hints (retrospective statements) to help students connect  ideas. Research indicates that both prompts and hints fost er cognitive processing and conceptual understanding (Hilbert et al., 2008). Nathan and colleagues (2014) found that mathematically relevant directed actions enhanced p roof performance when accompanied by pedagogical language linking the relevance of directed actions to geometric conjectures. This result suggests that pedagogical language activates participants ’ verbal articulations for proof. In addition, Nathan  and Walkington (2017) also found that pedagogical language, in the form of hin ts about the mathematical relevance of directed act ions, increased the likelihood of students generating correct mathematical insights and valid proofs in the context of an embodied video game that tracked student s’ movements. Moreover, such mathematically relevant p edagogical hints were also associated with increased occurrences of dynamic depictive gestures, which have been shown to be predictive of constructing valid proofs. While these studies demonstrate associations betwee n mathematical pedagogical language and geometric reasoning, further research is needed to investigate their causal relationship to understand the robustness of pedagogical language for promoting embodied mathematical reasoning. The current study Central to the current investigation is that action-and language-based factors are manipulable to influence geometric reasoning. In essence, our logic model (F igure 1) focuses on simulated actions (Hostetter & Alibali, 2019), operationalized using directe d actions and pedagogical language and observed as outcomes in students’ speech and gesture production.  Specifically, we focus on students’ generation of dynamic depictive gestures and transformational speech (Pier et al., 201 9). Previous work using gesture and computerized text a nalysis of students’ justifications and proofs demonstrated ho w transformational speech coupled with dynamic depi ctive gestures predicted valid proof production across a variety of mathematical conjectures (Pier et al., 2019).  In all, the logic model (Figure 1) posits that through the coordination of action and language, students expre ss their conceptual understanding and produce valid mathemat ical proofs when their explanations include dynamic depictive gestures and transformational speech.

O ur study explored the role of mathematical pedagogical language in communicating the relevance and meaning  of task-relevant directed actions on students’ abilities to determine the veracity of geometric conjectures. Sp ecifically, we operationalize our independent variables usin g pedagogical language (i.e., hints) to influence  directed actions as a mediation of students’ insigh ts, proof performance, and gestures that students spontaneous ly produced during geometric reasoning.  Our research questions ask: How do pedagogical hints, either explicitly linking actions to mathematical conjectures (i.e., math hints) or to social norms of the village (i.e., social hints), impact students’ verbal geometric reasoning as measured by insights and proofs (RQ1)? And how do these peda gogical hints impact students’ gesture production (RQ2)? We hypothesize that  students who receive the mathematical hints  will be more likely to (1) generate correct insights and proofs, and (2) produce gestures during explanations of geometric conjectur es, compared to those who receive social hints (i.e., control condition).

## Participants and procedure

As part of a larger study, ninety-four undergraduate students (70% female; x̄ AGE = 20.14; 19% Non-Native English Speaker) were recruited at a large Midwest institution. Fifty-nine students identified as white/non-Hispanic, 24 as Asian, 3 as Hispanic, 3 as African American, and 5 as Other race/ethnicity. Inclusion criteria required that students had completed at least one high school geometry cou rse enabling researchers to assess students’ familiarity with justification and proof practices. As compensation for completing the study, each participant received  a $20 gift card.

In this between-subjects experiment, participants were randomly assigned to either the mathematically relevant or socially relevant condition (Figure 2). Participants first completed a survey via Qualtrics that included a subset of 15 questions from the Spatial Reasoning Instrument  (SRI; Ramful et al., 2017) and a subset of 12 questions from the Diagnostic Geometry Assessment (DGA, Masters, 2010). Next, all students played through 8 geometry conjectures through The Hidden Village (THV) via remote administration over Zoom, where they were asked to provide rationales for the veracity of eac h conjecture. After completing all eight geometry c onjectures, participants were randomly assigned to one of the two hint condi tions. In the social condition (i.e., control condition), participants received hints about how movements communicated a social convention of the village. In the math condition, participants received hints on how movements could represent a mathematical con cept of conjectures (see an example in Figure 3). Subsequently, 4 of the previous 8 conjectures we re repeated, and depending on the condition, each was accompanied by either social or math hints, such that before each conjecture, students were given either a social context for the conjecture’s directed actions or the mathematical conjecture along with its direct ed actions. Administration of all  conjectures was counter-balanced using a Latin Square design.

## Coding and data analysis

Video recordings of students playing THV and providing explanations as to the veracity of geometric conjectures produced an assortment of data. Responses were segm ented by conjectures, resulting in 376 post-intervention video clips (94 x 4 conjectures). Al l clips were then transcribed and coded for mathema tical insight, transformational proof, and gesture by a member of the research team using Transana 3.21 (Woods & Fassnacht, 2019). All categories were given a 0/1 (present/absent) fo r the entire clip of each conjecture. Insight was coded based on  participants’ understanding of key mathematical ide as, or “gist,” for each conjecture. For transformational proof, we followed Harel and Sowde r’s (2005) definition of transformational proof tha t states proofs must be (1) generalizable, (2) involve logic al inference, and (3) operational thought. For our purposes, evidence of operational thought included dynamic depictive gestures and speech that described operations and transformations of objects. For gestures, we coded for representational gestures using McNeil’s (1992) definition. Representational gestures were then sub-coded as non-dynamic depictive (representing mathematical objects and or their properties) or dynamic depictive (represen ting a transformation of a mathematical object). We  also used Coh-Metrix, a validated text data-mining tool, to further analyze participants’ verbal reasoning for each conjecture (McNamara et al., 2014). Prior studies (e.g., Nathan et al. 2021) identified three variables of interest for insigh t and transformational proof: verb use, first-person pronoun use, and situational model cohesion. These studies also identified four variables of interest for depictive  gestures: comparative connectives use, verb use, second-person pronoun use, and first-person pronoun use.

Quantitative analysis used mixed effects logistic regression models on the outcome measures of insight, transformational proof, and gesture using the glmer command in the R software package lme4 (Bates et al., 2014). Participant ID and conjecture were modeled as random effects. For insight and proof, initial models included intervention condition, prior performance on the fi rst attempt (before the language intervention), and scores on spatial ability and the geometry pretest as covaria tes. In subsequent models, we added gestures and me asures of transformational speech from Coh-Metrix. For gesture models, intervention condition,  transformational speech, and scores on spatial ability and the geometry pretes t were fixed effects. Spatial ability scores, geometry pretest scores, and all Coh-Metrix variables were scaled and centered prior to analysis. Odds ratios were calculated by exponentiating the log odds ratios for interpretability.

## Discussion and conclusion

The current research investigated the impact of ped agogical language in the form of hints on the influence of directed actions on students’ geometric reasoning. While our results did not demonstrate that mathemat ical hints directly promote d mathematical insight and proof, data analyses revea led that students who were given mathematical hints that explicitly connected the re levance of directed actions to the geometry conject ures were more likely to produce representational gestures, e specially dynamic depictive gestures that have been shown t o facilitate mathematical insight and proof production (e.g., Nathan et al., 2021; Walkington et al., 2022). This result partially supports our logical model (see Figure 1), suggesting that the coordination of action and la nguage is crucial for students to perform simulated actions that lead to generalized mathematical reasoning. For RQ1, we found no significant differences betwee n the social and mathematical hint conditions for insights and proofs. However, there were some notab le characteristics in participants’ speech. Specifically, students who generated correct insights were more likely to use verbs and less likely to use first-person pronouns, suggesting that students were thinking about action s (i.e., transformations) and shifted away from an egocentric perspective. Similarly, students constructing trans formational proofs also reduced their use of first-person pronouns, further suggesting an allocentric shift i n understanding the problem space. Moreover, studen ts with valid transformational proofs were more likely to f orm a cohesive situational model, that is, construc ting a situational model with internal cohesion. We argue that forming such situational models is facilitated through the process of simulating actions on mathematical objec ts (Hostetter & Alibali, 2019) that are subsequentl y invoked during problem solving to help reason geometrically.

For RQ2, we see that students in the math hints condition were more likely to produce dynamic depictive gestures (i.e., gestures that simulate mathematical  transformations), which are significantly correlat ed to successful transformational proof performanc e (Nathan et al., 2021; Pier et al. 2019). This res ult aligns with our proposed logic model (see Figure 1), suggesting tha t pedagogical language directing one’s attention to  the relevance of actions may moderate the relationship between cognition and actions by influencing gestur e production. Students’ non-dynamic representational gestures which depict stat ic properties of the mathematical identities showed a corroborating decrease in verb usage. Additionally, students receiving math hints were more likely to use second-person pronouns (i.e., you), suggesting an explanatory nature to students sharing their insights in the processes of producing a valid proof.

For students between conditions, one possible reaso n for the lack of significant differences in insigh ts and proofs is that those who were given the math hi nt might not have fully grasped the conceptual conn ections between the directed actions and geometry conjectures, limiting its’ effectiveness. Another possibility is that our measures of insight and proof, as logistic variables, may not be sensitive enough to capture the progress ion (i.e., development) of cognitive processes that occur as students conce ptualize geometric thinking through embodied physical actions and perceptions (Alibali & Nathan,  2012; Williams-Pierce et al., 2017). Future studies will be needed to delve deeper into students’ geometric thi nking and the role that embodied actions and pedago gical language plays. The current state of our data limit s the conclusions we can reach from our analysis and warrants the need for additional evidence to support the role of pedagogical language in mathematical reasoning. To address this, our research team plans to revisit the raw da ta to determine whether the gestures students produ ced during their explanations were replays of the directed act ions. Such gestural replays bridge the concrete actions to the generalized mathematical reasoning by highlighting the relevant information of directed actions (Walki ngton et al., 2022; Xia et al., 2023). By exploring these ge stural replays, we hope to find evidence that stude nts wer e making conceptual connections between directed actions and geometry tasks, assisted by pedagogical language.

In conclusion, this randomized controlled experimen tal study  extends previous research (Nathan & Walkington, 2017;  Nathan et al., 2014) by providing causal evidence that mathematical pedagogical langu age coupled with action can influence gesture productio n and transformational speech, which have been foun d to independently contribute to the construction of gen eralized mathematical reasoning. An important impli cation is that there is value in eliciting the body-based resources that students have access to, but c onnecting the potential relevance of these actions may further the utility of these resources during complex mathematical reasoning.
