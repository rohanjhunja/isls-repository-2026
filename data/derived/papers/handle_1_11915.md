# Unpacking Visual Attention Strategies in Collaborative Learning: A Gaze Analysis in Tech-rich University Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores differences in gaze behavior between higher-and lower-performing groups in a university-level CSCL setting. Sixty-eight engineering students completed a collaborative task, with performance judged by design artifacts. Using computer vision, gaze patterns were analyzed. Higher-performing groups focused more on their own screens, while lower-performing groups looked more at peers and off-task areas. Findings reveal distinct visual attention strategies and highlight the potential of multimodal analytics to examine interactions in tech-rich collaborative learning environments.

## Introduction

Collaborative learning is a foundational approach i n 21st-century education, especially in engineering, where teamwork and problem-solving are critical (Atman et al., 2007). Rooted i n socio-cultural learning theory, it involves students co-constructing knowledge through interaction and coor dination (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995). Recent work highlights the importance of multimodal  interactions—including verbal and non-verbal cues like gaze and gesture—as essential components of such collaboration (e.g., Lyu et al., 2025), with eye gaze serving as a key indicator of cognitive and social engagement  (Whitehead et al., 2024). Building on advances in computervision-based gaze estimation (e.g., Valtakari et al., 2023), this study investigates how students allocate vi sual attention across digital and physical elements during collaboration in authentic, tech-rich classrooms. While prior work has explored eye contact and object-focused gaze, less is known about how these pattern s relate to group performance. This study aims to address this gap by asking: W hether and how do visual attention patterns differ between higher-and lower-performing collaborative learning groups in technology-rich classroom environments?

## Methodology

This study involved 68 fourth-year Mechatronics Engineering students (average age  23.5) from a Singapore university, randomly paired into 34 dyads to comple te a 1-hour face-to-face collaborative design task. Students used the Miro platform, printed worksheets, and personal devices to design an automated robotics vehicle capable of detecting and delivering tennis balls. All sessi ons were video-recorded, with front-facing cameras capturing students’ upper bodies and resources. After removin g low-quality or no n-dyadic recordings, 30 hours of usable footage remained. A pre-trained YOLOv8 model was used to detect seven gaze targets (e.g., peer’s face, laptops, worksheet), and a gaze estimation model (accuracy =  83.4%, Cohen’s Kappa = 0.95) processed one frame e very 5 seconds, generating 720 data points per dyad. Gaz e data were aggregated by object, and independent t-tests compared attention patterns between groups. To dete rmine performance levels, each dyad’s design artifa ct was assessed on variety, elaboration, n ovelty, and functionality (0 –5 scale). Inter-coder reliability was strong (α = 0.81–0.91), and top/bottom 20% of groups (n=7 each) were labeled higher-and lower-performing for comparison.

## Findings and conclusion

To understand the gaze behavior of higher and lower  performing groups, the mean frequency and percenta ge of each object is reported in Table 1. Independent samples t-tests were conducted to compare gaze behavior between higher-and lower-performing groups across different objects of focus. Higher-performing groups focused significantly more on their own screens (M= 0.66, S D = 0.14) than lower-performing groups (M = 0.49, SD = 0.08), t(12) = -2.92, p =.016, Cohen’s d = -1.56, indicating a large effect. The lower-performing groups tended to look more at their partner’s screen (d = 0.89) and face (d = 0.61), while higher-performing groups were slightly more likely to focus on their cellphones (d = -0.39). The lower-performing groups also showed higher off-task gaze (M = 0.13, SD = 0.03) than higher-performing groups (M = 0.05, SD = 0.06), t (12) = 2.08, p=.063, Cohen’s d = 1.11. These findings suggest that the higher-performing groups focus more on task-relevant elements, particularly their own screens, while lower-performing groups exhibit more partner-focused and off-task gaze behaviors.

This study explored students’ gaze behaviors in tec h-rich collaborative learning environments using computer vision-based models to detect visual attention toward self  and partner screens, peer’s face, worksheet, cellphone, or off-task directions. Findings  revealed that higher-performing groups focused more on their own screens, while lower-performing groups gazed more at their partner’s fac e, screen, and the air. These results provide new empirical insights into visual attentio n strategies in CSCL contexts  and differ from prior studies where peer-focused gaze was positively linked to collaboration  (Chen et al., 2021). In contrast, drawing on Whitehead et al. (2024), laptop-oriented gaze in this study may reflect metacogniti ve activities like planning and monitoring, while peer-oriented gaze supports socioemotional interaction —possibly less critical here as the students already had established rapport. From a gr oup awareness perspective, working on a shared digi tal platform may have enabled students to track partner  contributions via their own screen, reducing the n eed for physical gaze shifts. These interpretations suggest  new directions for investigating the role of digit al tools in collaborative attention. Methodologically, this study demonstrates that deep learning-based gaze estimation offers a cost-effective alternative to traditional eye trackers, reducing barriers for classroom-based gaze analysis. The findings also extend Stahl’s (2006) group cognition theory by illustrating how cognition emerges not only through peer discourse but also through embodied interactions with tools and the environment. Future multimodal learning analytics approaches could integrate gaze data with  speech, log data, neural, and bodily signals to fu rther investigate group cognition (e.g., Lyu et al., 2023; 2025).
