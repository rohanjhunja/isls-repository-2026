# Reexamining the Measurement of Metacognitive Regulation in Online STEM Learning: Validity and Fairness Evidence

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study reexamines the measurement of metacognitive regulation in online STEM learning in the context of broad access institutions serving more diverse student populations than the selective contexts where these measures were originally developed. Using data from 1,517 undergraduates enrolled in gateway STEM courses, we analyzed three regulation-of-cognition subscales from the Metacognitive Awareness Inventory: Comprehension Monitoring, Debugging, and Evaluation. We used exploratory factor analysis, item response theory, and differential item functioning (DIF) to evaluate dimensionality, reliability, and measurement invariance. Results supported a three-factor theoretical model, though a one-dimensional model provided better parsimony and fit. Items showed moderate discrimination and difficulty, with debugging items being harder to endorse. Measurement invariance was maintained across time, gender, and first-generation status, with minor DIF across students from different racial and ethnic backgrounds.

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated a lasting transformation in higher education, rapidly expanding online learning. In the U.S., the share of undergraduates taking at least one online course rose from 37% before the pandemic to 75% in 2020 and has remained above 50% since (Barshay, 2024). Many students now prefer virtual or hybrid formats, prompting institutions to expand online offerings (Garrett et al., 2023). Yet, student satisfaction and completion rates in online STEM courses remain lower than in other fields, partly due to the heavy reliance on lecture-based instruction  and increased demands for autonomous learning (McIntyre et al., 2025). These conditions highlight the importance of self-directed learning (SDL) and metacognitive regulation for success in these environments (Yarnall et al., 2023).

Metacognition, broadly defined as awareness and regulation of one’s cognitive processes, is central to SDL (Flavell, 1979; Schraw & Dennison, 1994). Within this framework, the regulation of cognition --planning, monitoring, and evaluating one’s learning--supports adaptive strategy use in response to task demands. These processes are often operationalized as comprehension monitoring, debugging, and evaluation and are especially critical in online STEM learning, where students must frequently check comprehension, troubleshoot confusion, and reflect on progress (Zepeda & Nokes-Malach, 2023). While metacognitive regulation is consistently linked to deeper learning, many students  still exhibit limited monitoring accuracy, underuse corrective strategies, and struggle to evaluate their learning (Tuononen et al., 2023). Although the Metacognitive Awareness Inventory (MAI; Schraw & Dennison, 1994) is widely used to assess these skills, studies have reported inconsistent factor structures and mixed validity evidence across contexts (González-Cabañes et al., 2022). Recent studies have shown that its regulation-of-cognition subscales exhibit acceptable reliability and conceptual coherence across cultures (Gutierrez de Blume et al., 2024). However, little is known about its performance across learner subgroups within higher education, such as first-generation, gender, and racial/ethnic groups. These students often differ in prior exposure to learning strategies and self-regulatory experiences (e.g., Stephens et al., 2012), potentially leading to differential interpretation of items and systematic measurement bias. Furthermore, despite advances in trace measures  of SDL derived from behavior within an online environment (Du et al., 2023), self-reports like the MAI remain widely used. Evaluating their dimensionality and fairness in broad-access contexts is therefore essential. This study examines the three regulation-of-cognition subscales of the MAI --Comprehension Monitoring, Debugging, and Evaluation--to clarify their psychometric structure and stability in online STEM learning in broad access institutions. We explore how these dimensions interrelate and function across diverse student groups to determine whether metacognitive regulation operates similarly  across demographic and educational backgrounds.

1322 This study is part of a larger project evaluating online instructional supports designed to enhance students’ SDL in online STEM courses offered by broad-access institutions. A total of 1,517 undergraduate undergraduates enrolled in introductory STEM courses in four U.S. broad-access institutions participated in this study. Participants were enrolled in online or hybrid courses spanning various disciplines, including biology, chemistry, computer science, earth science, mathematics, and others. Of these students, 53% were enrolled in course sections that implemented the instructional supports. Demographic characteristics of the sample included 66% identifying as female, 45% as full-time students, 50% enrolled entirely online, 30% as first-generation college students, and 48% receiving Pell Grants. The racial/ethnic composition was 32% White, 22% Latine, 21% Black/African American, 7% Asian, and 7% Multiracial, with the remaining students not reporting racial/ethnic information. As mentioned, we used three subscales from Schraw and Dennison’s (1994) MAI regulation of cognition scale: monitoring (7 items), debugging (5 items), and evaluation (6 items). Each item used a binary True/False format. Half of the students (50%) completed the measure twice --once prior to the delivery of the instructional supports in intervention course sections and once at the end of the course. Twelve percent completed it only at the end, and the remaining students completed it only at the beginning. We conducted a multi-step psychometric analysis to evaluate the underlying structure of the three metacognitive regulation measures using data collected at both time points. The analysis began with Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) to identify potential item groupings, followed by Item Response Theory (IRT) modeling to examine dimensionality and item-level properties. A two-parameter logistic (2-PL) model was estimated using marginal likelihood. To assess dimensionality, we compared three models: (1) a one-dimensional baseline model,

(2) the EFA-derived model, and (3) a theory-based model. The model with the lowest Bayesian Information

Criterion (BIC) was selected as the best-fitting model. When the theory-based model was not the best-fitting model, we compared it with the selected model in terms of item quality and reliability. Item fit was evaluated using item difficulty, discrimination, and the S-X² (Orlando-Thissen) statistic, with an RMSEA cutoff of 0.08. Next, we conducted Differential Item Functioning (DIF) analyses on the combined pre-and post-test data to detect potential response shifts due to timing or context (i.e., whether observed DIF was driven by the intervention or by test administration timing). Notably, the modeling treated time as independent across individuals, as a nested time-within-person model would have been computationally intensive. Therefore, pre-and post-test data were analyzed as two separate groups. A second set of DIF analyses was conducted using only the pre-test data to test for measurement invariance across first-generation status, gender, and race/ethnicity. A step-down method was employed to identify and remove items exhibiting DIF, with a one-step fallback procedure applied when necessary. Both uniform and non-uniform DIF were tested, and items were flagged based on FDRadjusted p-values below.05. All IRT and DIF analyses were conducted using the  mirt package in R (Chalmers, 2012). Finally, Cronbach’s alpha was computed using the pre-test data to verify the internal consistency of each subscale.

Structure of Metacognitive Regulation and Item Characteristics. The EFA revealed a three-factor structure, with Factors 1, 2, and 3 accounting for 15.7%, 8.0%, and 6.8% of the total variance, respectively. As shown in table 1, items related to Monitoring and Evaluation tended to load together, while most Debugging items loaded on a separate factor --except for DB_01, which appeared to align more closely with Monitoring or Evaluation processes. Interestingly, the third factor appeared to reflect items emphasizing strategic evaluation or consideration of multiple options or alternatives.

When comparing the three IRT models --the one-dimensional (1D) model, the EFA-based threedimensional (3D) model, and the theory-based 3D model--the 1D model demonstrated the best balance between model fit and parsimony (Log-likelihood = −14,720.79; BIC = 29,723.10). The EFA-based and theory-based 3D models yielded higher BIC values (Log-likelihood = −14,967.77; BIC = 30,217.05; and Log-likelihood = −15,324.34; BIC = 30,930.20, respectively). When comparing item difficulty, discrimination, and item fit between the 1D and theory-based 3D models, Monitoring and Evaluation items behaved similarly across models, suggesting good internal consistency with theoretical expectations. However, Debugging items (particularly DB_04 and DB_05) showed large differences between models, characterized by high difficulty and extreme discrimination values, potentially indicating response bias or ceiling effects. While the theory-based 3D model better distinguishes between dimensions, it may also inflate discrimination estimates for items with sparse response patterns. All items demonstrated acceptable fit, with RMSEA values below 0.08 in both models, supporting overall adequacy of the item-level fit. Regarding reliability, Cronbach’s alpha value for 1D was.80, and for the individual dimension monitoring, debugging, and evaluation, the values were.71,.55, and.66 respectively.

Regarding measurement invariance across the three subscales, we found that for time invariance in both the 1D and theory-based 3D models, none of the items showed significant DIF across pre-and post-test administrations after controlling for the false discovery rate (FDR; p >.05 for all items). This indicates that measurement invariance was upheld and that item functioning remained stable over time. Similar results were observed f or first-generation status, where 1324 no items exhibited significant DIF in either the 1D or theory-based 3D models, suggesting consistent measurement across student background groups.

For gender, one Evaluation item (EV_04) showed significant DIF in the 1D model but not in the theorybased 3D model, indicating that the multidimensional specification may better account for gender-related response variation. In contrast, for ethnicity, several items displayed DIF in both models, though the specific items differed. In the 1D model, six items were flagged as exhibiting significant DIF in at least one pairwise comparison among racial/ethnic groups: CM_03, CM_06, DB_01, DB_04, DB_05, and EV_02. Among these, CM_06 showed the strongest DIF effects, particularly in comparisons involving Asian versus Black/African American, Latine/Hispanic, Multiracial, and White students.  In the theory-based 3D model, six items also showed DIF-primarily those related to Monitoring and Evaluation: CM_02, CM_03, CM_06, CM_07, EV_01, and EV_05. However, none of these items exhibited DIF to the same degree of severity as CM_06 in the 1D model. Overall, these findings suggest that while some racial/ ethnic group differences exist in item responses, the theory-based multidimensional model better mitigates severe measurement bias across groups. courses might view monitoring and evaluation as intertwined elements of a reflective cycle, aligning with models of cyclical self-directed learning. In contrast, debugging emerged as a distinct, action-oriented process focused on problem correction, while a “strategic consideration” factor reflected higher-order reflection, indicating that advanced learners integrate metacognition into planning and problem solving. However, our findings seemed to reveal that these three dimensions also can better be considered as one dimension of regulation of cognition, instead of separating them into three different dimensions. Evidence of measurement invariance across time supports the use of the adapted MAI to detect changes in metacognitive regulation, with minimal DIF observed across gender and first-generation status. However, because pre-and post-test administrations were modeled as independent groups, potential within-person dependency was not explicitly accounted for. As computational constraints continue to ease, future research using longitudinal or multilevel IRT approaches may provide  even more precise estimates of change and invariance over time.

Finally, the presence of minor DIF across racial and ethnic groups underscores the importance of further examining potential sources of cultural or contextual bias. Future research should incorporate qualitative methods, such as cognitive interviewing, to better understand how students interpret items and whether observed differences reflect construct-relevant variation or measurement bias. Such efforts are critical for refining  widely used instruments like the MAI to ensure valid and equitable measurement in increasingly diverse learning environments.
