# Historical Dispositions for Designerly Citizenship: An Attempt to Bridge Social Studies Education with Learning Sciences

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, I coordinate the salient literature at the intersection of two fields: learning sciences and history/social studies education. Specifically, I take up the notion of dispositions from a sociocognitive perspective to offer greater clarity for the term, which is used widely but imprecisely when referring to the purpose and aims of history education for the public good. I then draw on conceptions of dispositions as the interplay between ability, sensitivity, and inclination in situated context. I describe an overarching Historical Disposition that views history as both useable and useful in informing present-day decisions in pursuit of re-imagined possible futures. I further frame this work within designerly citizenship, which reimagines citizenship as participating in collective designerly agency. I conclude by discussing the implications of this work for how history and social studies education might be reimagined in concert with the learning sciences.

## Introduction

Problems like the global climate crisis, preserving  democratic institutions, and the systematic margin alization of nondominant communities are “wicked” problems in that they are marked by great complexity. A hall mark of problem wickedity is that the very solutions intend ed to ameliorate some aspects of the target problem  tend not only to exacerbate existing challenges but in fact often catalyze new ones (Buchanon, 1992; Rittel & Webber, 1973). In previous work, my colleagues and I (Kessner et al., 2020) suggested educators can accep t greater responsibility for confronting such wicked problems  by taking up as an explicit learning aim the devel opment of young people’s capacity for designerly citizenship, which we described as “a way of viewing ourselves and others as members of a community of co-citizens” who actively participate in decisions reg arding the distribution of social goods (e.g., respect, autonomy, dignity), and who see the world as a confluence of (re)designs in progress over which they and th eir co-citizens possess designerly agency (p. 1119). In th is view, designerly citizens are personally—but not individually —responsible for (re)imagining possible futures (Gut iérrez et al., 2019) and (re)designing the world around them to align to those futures. This view of civic participation as an act of design emerges at the intersection of citizenship and designerly thinking. Concerning the former, I draw largely from the field of social studies education, and the latter, from the extant literature on thinking like designers. What emerges is a way of thinking about citizenship as an active, participatory process of (re)designing the world to reflect community valuations of social goods and to distribute those goods in ways that align to community values. As I conceptualize it, designerly citizenship works  similarly to consumer activism as discussed by Matthew Desmond (2023). Certainly, the latter falls under the umbrella of the former. Activist consumers leverage traditional market forces like demand and corporate greed to shift corporate behavior in resp onse to consumer values. Activist consumers recognize their role as creators of demand, to which producers respond by c reating supply. Activist consumers, however, recognize supp ly comes with inherent labo r and material costs, and those costs bear moral and ethical implications for the kind of world in which we live. For example, I need a sweater. I can purchase a swe ater from Company A or Company B. My choice matters, because each dollar I give to either compa ny is essentially a vote in favor of them continuin g their business as usual. If Company A pays marginalized g roups slave wages, offers poor working conditions, and dumps chemical runoff into the local watershed, pur chasing my sweater from Company A is a vote for tho se practices. Purchasing from Company B, however, who pays a living wage, offers paid parental leave, and produces their products at net-zero carbon, is therefore a vote for a very differe nt kind of world. Company B’s sweater likely bears a nontrivial price increase, but the willingness to pay that increased cost is not only part of the choice but indeed illustrates a crucial aspect of designerly citizenship. Designerly citizenship relies on seeing the world a s a set of interacting systems and institutions, ea ch with their own sets of wicked problems and laden with issues of power and privilege. Even should one be willing and glad to pay more for a sweater from  Company B to make their bid in favor of dignified t reatment of their fellow human beings and the environment, they should also recognize this remains a non-choice for many due to inequitable distribution of social and material goods along racial and socioeconomic lines  (e.g., educational quality and opportunity, social mobility). Designerly citizens therefore see the sweater problem as one not just of choosing between two companies offering similar products but  one of societal organization  and of a common good (Reich, 2018). They therefore also turn to the political realm as a space in which to confront these inequities. Thus, whereas one can vote with their dollars, they  can also vote with their votes, for example in fav or of representatives or policies supporting structural c hange like raising the minimum wage or supporting u nions. Similarly, we can vote with our time, our labor, or any other “currencies” we have available to us. Nevertheless, a disposition for seeing the world in  such a way is insufficient on its own. One could exercise designerly agency on the world for ill as easily as for good. Something additional is needed in conceptualizing designerly citizenship as a learnin g aim if it is to be useful in confronting the wick ed problems facing our world and for informing the kinds of pos sible futures we seek to (re)imagine together. Considering existing education infrastructures already include core domain subjects like STEM, ELA, and social stu dies, it makes some sense to begin there. In this paper, I b egin with history and social studies education, owi ng to my own background as a history and social studies teac her, teacher educator, and scholar. In what follows, I outline an overarching conceptual framework of historical d ispositions that views history as useable and usefu l for thinking about the present and planning for the fut ure. I suggest eight component stances as useful fo r adding specificity and greater texture to the ideas I forward. In so doing, this work contributes to the field of history and social studies education by grounding dispositions, a term used widely but imprecisely to describe the aims of this core school subject, more firmly in the salient theory and literature. By doing so, I hope to consolidate support for dispositions as learning aims while offering a clea rer conceptualization that makes them more feasible  for this purpose. Additionally, though I present this work i n the context of history and social studies educati on, it is also intended as an act of invitational scholarship (Bar ab et al., 2009); that is, it is an invitation to o ther scholars to consider how dispositions, as I describe them here, might be leveraged as learning aims in other content areas and across education more broadly.

The purpose of history education By ‘history education’ I mean historical study that is inseparable from the social studies (e.g., economics, civics, geography). History loses much of its texture and n uance if not informed by the social studies —and the social studies without history lacks the framing that comes from the context and perspective history offers (Thornton & Barton, 2010). Therefore, for present purposes, I use the word ‘history’ in what follows to mean the layered study of the past from multiple perspectives, including t hose from economics, political science, human psych ology, geography, and all the disciplines that add texture and nuance to our understanding of the past. What is the purpose of such historical study, particularly in publicly funded schools? Some might argue that history’s place in the formal curriculum requi res no justification, that the study of history is its own reward and serves its own purpose. On this point I agree w ith Wineburg (2001), who noted that, “the answer to  this neglected question is hardly self-evident” (p. 5); and with Barton and Levstik (2004), who contended: History needs justification, in part, because in an overcrowded curriculum, there is no obvious reason why schools should include the topic at all, rather thandrop i t in favor of more economics or geography —or for that matter, more mathematics or chemistry. (pp. 26-27) There is no simple or agreed-upon answer to this question, and interest in answe ring it at all has waxed and waned over time (Orrill & Shapiro, 2005). Some have argued for history’s potential for helping us know and humanize ourselves (Collingwood, 1943/2014; Wineburg, 2001). Others have advocated a disciplinary approach to history in which students learn skills relevant to civic live (e.g., van Boxtel & vandrie, 2018; Wineburg, 2001). Others describe the benefits of an historical consciousness of the ways in which the past influences and continues to shape our present (e.g., Nordgren, 2016; Seixas, 2004). The set of purposes I consider here, however, are more in line with Barton & Levstik (2004), who unequivoc ally argue for a vision of history education th at capitalizes on its “potential to prepare students for participation in a pluralist democracy” and which is “ultimately concerned with how history can help citizens engage in collaboration toward a common good” (p. x). I agree also with Banks (2006), who contended that the purpose of social studies education is to prepare students as citizen-actors able to interrogate forms of knowledge and information to o ptimize their decision-making abilities. I argue history education serves an invaluable purpose in schools: the preparation of young people to don the mantle o f citizenship, which includes using nuanced understanding of the past to make decisions in the present in pursuit of a desirable shared future.

Dispositions: A sociocognitive perspective Dispositions, as I use the term here, are ways of seeing, knowing, and being in the world (Gresalfi, 2009; Thomas & Brown, 2007), operationalized as behaviors exhibi ted with high levels of frequency. Dispositions bri dge the gap between possessing the abili ty (skill) to do something and actually exercising that ability in novel situations to and within which such abilities are suited (Perk ins et al., 2000). Unlike skills, which require us to make the conscious decision to use them, dispositions are automatic and operate beneath the surface of consciousness. They are like traffic signals in this way; we allocate little of our attentional economy (Gee, 2017) to them, but they help us navigate the myriad of complex situations we enc ounter in our lives quickly and efficiently. In fac t, research in the cognitive sciences indicates a significant amount of our decision making is undertaken at the subconscious level (Soon et al., 2008), or what Kahneman (2011) called System 1, or the fast-thinking system, of our brains. Dispositions are not merely habits. From a cognitiv e perspective, enacting a disposition requires one to “possess” (a) the ability to take certain actions o r exhibit certain behaviors, (b) the sensitivity to  identify novel situations in which those actions  or behaviors are warranted and useful, and (c) the inclination to take certain actions or exhibit certain behaviors when they are warranted and useful (Perkins et al., 2000). Yet dispositions are also not simply cognitive tools to be acquired. Rat her, though dispositions certainly require practice  to develop (Gresalfi, 2009; Thomas & Brown, 2007), they are more akin to intuition, an immediate way of seeing the world around us that remains markedly different from more  conscious, calculated reasoning (Seligman et al., 2016). Like intuition, dispositions are formed, tested, and honed when we metabolize experiences over time and recognize patterns therein (Gee, 2017; Seligman et al., 2016). Dispositions are the actions we reliably take based on these understandings of ourselves and the world and people around us. We can use the expansive reco rd of human experience, or history, as a bank of vicarious experience, then, to add an immense amount of what Shaffer

(2017) called “big experience”—whether first-or secondhand— to metabolize into the intuitive perceptions and

responses—dispositions—that guide our actions.

The historical disposition: History as useable and useful in the world Those possessing the historical disposition recogni ze that the past offers usable knowledge with which  to make present decisions in pursuit of a desirable shared future. There is an implicit yet fundamental assump tion that history can and should be “put to work” to help inform the decisions we make in the present. As Shreiner (2014) noted: “Implicit in arguments about history’s impor tance for citizenship is that citizens must not onl y know history, but also must be able to effectively use history to reason about issues within the civic domain” (p. 314). Others advocate more explicitly for putting history to work for contemporary decision making. Neustadt and May

(1986) expressly called for policymakers to make ex plicit use of the past when making decisions, a sen timent

Brands, Suri, and colleagues (2016) echoed 30 years  later when explaining their own goals to “offer in sights on how the study of the past can more usefully serve t he present” (p. 2) and “engage policymakers and his torians alike in thinking through the requirements for crea ting and deploying a more useable past” (p. 3) with an eye to contemporary policy decisions. In the same vein, th e Campaign for the Civic Mission of the Schools (Go uld et al., 2012) cite K-12 education as the training ground for citizens’ a bility to use knowledge of the past  when considering laws, policies, and other government ac ts. Barton and Levstik (2004), too, advocated a use able past, contending citizens should use historical precedent s and analogies to inform their own positions and a ctions. As Brands and Suri (2016) contended, “there is no shor tage of contemporary policy issues that demand grea ter historical awareness” (p. 5). Using history as a le ns to inform the decisions we make in the present, then, allows us to better position ourselves to address the poss ibilities, challenges, and choices of our own time (Zelikow, 2016).

One way scholars advocate putting history to work t o help us make decisions in the present is by leveraging it as a means of making sense of the wor ld around us. History offers a way to understand ou rselves and others, as well as our own and others’ beliefs,  motivations, and perceptions of how the world work s. Barton and Levstik (2004) suggested people already “use information about the past to make sense of the world,” although additionally noting people do this in myriad ways a nd for a variety of purposes. MacMillan (2010) further suggested that “history, by giving context and examples, helps when it comes to thinking about the present world” (p. 167). Additionally, history helps us understand  “those with whom we have to deal,” and equally imp ortantly, “ourselves” (p. 141).

This idea of history as useable to make sense of, and as a guide to take action in, the contemporary world relies on a conception of the historical record as a bank of vicarious experience with which to make d ecisions. MacMillan (2010) noted people learn in two ways: through personal experience, or from others, and that learning history offers an opportunity for the latter. Zelik ow (2016) agreed, and described the study of histor y as the accumulation of vicarious experience. Though not sp eaking from a history education context, Seligman a nd colleagues (2016) offered a useful way to understand this idea. They explained how, when faced with no vel problems, the human brain mines past experience —memories—for strategies that worked in earlier, similar situations. Historical study, as a form of intergen erational communication about what works and what d oes not, in fact operates in both the ways MacMillan (2010) suggested people learn. In one sense, learning history allows us to learn from the successes and failures of othe rs—in fact, many others. In another sense, on the level  of the species, the historical record operates as a kind o f accumulated set of human memories, the kind Selig man et al. suggested await our perusal as we search for strategies that may be helpful as we confront problems in the present. Wood (1999) contended the study of history through the lens of memory was “decidedly in fashion” (p. 1). Scholars writing on historical consciousness (e.g.,  Nordgren, 2016; Rüsen, 2004; Seixas, 2004) also ha ve long been interested in this idea of collective memory. Seixas (2004), for example, considered a discussion of memory studies as a necessary prerequisite for discussions  of historical consciousness, describing it as the scholarly examination of “the structures that enable societie s to hand down beliefs about the past form one gene ration to the next, the purposes for which those beliefs are mobilized, their nature and shape, and the ways that they change over time” (p. 5). Rüsen (2004), too, contended we leverage our shared memories (in the form of narr atives) to determine which actions are acceptable —and which are not —in the present. Using an old narrative from the Scottish Highlands, Rüsen illustrated well the poin t forwarded by Seligman and colleagues (2016): huma ns use their understandings of history to navigate complex contemporary problems, problems that present a multitude of possible decision-making options, which in turn carry an unknown numb er of possible consequences. By leveraging the historical record as vicarious experience, scholars suggest history provides us a way to reason about and resolve personal and public problems. Banks (2006) contended that “refle ctive decision-makers and citizen actors must use the knowledge from all the various social science disciplines to solve personal and pu blic problems” (pp. 85-86).

MacMillan (2010) advised us that “history can help us to be wise,” and “it can also suggest what the likely outcome of our actions might be” (p. 153). v an Boxtel and vandrie (2018) agreed, contending hi storical study “supports the analysis of current problems or changes in society and reflection on intended and unintended consequences of human action” (p. 149). Historical study is uniquely suited to equip citizens with the  causal reasoning necessary to take purposeful, reflective action in the contemporary world (Banks, 2006). Indeed, Barton

(2017) noted that the study of causation is “at the heart” of history education (p. 453). The cause-effect reasoning

with which historical study equips citizens is operationalized in three important ways. Leveraging the past to think about the present offers guidance during the decisi on-making process. As MacMillan (2010) contended, “by examining the past, we can get some useful lessons about how to proceed and some warning about what is  or is not likely to happen” (p. 154). MacMillan further pointed out that knowing others’ histories helps us “understand their values, their fears, and their hopes.” Knowing our own and others’ history thus allows us to better anticipate “how they are likely to react to something [we] do”  (p. 144). Just as important, knowing our own histo ry allows us to know ourselves more deeply and situate our decisions within a framework of our own values. Second, putting the past to work for us as we make decisions in the present can assist us in identifying the warnings history offers concerning where our decisions have gone wrong in t he past. MacMillan contended that “history reminds us usefully about the sorts of situations that have ca used trouble in the past,” and that studying simila r situations in the past offers “one way of narrowing the odds” against repeating those mistakes. Finally, studying the historical record can illustrate alternatives to decisions we have made in the past that either failed outright to meet our goals, rendered unforeseen or undesirable consequences, or failed to meet our expectations (MacMillan, 2010). Action-oriented The historical disposition is fundamentally action-oriented; it operates on the assumption that, whatever historical knowledge we metabolize into useable pasts, we do s o in order to use that knowledge to take action in and participate in the world. Or as Glenberg (1997) might put it: “[historical] memory is for action.” As Neustadt and May (1986) contend, it is about how we use this kno wledge and these understandings when deciding what to do in the present as we work towards our envisioned future. The study of history should operate on this higher plane. As Banks (2006) says: “The social studies should he lp students attain the skills needed to recognize a nd solve human problems, analyse and clarify values, and mak e sound, reflective decisions that will contr ibute to the perpetuation and improvement of their communities, nation, and world.” (p. 81). In so doing, good hist orical knowledge can illuminate pathways for creative action (Feaver & Inboden, 2016) by illuminating how others have acted in similar circumstances when facing similar challenges and constraints. Future-facing and aspirational Neustadt and May (1986) suggested that “the use of the past can stimulate the imagination” and help us envision alternative futures (p. xv). As such, the historica l disposition is action-oriented, but it is also aspirational in how it views the actionable choices we have in the world. By this I mean we tak e action in the world, with an eye to the future we seek to craft through these actions, in order to improve it and our lives therein. This is, perhaps, a truism; who, after all, strives to take action in the world in order to secure an outcome for themselves worse than the status quo? Yet Barton & Levstik (2004) contend that the improved future to which we aspire by taking action ought to be shaped by the outcomes we seek not only for ourselves but for others. Indeed, by participating in civil society, we develop the dreams and aspirations we h arbor for ourselves and those around us —our families, communities, nations, and the global world in which  we all live. This aspirational stance shapes “the very goals to which democratic governments are meant to respond” (pp. 31-32).

## Context matters

Nevertheless, scholars acknowledge the limitations of using historical analogies as direct comparisons  to contemporary issues. In other words, context matter s. Brands & Suri (2016) cautioned that “the differe nces between two time periods are almost always more important than the similarities,” and that “lessons ripped from one context and dropped into another can easily mis lead more than they can inform” (p. 4). MacMillan (2016) also urged against reckless analogical reasoning, s tating that “analogies from history must, of course, be treated with care. Using the wrong one not only can present an oversimplified picture of a complex situation in the present but can lead to wrong decisions” (p. 157). Zelikow (2016), too, warned against using history as a stor ehouse for facile generalizations applied across time and space. Rather, the “historian’s microscope” is only a powerful tool for appreciating the complexity of the past. It can  provide us with “an understanding of historical an tecedents to contemporary issues and the global and interregiona l relationships that have helped shape contemporary  global problems (Shreiner, 2017, p. 359), but not for disc overing a step-by-step roadmap for the present or future. Only once we appreciate this complexity, Neustadt and May (1986) argued, can we begin to understand what the past really has to say about the validity of the inferences we make when engaging in analogical reasoning. Skeptical Any historical claims and deployments of the past m ust be scrutinized and corroborated. As MacMillan (2010) noted, “it is useful, too, to be reminded, as a citizen, that those in positions of authority do not always know better” (p. 168). Thornton and Barton (2010) suggested “stu dents should engage with multiple perspectives, bot h the variety of viewpoints that existed within a given h istorical period and the range of ways in which his tory is used and interpreted today” (p. 2491). Students and citi zens must  move beyond what deliberate blinding, or the acceptance of one historical master narrative (Seixas, 2004). In so doing, students and citizens must move beyond even important staples of progressive history instr uction, such as examining evidence, considering con flicting accounts, and identifying cause and effect, towards establishing a useable history for knowing what meanings the past holds in the present (Seixas, 2004). Historica l study develops the ability to consider the purpos es served by particular historical interpretations (Wineburg et al., 2007), t o develop the ability, sensitivity, and inclination  to drill down into others’ deployments of the past to identify the motives behind them and ask critical q uestions about them.

Inquisitive frame History can help frame questions about the present and future: why the present looks the way it does, why people act the way they do and value what they value, and what the future might look like and how it might look different. MacMillan (2010) noted that  “without good questions it is difficult to begin to  think in a coherent way at all. Knowledge of history suggests what sort of informat ion might be needed to answer those questions” (p. 167). Neustadt and May (1986) recommended identifying sim ilarities and differences within historical analogi es and interrogating what we know about them to bring into  relief the information still needed. Similarly, Pe ck (2016) suggests contemporary activists, scholars, and government actors might use the past to form questions that guide their actions, suggesting policymakers should “use the past to test [their] assumptions about the present, to inform a series of empirically verifiable inquiries about how our current ‘humanitarian’ policies do in fact work” (p. 228). A social doing Using the past to inform decision making in the pre sent and engaging in future-shaping are social endeavors, as the kind of decisions for which historical study is useful are generally of larger scale and require group deliberation and action. Dewey (1916)  argued that “a Democracy is more than a form of gov ernment; it is primarily a mode of associated living, of conjoint communicated expe rience” (p. 87). van Boxtel and vandrie (2018) des cribed historical reasoning as “an integrative and sociall y situated activity” valuable for helping students and citizens “orientate in the present and to participate in society as citizens” (p. 149). Grounded in evidence Staying grounded in evidence when framing questions, testing assumptions, forming opinions, and delibe rating with others around personal and collective action i s of paramount importance. History teaches us to co ntinually examine our own and others’ assumpt ions and consistently ask, “Where’s the evidence?” (MacMillan, 2010, p. 170). Barton (2017) contended that developing stude nts’ ability to use empirical evidence to make and support claims is “one of the most important goals of histo ry and social sciences education” (p. 458). MacMillan (2010) argued that the kind of evidentiary thinking promin ent in historical study is an invaluable tool for t esting the assumptions that guide our actions: “History demand s that we treat evidence seriously, especially when  that evidence contradicts assumptions we  have already made” (p. 167). van Boxtel and vandri e (2018) argued that the purpose of using history to reason about the il l-defined problems requiring our decision-making attention is to reach justifiable conclusions, justifiable insof ar as the conclusions to which we come and the claims we make are supported with convincing evidence. Guldi and A rmitage (2014) argued using historical evidence all ows us to engage “false myths about the future” and talk about “where [and when] data come from” (p. 12). Thi s allows us to deliberate with others about the ill-defined problems facing our societies, the most pressing and meaningful of which lack established solution procedures and r esist quick or easy consensus (van Boxtel & vandri e, 2018). Though argumentation plays an important role in con sensus building around ill-defined problems (Weinstock et al., 2006), it is in supporting our claims with evidence that argumentation leads draws us closer to consensus (van Boxtel & vandrie, 2018), allowing us to leverage “ history’s power to sort truth from falsehood when w e speak about our past and present situation[s]” (Guldi & Armitage, 2014, p. 13). Criticality The historical disposition is incomplete without de ep concern for and focus on questions of justice: w ho is included in, and who is excluded from, our shared p resent and proposed visions for the future. History, from the questions we ask of the past to the  versions and interpretations of the past to which t hose questions lead, to who is able to take action in the world, necessarily pr ivileges some voices over others within self-perpetuating, systemic, institutional levers of power and privile ge. Furthermore, this exclusion of voices flies dir ectly counter to the project of pluralist democracy and hinders the aspirational and social nature of a useable past. Like any curricular venture, the history curriculum  plays a role in how we conceptualize, reinforce, a nd reproduce the views of our past, present, and future to which we hold, particularly concerning questions of equity and inclusion (Brown & Brown, 2015). An indispensab le feature of the historical disposition that addre sses this imperative of inclusion is critical examination not  just of history’s virtues and successes but —and in fact more importantly—also its failings (Epstein, 2009). “Such an approac h,” argued Segall et al. (2018), “opens up opportunities to force attention to not only what h istory says about the past but also to what that sa ying ‘does’ to encourage particular understandings about the  (and their own) present and future” (p. 286). den H eyer (2011) proposed, too, a study of history with justice at i ts core, in which students and teachers consider th eir own roles in (re)producing levers of inclusion and exclusion. History teaches us that the answers to questions about the past, present, and future in large part “depend on what w e in the present ask and what we think is important ” (MacMillan, 2010, p. 167). That is, the very questi ons we see as “askable” depend on who is “allowed” to ask questions and what the askers view as important withi n their own frame of experience. As Segall et al. (2018) advocated, “questions ought to be asked about whose  discourse, whose standards, and whose past are mad e legitimate and/or illegitimate” (p. 287). Yet even with egalitarian access to question framing and answer shaping, history does not produce answers for all time (MacM illan, 2010). Rather, it is a process of persistent ly asking difficult questions of the past, present, and future and taking action to include those who have been excluded. For this process to work, however, students and citizen s “need to understand what makes people able to tak e action, and what stands in the way of action” (Barton, 2017, p. 457). Epstein (2009) argued that such a criti cal stance towards the learning and teaching of history may “better equip young people to acknowledge and understand the roots” of contemporary issues of inclusion and excl usion and to “imagine themselves and act as citizen s capable of change in contemporary society” (p. 137).

## Discussion and implications

In this work I have sought to begin a conversation in history and social studies education about how w e might define the aims of our work, with an emphasis on the role it might play in aiding us as a society in coming to some sort of agreement on the importance and quality of facts, how we know what we know, and how to work wi th each other to plot the course of our shared future.  Lack of such agreement will not obviate the shared  nature of our future—it will only make that future more fraught. Which h istory, though, best serves us in pursuing this epistemological coherence? And how much? And what k inds of decisions should we make with it? The question of how much history is useful for this endeavor is an interesting one. I agree with Guldi and Armitage (2014) that a focus on the longue durée serves us best. While context and details change over time, the longue durée of history reveals patterns and has lessons to teach. For example, the Tang Dynasty and Roman and Persian Empires all rose to global economic, military, and cultural dominance through policies of tolerance and inclusion—and began their fall when that tolerance and inclusion was undermin ed. Time and again, civilizations prosper wh en they engage openly with others and fail when they isolate themselves from the world. These are the kinds of historical lessons we would do well to heed today.

The decisions we might make with history come in many shapes and sizes—I have a larger, societal scale in mind. Policymakers and high-level decisionmakers can benefit from greater historical understanding, but what I propose goes further by democratizing this knowle dge on a societal scale. Certainly, politicians typ ically make the singular large-scale decisions that alter the trajectories of soci eties, but in democratic societies it is the people—the voting, letter-writing, protesting public—that decides who is positioned to make such decisions. With the historical disposition in the hands of pluralistic societies, it is my hope that those in positions of power will be better, more purposefully, and more thoughtfully se lected, thereby improving the decisions we make on a global societal level and making our shared futures more just, equitable, and inclusive. The implications of reconceptualizing history education as the development of the historical disposition are many. History education may benefit, particularly within the current political climate, from reviving historical study as a project of democratic preparation. Focus ing on the development of the historical dispositio n—rather than content knowledge or skill mastery —offers an opportunity to democratize the use of his tory for decision making. We want our leaders to know their history a nd use it responsibly, but if this disposition is n ot widely shared, it will be only the few who know it. Historical study also offers the opportunity to continue the important conversations we have within liberal democracies —over immigration, healthcare, the wealth gap, cl imate action—rather than begin them anew with each generation. I t offers us the promise of conversing over the type and quality of evidence we use in deliberating over these issues rather than shouting ideological platitudes across a political and cultural chasm.

But can the historical disposition be taught? If so, how? These are questions for future research, tho ugh I suspect the answers are incongruent with the stat us quo of history and social studies education, thr ough which so many young people are subjected t o dehumanizing pedagogies privileging lecture, fact  recall, and multiple-choice tests, rather than how the knowledge, skills, and concepts of history can be leveraged as tools in pursuit of the common good. As we move into the post-truth era, an era that has witnessed the rise of “f ake news” and “alternative facts” and the decline of shared values and conceptions of evidence, the conceptualization of domainspecific dispositions and developing the means of developing them is an imminently important pursuit.
