# A CSCL Taxonomy Building Workshop

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract

This workshop aims at collaboratively developing a shared taxonomy for the Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) community that captures diverse epistemological, methodological and technological perspectives to help build a common ground for CSCL research. Through pre-workshop Delphi input, hands-on application, and iterative refinement, participants co-construct a framework to support clearer communication, cumulative knowledge building, and more transparent dissemination in CSCL proceedings and journals. The resulting taxonomy and follow-up activities aim to strengthen community identity, foster collaboration, and guide future CSCL research and publication practices. We particularly welcome researchers from all domains to contribute their vision of computer-supported collaborative learning, with a minimum of 8 to a maximum of 40 participants. Regarding resources, we only need a large seminar room with WiFi and a projector that enables group work through flexible tables (i.e., no lecture hall with fixed rows). In-person participants will be asked to bring their personal laptops. Description of the event Theme and goals In recent years, the field of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) has continued to expand in scope, methods, and theoretical foundations. While this diversity reflects the richness and vitality of the field, it also creates challenges for integration and cumulative knowledge building. Researchers draw on a wide range of epistemological, methodological, and technological perspectives, and often work in parallel rather than in concert. This fragmentation can make it difficult to identify shared concerns, trace the development of ideas, or recognize complementarities among approaches. At the same time, the multiplicity of perspectives represents an invaluable resource. If systematically understood and connected, this multiplicity can foster deeper insights into learning processes and the design of collaborative technologies. Previous CSCL workshops have identified the need for a common language across the different domains that are present in the CSCL community. Common language p resents the first step to common ground, a concept that is key to the knowledge building in a scientific community, particularly in one that emphasizes the strength of co-existing paradigms, requiring awareness of semiotic and semantic differences (that may otherwise lead to issues like gatekeeping or siloed research, Kuhn, 1962). Particularly, the 2025 CSCL workshop participants have identified the need for community building in the CSCL field and the potential of a collaboratively created CSCL taxonomy as an artefact for building common ground. Consequently, this workshop has two goals: (1) community building and (2) taxonomy development. To build community, the workshop brings together members of the CSCL community to overcome fragmentation and to harness the richness of the field’s diversity. It will help strengthen a community identity by creating an awareness of what constitutes CSCL and foster a feeling of agency through a common activity centered around the community itself (Wise & Schwarz, 2017). The second goal is to develop a taxonomy for classifying future CSCL research, especially contributions to future conference proceedings and ijCSCL articles. This taxonomy aims to offer a shared framework that reflects the community’s pluralistic character while enabling more coherent navigation, communication, comparison, and synthesis of research. As a tangible outcome and lasting artefact, it aims to promote collaboration across paradigmatic differences in the future. In addition, the process of developing such a taxonomy itself constitutes an effort of community building, thereby also serving the first goal of the workshop. In summary, by collaboratively developing and validating such a taxonomy, supported by a broad proportion of the CSCL community, the workshop seeks to lay the foundation for a more connected and integrated future for CSCL research. Theoretical background and relevance to the field and conference CSCL is inherently multidisciplinary, integrating perspectives from at least the learning sciences, educational sciences, psychology, and computer science. This diversity enriches the field but also introduces substantial variation in researchers’ underlying epistemic stances. According to the AIR model (Chinn et al., 2014), such stances encompass differences in 3 ways. First, researchers have different aims—they seek to achieve different things with their work. Second, they hold different ideals—they disagree on what counts as valid knowledge and good evidence and the criteria by which success is evaluated. Third, they use different reliable processes—they favor different methods for how data is created and interpreted, that is, the methods considered most appropriate to reach their aims. While this pluralism fosters innovation and theoretical breadth, it also poses challenges for mutual understanding in our field. Researchers examining similar phenomena may struggle to find, understand, and interpret each other’s research contributions because they pursue different aims, evaluate knowledge by different criteria, and employ different methods. This is reflected, for example, in the co-existence of different major theoretical traditions (e.g., constructivist and sociocultural, cognitive/information‑processing), a focus on different mid-level theories or theory-driven pedagogical frameworks (e.g., knowledge building, cognitive load theory, theory of immersive collaborative learning) or interactional constructs (e.g., transactivity, socio‑cognitive conflict) and competence frameworks (e.g., collaborative problem‑solving skills), and different analytical and design methodologies (e.g., collaborative analytics or design-based research) (Brown, 1992; Dillenbourg, 1999; Graesser et al., 2018; Kirschner et al., 2018; Makransky & Petersen, 2023; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014; Vogel et al., 2023; Vygotsky, 1978; Wise et al., 2023). As a result, communication across perspectives can become fragmented, leading to disciplinary silos and limited knowledge integration within the CSCL community (Wise & Schwarz, 2017), thereby hindering the advancement of knowledge. To remedy fragmentation, a workshop at the CSCL 2025 conference aimed at developing a shared paradigm (see also section “Relationship to similar events conducted in the past” below). However, one main outcome, also advocated by a panel of senior CSCL scholars, was that attempting to unify the CSCL community under a single theoretical or methodological framework would weaken the field’s richness in diversity. Its multi-perspectivity is not a weakness to be corrected, but a strength that enables deeper and more comprehensive inquiry. As a result, the goal should be to bridge epistemic diversity to enable constructive dialogue rather than to simply erase different perspectives and epistemologies. A step toward bridging these differences is to establish a shared vocabulary that enables researchers to articulate their approaches in a comparable manner (Wise & Schwarz, 2017). When organized systematically, such a vocabulary can take the form of a taxonomy—a structured set of dimensions and descriptors that capture from which viewpoint researchers approach their investigation, which phenomenon they study, and how they study it. However, one main outcome, also advocated by a panel of senior CSCL scholars, was that attempting to unify the CSCL community under a single theoretical or methodological framework would weaken the field’s richness in diversity. Its multiperspectivity is not a weakness to be corrected, but a strength that enables deeper and more comprehensive inquiry. As a result, the goal should be to bridge epistemic diversity to enable constructive dialogue rather than to simply erase different perspectives and epistemologies. A step toward bridging these differences is to establish a shared vocabulary that enables researchers to articulate their approaches in a comparable manner (Wise & Schwarz, 2017). When organized systematically, such a vocabulary can take the form of a taxonomy—a structured set of dimensions and descriptors that capture from which viewpoint researchers approach their investigation, which phenomenon they study, and how they study it. A CSCL taxonomy could thus provide a common reference point for describing research aims, methodologies, collaboration phenomena (e.g., participation, agency, different types of awareness, social and cognitive processes etc.), and the tools used to support them. In doing so, it would strengthen our ability to engage in cumulative dialogue while maintaining our diverse intellectual foundations. After the development of such a taxonomy, one potential use case would be to implement the taxonomy into the submission process and make it a part of each contribution to the CSCL conference proceedings and to the ijCSCL. This would serve a threefold function: First, it would encourage authors to explicitly clarify their epistemic position and research orientation by selecting the appropriate descriptors. Sharpening authors’ clarity about their epistemic aims and ideals helps raise awareness for what is considered rigorous research in the CSCL community, clarifying expectations for authors and easing review processes for journals, including ijCSCL. Second, it would guide readers in their expectations and understanding of CSCL papers to avoid misconceptions and mismatched expectations. Currently, readers may not immediately notice epistemological differences between them and the authors of a research contribution. If the contribution was clearly labeled based on a common taxonomy instead, this misconception could be avoided and readers would be informed of the epistemological prerequisites for reading a specific contribution. Third, the taxonomy would make it more likely that CSCL researchers find and read all the research contributions that are relevant to them. At the moment, researchers risk missing out on research contributions that may be informative to them as they are addressing the same phenomenon, just because these contributions do not use the same vocabulary they are familiar with. A shared taxonomy makes it possible to search for and filter research that addresses the same phenomenon but from different epistemic stances. Therefore, such a taxonomy increases the agency of readers to seek out different epistemic stances than their own (or the one they are looking for). Other potential use cases include newcomers’ onboarding and providing the basis for review articles and field development. Outline of planned activities Pre-workshop: Drafting a taxonomy Before the workshop (see also Figure 1), we will collect the community members’ comments and opinions on a draft of a potential taxonomy for CSCL. To this end, we will use an adapted version of the Delphi approach (Dalkey & Halmer, 1963). First, the organizing committee will prepare a first version of the taxonomy. Second, individual expert researchers whom we will select to represent the variety of perspectives within CSCL will be asked to enrich and refine the draft. Third, the revised version will be shared with the enrolled workshop participants online. The participants will be asked to give feedback on the taxonomy anonymously, and to state whether they would suggest any additions or deletions for the categories by justifying their answers. Using their input, workshop organizers will prepare the third version of the taxonomy prior to the workshop. Figure 1 An Overview of the Pre-, While-, and Post-Workshop Activities. While-workshop: Refining the taxonomy After a short introduction (presenting context, aim and structure), a warm-up (sharing research interests and personal meaning of CSCL), and a short presentation of the latest taxonomy version, each participant applies the taxonomy to one’s own paper (or a default paper as fallback). This activity familiarizes participants with the latest taxonomy draft and gives them firsthand experience using it for later evaluation. Then, the participants (virtual and onsite) are put into random groups of three or four, and discuss their application experience, the aspects of CSCL they have found hard to categorize, the guiding principles or criteria they have used to evaluate the categories, and the parts of the taxonomy they think should change. The discussion is documented by adding comments on the related parts of the taxonomy shared on a Miro board. In each group, a member is asked to take the role of a moderator to ensure equal contribution. Then, groups share their discussions by highlighting the most problematic areas of the taxonomy or pressing concerns via pitch presentations. During the presentations, organizers note the to-dos for each part of the taxonomy. Next; participants regroup by selecting which specific part of the taxonomy they would like to work on. These groups then work on the corresponding to-dos. Similar to the previous round, a member moderates the p resents ideas for improvement of the part of the taxonomy they worked on. Each pitch presentation is followed by a Q&A so that other participants can make suggestions as well. Depending on the remaining time, participants are asked to share on the Miro board how they imagine the taxonomy to be used in CSCL research or design contexts, and how they envision the “success” of the taxonomy in terms of both academic and practical impact. The workshop finishes with participants sharing their contact information and stating whether they would like to be interested in post-workshop activities. Post-workshop: Follow-up activities If there are any unresolved areas regarding the taxonomy, we will invite the participants and the broader CSCL community to comment on the latest version of the taxonomy to finalize it and to collaborate on its implementation. Then, we will a) write a brief report, b) conduct a systematic review applying the taxonomy on a range of research works, and c) team up with future CSCL program committees and ijCSCL editors to integrate the taxonomy into the submission systems. Expected outcomes and contributions The primary workshop goal is to develop a CSCL taxonomy codebook, which will be published as a brief report for ijCSCL. Also, during the workshop, participants will critically examine, refine, and align their conceptual understandings of key dimensions of CSCL research and practice. In the long term, this shared, systematic framework can serve as a foundation for future empirical and theoretical work by informing the paper writing processes in the CSCL community and increasing the quality of the papers produced. We also envision that the taxonomy will be implemented in the submission processes of the ijCSCL and the CSCL conference. Furthermore, a systemic review for CSCL research using the taxonomy will be conducted, presenting a significant contribution to the field to classify and evaluate previous CSCL research. Moreover, collaboration among various CSCL researchers is expected to be strengthened following while-and post-workshop activities. The process of co-constructing the taxonomy might foster new connections and potential research partnerships, leading to a continued dialogue and joint publications around the use, validation, and further development of the taxonomy. The workshop thus aims not only to produce a tangible scholarly output but also to build a sustained, collaborative research network that advances the field of CSCL. To ensure continuity, this proposal has been shared with the previous organizers and support from the ijCSCL editors has been assured.
