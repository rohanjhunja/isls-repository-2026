Learning analytics dashboards have become essential tools for understanding student engagement, with recent research documenting a shift from analytics-driven to learning-focused designs (Paulsen & Lindsay, 2024). However, a critical systematic review found no evidence that these dashboards improve academic achievement, with most studies reporting negligible or small effects (Kaliisa et al., 2024). This limitation is particularly significant in asynchronous learning contexts, where collective behavioral patterns at the group or cohort level remain largely invisible to educators.

Traditional learning analytics approaches struggle to capture the temporal evolution of behavioral structure within learner populations. Standard clustering methods provide static snapshots but cannot track how clusters persist, merge, or fragment across weeks. More importantly, even when structural patterns are detected, translating these mathematical abstractions into actionable educational insights remains challenging. Educators need tools that not only reveal structural dynamics but also support interpretation without requiring expertise in computational methods.

Topological Data Analysis (TDA), and specifically Zigzag Persistent Homology, offers a mathematical framework capable of tracking structural changes in non-monotonic temporal sequences (Carlsson & de Silva, 2010)—precisely the kind of data generated in flexible learning environments where learners enter and exit at different times. While TDA has been introduced to the learning analytics community as a promising approach (Munch, 2017), its practical application in educational dashboards remains emerging. More critically, there exists no established framework for interpreting topological indicators (such as, the number of connected components) in educational contexts.

The dashboard’s interpretive framework is designed precisely to surface such distinctions and support educators in responding appropriately. This interactive demo presents TopoLA Dashboard, a tool designed to bridge the gap between methodological innovation in TDA and practical educational decision-making. Rather than presenting topological metrics as definitive answers, the dashboard provides layered interpretation support: a three-layer interpretation a pproach that combines (1) objective metrics, (2) pattern descriptions grounded in learning analytics research, and (3) reflective prompts that invite educators to apply their contextual knowledge. This design philosophy acknowledges that TDA in education is an emerging field where interpretation frameworks are still developing. The remainder of this paper describes the dashboard's architecture and key features, and outlines our interactive demo plan.

Experimenting with Complex Phenomena in a Collaborative Mixed Reality Agent-Based Participatory Simulation Sharleen Loh, sharleen@mit.edu, Massachusetts Institute of Technology Daniel Wendel, djwendel@mit.edu, Massachusetts Institute of Technology Mukhlisa Nematova, mn109@wellesley.edu, Wellesley College Aditi Wagh, awagh@mit.edu, Massachusetts Institute of Technology Abstract: WIT is a collaborative platform that leverages agent-based participatory simulations and headset-based mixed reality to immerse learners in exploring complex phenomena. This session will demonstrate several key features of WIT through an example weather simulation, including its modeling of complex phenomena, modes of interaction, and data visualization tools. Preliminary findings that illustrate the platform’s promise in encouraging learner inquiry of abstract phenomena, such as air pressure within complex systems, will also be discussed. Real-world phenomena often consist of complex systems. Understanding the processes and dynamics of these systems has been key to solving many real-world problems in the 21st century (Thurner et al., 2018) and benefits society-wide perspectives of our world (Sterman, 1994). However, because these complex phenomena occur at scales that are not immediately visible or perceptible, they can be challenging to learn about (Wilensky & Resnick, 1999). Agent-based participatory simulations (p-sims) can support understanding complex phenomena as learners experience first-hand how local interactions create often unpredictable results across the system (e.g. Colella, 2013; Rates et al., 2016; Wilensky & Stroup, 1999). Mixed reality (MR) that overlays virtual objects on top of the real world has several affordances to support exploration of these phenomena. By representing otherwise invisible elements underlying complex phenomena as tangible virtual objects, MR supports direct exploration of these objects and their interactions (e.g., Roy & Radu, 2021). Moreover, MR can also support multiple learners to collaborate face-to-face in a shared physical space (Billinghurst & Kato, 1999). The WIT platform This paper reports on the design and development of a platform called We’re in This Together (WIT). WIT leverages MR to create co-located, collaborative participatory simulations (p-sims) in which learners can explore complex phenomena in science together in a shared physical space (Roy et al., 2024). WIT combines the affordances of p-sims and agent-based models by immersing learners in an interactive 3D simulation of a complex phenomenon, enabling them to add or manipulate agents and the represented environment. The platform can synchronize up to 10 people using Meta Quest headsets, anchoring a virtual p-sim in a shared physical space such as a classroom. Our long-term goal is for the platform to serve as the foundation both for the development of new mixed reality p-sims and further research of the genre. To investigate this genre of learning experiences in WIT, we are iteratively developing a functional prototype p-sim for learners to explore, interact with, and make sense of weather dynamics. We co-designed this p-sim with two middle school teachers, who suggested weather as a topic for its curricular relevance to middle school students and conceptual dependence on three-dimensional processes that are not directly visible and explorable. In the weather p-sim, air parcels float across the room, conditionally creating clouds that rain onto varied terrain biomes that are spread across the tabletops. Learners can interact with the simulation by heating or cooling the air parcels, creating pressure systems, or raising and lowering the terrain to create mountains and valleys. Learners can work in groups to collaboratively investigate how these modes of interaction affect weather and explore how collective actions can create long-term weather patterns such as increased rain frequency. WIT design Modeling complex phenomena In WIT, our goal is to model the complexity of natural phenomena using agent-based modeling in a way that is physically immersive, explorable, and understandable for learners. Our initial prototypes used simple event-based models (EBM) with programmed discrete responses to high-level events (Baldwin et al., 2015). Although EBM supported rapid iteration of the overall experience, it lacked the depth for learners to inquire beyond high-level outcomes. Agent-based models (ABM) simulate the behaviors and interactions of individual agents that give rise t o global, emergent outcomes. In agent-based p-sims, learners control and/or take on the role of agents within a simulation to examine how their actions impact global outcomes in a system. We re-designed the weather p-sim’s air and terrain systems from prescriptive EBM to emergent ABM, enabling learners to explore agent dynamics and discover the underlying rules of complex phenomena (Jacobson & Wilensky, 2006). While we developed the weather p-sim as a prototype, our platform modeling features are designed to be flexible and applicable to future WIT p-sims across different phenomena.

Modeling fluids using smoothed-particle hydrodynamics In the initial weather p-sim prototype, air was modeled as large, oblong “air masses” that floated across the room. Although the simplified model helped focus learners’ attention on a few key tangible objects, it lacked the causal processes and emergent dynamics of weather that we aimed for learners to explore. For example, rain was programmed to form whenever air masses intersected, rather than emerging from underlying processes such as how cooling air decreases its capacity to hold moisture, limiting the depth of inquiry available to learners. Additionally, our consultations with meteorologists and climate scientists emphasized understanding 3dimensional atmospheric dynamics. Although pressure systems are commonly understood as regions that push or pull air near the surface, they are more accurately modeled as vertical columns that circulate air between the upper and lower atmospheres.

To model the atmosphere’s complexity, we redesigned the air masses to be represented as multiple small air parcels that dynamically influence neighboring parcels based on their temperature, moisture, and pressure (see Figure 1a). This is achieved using a modified version of smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH; Monaghan, 2005; Ni, 2025). This SPH representation of air enables learners to experiment with local interactions and investigate how their micro-level actions give rise to macro-level weather patterns. Although implementing SPH achieved our goal of increasing the weather p-sim’s capacity for representing key processes underlying weather in the atmosphere, it also increased the visual clutter of virtual objects in the p-sim. Our initial goal was to simulate a full 3D atmosphere, scattering air parcels throughout the entire room. However, this meant that many parcels floated along learners’ eye level, obscuring the view of the rest of the simulation. The increased clutter interfered with the effects of learners’ actions on the system: learners often could not tell whether air parcels were rising and falling by default or because of the influence of a pressure system object. To address this issue, we simplified the simulation to primarily represent the upper and lower atmospheres. Most air parcels move around on one of two layers: the lower atmosphere near the terrain or the upper atmosphere near the ceiling. Air parcels may fall or rise between the two layers at specific areas of high-or low-pressure respectively. This iteration maintains the complexity and spatiality of air while drawing learners’ attention to key causal relationships, like how pressure systems influence the vertical movement of air. Modeling environmental dynamics using a tile-based system In the weather p-sim, terrain biomes like deserts and rainforests are projected onto tables throughout the physical room. We have developed a dynamic system that splits each table into a grid of terrain tiles (see Figure 1b). Each tile tracks its own temperature and moisture. A terrain tile’s temperature value depends on its latitude and altitude. A tile's latitude is determined by its position in the room; one side of the room represents the cold North Pole (90° latitude), while the other side represents the hot equator (0° latitude). A tile’s temperature decreases as it is raised t o create mountains. When a tile is rained on, its moisture value increases. Each tile’s biome type changes depending on its moisture and temperature levels. For example, learners can transform a desert into grasslands by increasing the tile’s moisture with persistent rain over time. This enables learners to experiment with how simulation agents and the agents’ environment interact with each other. Modes of interaction in WIT p-sims To enable learners to explore and examine the dynamics of the phenomenon, we have designed different modes of interaction within the platform. The weather p-sim has three modes of interaction: ● Object mode to create tangible virtual objects: Learners can use their hands to place or move tangible virtual objects that behave as agents. In the weather p-sim, learners can place high-or low-pressure system objects (see Figure 1b) to create columns that make air parcels rise or fall between the upper and lower atmosphere layers. Our intention with this mode is to enable learners to add, visualize, and manipulate otherwise invisible elements as tangible objects and focus their investigations on these objects to create persistent changes in the system.

● Embodied mode to directly control an agent: Learners can use part of their body, like their hand, to change agents’ properties. Our intention with this mode is to encourage learners to use movement for rapid testing. For instance, in the weather p-sim, learners can change the temperature of their hand to heat or cool nearby air parcels (see Figure 1c).

● Environment mode to modify the simulated setting: Finally, learners can make changes to the simulated environment to examine how these changes affect agents. For instance, in the weather p-sim, learners can grab terrain tiles and raise or lower them to create mountains and valleys. Learners can then observe how air parcels cool as they rise over mountains, often forming rain clouds along the mountainsides, or warm and remain dry as they descend valleys and canyons. Data visualization in mixed reality To support learners to connect their explorations in the p-sim to the represented phenomena, we have developed specific ways to visualize and engage with the generated data. ● Embedded visualizations: We have built data visualizations directly into the tangible objects in the p-sim so learners can track their changing properties. For example, air parcels change color according to their temperature and can be toggled to display how much moisture they have. Similarly, terrain tiles change color according to their biome type (e.g. tandeserts or dark green rainforests) and grow more plants as their moisture levels increase. These embedded visualizations are intended to enable learners to interact with the simulation and reason about how their actions affect the visualized properties simultaneously (e.g., Willett et al., 2016). By embedding data visualizations throughout the entire physical space, we also intend for learners to identify spatial patterns across the global system, such as how all air parcels in the upper atmosphere are colder than air parcels in the lower atmosphere. ● Data sensors: Learners can use data sensors to collect data from individual agents or environment tiles. While embedded visualizations are intended for learners to broadly estimate patterns in values of a property across multiple agents, data sensors are intended for learners to collect data from one agent at a time. Using a data sensor, learners can view numeric values and graphs for multiple properties on a panel. Learners can also trace the movement of an agent across space and over time. When a learner selects an air parcel using the data sensor, the parcel’s movement is visualized in the simulation space via a trail tracing its recent path. This feature provides spatial information for a single agent over time so learners can analyze its movement behaviors.

By leveraging embedded visualizations and the data sensor tool, learners can investigate the modeled phenomenon in both breadth and depth.

Observations from pilot tests Over the last year, we pilot tested WIT to investigate how learners interact with the simulation. As WIT represents a new genre of mixed reality p-sims, our goal is to present early analysis of how students engage with the medium. Two pilot tests were conducted with a total of four groups of 5-6 8th grade students (n=22) at a local middle school. In the pilot tests, we first gave students a brief orientation to how air parcels and terrain were represented in the model, then students spent between 10-30 minutes exploring the weather p-sim using Meta Quest 3 headsets. Three researchers and up to three of the students’ teachers circulated the room to observe and ask students p rompting questions about their sensemaking process. At the end, the students participated in a 5-minute focus group exit interview to share their takeaways about the phenomenon and feedback on the experience. We are currently analyzing video data from four student groups. This has involved generating content logs (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) of the videos to document how students used the different interaction modes and engaged with available data visualizations to make sense of weather dynamics. These content logs were used to generate initial observations and hypotheses about how students used these available features to explore weather dynamics, like how temperature and pressure influence rain patterns in this p-sim. Here, we share preliminary Our initial analysis is revealing what we see as a key difference between how students engage in these two modes. As previously stated, in the weather p-sim, the students’ goal was to influence precipitation in a geographical system. In general, students spent more time experimenting with pressure systems in the object mode. However, students more often verbalized correct observations about the causal relationship between temperature changes and precipitation while using the embodied mode. In the embodied mode, students tended to quickly discover the relationship between temperature changes and precipitation. For example, one student, Alex, articulated a misunderstanding that warmer temperatures will result in greater precipitation when he stated, “We need to make it warmer so that [the air will] expel more rain.” His friend, Jamie, corrected him: “No, it’s cold… See Alex, the [moisture] number’s going up.” Jamie demonstrated this by holding their hand over an air parcel, causing the air parcel to turn dark blue and create a rain cloud. After testing this himself, Alex agreed, “Oh, nice… I'm wrong. I forgot how clouds worked. But you're right.” After discovering and confirming this effect with each other, the students used the embodied mode to discover additional first-order relationships, such as how persistent rain changes terrain moisture level and how temperature affects air parcels’ capacity for moisture. In contrast, students engaged with the object mode in more exploratory ways and experimented with combinations of multiple pressure system objects more frequently. For instance, Riley walked over to a table and picked up another student’s pressure system object, then stacked both of their low-pressure system objects together, which amplified the resulting upward airflow pattern. When a teacher asked what they were thinking, Riley observed, “It makes the air kind of cold? Like, I guess… it goes up and then it’s cold,” correctly describing the relationship between low-pressure systems, altitude, and temperature. In both modes, students often identified first-order effects while exploring by themselves or with peers. Notably, students also identified higher-order, emergent effects when scaffolded with prompts from the researchers and teachers. For example, one researcher prompted Jamie to consider how high-and low-pressure systems could create wind. Jamie tested this by putting one high-and one low-pressure system near each other, then stepped back to observe, “They’re going in like a big circle.” Excited, one teacher asked, “What do you call that?” Jamie promptly answered, “Oh, convection currents!” correctly connecting back to prior knowledge they had learned the year before.

This pilot study illustrates how students have used the “embodied” and “object” modes to discover firstorder effects and explore more complex relationships. As we continue to analyze the data, we are exploring the implications of this trend in terms of design and how to support learners’ experimentation and sensemaking in the WIT p-sims. Our initial findings support the platform’s promise as an immersive space for learners to explore spatial complex phenomena. In future work, we are interested in exploring how this genre of learning technology can support students to gain deeper insight into emergent relationships in complex phenomena. Future work may include swapping the abilities associated with the “embodied” and “object” modes to further examine how students use the affordances of each mode and analyzing how teachers scaffold students’ inquiry inside the simulation.
