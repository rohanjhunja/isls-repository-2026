# Exploring Students’ and Mathematics Teachers’ Conceptions About the Work of Mathematical Scientists and Possible Relations to Mathematics Teaching

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The aim of this paper is to investigate students’ and teachers’ conceptions about the work of mathematical scientists, and to explore possible relations to mathematics teaching. Therefore, we conducted an exploratory interview study with 14 9th graders and 5 mathematics teachers. The results show that students have inadequate and limited conceptions about the work of mathematical scientists. The belief that mathematical scientists do the same kind of mathematics as students do in the classroom – namely calculating – but on a higher level supports the assumption that students’ conceptions of the work of mathematical scientists are also based on their experiences in the classroom. We discuss the findings in light of previous research on images of mathematics, naïve epistemological beliefs about mathematics and conceptions about mathematicians. We further derive ideas for fostering more accurate conceptions in students.

## Introduction

Several researchers (e.g., Christidou, 2011; Finson, 2002; Nachtigall & Rummel, 2020) have assumed tha t students’ conceptions about science and scientists – especially in the field of STEM – are interrelated with their interest in and their attitudes towards science, and, therefore, affect their achievement as well as their study and career choices (see also Figure 1).

Figure 1 Model on the effects of students’ beliefs about science and scientists (Nachtigall & Rummel, 2020, p.641) Students’ who have incomplete or negative conceptions of science and scientists are, for example, less likely to pursue science courses and enter a science-related career (Stamer, Kubsch et al., 2019). Consequently,  it is necessary to explore and understand students’ conceptions. Only if educators have some understanding of students’ conceptions, they will be able to effectively and positively impact these conceptions through instruction (Finson, 2002).

Previous studies have shown that students often have limited, inaccurate, and stereotypical conceptions about science and scientists. For example, the results of the study by Newton and Newton (1992) show t hat the majority of students conceptualize science as the p rocessing of substances for making new materials (a  kind of applied chemistry) or as the study of living things, especially of animals. Research on students’ imag es of scientists further demonstrates that many students picture scientists as older or middle-aged Caucasian men who wear lab coats, glasses and/or beards, and work in laboratories surrounded by symbols of research, kno wledge, and technology, such as test tubes, Bunsen burners,  flasks, and bottles (see Christidou, 2011; Finson,  2002). Especially with regard to mathematics Barbeau (1990, p. 42, as quoted in Picker & Berry, 2000) conclud es that there is “probably no area of human activity […] as  afflicted as mathematics with a gap between the pu blic perception of its nature and what its practitioners believe it to be.” Furthermore, Furinghetti (1993, p. 34) argues that mathematics “is a discipline that enjoys a pec uliar property: it may be loved or hated, understoo d or misunderstood, but everybody has some mental image of it.” Inaccurate or stereotypical conceptions abo ut mathematics and mathematical scientists may hinder students’ study of mathematics (Picker & Berry, 2000). The results of a study by Köller et al. (2000), for instance, show that students’ beliefs about the nature of knowledge ICLS2022 Proceedings 155 © ISLS and the process of knowledge acquisition in mathematics influence their interest, their learning, and, consequently, their achievement in mathematics. It is likely to a ssume that students’ conceptions about typical acti vities of mathematical scientists may also influence their in terest, their learning, and, thus, their achievemen t as well as their study and career choices in mathematics (see Figure 1). However, in contrast to students’ beliefs about the nature of knowledge and the process of knowledge ac quisition, little research has been focused on stud ents’ perceptions about mathematical scientists until now. Especially, so far, students’ conceptions about the work of mathematical scientists have not been explicitly investigated in detail. Against this background, it appears essential to in vestigate students’ conceptions about the work of mathematical scientists. Therefore, the aim of this  paper is to investigate students’ perceptions abou t typical activities of mathematical scientists. Subsequently, students’ conceptions can be changed and, as a re sult, their thinking about their roles as future mathematicians  can be facilitated and extended (Rock & Shaw, 2000). However, if inadequate and stereotypic conceptions can be found, it is important to explore where thes e perceptions arise from to then be able to derive sp ecific interventions to foster more adequate concep tions. Obviously, students’ perceptions about the work of mathematical scientists are likely mainly based on their classroom experiences. Thus, additionally, teachers’ perceptions about typical activities of mathematical scientists are also investigated, in order to explore possible relations to mathematics teaching. Students’ conceptions about mathematics and the work of mathematicians Students often have a negative image of mathematics (e.g., Furinghetti, 1993; Howson & Kahane, 1990; National Research Council, 1989) and naïve epistemological b eliefs about mathematics (e.g., Köller et al., 2000; Schoenfeld, 1988) as well as limited conceptions about mathematicians and their work (Malkevitch, 1997; Picker & Berry, 2000; Rock & Shaw, 2000).

With respect to images of mathematics, several researchers (e.g., Furinghetti, 1993; Howson & Kahane, 1990; National Research Council, 1989) have shown t hat many people have a negative image of mathematic s. The National Research Council (1989, p. 43) portrayed the following picture of mathematics: Unfortunately, as children become socialised by sch ool and society, they begin to view mathematics as a rigid system of externally dictate d rules governed by standards of accuracy, speed and memory. Their view of mathematics shifts gradually from enthusiasm to apprehension, from confidence to fear. Eventually, most students leave mathematics under duress, convinced that only geniuses can learn it.

Consequently, Howson and Kahane (1990) talk about the “mathematics’ image problem”. A comparable picture was found in previous studies with regard to epistemological beliefs about mathematics (e.g., Köller et al., 2000; Schoenfeld, 1988). Epistemological beliefs are individuals’ representations about the nature of knowledge and the process of kn owledge acquisition (Hofer & Pintrich, 1997). Focusing on mathematics, Schoenfeld (1988) first investigated epistemological beliefs of students in a qualitative study. The results show that students believed that finding the right answer and expressing it in the right form is what matters. Moreover, students viewed themselves as passive con sumers of others’ mathematics. Schoenfeld assumes t hat these perceptions are largely affected by the exper iences that students make in mathematics classes. I n the mathematics classroom – according to Schoenfeld – t he subject matter is presented, explained, and rehe arsed; there is little sense of exploration or of the impo rtance of understanding. He concludes that through such mathematics teaching, students learn only to master algorithmic procedures, but not to think mathematically. The results of the study by Köller et al. (2000) also show that students primarily emphasize the schematic-algorithmic orientation of mathematics and of mathematics teaching. The majority of the students agreed with the following statements: “Mathematics is the retention and appli cation of definitions, formulas, mathematical facts, and procedures.”, and “Doing mathematics means: applying general rules and procedures to specific tasks.” Moreover, students seem unfamiliar with a relativistic scient ific-theoretical position that emphasizes the const ructive and processual character of mathematics. Furthermore, t he results by Köller et al. (2000) show that studen ts’ epistemological beliefs about mathematics influence  their interest, their learning, and, thus, their a chievement: Students with the belief that mathematics is simply the application of solution algorithms to given tasks are less interested and obtain lower achievement, whereas students with a relativistic conception of mathematic s demonstrate higher interest in mathematics and, as a likely consequence, gain higher achievement. But not only the images of mathematics and students ’ epistemological beliefs about mathematics are inadequate. Many people also lack knowledge about what it is that mathematicians actually do (e.g., Malkevitch, 1997; Picker & Berry, 2000). From mathematicians’ p oint of view, important mathematical activities of mathematicians mainly relate to participating in or iginal research, conjecturing, generalizing, and ex ploring as ICLS2022 Proceedings 156 © ISLS well as communicating, proving, and making connections (Brandt et al., 2016). Malkevitch (1997, p. 93) pointed out that students “can not connect the value of the study of mathematics with what mathematicians really do. […] students have learned when to ‘call’ or hire a doctor, electrician, geologist, or plumber, but not when to ‘call’ or hire a mathematician.”  Students’ beliefs about the work of mathematicians probably constitute a part of their epistemological beliefs about mathematics. However, these beliefs about the work of mathematicians or scientists more generally are of particular interest with respect to students’ study and career choices “because many young people base their occupational choices on these simplified conceptions” (Stamer, Kubsch et al., 2019, p. 85; see also Figure 1). However, so far, research has not focused on explicitly investigating students’ conceptions about typical activities of mathematicians in detail. To our knowledge, there are only two studies conducted  by Rock and Shaw (2000) and Picker and Berry (2000) that fo cused primarily on students’ conceptions about mathematicians. These studies offer first insights into students’ conceptions about the work of mathem aticians: Students believe that mathematicians do application s similar to those they have seen in their own math ematics classes (e.g., arithmetic computation, area and per imeter, and measurement). Both Rock and Shaw (2000)  and Picker and Berry (2000) attribute the inadequate and limited conceptions about mathematicians (and their work) to the experiences that students made in mathematic s classes. Mathematics classes, in turn, are influe nced by teachers’ beliefs related to mathematics. For examp le, the results of a study by Stipek et al. (2001) show that teachers with more traditional beliefs about the nature of mathematics, the teaching, and learning of mathematics, i.e., who believe that mathematics is a static body of knowledge involving a set of rules and procedures that they have to transmit to the students, are less likely to implement inquiry-based mathematics instruction. Presumably, teachers’ conceptions about typical activities of m athematical scientists may also influence their cla ssroom teaching.

## Research questions

As introduced before, the results of the study by Köller et al. (2000) show that students’ epistemological beliefs about mathematics influence their interest, their learning, and, thu s, their achievement. Similar impacts may be assumed for students’ conceptions about the work of scientists in general (see Figure 1) or of mathematicians in particular. Students’ conceptions about typical activities of mathematicians may particularly influence their study and career choices (Stamer, Kubsch et al., 2019). A gainst this background, the present study aims to e xplicitly investigate students’ conceptions about typical activities of mathematical scientists (research question 1). Given that these conceptions are likely rooted in student s’ experiences in mathematics classrooms – which in  turn are influenced by teachers’ beliefs related to mathemat ics (Stipek et al., 2001) – the present study also aims to investigate teachers’ conceptions about the work of mathematical scientists (research question 2). The purpose of the present study is then to uncover possible relat ions between students’ conceptions about the work o f mathematical scientists and the experiences they are given in mathematics classes (research question 3).

## Method

To investigate our research questions, we conducted  an exploratory interview study with 14 9 th graders (age: M = 14.86, SD = 0.66; 78.6% male; interest in mathematics betwee n 1 = no interest and 6 = high interest: M = 4.14, SD = 1.23; school grade in mathematics between 1 = ve ry good and 6 = unsatisfactory: M = 2.14, SD = 0.86) and 5 mathematics teachers (one male; teaching experience in mathematics: 2 to 13 years) who taught in grades 9 or 10 from urban schools in Germany. Bo th students and teachers volunteered to participate  in our interview study by responding to a written inquiry that we had sent to local schools. We interviewed the students as well as the mathematics teachers in June and July 2021 using semi-structured interviews. The interviews with the students lasted between 9 and 17 minutes and wi th the teachers between 16 and 34 minutes. The inte rview protocol contained the following three parts: (1) V iew on mathematics and doing mathematics, (2) Typic al mathematical activities of mathematical scientists,  (3) Typical mathematical activities during mathema tical experimentation. The interview protocol for the mat hematics teachers included additional questions about their teaching of mathematics in all three aforementioned parts of the protocol. We used the questions of the first part as an introduction to the interview and the topic. The third part was used to finally illustrate typical mathematical activities by using a specific classroom example and to enable the students and teachers to relate these activities to the work of mathematical scientists. To investigate the research questions of the present study, we look at the students’ and teachers’ responses to the questions of the second part of the interview. In the second part of the interview, we asked the students and teachers the following question: “What do you think are typical activities of mathematical scientists who work, for example, at universities?” We also asked for similarities and differences between what mathe matical scientists do and what students do. In addi tion, we asked the teachers: “To what extent do you teach typical mathematical activities of mathematical scientists in your 9th or 10th grade mathematics classes?” We systematically analyzed the students’ and teachers’ responses through ICLS2022 Proceedings 157 © ISLS different coding procedures that were guided by the  analysis process of constructivist grounded theory methodology (e.g., Charmaz, 2006). Charmaz’s constructivist grounded theory methodology is particular suitable for exploratory investigations of little-known phen omena due to the largely exclusion of prior knowled ge. It focuses on analytically capturing and interpretivel y understanding the characteristics and contexts of  the phenomenon. For this purpose, we first analyzed fra gments of data (i.e., words, lines, and segments) t o assess their relevance for answering the research questions (initial coding; e.g., Charmaz, 2006). This proce ss allowed us to remain open for exploring any conceptions abo ut typical activities of mathematical scientists th at can be detected in the data. In doing so, terms used by students and teachers were adopted as in vivo codes. For example, the following segment from a statement of T4 “that they [mathematical scientists] […] make assertions which they then perhaps try to prove somehow with the help of given theorems or somehow deduce things themselves, so that they can then somehow prove their assertions or relations which they want to show […]” was first coded with the in vivo codes proving and deducing. This first step of coding led us to a collection of various individual activities of mathematical scientists from the stud ents’ and teachers’ point of view. In a second step  (focused coding; e.g., Charmaz, 2006), we selected what we t hought were the most useful in vivo codes and teste d them against extensive data. Specifically, we compared data with data and then data with codes. Thus, we obtained core conceptual categories. These categories no longer included only individual activities, but rather represented more general and abstract conceptions. For instance, the statement of T4 above was assigned to the more gen eral and abstract conception doing research. Throughout the whole coding process, the authors collaboratively negotiated the coding of the data. For this purpose, we repeatedly compared our understanding of the data to achieve greater precision and consistency (see, e.g., Corbin & Strauss, 1990). In addition, we used, for example, a clear protocol of codes and memoing to improve reliability through transparency.

## Results

Students’ conceptions about the work of mathematical scientists Based on the coding procedure described above, we i dentified the following conceptions of students (re search question 1): Students believe mathematical scientists … ● do the same kind of mathematics as the students in the classroom but on a higher level, ● optimize existing procedures, ● teach mathematics, ● conduct didactic research, and ● carry out professions that require mathematical calculations. The majority of the 14 students (n = 11) believe that mathematical scientists do the same kind of mathematics as they do in their mathematics classroom but on a higher level. When asked for similarities and differences between what mathematical scientists do and what students do, S4, for example, answered on a rather general level: “[…] of course, we do something slightly different, but somehow also the same.” S6 responded more concretely: “[…] [mathematical scientists] also calculate like we do. But I think they get much more difficult tasks and have to solve much more complex things with their heads.” A nother example for the conception that mathematical scientists do the same kind of mathematics as schoo l students do but on a higher level, can be found i n the following statement from S8: “[…] they [mathematical scientists] also convert formulas, do calculations and, yes, they also simply look at things like triangles […].  So of course, it’s much more complicated what they  do at universities than here in schools.” S3 added that mathematical scientists “also have to figure things out and also do math with their heads and with calculators and w ith computers maybe even.” S6 also mentioned, in addition to mental arithmetic, the use of the calculator, th e set square, and various shapes as other similarit ies between what mathematical scientists do and what students do. Moreover, from the perspective of students, mathematical scientists often use “things that we don’t even kno w here at school yet” (S5), because the students “s till do the somewhat simple things and try to figure them out. They [the mathematical scientists] do the difficult ones one step above” (S12). Furthermore, mathematical scientists apply mathematical knowledge, whereas students learn mathematics: “[…] we [students] sit to many in a room and learn there, but I think […] [in] science, there they sit alone and already calculate, so they don’t learn, but already apply the knowledge” (S5). Some of the students (n = 4) also revealed the conception that mathematical scientists optimize existing procedures. For example, from the perspective of S1, mathemat ical scientists deal with “how to calculate something or how to make something simpler than it really is now. […] And mathematicians […] also try to bring their own way in or find a simpler variant.” S10 also believes that mathematical scientists try to “simplify new or the mathematical rules students have […] [and] find new ways to solve something.” ICLS2022 Proceedings 158 © ISLS In addition, from the perspective of some students (n = 3), mathematical scientists teach mathematics: “I think teaching. […] Yes, teach other people and instruct them” (S4). Also for S1, mathematical scientists “must also be able to explain things to people or also represent things in a simplified way.” Furthermore, S2 even focuses on didactic research as mathematical scientists conduct interviews and test different (teaching) materials – similar to the present study.

Some of the students (n = 3) referred rather to other professions or areas in which mathematical calculations are required, for instance, to the application of mathematics i n economics or in handcraft. When asked for typical activities of mathematical scientists S7 answered: “I would imagine now, with business I would imagine just something, with numbers, with orders.” Teachers’ conceptions about the work of mathematical scientists With regard to research question 2, we observed the following conceptions of mathematics teachers: Mathematics teachers believe mathematical scientists … ● do mathematical research, ● teach mathematics, ● solve abstract problems, and ● provide bases for models.

Most of the teachers (n = 4) named “ mathematical research” (e.g., T1) as a typical activity of mathematical scientists. From the perspective of T3, mathematica l scientists conduct research in order to develop n ew or to optimize existing procedures: “[…] research, connec t any theorems and find something new or a faster w ay somewhere […].” A similar conception was also shown  by T4 who considers mathematical research mainly i n the context of deducing and proving: “that they [mathematical scientists] want to explore various relations in more detail and make assertions which they then perhaps try to prove somehow with the help of given theorem s or somehow deduce things themselves, so that they can then somehow prove their assertions or relations which they want to show […].” Moreover, T4 added that mathemat ical scientists actually find out new things, where as students find out what has already been found out: “that what the students candiscover […] has alread y been discovered, […] and they have to think about it, [… ] to understand it […]. And what an applied mathema ticiandoes, for me, is to find out something new.” T3 also mentioned that a goal of mathematical research is to find out what is actually new through deducing and proving, which is a difference between what mathematical scientists do and what students do. In contrast, according to T3, students only use mathematical knowledge: “[mathematical scientist] apply what they already know and try to prove things and the students […] only use, but don ’t get anywhere with it, but are always confronted with the same tasks.” There is also a large discrepancy from the other teachers’ perspective between what mathematical sci entists do and what students do. For instance, T2 differentiates as follows: “So I would say at school, you don’t learn mathematics at all, you learn calculation. You actually learn calculation.” In addition, a few teachers (n = 2) identified teaching as a typical activity of mathematical scientists. For example, T5: “Mathematics teaching in science […] [for the] respective students […].” Furthermore, T1 named solving abstract problems  as a typical activity of mathematical scientists: “[…] a lot of dealing with abstract problems.” T2 mentioned creating the basis for models “in order to be able to use them in the real world.” Possible relations to mathematics teaching With respect to possible relations to mathematics teaching (research question 3), the teachers’ answers show that the teaching of typical activities of mathematical scientists plays rather a subordinate role in their classes. In the mathematics lessons of T3, for instance, typical activities of mathematical scientists are taught “rather seldom”. If anything, the teachers try to teach deducing or proving as typical activities of mathematical scien tists, as evidenced by the following statements: “[…] so one tries to proceed very logically and to conclude thi ngs deductively again and again […]” (T1) or “Now, classic example: Pythagorean theorem. You just let them prove it” (T2).

From the perspective of the teachers, the following reasons often prevent the teaching of typical activities of mathematical scientists in mathematic classes: ● the requirements in graduation, ● the lack of time, ● the abilities of the students, and ● their own distance from the university context.

For instance, T3 said that typical activities of mathematical scientists are not required in the curriculum and, consequently, not required in the final exams: “sin ce it is not really intended by the curriculum, it’s of course always difficult with regard to any final exams, where only pure calculation is required or the same standard formulations are somehow tested, to take time and say: ‘We will do it anyway.’, because you actually know that they don’t need it at least for this exam and most of them don’t need it later either.” T1 also emphasizes – besides the lack of time – that class tests often do not require typical activities of mathematical scientists: “But what is required in class tests, for example, is a completely different matter. So, the whole system is a bit ambivalent.” Another reason for T1 and T3 why the teaching of ty pical activities of mathematical scientists plays a rather minor role in the classroom are the abilities of the students. Among others, T1 emphasizes the “discrepancy between the level at which one has to work in school in some cases […] and what is required later in the university context […] or what would be needed for exact mathe matical work.” T3 also stresses that there is seldo m the opportunity to teach typical activities of mathematical scientists and that then often the majority of  students are not able to follow: “[…] from time to time, you try  to tickle out a little bit, especially when you no tice that the attention is there and that a setting arises where you can think a little further and one or the other is perhaps at a point where it is also possible. But the majority can’t follow and at this point it is always difficult to promote this [typical activities of mathematical scientists] in a larger group […].” In addition, T3 also considered their own distance from the university context as a further reason: “The longer you are away from university work, I would s ay, the more insecure you feel about being able to pass on such activities.”

## Discussion and implications

To conclude, the results of our exploratory intervi ew study show that students have inadequate and lim ited conceptions about the work of mathematical scientis ts. The majority of the students believe that mathe matical scientists mainly calculate – as they also do in th eir own mathematics classes – but on a higher level. Rock and Shaw (2000) as well as Picker and Berry (2000) found similar conceptions about mathematicians in students. Our results are, moreover, in line with previous findings on students’ epistemological beliefs about mathematics (e.g., Köller et al., 2000; Schoenfeld, 1988). Specificall y, we found that the schematic-algorithmic orientat ion of mathematics is also present in students’ conception s about the work of mathematical scientists. Howeve r, in everyday life as well as professional contexts, cha llenges are increasingly posed that cannot be met b y the schematic application of mathematical rules. Rather, mastering these challenges requires the use of mathematical tools to process and evaluate them, such as problem solving, reasoning or modeling (Hammer et al., 2016). Our results are especially critical in light of the fac t that we surveyed mainly students with a high inte rest and good grades in mathematics. These students may also be interested in studying mathematics at a university after having graduated from school. But their limited conception s focusing on the schematic-algorithmic orientation  of mathematics may be particularly problematic in the university context: The mathematics curriculum at a university initially focuses on the scientific character of mathematics and, especially, mathematical proving, and not on the performance of calculations. Students’ l imited conceptions about typical activities of math ematical scientists may lead to unrealistic expectations about the requirements of mathematics studies which, in turn, may hinder the effectiveness of individual learning processes (see Rach et al., 2014). If already students  with a high interest and good grades in mathematics have inadeq uate and limited conceptions, it raises the questio n what conceptions students with low interest and/or poor grades in mathematics have about typical activities  of mathematical scientists. Further research should therefore also investigate the conceptions of student s with low interest and/or poor grades in mathematics.

Consequently, the results of the present study highlight the need to change students’ conceptions about typical activities of mathematical scientists and t o foster more adequate conceptions. As mentioned ab ove, it is therefore essential to know where these conceptions arise from in order to be able to derive specific interventions for fostering more adequate conceptions. The present study suggests that students’ conceptions are based on their classroom experiences. Mathematics classes, in turn, are influenced by teachers’ beliefs related to ma thematics (Stipek et al., 2001). Our results additionally sho w that teachers have limited conceptions about the work of mathematical scientists, too, as the teachers who p articipated in our study named only some of the imp ortant activities of mathematical scientists, usually remaining rather vague about the concrete activities. A lthough the majority of the teachers identified doing mathematical research – often in connection with deducing and proving

– as typical activities of mathematical scientists, they did not describe it in more detail and did not name further

activities mathematicians consider as important, su ch as conjecturing, generalizing, and exploring or communicating (see Brandt et al., 2016). This result may be attributed to the teachers’ own classroom experiences ICLS2022 Proceedings 160 © ISLS as students, too, and also to their experiences fro m studying mathematics at university. During studyi ng mathematics at university, the teachers probably ga ined only a few insights into the work of mathemati cal scientists. In lectures, mathematics is “formal, pr ecise, ordered and abstract” and is often presented  only “in ‘finished’ form” (Hersh, 1991, p. 128), but not the  way mathematical scientists actually work and figu re things out, for example, by conjecturing, generalizing, and exploring or communicating. Therefore, it is alsonecessary to broaden teachers’ conceptions about typical activities of mathematical scientists. One promising opportunity to foster more adequate conceptions about typical activities of mathematical scientists in both students and teachers relates to offering insights into the work of mathematical scientists. This could be achieved, for example, by cooperating with mathematical scientists as role models or mentors who tell students and teachers about their work or by using narratives written by and about mathematical scient ists. Another possibility to promote more adequate conceptions about typical activities of mathematical scientists is to carry out these activities by oneself. One potentia lly promising approach could relate to the authenti c contextualization of learning settings. Authentic learning contexts try to provide authentic activitie s that reflect the way the knowledge will be used in real life (Herrington & Oliver, 2000). With regard to mathematics teaching, an authentic learning activity may include mathematical modeling with hands-on experimental tasks (see Carreira & Baioa, 2018). However, both such visits of mathem atical scientists and such tasks are always very ti me consuming. As the findings of our interviews with mathematics teachers show, there is a lack of time in school to teach typical activities of mathematical scientists. Therefore, it would be useful to teach typical ac tivities of mathematical scientists outside the classroom. One possibly fruitful opportunity may be offered by out-of-school labs. Projects in out-of-school labs aim to promote adequate conceptions about science and scientists by enabling students to authentically experience scientific ways of thinking and working (Scharfenberg & Bogner, 2014). As part of such projects, students could, for instance, also collaborate with real mathematical scientists. Unfortunately, real mathematical scientists often do not have the time to work with students. For this reason, Stamer, Pönicke et al. (2019), for example, used videos as part of a p roject in an out-of-school lab in which scientists could be observed at work. The results showed that students recognized all explicitly intended authentic scientific activities in the videos. Given this finding, observing mathem atical scientists at work also appears as a benefic ial way to foster more accurate conceptions about mathematical scientists and their work in students. For future research, it would additionally be interesting to examine the effect of those authentic insights on students’ interest in and their attitudes towards mathematics.
