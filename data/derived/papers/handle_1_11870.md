# Using Markov Chains to Understand Clinical Decision-Making Process

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Clinical reasoning is a critical yet complex cognitive process of diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making in medical practice that has long challenged precise understanding and assessment. Sequential analysis can be used to uncover patterns and trends in clinical practices, contributing to improved training and ultimately leading to better patient care outcomes. In this study, 21 board-certified anesthesiologists participated in a simulated-based learning scenario requiring them to promptly recognize patient’s condition and initiate appropriate treatment. They were assigned into either the low-performing or high-performing group based on their performance. We utilized Markov Chain Transition Matrix, a robust statistical model for sequential data, to analyze participants’ practices using team reflection behavioral observation system and identified statistically significant differences between their transition matrices. The high-performing group had a much higher transition probability from evaluating information to implementation and from planning to planning. The implications are then discussed.

## Introduction

Clinical reasoning is the systematic process by whi ch healthcare professionals gather, interpret, and analyze patient information to make informed diagnostic and  treatment decisions, integrating their own (biomedical and clinical) knowledge with initial patient information (Gruppen, 2016; Schmidt & Boshuizen, 1993; Richters et al., 2024; Smith et al., 2008). These complex skills are challenging to teach, mast er, and assess  (Gruppen, 2016). Traditional assessment methods like written assessments (e.g., multiple-choice questions, script concordance tests, and post-encounter notes), performance-based assessments (e.g., direct observation, standardized patient encounters, and simulations), and p rocess-focused assessments (think-aloud protocols, chart-stimulated recall, concept mapping) have been fundamental in measuring different aspects of a clinician’s diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making abilities  (Daniel et al., 2019). These methods, commonly linked with  inferential statistical techniques like ANOVA and t-tests, have offered important insights into group d ifferences regarding learners ’ behaviors and self-reported data. However, these approaches frequently fail to adequately capture th e dynamic aspects of clinical decision-making, which involve nuanced differences in the sequence of actions take n by clinicians in a certain scenario (Banning, 2008; Smith et al., 2008). During clinical diagnostic and treatment reasoning process, diverse states of clinical management occur in sequences and potentially impact one and another  (Brandl et al., 2024). Recognizing patterns and trajectories in learners’ clinical reasoning, including moments where their d ecision-making deviates from the ideal path, can help tailor instructional support (Banning, 2008). Gaining insight into the clinical decision-making process allows for real-time tracking of learners’ reasoning patterns, making it possible to offer timely support and interventions when needed.

Sequential analysis, a method to analyze sequential data and identify me aningful temporal patterns in big data, has been widely used in educational research to examine students’ learning behaviors (Baker et al., 2016; Romero & Ventura, 2020). For instance, Li et al. (20 20) examined the temporal dynamics of students’ self-regulated learning  (SRL) behaviors in a computer-aided engineering design learning environment. Temporal analysis allows them to uncover meaningful transitions among different types of SRL learning strategies th at potentially contribute to positive learning outcomes. Another study also applied sequential analysis to uncover patterns in transformative and non-transformative discourse in online collaborative le arning settings (Zhu et al., 2019). As one of the most used learning analytic methods, sequential analysis has brought many valuable insights into understanding learning behaviors and predicting learning outcomes (e.g., Baker et al., 2016; Du et al., 2024; Saqr & López-Pernas, 2023; Zhu et al., 2019). Yet, medical education research has not fully ben efited from this powerful method.

To address this gap, the current study aimed to explore how utilizing sequential analysis  can overcome the shortcomings of traditional assessment methods in capturing the dynamic aspects of clinical decision-making. Specifically, we applied Markov Chains transition matrix, a powerful statistical model to examine temporal data, to understand the clinical decision-making process in a medical high-acuity simulation scenario.

## Participants and setting

The study involved 21 board-certified anesthesiologists (14 males and 7 females) who participated in a simulationbased maintenance of certification course at a Midw estern academic medical center over a five-year period. The simulated learning scenario centered on the intraoperative management of a 36-year-old female patient with acute cholangitis undergoing a laparoscopic cholecystectomy. During the procedure, the patient experienced malignant hyperthermia (MH), requiring the primary anesthesio logist to promptly recognize the condition and init iate appropriate treatment. Established treatment protoc ols for MH include discontinuing triggering agents, administering dantrolene, and providing supportive care, which are well-documented and widely accessible. The simulated training scenario involve d a team of medical crew, including nurses, surgeon a ssistants, and surgeons, like real-life surgeon scenarios. The anesthesiologist participants’ task as team leaders was to recognize the patient’s condition  and make a treatment plan for the patient, which is als o known as clinical decision-making. They were expected to ask questions about the patient, evaluate the information, state plans, and eventually implement their treatment plan.

Measurement Team reflection behavioral observation (TuRBO) system The research team first transcribed the video and then annotated the data using the Team Reflection Behavioral Observation (TuRBO) System  which was specifically designed for acute care team s (Schmutz et al., 2021). It serves as a framework  to observe and evaluate team reflection during clinical action.  TuRBO allows team members to pause amidst dynamic activities to collaboratively collect information, evaluate the current situation, and conduct short-term planning.  Researchers examined its validity and suggested tha t TuRBO effectively assesses teamwork aligning with performance outcomes (Schmut z et al., 2021), as well as provides explicit behavioral indicators for training purposes, particularly in acute care team settings. We applied TuRBO codes to the transcripts of our video recordings. Two researchers independently coded six data files, then discussed their findings  and resolved any discrepancies. They reached an int er-rater reliability of.73 using Cohen’s kappa. Following this process, they completed annotating all data files. Then, we combined similar TuRBO codes and summarized a clini cal behavioral category for our study. An additiona l “implementation” category was introduced to account for the critical  importance of recognizing malignant hyperthermia (MH) and subsequently implementing appropriate treatment. ## Data analysis

A Markov Chain transition matrix is a mathematical tool used to represent the probabilities of moving between different states in a Markov process. This process models transitions between states based on predefin ed probabilistic rules.  This approach enables researchers to study the lear ning process from an event-driven perspective while identifying patterns and transiti ons within the data  (Viberg et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2019). Markov chain methods have been utilized in educatio nal research to offer sequential recommendations for learning behavior /outcome prediction, course selections, and identifying at r isk students (e.g., Beal & Cohen, 2008; Yildiz, 2025). ## Discussion and conclusion

We applied Markov Chain transition matri x to examine the clinical decision-making process data in a simulated medical training setting. Results suggest that the transition matri x of the low-performing group and the high-performing group are statistically significantly different from each other. While the two groups shared similar top three common transition pairs, we identified two notable pairs (evaluating information → implementation and planning → planning) that are different in transition probability. The study has both pedagogical and methodological implications for understanding the clinical decision-making process. First, both high and low-performing groups frequently transitioned from various states back to seeking information, reflecting the iterative nature of evidence generation during collaborative diagnostic reasoning in high-stakes, time-sensitive scenarios where incomplete or evolving in formation is common  (Salas et al., 2009; Smith et al., 2008). Second, the high-performing group had a 70% higher probability of transiting from evaluating information to implementation, indicating a more efficient information gathering  or information integration into their decision-making process. This group seems to better translate diagnostics assessments into therapeutic actions, which is a critical skill in emergency situations w here timely intervention significantly impacts pati ent outcomes. Third, the high-performing group had a 350% higher probability of t ransiting from planning to planning, highlighting the critical role of plannin g in clinical decision-making process. We hypothesize that the high-performing group could state their plans and priorities more frequently, contributing to in-time diagnosis and treatment. This also suggests that they engage in more iterative planning, continuously usi ng the input received to formulate a diagnosis and management plan.

Methodologically, Markov-Chain-based method provides a promising approach to under stand the dynamic nature of clinical decision-making. We modeled the sequence and transitions bet ween different behavioral states (i.e., seeking information, evaluating information, planni ng, and implementation) to uncover patterns in decision-making processes that may not be apparent through o ther traditional assessment methods (Banning, 2008). In our study, Markov Chain transition matrix helped identify both common patterns and notable differences between the high-performing and low-performing groups. In addition, t his data-driven approach emphasizing the behaviors exhibited during the simulation could potentially enable in-time and/or post-simulation feedback on these advanced skills.

There are several limitations. First, the limited sample size of this study may impact the statistical power. Researchers and practitioners shall be cautious when generalizing our results to other scenarios. Second, potential confounding factors such as individual differences in prior experience and variations in team composit ion were not fully accounted for in our analysis, which might influence team performance and decision-making patterns. Future research should extend the current in terms of sample size and learning scenarios to fully leve rage the potential of this method in medical education.
