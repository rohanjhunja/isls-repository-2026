# Affective Contextures of Collective Care: Sustainability in Nursing Work

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, and as part of a broader research program on the role of collective care in sustainability transformations, we discuss how nurses working in hospitals in the U.S. co-create and rely on informal forms of collective support to compensate for the lack of sufficient organizational support. By exploring collective care as affective contextures that are part of everyday nursing work, we aim to show how the affective aspects of everyday life are intertwined with organizational, institutional, and economic dimensions and how such intertwining is key for understanding ecosocial transitions to more sustainable ways of living and learning. We draw on a narrative case study based on interviews with nurses in the U.S. context to illustrate how practices of collective care, though often at the expense of nurses’ own health and well-being, also mobilize creative, informal forms of resilience and communal support that prefigure more humane, sustainable practices.

## Introduction

This paper addresses the dislocation between the communal, collective fo rms of support that exist informally across a wide range of institutional practices —including educational institutions —and the formal institutional structures that treat the individual subject as the  primary unit of formalized accountability. The pra ctices of formalized accountability and quantification of per formance of the “audited subject” (Strathern, 2000)  became a means of organization and regulation of public institutions in neo-liberal systems, allowing states and corporations to evade the responsibility of collective support, which is instead delegated to individuals, who are responsible for managing and monitoring themselves. As part of a broader research program on the role of c ollective care in sustainability transformations, we discuss how nurses working in hospitals in the United States co-create and rely on informal forms of collective support to compensate for the lack of sufficient state and organizational support. By exploring the acts of collective care that are p art of everyday nursing work, we aim to show how affective dimensions are intertwined with organizational, institutional,  and economic dimensions and how such intertwining is key for understanding ecosocial transitions to more sustainable ways of living and learning. Crucial to envisioning scholarship responsive to the eco-social crises brought about by neoliberalism (Jorne t, 2024), we call for renewed attention to the problem of the in dividual as a unit of analysis. With our discussion and analysis of collective care,  we hope to make visible examples of the unaccounted  but essential affective work that goes into sustaining otherwise (un)sustainable forms of human organization. We empirically illustrate how collective caring pra ctices not only produce and sustain institutions bu t also constitute the collective substance upon which  these institutions reside. A core component of tha t collective substance is what we call affective contextures — affective, irreducibly social relations that mutual ly shape individuals and institutional contexts within situated practices. In introducing this concept, we draw on scholarship that challenges the conventional understanding of context as a bounded container for situated practices (Jornet & Erstad, 2018; Roth & Jornet, 2018). We also draw on feminist approaches to labor and care, part icularly through the lens of Marxist feminism and feminist STS (Fras er, 2022; Orozco, 2015; Tronto, 1993), to position the informal and unaccounted forms of care as critical for nurses to complete their daily formal duties. We align with Lynch (2021) that rather than responses to a broken s ystem, relying on informal forms of support is a defining feature of neoliberal governance.

This paper explores these dynamics through the following research questions: RQ1: How do informal practices of collective care a mong nurses—understood as affective contextures — sustain both their well-being and market-driven healthcare institutions while shaping the (un)sustainable organization of nursing work?

RQ 2: How can collective care, as an affective and relational practice, expand our understanding of ec osocial transitions toward more sustainable ways of living and human organization?

## Theoretical framework: The interweaving of affective contextures into

collective care Our understanding of care is shaped by feminist science and technology studies (STS) scholars recognize medical, scientific, and technological practices as inherent ly intertwined with ethical considerations and poli tical stakes (e.g. Lindén & Lydahl, 2021; Murphy, 2015). In current Western economies, care is commonly viewed as a private emotional disposition, with caregiving relegated to the domestic sphere, often disproportionately falling to women and low-wage caregiving professions. Caregiving is thus bot h monetarily and symbolically devalued, making it primarily the domain of the less powerful (Fraser, 2022). As the feminist political scientist Joan Tro nto (1993) explains, “Care is difficult work, but it is the wo rk that sustains life” (pp. 117-119). Tronto reframes care as a foundational form of labor that upholds society and  communities.  Applying market principles and economic metrics to health care raises concerns and criticisms (Guarrasi et al., 2024; Mol, 2008; Waitzkin, 2018).

In this paper, our interest in care relates to the broader problem of sustainability, that is, the  capacity to maintain or support processes of human activity ove r time in ways responsive to everyone’s needs and r ights. While the concept of care has been thematized in li teratures on sustainability and sustainability education (e.g., Moriggi et al., 2020), its connections to affective relationships, linking individuals and social contexts, have been suggested but not adequately theorized (Røkenes & Jornet, in press). We address this gap by advancing the notion of affective contextures of collective care to discuss its relevance for sustainability in healthcare. By calling for a broader understanding of the affective dimensions of care, we recognize their role in sustaining not only the wellbeing of individuals but also institutions and economies. In developing the notion of affective contextures, we draw on prior work showing how social contexts can be examined as affective, political, and materi al assemblages, whether these are in the context of  healthcare (Guarrasi, 2015; Street, 2012), schools (Jornet, 202 3), design work (Jornet & Roth, 2018) or sites of r oad construction (Knox, 2017). Extending these works, w e specifically use contexture to trace the weaving of heterogeneous, affective entanglements among practitioners in and outside of work. The notion is related to what post-structuralist French philosophers Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari refer to as “lines of flight,” thematizing how individuals seek to escape from oppressive social and institutional landscapes. They described this movement as “deterritorialization” (p. 9), a flight toward creating new territories of (well)being. With contextures we further emphasize the cloth-like materiality of weaving threads of the affective fabric of care.

## Methods and modes of inquiry

This paper is part of a broader collaborative proje ct undertaken by the authors to create original comparative insights about sustainability, affect, and collecti ve care in education and healthcare, using various ethnographic methods, including observation, interviews, and col laborations with the participants. For this paper, we adopt a narrative case study approach, defined as “a resear ch instrument that is used for the in-depth study of various social and clinical problems, to understand stages or phases in processes and to investigate a phenomenon within its environmental context” (Brandell & Varkas, 2001, p. 376). We draw on ten open-ended interviews with participants recruited from a  pool of nurses who have  at least two years of experience in clinical settings in Minnesota, United States. To recruit participants, we utilized snowball sampling through referrals from initial participants. We interviewed seven women, two men, and one non-binary individual. Nine out of the ten interviewed participants are Caucasian, while one is African American. As data collection continues, we aim to include a more diverse group of nurses. Although snowball sampling may have contributed to the lack of diversity in our da ta, this initial sample reflects the typical demographi cs of Registered Nurses (RNs) and Advanced Practice Registered Nurses (APRNs) in Minnesota (Minnesota Board of Nursing, 2023). Both nursing licenses require a higher education degree, with APRNs having completed graduatelevel education. Each interview was conducted online via Zoom and lasted approximately 45 to 75 minutes. All interviews were audio or video  recorded and transcribed verbatim. They were conduc ted in a semi-structured format, with interview questions relating to the participants' experiences working in healthcare. To analyze the data, we conducted narrative analyses, identifying recurring narrative patterns related to the sustainability of nursing work,  personal definitions of quality patient  care, formal and informal ways of connecting with co-workers to help each other complete daily tasks, and formal and informal forms of collective organizing at work. Our analyses s ought to trace socio-affective, relational challenges and coping resources articulated in the nurses’ narratives, and contradictions and tensions thereof. In doing  so, we draw on insights on cultural-historical tensions as key to developmental processes in professional (health) work contexts (Engeström & Pyörälä, 2021), as well as on the framework on affective contextures of collective care elaborated above.

## Findings

The main theme emerging from nurses’ narratives is the tension between the commitment to delivering compassionate, quality care and the pressures that prioritize cost-effectiveness. We examine how these institutional and professional tensions manifest as a lived, embodied reality of un/sustainability for nurses, taking a toll on their emotional and physical health. Our analyses demonstrate how the threads of collective care among nurses support both their everyday work and healthcare institutions. However, such rhizomatic forms of resilience also deplete them and contribute to challenging work and life conditions that are difficult to sustain. The un/sustainability of market-driven healthcare In our conversations with nurses, the institutional  context of the healthcare system emerges as a set of lived tensions in everyday nursing work. For nurses, qual ity patient care is connected to being present for the patients, spending time with them, listening, attending to th eir emotional, physical, and mental well-being, and meeting their goals while providing safe and evidence-based care. However, the current business model org anizes healthcare delivery according to market-based mechanisms to increase efficiency and cost-effectiveness. In such a context, nurses are asked to “do more with less.” The contradictions between the nurses’ professional goals and the means provided to them to accomplish them are f elt intensely. Sarah, a nurse with 24 years of expe rience in healthcare, shares her insights: It's just not sustainable. It's exhausting! I never  feel like I'm caught up. I feel like I'm always passing things on to the next oncoming provider and  it just feels like a race every day, like if I wasn't so pulled in so many different directions, I  could provide better patient care. I can go in and meet with the patient and pretty quickly identify like the treatment goals. But those are my goals. Those are evidence-based practice goals. It's not the patient's goals, not necessarily. Sarah’s comment illustrates what Engeström & Pyöräl ä (2021) call the tension between the object of act ivity— the official, task-based care work nurses are required to provide and the means of carrying out the object of activity

— nursing caring about how they do this care work. Nurses experience this tension as an inability to fully be there

for their patients, to spend enough time with them, and to provide the care they believe would benefit their patients. This also brings a sense of guilt and emotional d ifficulty in dealing with this feeling of failure. Facilitating effective relations of care is then seen as being in tension with economic imperatives and organizational pressures to regulate and standardize healthcare practices.

Carla, a nurse in her early 40s, shares how her fir st job out of school —seven years at a demanding inpatient rehabilitation center —took a significant toll on her physical and mental health due to the intense pressures to deliver cost-effective care.

You feel like a failure every single day. The emotional side of it was too difficult. You felt like you were rushing, and then so I never really got mu ch better at that. To be honest, you just get more callous about it, which is sad.

Carla's feeling of becoming "callous" shows how everyday work experiences may be seen as affective contextures interweaving systemic conditions with individual bodies, affecti ng their bodies and transforming their skin by making it callous. Carla's use of callousness as an  embodied metaphor indicates how these experiences are felt physically on the skin. The goal of nursing work is  to provide quality patient care. However, the shor tage of resources to deliver this level of care leads to feelings of failure, exhaustion, and burnout among nurses. Nurses face challenging workloads, particularly due to high nurse-to-patient ratios. They often feel overwhelmed and unsupported. The high turnover of nurses leads to some of them be ing the most senior nurses on the shift with less than a year of experience. In contrast, Rosie, who has worked in the labor and delivery unit for over a decade, describes her work as "the best job in the world."  She highlights the rare 1:1 or 1:2 nurse-topatient ratio in her unit as an important variable. While this might not be cost-effective, it is essential in the labor and delivery unit due to the high-risk nature of the patients whoneed hands-on monitoring due to the immediate, life-threatening risks. In obstetrics and gynecology, the high potential for malpractice suits has become a variable that contributes to improved working conditions  (Hamasaki & Hagihara, 2015). In contrast, the legal risk in rehabilitation units is statistically much lower, but patients need the type of care that is formally undervalued. As Rosie explains: “These patients might need 20 thing s, plus all the meds, plus, fluff my pillow and tur n me, and I have to go to the bathroom, and you know, these things don’t count to administrators.” Affective contextures of collective care In the nurses’ narratives, references to unsustainable institutional structures  contrast sharply with the more informal, relational forms of support that nurses c reate within their units. This collective care is n ot only a professional courtesy but also a survival strategy in an unsustainable work environment. These forms of collective care are informal, relational, and deeply rooted in  trust and mutual support. Here, contextures are weaved as relationships of trust that are formed through spending time with each other, not j ust at work, but also attending "happy hours" with colleagues  after work, organizing book clubs, celebrating birt hdays, and creating emotional bonds and close friendships in the process. These informal contextures are often sustained by social events outside of work. Yet, they maintain the conditions of non-economic background (Fraser, 2022), which are necessary for the sustainable functioning of quality patient care in the hospital. There are a lot of happy hours after work. I work w ith a lot of younger nurses, and they are happy hour, like 3 nights a week. They usually organize it throughout the day like, Hey, let's go to Tom Reed after work tonight. For a while, I avoi ded the happy hours. But you are kind of missing out on team building. Really. I just feel l ike, if you are never going to any of those events, you aren't going to be as close with your coworkers at work [and] the nurses are the only ones on the floor. You have to help each other with patient care. As the excerpt illustrates, informal, communal support not only helps nurses cope with their demanding workloads but is also inextricably entangled with a nd perpetuates the harmful system that exploits the ir labor, bodies, and health. The nurses’ narratives show how affective contextures have a rhizomatic structure (Deluze & Guattari, 1987), extending beyond work contexts, not only into informal, social gatherings among coworkers but also to nurses’ private spaces and homes. Healthcare work is also specific because it deals wi th life-and-death situations that can be traumatizing and hard to share and process ou tside of the circle of what she called “work family.” These regenerative practices involve complexities t hat blur the boundaries between nurses' professiona l and personal lives.

The dialectics of depletion and regeneration of collective care In the context of collective care, we suggest that rhizomatic affective weaving is central to how nurs es and communities adapt to ongoing challenges and unsusta inable demands. Nurses co-create and rely on informal forms of collective support to compensate for other wise lacking formal and institutional support, rein forcing the resilience of the healthcare system in the process.  Nevertheless, this often comes at the expense of n urses’ own health and well-being, reproducing a system that thrives by depleting i ts own resources. The quote from Ellen gives a poignant description of the difficult conditions they experience as “being in war”: It's like being in war. Nurses talk about going into battle together and having each other's back because it's like a life, and that is the kind of bare life situation. And how this healthcare system is destroying nurses, and how the entire hospital care is so founded and sustained by nurses. But at the same time, you become this expense.

This account highlights the strong bonds  of solidarity and interdependence among nurses mobilized by the intense and stressful working conditions. They function as a regenerative force that not only benefits individual nurses but also supports the capacity of the healthcare system to provide quality patient care. The bonds formed through shared experiences of “being in the trenche s” together, as Ellen describes, carry emotional intensity that creates profound, complex connections that are both supportive and challenging to navigate.  At the same time, these practices carry transformative and emancipato ry potential beyond their immediate functions, cultivating seeds of resistance.

## Conclusion: Collective care, resistance, and sustainability in healthcare

We examined affective contextures that develop in a nd through collective care in the context of nursin g work. While the examples we discuss are from the healthca re context, they also h old relevance for education and sustainability more broadly. Our analyses show how,  in practice  contexts driven by neoliberal logic, c ollective caring emerges as a necessary resource to both the system and participating subjects despite often rem aining an invisible, unaccounted-for aspect of practice. Foregrounding these communal, affective forms of care not only makes visible the fabric of personal and institutional re lations that sustain current health and educational institutions but also serves as a means to politicize affectivity. For collective care practices bring about a potential for resistance against systems that officially dismiss the value of affective labor. We suggest positioning them as an integral part of the economic and material relat ions that shape and sustain the daily work of nurses today, making the politics of affective relations visible to extend the literature on sustainability.
