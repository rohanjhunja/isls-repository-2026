# “I Didn’t Drink Any Water That Day”: Marginalized Children’s Understanding of Climate Change–Induced Water Issues in NOLA

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Climate change education in the early years of schooling is often overlooked, even though children already experience its effects in their daily lives. In New Orleans, where histories of marginalization are tied to place, the impacts of climate change– related water pollution are both immediate and lived. This qualitative case study draws on a justice-oriented, critical pedagogy of place to examine how kindergarten, second-grade, and fourth-grade students made sense of water quality issues through an interdisciplinary unit focused on Lake Pontchartrain. Children’s responses ranged from personal accounts to affective reflections and critical commentaries on the challenges facing their communit ies. Findings highlight how justice-oriented, place-based climate education can help marginalized children connect disciplinary understanding with lived experience, fostering early awareness, care, and agency. Major issue addressed For children in and around New Orleans, Louisiana (NOLA), daily life is shaped by close ties to the lakes, estuaries, and wetlands that define the region’s livelihood, culture, and recreation (Colten, 2002). Yet these same connections make them and their families especially vulnerable to climate change impacts such as sea level rise, stronger hurricanes, water contamination, and flooding (Environmental Protection Agency, 2016). C limate change education in the early years has often been overlooked, even though children already encounter its effects in their everyday lives. This study responds to this gap by examining how marginalized children made sense of water quality issues in disciplinary, affective, and ethical terms through Our Water, Our Future—a project that engaged kindergarten, second-grade, and fourth-grade students in an interdisciplinary, placemaking unit centered on Lake Pontchartrain in NOLA, a city where histories of marginalization are inscribed in place and community (Fackler et al., 2026). W e ask: What types of understanding emerge for marginalized children in an interdisciplinary placemaking unit on water quality? Theoretical approach Justice-centered, critical pedagogy of place Justice-centered science pedagogy, grounded in culturally sustaining pedagogy, positions learners as transformative intellectuals by weaving together equitable learning expectations, antiracist teaching, and agency (Morales-Doyle, 2017). Central to this vision is treating marginalized children as producers of knowledge and culture who develop complexity in their thinking, commitment to their communities, and credibility as scientifically capable individuals, reimagining science education as a catalyst for both academic achievement and social transformation (Adah Miller & Fackler, 2024). This vision aligns with the critical pedagogy of place, which situates learning within local communities and issues (Gruenewald, 2003). Placemaking pedagogies emphasize experiential, multidisciplinary learning that connects disciplinary knowledge to the attributes of a place, fostering ecological literacy and care for the environment (Visintainer, 2023). Meaning making in science as stance We conceptualize meaning making as a layered process through which children interpret events, discourse, feelings, and artifacts in relation to place, knowledge, and experience. Drawing on Goodwin’s (2007) framework, we understand participation in meaning-making as stance, whereby children continually shape their roles as intellectual, moral, and social actors. These multidimensional forms of meaning-making, encompassing instrumental, epistemic, affective, moral, and cooperative stances, reflect how children embody, explore, and negotiate their understandings in interaction with others, places, and experiences.

Methodological approach As part of a larger project on place-based climate change education, this exploratory case study  (Merriam, 2007) examined the co-design and co-teaching of an interdisciplinary unit on climate change–induced water pollution. 2767 The lessons took place at an elementary school near Lake Pontchartrain, serving a racially and linguistically diverse student population, primarily multilingual newcomer s, Black, and Latine students (71.5% minority enrollment). During the week-long unit, 73 students integrated science, art, literacy (through a bilingual comic developed by the research team), social studies, and engineering to examine water pollution, connect to the local lake, identify sources of contamination, and design prototype water filters. They also drew on family and community experiences to advocate for clean water and to imagine collective action for the environment. Data sources included audio and video recordings of classroom instruction and small-group discussions captured by a Swivl  robot, student artifacts (e.g., water filter designs, models, and worksheets), end-of-unit emotion checks, and focus group interviews (n=6 groups, 10 minutes each).  We examined how children in a climate-vulnerable region constructed meaning around local water issues. The analysis was iterative and primarily deductive, adapting the analytic approach of Davis and Schaeffer (2019).  Our coding captured three dimensions of students’ talk and participation: (1) framings  of knowledge and concern (e.g., place-based knowledge and environmental fairness), (2) water-related dialogue (e.g., personal experiences and conceptions of water as a vital resource), and (3) layers of meaning-making across disciplinary, affective, moral, and agency domains.  After coding, we used constant comparison (Maxwell & Miller, 2008)  across data sources to refine and extend codes into three emergent themes characterizing students’ multi-layered meaning-making.

## Major findings

Due to space limitations, we provide a condensed summary of Theme 2. Theme 2: “Don’t put your foot in that”: Connecting lived experiences to water This theme unpacks how deeply children’s understandings of water pollution wer e intertwined with personal, family, and community experiences. When making sense of  water problems, students often drew on their encounters with local bodies of water, grounding their thinking in familiar places and family routines. While discussing what they know about Lake Pontchartrain, one kindergartener  shared, “Whenever I went to Lake Pontchartrain, we caught a fish,” situating classroom conversations within lived experience and blurring the line between learning about and living with the local environment.  During interviews, children extended these connections to intergenerational learning moments. A second grader explained, “My mom used to litter, but she stopped that because I said it’s not good… one time one of my brothers threw tons of trash in the re [lake] and I had to clean it” revealing an awareness of care and responsibility in daily life. Another described rescuing a turtle entangled in plastic near her grandmother’s home, saying, “I saw the turtle  and saved it.” These moments show how students integrated experiences of care, observation, and action into their developing understanding of water pollution. These connections also appeared in their artwork, reflecting memories and emotions tied to the lake. Building on these connections, many students shared broader experiences and concerns that shaped their understanding of water safety and contamination. A kindergartner recalled, “When we went to Lake Pontchartrain, my dad said, ‘Oh, don’t put your foot in that’” while others shared memories of hurricane-related flooding and contamination. One fourth grader explained, “[After a hurricane] we had contaminated water in our neighborhood for two weeks, so we couldn’t drink it,” and another added, “On the first day after the hurricane, I didn’t drink any water that day. The next day, we went to the store to buy some.” Such stories show how children’s understandings of water contamination were grounded in lived experiences of environmental vulnerability. emotions, and moral understandings.  This indicates  that children are capable of noticing and discussing environmental issues when learning is grounded in place  (Davis & Schaeffer, 2019; Gruenewald, 2003). Their dialogues reflected varying stances (Goodwin, 2007) as they integrated visual, experiential, and narrative evidence to explain how humans contribute to the degradation of Lake Pontchartrain. Their narratives of water, hurricanes, flooding, contaminated water, and cleanup blurred the line between knowing about  and living with the environment, highlighting how  local practices, experiences, emotions, and ethics collectively shape meaning-making. Expressions of frustration, anger, sadness, and hope, such as “It’s bad choices” and “You can always try again” revealed moral and emotional investment in community care, echoing Bang et al.’s (2013) call for science learning that addresses  community resilience.  Rather than viewing children as too young for complex environmental issues (e.g., Strife, 2012), our fi ndings highlight the potential of justice-oriented, critical pedagogy of place to cultivate awareness, responsibility, and care among marginalized children.
