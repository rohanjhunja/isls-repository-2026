# Managing Deference, Leadership, Vision, and Voice: Dilemmas From Antiracist School-University Partnerships

Managing Deference, Leadership, Vision, and Voice: Dilemmas from Antiracist School-University Partners hips Nicole Louie, University of Wisconsin–Madison, nlouie@wisc.edu Leema Berland, University of Wisconsin–Madison, leema.berland@wisc.edu Over the past two years, our university-based team and leaders at two elementary schools have partnere d to collaboratively design and instantiate antiracist s chool communities. 1 Our partnership with the first school, Eastwood, emerged from and evolved according to the ir needs, rather than following any explicit vision.2 Over time, it became clear that our implicit visions for  an antiracist school were not aligned. We approach ed the principal at the second school, Lawrence, with a firmer sense of what we were interested in working on together. She responded enthusiastically, and with a seemingly shared vision, we dove into partnership. But again, as time passed, we had difficulty holding to our vision. Wi th this poster, we aim to engage conference partici pants in discussions about how tonegotiate a shared vision in school-university partnerships, in the context of antiracist work in which competing visions and understandings are especially likely to present not only conceptua l difficulties but also ethical, interpersonal, and d eeply personal challenges. We give particular atten tion tonegotiating the role of parents of color, 3 who are increasingly seen as essential players in antiracist school transformation but whom schools are rarely equipped to authentically engage as partners (Ishimaru, 2020). A participatory approach to building antiracist school communities Our approach to building antiracist school communit ies is rooted in two bodies of thought. First is sc holarship emphasizing the cultural wealth, knowledge, and cap ital in communities of color (e.g., Yosso, 2005). S tudents and parents of color have intimate understandings of how “the school system is marginalizing, criminalizing, and otherwise failing our Black and Brown kids” (Oluo, 2019, p. 124). These understandings uniquely position them to resist oppressive systems with determination, jo y, and hope, and to radically reimagine just and hu manizing schooling. Their voices and perspectives are thus indispensable for efforts to advance antiracism in education. We complement this asset-based, anti-deficit perspe ctive on students and parents of color with scholarship outlining participatory methods for stu dying and enacting educational change toward justic e (e.g., Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). This scholarship emphasizes the importance of dismantling typical hierarchies among students, parents, teachers, school administrators, and university researchers. Participants must learn to coordinate their varied knowledge, practices, perspectives, and goals to forge new ways of thinking, doing, and being. These new ways must support participants to learn with and from one another, across differences, rejecting t he dehumanizing “racialized institutional scripts” that denigrate the knowledge, authority, and power of students and parents of color (Ishimaru, 2020, p. 121).

Taking a participatory approach to partnership impl ies that no party, including university researchers, should enter collaborative work seeking to impose t heir vision on others. We have instead attempted to  build a shared vision with our partners. While we have found easy agreement around the goal of building antiracist school communities, what such communities are has proven to be slippery and abstract, and our efforts to collaboratively establish a more concrete vision—particularly with school principals, who have mediated our access to students, parents, and teachers—have been unsuccessful, as we describe below. Eastwood: general agreement but misalignment on specifics The majority of Eastwood’s students are students of color. The staff, meanwhile, is 90% white. In the spring of 2019, Eastwood teachers and administrators asked for our support in their efforts to become an antiracist school community. That summer, members of our team led a s ix-day retreat, during which they pushed Eastwood principal Amy and other school staff (who attended on a voluntary basis) to acknowledge their own raci st assumptions and to change rules for behavior in the  school’s hallways, which made the school feel pris on-like. The following school year, Amy invited us to lead portions of the monthly meetings of the school-based leadership team (SBLT). We agreed, expecting to use that time to develop a shared vision of an antiracist school and pursue concrete plans for enacting that vision, with parents as full partners. But, as we worked together, it became clear that Amy did not share our expectations. For example, meeting time was often filled with Amy’s priorities, which were shaped by district requests and mandates (e.g., the looming deadline for the mandatory school improvement plan). Furthermore, as the partnership continued, w e realized that although we had come together aroun d the general goal of building an antiracist learning com munity at Eastwood, we had specific misalignments w hich reflected different underlying conceptions of what an antiracist learning community was and how to build it. For example, the kind of collaboration with parents that we came to articulate as central to our work did not align with ICLS2022 Proceedings 2008 © ISLS Amy’s understanding of antiracist change, although she had been able to honestly agree with our initia l vague statements about parents as partners. In particular, she seemed to prioritize what she called the “inn er work” of “looking in the mirror and saying, I’m racist.” Her understanding of this work did not seem to require interaction with families as much as internal reflection. Our p oint here is not to evaluate the merits of focusing  on “inner work,” but to observe that this focus got in the way of working with parents of color as partners in a meaningful way to change school policies, relationships, or instruction. Lawrence: waiting to be “ready” Lawrence was demographically similar to Eastwood, e xcept for being significantly smaller. We began our partnership at Lawrence with some of the lessons we had learned at Eastwood in mind—namely, a need to begin with an explicit discussion of vision and the role of parents. This discussion was optimistic; as a sm all neighborhood school with most families living just a short walk away, Lawrence was well-positioned to bring parents in as partners. Further, principal Nadine made routine home visits, showing that she valued relationships with parents and families; she also identified deve loping stronger relationships between teachers and parents as an important area for growth, noting that many teachers were afraid of contacting parents and that these fears were likely racialized (given that most Lawrence teachers were white while most parents were not). But in subsequent months, each time we raised the idea of inviting parents to our meetings with faculty and staff, Nadine argued that faculty and staff weren’t “ready.” This may have be en in part because of concerns we had discussed abo ut the possible harm of bringing parents in only to have t heir experiences and concerns belittled (although some team members had also argued that parents of color were used to having that happen, and that we should manage this possibility in the moment rather than allow it to prevent us from engaging with parents and caregivers). Another concern Nadine repeatedly raised was that faculty and staff needed to work on their relationships with one another first, because if they did not feel comfortable having critical conversations amongst themselves, they could not be expected to have such conversations with parents. As the pandemic deepened and with it, teachers’ isolation, this concern became increasingly salient for Nadine. Again, our purpose is not to evaluate its validity, but to note that it stopped us from bringing parents and caregivers into our partnership for more than a year.

## Discussion and implications

At both Eastwood and Lawrence, the university team struggled to balance deference and leadership. On the one hand, we recognized that Amy, Nadine, and their col leagues were in their schools every day and had int imate knowledge of their students and staff. Consistent w ith our principles for collaboration, we wished to honor this knowledge. On the other hand, these same principles required us to press our partners to bring parents to the table, honoring their voices and building on their knowledge. This was not necessarily a problem; we saw pushing our school partners beyond their comfort zones as part of our role, and our partners valued that. However,  we have spent more than a year at each school and still hav e yet to succeed in engaging parents in anything mo re than preliminary conversations about antiracist change at their children’s schools. Building knowledge about how to navigate the challe nges we have described is absolutely essential if efforts to study and effect educational change are ever to center the voices and perspectives of paren ts of color, as participatory approaches to research and design suggest. More generally, better understanding how t o democratically negotiate a shared vision is essential to many kinds of partnership in education. We look forward to discussing these issues with conference participants.
