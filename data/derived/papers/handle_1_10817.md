# Potentials for Complementing Classroom Ecological Investigations with Professional Data

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In science classrooms, students need firsthand experiences conducting investigations: pose questions, plan empirical activities, collect and analyze data to support knowledge claims. These experiences make visible the work of aligning phenomena, empirical systems, data, and explanations. But classroom investigations are constrained by school resources and organizations, leaving some student questions unanswerable or unresolved through firsthand investigations. This paper explores how classroom ecological investigations can be complemented by students’ exploration and analysis of open-access professional data.

## Introduction

Students need firsthand experiences in planning and conducting scientific investigations, as these experiences make visible the multifaceted work involved in aligning phenomena, empirical systems, data, and explanations (Manz et al., 2020). Despite the necessity of  firsthand classroom investigations, their scope is constrained by schools’ resources and organizational structures. For example, the duration of student investigations  is often limited by the timeframe of a curricular unit. Off-site investigations such as ecological field survey s are often confined to specific windows  according to academic calendar (e.g., after mandate d accountability testing) and concerns about student safety (e.g., avoiding months with frequent hazardous weather). These constraints may render some key questions students pose unanswerable or unresolved through firsthand investigations. This paper proposes and presents some preliminary findings on how open-access professional data can complement firsthand classroom ecological investigations.

Previous efforts to incorporate professional data in science classrooms often prioritize using such data to demonstrate curricular concepts. For example, Hug and McNeil (2 008) examined how students analyzed data collected by scientists to make claims and predictions about differential survival in the contexts of sexual selection and natural selection. They found that students treated secondhand professional data differently—focusing more on identifying patterns in data, drawing conclusion s from data, and making connections to content know ledge— compared to their approach with  their own  firsthand data —focusing more  on considering data limitations. In ecology education, efforts to incorporate professional data  have largely prioritized teaching quantitative skills needed in analyzing ecological data. This is often done by providing “data nuggets” (Schultheis & Kjelvik, 2015) or “teaching modules” (Klug et al., 2017) where students read ab out scientists’ research projects and questions and analyzed data collected by the scientists  to address the scientists’ research questions. T hese prior works provide valuable evidence that students can benefit from analyzing professional da ta. This paper contributes by exploring how students may use professional data to answer questi ons they themselves pose and t o extend their own investigations.

## Research context and instructional design

This exploratory work was part of a design-based classroom research project that investigated the development of middle-school students’ understanding and practice of mode ling in the context of data-rich citizen-science ecological investigations. In a focal 7th-grade classroom, students participated in two citizen-science projects, one focused on intertidal ecosystems and the other  on terrestrial temporary-pond ecosystems. Both projects engaged students in survey efforts aimed at monitoring whether and how the relative abundance and distribution of species might change  over time  in response to climate change.  I n each of these ecosystem investigation s, students constructed and employed multiple types of mode ls to support their inquiry, including physical analog models (Dickes et al., 2022) and chance models (Wisittanawat et al., 2022). For example, students constructed and used chance models to account for sampling variability in observed sex ratios of invasive crabs. Students designed and assembled microcosms to investigate animal behaviors (e.g., food prefere nce, refuge), and they also constructed and used chance models to account for sampling variability in their observed microcosm outcomes. As field and microcosm investigations were underway  in the classroom, I started to note questions that students posed with high frequency, but we were unable support firsthand investigations. For example, given the curricular emphasis on ecosystem responses to climate change, students oft en posed various questions about whether and how rising temperature would affect org anisms and their habitats. Students often proposed microcosm experiments that involved varying and controlling microcosm temperatures, but we lacked affordable means to do so at a meaningful range.  I also started to note questions that students were investigating in their microcosms but might require more than available time or resou rces to replicate or redesign to resolve emergent material and conceptual issues.  I started curated professional data that students c ould analyze to address these questions. Because of the exploratory nature of thi s component of the work, we did not formally introd uce professional data systematically  to all students. Instead, I sought opportunities to introduce professional data as opportunities arose, for example, when some student teams finished their group work before the rest of the class. Data sources Data sources include d video recordings of classroom interactions and stud ent work artifacts. I identif ied video episodes where students explored and analyzed profe ssional data. I reviewed these episodes to determine how students related professional data to their own inv estigations as well as to identify potentials for future use of professional data.

For this paper, I focused on one group of students who designed and carried out microcosm investigations to study how larval density influenced tadpole grow th. Students used six same-size plastic jars, each housing either five or ten tadpoles, representing low and h igh larval density. Each day, all tadpoles in each tank were weighed, and each tank was fed with the amount of f ood proportional to the number of tadpoles.  After ten days, students created a display of their data in CODAP (a web-based data analysis platform) to visualize growth over time (Figure 1, left). I chose this focal case because, though still very brief, students spe nt more time than others exploring and analyzing professional data.  Students were provided with two professional data s ets. One set (Murray, 1990) involved a very similar set up, with tanks of low (one tadpole) and  high (eight tadpoles) larval density. One difference was that in this study, different replicates were fed different amount of food. Another data set (Le Sage, 2021)  included data of 1,017 tadpoles collected from 13 different pools. Tadpoles from each pool were divided into two groups: low density (about 1 tadpole per 2.5 L) and high density (about 1 tadpole per 1 L). Once a tadpole grew to almost a frog (that is, when its tail was < 2 mm long), the tadpole was weighed (in grams).

## Preliminary findings

How students used professional data To visualize tadpole growth, in CODAP, students cre ated a display that showed “mass per tadpole” over time. (See Figure 1, left.) Students highlighted different data points for each “density” conditions in different color and used a tool in CODAP to draw a trend line through e ach condition. From this data display, students con cluded that tadpoles in the low-density condition (5 tadpoles per tank) grew more t han the high density condition (10 tadpoles per tank). Students noted that they started at a similar mass per tadpole (gesturing fingers closed in) and grew apart (gesturing as increasing gap between fingers as tracing graph over time). Figure 1 Student-Generated Data Displays of their Own Data (left) and of Professional Data (middle and right) After students analyzed their own data, students were given the first professional data set. They created a similar data display of tadpole mass over time separated by high-and low-density conditions (Figure 1, middle). Unexpectedly, students found the trend to be reverse: tadpoles in high-density condition grew faster than those in low-density condition. One group member exclaimed that they (the students) must have done something wrong. Next, students analyzed another professional data s et containing over 1,000 of tadpoles, some raised in a high-density condition and others in low-density condition. This data set did not contain re peated measure of tadpole mass over time, but only one mass measure p er tadpole. To examine tadpole mass in different de nsity conditions, students created a display of tadpole m ass separate by conditions (Figure 1, right). Havin g done this, students exclaimed that they found the same results. Asked how they made  that conclusion, they explained that because the display showed that tadpoles in low-density condition grew more than those in the high-density condition, noting that the mean tadpole mass was almost twice as much in the low-density condition. Potentials for further use of professional data In this episode, students found that one professional data set contradicted their findings, and another professional data set was consistent with their findings. As stu dents only briefly interacted with these profession al data, it remained unclear how students might resolve the con tradictions between their data and professional dat a as well as between professional data. It was of note that s tudents immediately zeroed in on the attribute that they also used in their investigations (i.e., mass). This meant students did not explore other attributes that could help them reason about potential explanations for conflicting data. For example, the first data set included an attribute named “food amount,” with the description, “Each tank was fed a different amount of food. The baseline food amount is a = 0.005 g.” Separating data by “food amount” (Figure 2, right) would reveal that results were different in low-food and high-food conditions. In a low-food condition, tadpoles in low-density condition grew faster. On th e contrary, a high-food condition, tadpoles in high-density condition grew faster. Students had earlier  made the observation that they were no  left-over foods in the tanks when they tended the tanks each following  day, suggesting that their microcosms were more similar to the low-food condition in the professional data set. This could help students reconcile the contradiction as well as expanding their explanations to include an attribute they had not considered before.

## Discussion and next steps

Consistent with previous research, this paper presents evidence that students can benefit from an alyzing professional data. Here, students used professional data to compare with their own microcosm findings. The case presented here highlighted both promises and caveat s in incorporating professional d ata in classrooms. Hug and McNeil (2008) found that students did not assume a critical stance to professional data, but rather seemed to view it as authoritative.  In the case presented, s tudents assumed they did something wrong when profe ssional data contradicted their data. On the other hand, students were cheerful when professional data were consistent with their data. This suggests that multiple sources of professional data may be necessary to discourage vi ewing a single professional data set as a definitive source. Other questions remain about what data set characteristics help students perceive professional data as relevant to and potentially useful for their own investigations. In a way, the case presented here represented a best-case for second-hand data foraging. The professional data sets were of the same focal species and included same or very similar measures students also used in their own investigations. In other cases, available professional data were sometimes of different species or included different construc ts and measures than the ones students used.  I did not have  the opportunity to observe whether students would view these “adjacent” data sets as relevant and useful for the ir own investigations. Better understanding student perspectives on these issues will help with data cu ration efforts in the future. As open-access professional data, particularly ecological data, are becoming increasingly available, I hope to engage the community in a discussion about how these data can complement classroom scien tific investigations and the forms of support stude nts and teachers would need to meaningfully repurpose these data to further their own investigations.
