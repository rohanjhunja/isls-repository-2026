# (How) Do Children 3–6 Experience and Express Meaning in Life?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper discusses early findings of an ongoing study investigating whether and how children aged 3–6 experience and articulate a sense of meaning in life (MIL). The tripartite model of MIL—comprising significance/mattering, purpose/direction, and coherence/comprehension—was operationalized into three questions guiding a task where children documented what they found important in life using tablet computers. The children created photos, videos, recordings, and drawings, which were used as prompts for qualitative interviews. Preliminary findings suggest that young children have a clear sense of their lives as significant to themselves and, to some extent, as mattering to others. Their sense of purpose and coherence, however, is largely limited to short timeframes. Understanding MIL development in childhood is vital for education aimed at fostering meaningfulness in young lives. Educating for meaningful living also holds promise for promoting a sustainable lifestyle.

## Introduction

In its strategy for 2030, the Finnish Ministry of Education and Culture (2019) states as one of its three aims that “the conditions for a meaningful life are ensured equally for everyone” (p. 10). Though largely absent from the strategy, there are good reasons for emphasizing meaningfulness as a goal of  education. First, life’s meaning is central to the nature and justification of education, especially compulsory education. For “whatever we make central to education, we presuppose to be central to the life for which the education is a preparation” (Allen, 1991, 51). Most people do view a meaningful life as objectively good and worth pursuing, and many philosophers of education have argued that guiding students toward building a meaningful life should be seen as a central goal of education (e.g., De Ruyter & Schinkel, 2022; White, 2009). Second, a vast number of studies show a strong connection between the subjective experience of life's meaningfulness and physical and mental well-being (Steger, 2017). Since education should encourage a healthy lifestyle, this is another reason to promote meaningful living. Third, the emphasis on a meaningful life can function as a critique of materialist and neoliberal ideals of a successful life which are a major cause of the global ecological crises. As Salonen et al. (2023) argue, “the current political strategy for a sustainable future that emphasizes economic and technological progress is insufficient to bring about the change required,” and thus “an educational approach based on identities, values, ethics, and new worldviews is alsoneeded” (p. 615). By focusing on attractive visions of a good and meaningful life and not merely on the necessity of decreasing our living standards, educating for meaningful living could effectively foster a sustainable lifestyle in children and youth.

Educating students to pursue a meaningful life requires research on how the subjective experience and understanding of meaning in life (MIL)  develops in childhood and youth. While most empirical studies on MIL have focused on adults and adolescents (King & Hicks, 2021), a few studies present evidence that also elementary school children can experience meaningfulness (Taylor & Ebersole, 1993; Shoshani & Russo-Netzer, 2017; Kim et al., 2010; Kang, 2017). Shoshani (2024) studied even younger children: Israeli preschoolers aged 3-6 were asked about (1) what things (2) what activities (3) what things “ that exist in the world ” (4) and what “ thoughts and things you believe in” they would like to do/have with them on a desert island. She argues that her “findings indicated that preschool children are able to articulate their sense of meaning in life” (Shoshani, 2024, p. 581).

The study also found that MIL increased with age and was partly mediated by Theory-of-Mind development. This supports the claim that MIL begins developing in early childhood when children gain a better grasp of themselves, other people, and their relatedness to the world (Rong-Wei & Dan, 2020). Researching young children’s experience and understanding of MIL is important  because much of the scholarship implies that only cognitively healthy and mature adults and adolescents can experience MIL and thus to live meaningful lives  (Segersvärd et al., in press). According to most philosophers, MIL requires “a certain degree of self-awareness, intelligence, and agency” (Metz, 2022, p. 30). While arguably most humans develop self-awareness and agency from an early age, stressing the importance of cognitive abilities easily leads to excluding young children (as well as cognitively disabled individuals) from the realm of meaningful living. Studying the experience of MIL in young children is a difficult task, however, since the concept of life’s meaning is unfamiliar to many of them. To overcome this problem, researchers have used questions like “in your whole life what is most important to you?” (Taylor & Ebersole, 1993). But while replacing the word “meaningful” ICLS 2025 Proceedings 1584 © ISLS with a term such as “important” seems necessary when studying children, it is unclear what (if anything) answers to such questions reveal about children’s sense  of MIL. For example, many participants in Shoshani’s study mentioned their favorite sweets and “dolls, superhero characters, toys, and personal objects such as blankets, bottles, or teddy bears” as things they wanted to have with them on a desert island (Shoshani, 2024, p. 589). While it might be argued that such concrete objects can serve as sources of meaning for young humans, children’s lists of the things they like and enjoy are not strong evidence of the presence of MIL in young children.

## Theoretical background: The tripartite theory of meaning in life

None of the previous studies of MIL in young children employ a theory known as the tripartite model of MIL (George & Park, 2016; Martela & Steger, 2016). As the most robust model of MIL conceptually and empirically, it has ga rnered wide support among psychologists and philosophers, even if not all scholars  define every dimension similarly (Martela & Steger, 2023), and some include a fourth dimension. According to the tripartite model, MIL involves three dimensions: significance (or mattering), purpose (or direction), and coherence (or comprehension). Significance (Martela & Steger, 2016) or mattering (George & Park, 2016) is an affective experience that revolves around the perceived value, importance, and worthwhileness of one's existence and actions. MIL scholars have debated whether significance and mattering are one dimension, two dimensions, or two sides of one dimension (Martela & Steger, 2023). While mattering is defined as making difference to others or to the world (George & Park, 2016; Costin & Vignoles, 2020), Martela and Steger’s (2023) notion of significance focuses on “the subjective sense of life’s inherent value to the individual living that life” (p. 607). Purpose is a motivational dimension that can be defined as “ the extent to which individuals experience life as being directed and motivated by valued life goals” (George & Park, 2016, 206). When people have a strong sense of purpose, they feel motivated and driven by valued life goals, which align with their core values and identity. Coherence is a cognitive dimension that has to do with making sense of the world and one’s experiences and perceiving life as organized and predictable (Heintzelman & King, 2014). How might these three dimensions manifest in young children? Significance may be spelled out as one’s sense that “my existence is good for me”, while mattering is the sense that “my existence is also good for others”. The cognitive skills needed for such experiences develop early and their articulation is easy. Purpose or direction can be broken down into more immediate goals, long term purposes, and an overarching life mission (Hanson & Vanderweele, 2021). While young children typically lack long-term purposes and a life mission, they often have short-term goals as well as hopes about  their more distant future. C oherence in adulthood is supported by a life narrative that integrates one’s past, present, and future into an orderly whole (McAdams, 2018). However, taking a narrative approach to coherence may be potentially useful also with children. Most children are familiar with stories, and thus some of them may view their lives in narrative terms. The tripartite model seems fruitful for studying MIL in children, since measures of MIL designed for adults often employ concepts unfamiliar to many children. While this pertains also to existing measures of the tripartite MIL (e.g., Hanson & Vanderweele, 2021), the separation of the three dimensions allows operationalizing each subconstruct into a question that children can understand and that together capture the unique features of the MIL construct.

## Materials and method

In an ongoing research project Child in Time – Existential Resilience in Early Childhood (2023– 2027), the development of children’s experience of MIL is  researched in three phases in 2024, 2025, and 2026.  Research permits were obtained from local authorities, the guardians, and the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Helsinki. The study followed the ethical guidelines of The Declaration of Helsinki and the Finnish National Board on Research Integrity (TENK, 2023). The directors of daycare centers were approached through email, and guardians were informed about the study and invited to enroll their children in the study either via email or in  a parents’ evening. Children's willingness to participate was assessed through their verbal and non-verbal cues before the interviews took place. Three children were excluded after they showed unwillingness to be interviewed. The first data gathering took place in 2024 in two daycare centers in the Finnish capital region. Children (N=30) aged 3–6 were first provided with a tablet computer and given verbal instructions on using it. The children were told they could take the tablet home with them and that for one week they could take pictures, record videos, create drawings, and leave audio messages using the tablet (the use of other applications was restricted). The main question they were asked to document was “ What is important to you in life?” A s imilar question has been used before in researching MIL  in young children (Taylor & Ebersole, 1993; Kang, 2017). The children's guardians were provided with a list of the main question along with seven sub-questions, which they were asked to remind the children about. These seven questions included ones operationalizing the three MIL constructs: ICLS 2025 Proceedings 1585 © ISLS

## 1. What is important in you and in that you exist? (significance/mattering)

2. What wishes you have for life? (purpose/direction)

3. What kind of a story could be told about your life? (coherence/comprehension)

The questions were designed to discover whether the children’s answers would show signs of some or all of the three dimensions of MIL and thus serve as evidence of the early development of  MIL. The purpose of the openended formulations was to encourage storytelling, avoid suggestibility, and  discourage short answers such as “yes” or “no”. The questions were also specific enough to avoid responses that had little to do with MIL, as vague responses have arguably been a problem in previous studies. While the questions were believed to be challenging especially for the participants aged 3–4, their inclusion was seen as  important for investigating the age when children become able to experience and articulate a sense of MIL. After around one week the children returned their tablet computers to the daycare center. Qualitative interviews were then conducted to discuss the photos, videos, drawings, and recordings. The interviews were loosely structured around the main question and the seven sub-questions, allowing for follow-up questions and adjustments as the conversation naturally unfolded. At first, the children were asked which entries containing elements important to them they would like to tell more about. This way we attempted to avoid discussing entries such as experimental photos that did not include items of importance. The length of the interviews varied from 5 to 45 minutes, the typical discussion being around 20-30 minutes. The interviews were filmed on video camera.

## Significance/mattering

Many children’s responses to the question “what is important in you and in that you exist?” suggested  a sense of significance (a sense that “my existence is good for me”). As in previous studies (Shoshani, 2024; Kang et al., 2017; Pardon et al., in press), many children considered family, friends, pets, toys, and their favorite food as very important to them (Launonen et al., forthcoming).

Interestingly, few children answered the question clearly in terms  of mattering (suggesting that “my existence is good for others”). A few children expressed a desire and the ability to help others when they need it, and responses like this were made in context of other questions  (Launonen et al., forthcoming). Some responses led us to consider the relationship of mattering and the children’s sense of agency. Some expressed the question by sharing ways they can influence their everyday situations and surroundings for the better if they wanted to. Moreover, some scholars define mattering in a way that does not require intentional action or a difference-making goal. According to George and Park (2016), mattering “conveys the degree to which individuals feel that their existence is of significance, importance, and value in the world” (p. 206). What is central, then, is that the person in question somehow matters to others or makes a difference in their  lives. From this perspective, many of the children’s responses that conveyed the experience of being loved and valued by others, like their family and friends, can be seen as involving a sense of mattering (Launonen et al., forthcoming). Purpose/direction When asked about what wishes they had for life, very few children immediately responded by describing their goals and plans for their future. However, when asked specifically about what they want to be or what they want their life to look like when they grow up, some did have ideas about what they would want to have (e.g., a family, a home, children, pets) or what they want to accomplish in adulthood (Launonen et al., forthcoming). A few children, for example, wanted to make their hobbies – or whatever they enjoyed doing – into their professions. Importantly, some of them were practicing weekly for becoming better in hobbies like figure skating so that one day they might be come experts (Launonen et al., forthcoming). Given Hanson and Vanderweele’s

(2021) understanding of direction as consisting of goals, purposes, and mission, we might say that many children

at least had goals, but some also had rudimentary life purposes. Like one m ight expect, most children’s wishes for life were largely self-centered (Launonen et al., forthcoming). While studies suggest that other-centered or “beyond-the-self” life purposes typically develop in adolescence or adulthood if at all (Viljanen & Kuusisto, 2024), such purposes were present in a few of the children’s interviews.

Coherence/comprehension Many children had difficulties answering the question “what kind of a story could be told about your life?” (Launonen et al., forthcoming). A few explicitly said they would have to think more about it. Some also seemed ICLS 2025 Proceedings 1586 © ISLS to misunderstand the question as a request for a fictional story about their lives rather than a realistic depiction of their past, present, and possible future. The answers also sometimes seemed to reflect stories they had heard from books or TV programs.

Grasping the question correctly may have been difficult partly because it relied on McAdams’ (2018) theory of narrative identity, according to which individuals seek to interpret their past, present, and future as a consistent story (Launonen et al., forthcoming). While McAdams (2013) argues that narrative identity develops in the age of 15–25, in our project we wanted to see if young children are able to think about their lives as a story, as story is a familiar concept to most children. However, in hindsight, it  seems unsurprising that children understood the question incorrectly and had trouble answering it. In upcoming data gatherings, we plan to explain this question in more understandable terms.

Nonetheless, the notion of coherence was arguably apparent in some responses to other sub-questions. One child, for example, described her evening routines as a structured set of small events starting with the brushing of teeth and ending with “a blessing” from her mother (Launonen et al., forthcoming). Such responses exemplify what Sullivan et al. (2013) call everyday meaning or “the attempt to structure our social and natural environments into a series of dependable, recursive patterns and expected relationships, so that we may effectively engage in short-term goal-directed action within those environments” (Sullivan et al., 2013, p. 18). Such structuring of everyday meaning and coherence is arguably present from early childhood. After all, seeking coherence and interpreting our environment as a structured and meaningful whole is a n automatic and adaptive function of the human mind that facilitates prediction and behavior (Heinzelman & King, 2014). For young children coherence is built largely through schedules and routines and the predictability of everyday life.

## Discussion and implications

Preliminary findings suggest that children aged 3–6 are able to experience and articulate  their lives as having significance to themselves and, to some extent, as mattering to others  (Launonen et al., forthcoming). A mature sense of purpose in life and a sense of coherence that permeates one’s past, present, and future are likely to develop later in adolescence and adulthood. However, the findings also suggest that young children have short-term goals and hopes about their  future as well as  an everyday sense of coherence based on routine and predictability. As children grow into adolescents, purpose and coherence are likely to deepen and gradually encompass increasingly longer periods of time. But e ven if young children lack purposes and coherence in any robust sense, their experience of significance and mattering is arguably mature enough to justify claims about a partial sense of MIL in young children, especially if significance/mattering is seen as the most central  dimension of MIL (Costin & Vignoles, 2020).

This study has several limitations. For example, some children understood the questions differently from the researchers’ intentions. The guardians’ preferences of when and where the children were allowed to use the tablet influenced data collection, and their activity in remind ing the children of the questions influenced how much data was built around the questions and how much the children had thought about them before the interview. The findings have interesting theoretical and practical implications. Theoretically, the notion that a sense of significance/mattering develops first and is followed by purpose and coherence is  in line with McAdams’

(2013) theory of the development of “psychological self”. He argues that during their first years of life humans

become “social actors”, around the age of 7 –9 they also become “motivated agents”, and finally in adolescence they grow into “autobiographical authors”. These developmental stages overlap with the dimensions of significance/mattering, purpose/direction, and coherence/comprehension, respectively. In practical terms, t o foster the sense of MIL in young children, early childhood educators  should first focus on promoting a sense of significance and mattering. Importantly, a sense of mattering is also a psychological basis for beyond-the-self concerns and sustainable living. As De Ruyter and Schinkel (2022) point out, children may see incoherence in education which i s said to prepare them for the future, but which in reality pays insufficient attention to climate change that  overshadows that future. Interestingly, movements such as the School strike for climate may foster a deep sense of mattering. Greta Thunberg surely felt that her actions matter when she started her protests.
