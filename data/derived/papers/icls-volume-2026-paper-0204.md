# When Exploration Precedes Instruction: The Roles of Curiosity and Metacognition in Productive Failure

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Exploratory learning—solving problems before receiving instruction—can promote increased conceptual understanding by encouraging learners to generate and test ideas. The present study investigated how exploratory learning influences curiosity, metacogniti ve engagement, and conceptual understanding. Undergraduate participants (N = 149) learned about standard deviation in either an explore-first or instruct-first condition. Explore-first learners demonstrated higher inference-based understanding and reported greater monitoring than instruct-first learners. Curiosity did not differ by condition but moderated the relationship between instruction order and monitoring, indicating that curiosity amplifies metacognitive engagement when present. However, a moderated mediation analysis did not support the hypothesized sequential pathway from exploration to monitoring to debugging to understanding. These findings suggest that curiosity and metacognition jointly —but independently—contribute to the benefits of exploration. Implications for instructional design highlight the value of incorporating exploratory tasks that elicit productive uncertainty while supporting learners’ curiosity and self-regulation.

Exploratory learning before instruction is a constructivist-based instructional approach that reverses the traditional “instruction-then-practice” model by presenting learners with novel or ill-defined problems prior to receiving formal explanation (DeCaro  & Rittle-Johnson, 2012). Learners are encouraged to generate and test hypotheses using prior knowledge, identify inconsistencies in their reasoning, and construct preliminary solution attempts. These exploratory attempts —though often incorrect —serve as a foundation for later understanding, a process known as productive failure (Kapur, 2010; Kapur & Rummel, 2012). A large body of research shows that such initial exploration can enhance conceptual understanding—understanding of concepts, components, and relations (DeCaro & Rittle-Johnson, 2012)—and transfer, as learners are better prepared to integrate new information when instruction follows (Loibl & Rummel, 2014; Schwartz et al., 2011)  with studies in mathematics education specifically demonstrating improved conceptual understanding through exploratory learning and productive failure designs (Kapur, 2012; Kapur, 2014; Velic & DeCaro, 2025). Beyond the effects on conceptual learning, exploratory learning may also shape learners’ motivational and metacognitive experiences. When faced with novel or ill-defined problem-solving, learners often encounter an impasse that exposes gaps in their current knowledge (Lamnina & Chase, 2019). This realization can spark curiosity, which may in turn motivate individuals to seek information to resolve uncertainty (Loibl & Rummel, 2014). The recognition of what one does not know is therefore not merely a cognitive event but a motivational one, prompting deeper engagement and persistence in the learning process (Lamnina & Chase, 2019; Rotgans & Schmidt, 2014).

Curiosity has received growing attention as a critical factor in learning because it influences how learners allocate attention, select strategies, and regulate effort (Grossnickle, 2016). In exploratory contexts, curiosity may serve as a bridge between un certainty and engagement: when learners experience uncertainty about how to proceed, curiosity motivates active exploration to resolve uncertainty (DeCaro et al., 2025; Glogger-frey et al., 2015). As a result, curiosity may contribute to metacognitive enga gement—particularly in how learners monitor their progress, evaluate understanding, and adapt strategies in real time (Schraw & Dennison, 1994). Indeed, curiosity and metacognition are often intertwined, with curiosity initiating exploration and metacognition sustaining it through monitoring and regulation (Goupil & Proust, 2023). Finally, exploratory learning provides a valuable opportunity to investigate how curiosity interacts with key metacognitive processes. Learners who explore before instruction must plan approaches, manage information, monitor understanding, and debug errors  without explicit guidance (Schraw & Dennison, 1994). Curiosity may amplify these behaviors by motivating learners to close knowledge gaps and refine their thinking. Yet, empirical work has rarely examined curiosity and metacognitive engagement together in exploratory contexts. Identifying how exploratory learning influences these factors can inform theories of self-regulated learning and the design of environments that foster both motivation and understanding. 1502 Purpose The present study examined how exploratory learning influences learners’ curiosity, metacognitive engagement, and conceptual understanding. Participants engaged in a learning activity designed to introduce the concept of standard deviation through either an explore-first condition—where learners attempted to solve a problem before instruction—or an instruct-first condition—where learners received instruction before problem solving. We hypothesized that learners in the explore-first condition would report higher curiosity than those in the instruct-first condition  (Loibl & Rummel, 2014; Lamnina & Chase, 2019). We further predicted that heightened curiosity would coincide with greater metacognitive engagement, particularly in monitoring and debugging strategies (Schraw & Dennison, 1994). Finally, consistent with prior research on productive failure, we expected explore-first learners to show stronger conceptual understanding on inference-based assessments relative to instruct-first learners (Kapur, 2010; Schwartz et al., 2011). Participants (N = 149) were undergraduate introductory psychology students who were recruited in exchange for credit in their course. Participants were randomly assigned to the instruct-first (n = 72) or explore-first (n = 77) condition based on the version of the packet they received; packets were pre-randomized and interleaved prior to distribution.

Participants engaged in a learning activity on standard deviation adapted from Wiedmann et al. (2015) and Newman and DeCaro (2019). The task introduced a scenario involving two tea growers whose antioxidant levels were tracked across four years. Participants in the explore-first condition were asked  to come up with as many methods as possible to determine the most consistent tea grower. In the instruct-first condition, participants were simply asked to determine the most consistent tea grower. Participants in both conditions received identical instruction delivered through a text passage, worked example, and practice questions (Newman & DeCaro, 2019). The passage introduced the concept of consistency with a worked example detailing each step of the formula with a conceptual explanation of each step. At the end of the instruction, participants were asked to complete a practice question related to the worked example and answer a few questions comparing the worked example and the practice question. Participants completed surveys measuring curiosity (Naylor, 1981; I felt like seeking information about what I worked on), monitoring (Schraw & Dennison, 1994; I asked myself questions about how well I was doing during the learning activity), and debugging (Schraw & Dennison, 1994; I reevaluated my assumptions when I was confused). Additional measures were administered but are not reported here. Participants then completed a 10-item posttest measuring their conceptual understanding of standard deviation. Seven items measured scoring on items directly taught during instruction—taught concepts —while three items measured scoring on items that required learners to draw conclusions about ideas not presented during the instruction—inferenc e concepts. Scores for each were averaged and converted to percentages. Thus, the instruct-first condition first read the instructional passage and attempted the worked example, followed by the learning activity, survey, and posttest. The explore-first condition reversed the order of learning activity and instruction while all other activities are unchanged. All data was checked for assumptions prior to analysis. A series of ANOVAs examined differences in learning outcomes, curiosity, and metacognitive awareness between conditions. Learners in the explore-first condition scored higher on inference concepts than those in the instruct-first condition, F(1, 147) = 4.42, p =.037, ηp² =.03, while there was no difference in taught concepts, F (1, 147) = 0.82, p  =.366, η p² =.01. Curiosity was also no different between the instruct-first and explore first condition, F (1, 147) = 3.09, p =.081, ηp² =.02. Monitoring, however, was significantly higher in the explore-first condition F(1, 147) = 6.62, p =.011, ηp² =.04. Debugging did not follow the same trend, indicating no difference between conditions, F (1, 147) = 0.39, p =.532, ηp² =.00. Effect sizes for the significant outcomes —namely inference concepts and monitoring—were small-to-moderate. Although modest, these effects are consistent with brief instructional interventions and suggest meaningful improvements in conceptual understanding and metacognitive engagement. Moderated mediation analyses were conducted to analyze the pathway by which exploratory learning improves conceptual understanding. The moderated-mediation models were developed based on theorized relationships among instructional type, curiosity, metacognitive awareness, and inference concepts. Manipulated (causal) variables for instruction precede downstream metacognitive variables (e.g., monitoring, debugging; cf. 1503 Hayes, 2018), but curiosity may moderate (or accentuate) metacognitive variables, promoting inference of concepts. General predictions were that exploratory learning (instruction: X: INSTRUCT) increases inference concepts (Y: INFER CONCEPTS), by increasing  metacognitive engagement through monitoring (M1: MONITOR) which in turn promotes debugging (M2: DEBUG). Therefore, change in inference concepts scores caused by instruction (i.e., instruct-first vs. explore-first) should be mediated by corresponding changes in monitoring and debugging. However, conditional pathways may be moderated by curiosity (W: CURIOSITY). Descriptive statistics and correlations for these primary variables appear in Table 1. Consistent with prior work, learners who explored before instruction demonstrated higher inference-based understanding, suggesting that generating solutions before receiving explanations can improve conceptual understanding and transfer (Kapur, 2010; Schwartz et al., 2011). Exploration also promoted greater metacognitive monitoring, indicating that reaching an impasse encourages learners to reflect on their thinking and evaluate progress (Loibl & Rummel, 2014; Lamnina & Chase, 2019). The null result for debugging may reflect problem-solving and instructional sequencing: explore-first learners may have settled on effective strategies early, and instruct-first learners followed the taught method, leaving little need to adjust strategies. This interpretation is speculative, as learners’ attempts were not coded.

Although exploratory learning increased monitoring and inference concept scoring, the moderated mediation model did not support the predicted indirect pathway whereby exploratory learning improved understanding through sequential effects on monitoring and debugging, moderated by curiosity. Instead, only direct effects emerged: exploratory learning predicted inference performance, and curiosity moderated monitoring. These findings suggest that curiosity and metacognition may contribute to conceptual understa nding through partially independent rather than strictly hierarchical processes. In other words, exploration appears to stimulate both curiosity and monitoring, but their combined influence on learning outcomes may be more complex than initially theorized.

Curiosity did not differ significantly between conditions but moderated the relation between instruction order and monitoring, supporting the view that curiosity operates as a metacognitive feeling that motivates learners monitor their understanding (Goupil & Proust, 2023). Thus, curiosity may not always increase through exploration alone but may amplify metacognitive engagement. Overall, exploration before instruction appears to promote increased understanding of inference concepts while also benefiting curiosity and metacognitive monitoring, though these processes may operate in partially distinct ways. Designing exploratory activities that encourage learners to question, reflect, and seek resolution may heighten curiosity while guiding monitoring and self-regulation. Future research should explore how curiosity and metacognition develop together during exploration to better understand when and how exploration most effectively supports learning.
