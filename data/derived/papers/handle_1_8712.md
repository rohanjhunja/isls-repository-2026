# Draw-a-Computer-Scientist-Teacher? Designing for Expansive Identities in Undergraduate Computer Science

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster investigates integrating teacher identities with disciplinary identities in the domain of computer science. Initial findings from a study analyzing undergraduate perceptions of both computer scientists and teachers are presented. Drawing on socio-cultural theories of learning and identity, we argue, and our findings suggest, that surfacing perceptions of both computer scientists and teachers is a productive exercise to reveal areas of commonality and contradictions to overcome in our designs for learning in both domains.

## Introduction

Multiple rationales support increasing access and expanding competencies in computational thinking and literacy across the K-12 curriculum (Blikstein & Moghadam, 2019). Much of the work on teacher learning related to this domain has focused on designing professional development for teachers to better integrate these computer science (CS) skills into their classrooms (e.g., Grover & Pea, 2018; Yadav et al., 2016) with the skills themselves seen as having significant value independent from the CS di sciplinary domain in which they originated. While encouraging teachers to include CS skills in their teaching practice is desirable, we also think more CS disciplinary experts ought to recognize how teaching supports an  extension of their disciplinary practices (Weidler-Lewis, DuBow, & Kaminsky, 2017.) Our research is broadly i nterested in how to best support the integration of disciplinary identities with teacher identities and blends Learning Science topics of disciplinary learning, identity, and the fields’ nascent focus in teacher learning. We are primarily interested in creating novel entry  points into teaching from disciplinary domains both for disciplinary experts and for undergraduate students. We are currently in the process of designing complementary undergrad uate learning opportunities to support students’ pa thways into disciplinary practices, such as CS, and into teaching in K-12 settings. An impetus for the current work-in-progress study we are reporting here, came from the findings of data collected in introductory undergraduate CS courses from the “draw-a-computer-scientist” activity (Manzo, Doore, & Harmon, 2019). Building on the popular draw-a-scientist task (Chambers, 1983), the computer scientist version helps educators better understand student stereotypes of who they think becomes a computer scientist, and then shape curriculum and instruction to combat problema tic stereotypes and increase representations of who  is and can be a computer scientist. Our data included student responses to the task to list three words that come to mind when thinking of a computer scientist. Given our in terest in developing supports for undergraduates to  identify with and pursue both teaching certification and CS degrees, an open question emerged that we are now s eeking to answer: What are the similarities and differences between how undergraduate CS students describe computer scientists and how they describe teachers?  We argue answering this question will help us see opportunities to strengthen CS majors’ pathways into teaching as well as the potential contradictions that may need to be overcome in students’ perceptions of these disparate identities.

## Theoretical Perspectives

We take a broadly sociocultural approach to learning, acknowledging identities play a significant role in our ability to learn and act in the world and in disciplinary ways (e.g., Gee, 2000). We see our role as undergraduate educators to create possibilities for students to see themsel ves as future teachers and CS professionals by prov iding them with “trajectories of identification” (Polman, 2010) into these domains. While we recognize the relati onship between a teachers’ disciplinary identity and how they engage pedagogically as a teacher (Luehmann, 2016), we prioritize expansiveness insofar as we do not prescribe any one way of being a disciplinary or teaching expert, but rather embrace a multiplicity of identities in and across domains. We believe such a perspective is ne cessary if we are to increase representation and access into disciplines that have historic patterns of identity association (e.g., computer science is often characterized as white and male.)

## Methods

This study is a work-in-progress that builds on our prior work (Manzo, Doore, & Harmon, 2019). In our current iteration, we are administering the modified draw-a-computer scientist test used in our previous study  that now adds the questions: what adjectives best describe a teacher and what adjectives best describes a computer scientist teacher in addition to the questions on the original draw-a-computer scientist test (Martin, 2004). For our purposes here, we have analyzed data from two classes (participant n = 39). We will conduct a more thorough analysis of the comple te data, including word associations and pairs acro ss the participants. Here we are presenting frequency perc entages of terms to look for similarities, patterns, and contradictions across the terms.

## Discussion and Conclusion

The shared terms across the different domains provide compelling evidence for what characteristics we ought to foster while designing expansively for learning across these domains. A significant finding for us is the lack of any explicit contradiction in students’ perceptions  across domains (i.e., being “smart” is not inheren tly contradictory with being “caring”). This will help us in our designs for learning, however, equally important are the terms not in the top three. For example, “white ” and “male” were reported for computer scientists,  while “white” and “female” were reported for teachers, su ggesting we still have to work at combatting gender ed and racial perceptions of these fields. As our findings  demonstrate, and the Learning Sciences advocates, surfacing perceptions about identities in the domains we are designing for is an important step toward designing for greater access and equity.
