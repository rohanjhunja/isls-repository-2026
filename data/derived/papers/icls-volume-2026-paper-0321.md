# Bridging Hearts and Minds: A Scoping Review of Reciprocal Relationships Between Social -Emotional Skills and Socioscientific Issues-Based Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This scoping review maps the integration of Social and Emotional Learning (SEL) with Socioscientific Issues-Based Learning (SSI-L). Following a systematic search of three databases, 57 empirical studies from East Asia/Southeast Asia, Europe, and North America met inclusion criteria. All 57 studies measured SEL outcomes; 50 (88%) embedded deliberate SEL integration—26 (52%) explicit (targeting named SEL skills), 24 (48%) implicit (SEL emerged as byproduct). Science education is shifting toward cultivating a scientifically literate citizenry capable of engaging with complex societal challenges (Osborne & Pimentel, 2023; Sadler, 2011). Socioscientific issues-based learning (SSI-L) grounds instruction in ill-structured, controversial issues requiring students to navigate diverse perspectives and competing ethical values (Zeidler, 2014). SEL—encompassing skills to manage emotions, demonstrate empathy, and make responsible decisions (CASEL, 2020) —is increasingly  recognized as integral to science learning (Sinatra et al., 2014).

Despite this theoretical synergy, the empirical landscape remains fragmented. Existing reviews have examined SEL interventions broadly (Cipriano et al., 2023) and SSI-L outcomes in isolation (Sadler, 2011), but none have mapped their intersection. The field has begun to diverge: some studies treat SEL as an incidental byproduct of SSI engagement, while others deliberately design interventions to cultivate specific competencies— a distinction with profound implications for instructional design that remains unexamined. This scoping review addresses these gaps, guided by: (RQ1) What is the nature and extent of empirical research exploring SEL within SSI-L contexts? (RQ2) What instructional strategies integrate SEL within SSI-L? (RQ3) What SEL skills are most frequently targeted and measured, and what outcomes are reported?

## Theoretical framework

This review adopts the CASEL (2020) framework, which organizes SEL into five domains —Self-Awareness, Self-Management, Social Awareness, Relationship Skills, and Responsible Decision-Making—serving as our coding scheme. The inherent structure of SSI-L—ill-structured issues demanding argumentation, ethical deliberation, and multiple stakeholder perspectives—creates a natural site for SEL development (Kahn & Zeidler, 2019; Zeidler, 2014), yet whether this potential is systematically realized depends on instructional design choices that remain empirically uncharted.

## Method

Search strategy Following PRISMA-ScR guidelines, the search was executed on 10 August 2025. SEL terms were developed with reference to the CASEL framework and the Harvard EASEL Lab's systematic taxonomy, ensuring coverage of established skill labels (e.g., "perspective-taking," "empathy") and specific indicators (e.g., "self-efficacy," "conflict resolution"). SSI terms combined "socioscientific," "socio-scientific issue," and "science education," applied to titles, abstracts, and keywords across ProQuest, Scopus, and Web of Science. The full string is available from the authors. This strategy yielded 793 records.

Eligibility criteria and study selection 2096 The PICOS framework guided inclusion (Table 1). Following removal of 398 duplicates, 395 studies underwent title/abstract screening, yielding 101 for full-text review; 44 were excluded, resulting in 57 included studies (Figure 1). All included studies measured at least one SEL outcome; they were further classified by whether SEL was also deliberately integrated into the intervention design. Among the 50 integration studies —all of which measured SEL outcomes —explicit integration was coded when interventions deliberately targeted named SEL skills (e.g., guided reflection protocols, role-assigned perspective-taking, emotion regulation instruction); implicit integration when SEL emerged as a byproduct of standard SSI pedagogy. The remaining seven measured SEL outcomes without deliberate integration.

SSI-L and SEL: a structural affinity The methodological profile of this literature—56% qualitative or mixed-methods, no RCTs—reflects not a gap in rigor but a match between method and phenomenon. Social-emotional processes unfold through classroom interaction in ways that resist experimental isolation. Social Awareness dominance —88% of integration studies, 61% of outcomes (n=35) —follows the same logic: perspective-taking and empathy are not grafted onto SSI pedagogy but architecturally embedded in it. SSI's ill-structured issues inherently demand confronting competing stakeholder viewpoints and ethical tensions (Zeidler, 2014; Högström et al., 2024), creating a feedback loop in which the most naturally foregrounded competencies —that observable  through discourse analysis—accumulate disproportionate evidentiary weight while less visible competencies are systematically underrepresented. The intrapersonal gap is a design problem, not a content problem The starkest pattern is the explicit–implicit asymmetry in intrapersonal domains: Self-Awareness was targeted in 54% of explicit studies but only 17% of implicit ones; Self-Management in 50% versus 21%. Content cannot explain this differential—both clusters engaged comparable SSI topics —only instructional intention can. Bandura's (2001, 2023) distinction between collective agency—exercised through socially coordinated deliberation—and personal agency—exercised through self-regulation and self-reflectiveness—offers a parsimonious account: SSI pedagogy optimizes  for collective agency while remaining structurally silent on personal agency. Learners are carried through SSI discourse by group discussion regardless of their intrapersonal readiness—leaving self-regulation and epistemic self-awareness unaddressed. Gao et al.'s (2019) EC-SSI program offers the clearest counter-evidence: embedding emotion regulation as a prerequisite produced statistically significant gains absent in structurally comparable implicit contexts. The intrapersonal gap is not SSI-L's inherent limitation but the predictable consequence of neglecting personal agency in design (Leung & Cheng, 2023). From mapping to mechanism: what the field needs next The present findings establish association, not causation; direct comparisons are unwarranted given differences in context, population, duration, and measurement. Three questions warrant priority: the durability and transferability of explicit intrapersonal gains; whether personal agency strengthens collective SSI reasoning; and the cultural conditions under which explicit strategies yield equitable development. Both require longitudinal designs and validate  intrapersonal instruments —the absence of which is  itself a finding: measurement underdevelopment both reflects and perpetuates the field's blind spot (Smit et al., 2025; Cipriano et al., 2023).

## Limitations and conclusion

Four limitations bound interpretation: the explicit– implicit classification involved interpretive judgment beyond the reliability sample (κ=0.78, 26.3% of studies); methodological heterogeneity precludes effect-size aggregation; geographical concentration (40% East/Southeast Asia, 30% Europe) and short durations (4–8 weeks) jointly limit transferability and leave durability questions unanswered. Across 57 studies, SSI-L is a productive but structurally uneven context for SEL. Perspective-taking, ethical reasoning, and collaborative skills emerge reliably from well-facilitated SSI discourse; self-regulation and emotional self-awareness do not. The entry point for practice is deliberate intrapersonal scaffolding—structured reflection on students' emotional responses to contested issues — with Gao et al.'s (2019) EC-SSI program demonstrating that emotion regulation is teachable when treated as a prerequisite. For research, the priority is measurement: without validated intrapersonal instruments for SSI contexts, the gap will remain invisible by design (Smit et al., 2025).
