# Maintaining Academic Buoyancy in Higher Education: The Moderation Analysis of Age and Gender

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Academic buoyancy refers to a student's ability to overcome common learning-related challenges or setbacks encountered in school. The present study aims to examine the relation between age and gender and university students' reported academic buoyancy. Students from a Minority-serving and Hispanic-serving public higher education in Texas (N=118) completed an online survey assessing their academic buoyancy. On average, women reported having lower academic buoyancy than men. Further, age and gender play a notable role in predicting academic buoyancy. Finally, there was no differential effect of age on the academic buoyancy of women compared to men.

## Background

Extensive research has documented the roles of academic buoyancy in students' academic achievements (Martin, 2013; Putwain et al., 2020; Thomas & Allen, 2021). Academic buoyancy refers to aspects of students' ability to handle day-to-day learning-related challenges and setbacks (e.g., experiencing dips in motivation, getting low exam grades, receiving negative feedback from a professor). Guided by the SEVT framework (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020, 2023), we investigated how  academic-related performance (academic buoyancy) could be related to students' characteristics (gender) and developmental process (age) as students are situated within the context of higher education. Based on our review of existing literature, we have  developed the following hypotheses: H1: There is  a statistically significant difference in the level s of academic buoyancy experienced by students of different genders, including those who identify as men, women, and non-binary individuals, H2: Age is associated with academic buoyancy, H3: Age and gender are statistically significant predictors of  academic buoyancy, and H4: The effect of age on academic buoyancy similar for men and women.

## Procedure and measures

The current research study received ethical approva l from the University of North Texas (IRB-22-831), which is a Minority-serving and Hispanic-serving public higher education institution in Texas. Inclusion criteria were being a current student enrolled in higher education in the United States, and participants must be 18 years of age or older. Students were recruited from the Education department’s undergraduate and graduate classes in Spring

2023. Interested students took part in a single-session Qualtrics online survey, which included var ious

questionnaire measures, including a demographic questionnaire, the Academic Buoyancy Scale (Martin & Marsh, 2008), and two other measures that were not included in the current analysis. Participant's age was captured as a continuous variable.  In the present study, the age range of participants was between 18 to 55 years old. Over 79 percent of our students were 25 years old or younger. In terms of gender, 76.9% of the participants identified themselves as women, followed by identified men (20.7%), and non-binary (2.5%). Because of a limited representation of non-binary students in the current study, we could not include them in the current analysis.

## Results

To determine whether there were differences in acad emic buoyancy and fear of failure levels when considering gender (H1), we conducted t-test analyses between men and women. Levene's Test indicated that equal variances are assumed for academic buoyancy (F = 1.468, p =.228) between men and women. We found a statistic ally significant difference in academic buoyancy levels between men (n = 25, M = 4.85, SD = 1.18) and women (n = 93, M = 4.035, SD = 1.06; t(116) = 3.340, p <.001) with a large effect size (d = 1.08). On average, women reported lower academic buoyancy than men. Then, the  relation between age and academic buoyancy was assessed with simple regression analysis (H2). The regression model was statistically significant  F(1, 116) = 11.616,  p <.001. Hence, there was sufficient evidence to claim that the 9.1% of variability in academic buoyancy scores accounted for by the model was statistically greater than would have been observed by random variation (R2=.091). Next, a  simultaneous regression analysis was conducted with  age and gender predicting academic buoyancy to explore the extent to which age and gen der predict academic buoyancy (H3). We used the Dummy Variable Coding (DVC) technique and designated wome n as the reference group (0) to ensure the regressi on analysis was possible. The predictor variables accounted for 17.3% of the variance explained in the overall model (R2 =.173). the overall model was found to be statistically significant (F[2, 115] = 12.066, p <.001). Beta weights indicated that age (β =.293) and gender (β = -.283) are statistically significant predictors of academic buoyancy. To test the statistical significance of the interac tion between age and gender  (H4), academic buoyancy was first regressed on gender and age. These variables accounted for 17.3% of the variance in academic buoyancy. In the second step in this sequential regression, a ge and gender interaction were added. The addition of the interaction term did not lead to a statistically si gnificant increase in R2 (ΔR2 <.001, F[2, 114] =.017, p =.897). This means that the interaction term does not help explain academic buo yancy beyond the explanation provided by age and gender.

## Discussion

The goal of this study was to investigate the association between age and gender and academic buoyancy among university students. The study aimed to build on pr evious education research and enhance our understanding of how individual characteristics (gender) and develop mental processes (age) could be related to  a student's ability to overcome typical academic and everyday challenge s. The results of the study provide new insights into the relation between age, gender, and academic buoyancy among un iversity students. The findings revealed that, on average, women reported lower levels of academic bu oyancy than men in higher education. These results are consistent with the SEVT framework, which emphasizes the role of gender identity and social messages in shaping individuals' beliefs about their capability (Eccles & Wigfield, 2023). The study found that older students have higher academic buoyancy, likely due to their prior  experiences with success and failure equipping the m with better strategies to overcome academic challenges. In addition, we found that both age and gender are generally and equally important predictors of academic buoyan cy. Finally, findings indicated the magnitude of ag e on academic buoyancy is the same for both men and women. This study findings contribute to the field of education research by providing valuable insights to universities, educators, and families o n how individual characteristics and development influence a student's ability to co pe with academic challenges and setbacks.  However, caution should be exercised when interpreting findings as we mainly recruited participants from the Education department, where young women are often over-represented. Future studies should recruit more men and non-binary students from different colleges and department to re-investigate the relation between gender and academic.
