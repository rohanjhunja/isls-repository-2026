# Bridging Theory and Practice: A Design-Based Research Approach to Strengthening Evaluation in Science Museums

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This design-based research study examines the relationship between evaluation theory and practice in science museums. Through interviews with six museum evaluators and a broader survey, researchers found that museum evaluators have minimal engagement with formal evaluation theory, relying instead on on-the-job training, templates, and practical tools. This tenuous practice-theory relationship stems from limited formal evaluation training, with most evaluators entering the field through adjacent roles rather than intentional career planning. While evaluators do reference some pragmatic frameworks, they more commonly draw from concrete resources and theories from adjacent fields such as education and psychology. This work-in-progress outlines the first phase of a multi-phase DBR project aimed at co-designing a professional learning tool that strengthens evaluation theory use among science museum evaluators. This research demonstrates how learning sciences methodologies can advance understanding of learning in informal contexts while simultaneously contributing to practice-based theory development in evaluation.

Evaluation has become a critical tool for museums seeking to document and understand visitor learning outcomes and is vital for internal program development. It helps museums identify what's working well, what needs adjustment, and where resources should be allocated for maximum sustainability and effectiveness. Additionally, as competition for funding intensifies and funders are increasingly data-driven in their decision-making, museums that candemonstrate measurable outcomes have an advantage over those that rely solely on anecdotal evidence or good intentions. Learning in museums is intended to be intrinsic, voluntary, and free choice (Rogoff et al., 2016); the informal nature of this learning presents challenges for its assessment: nearly thirty years ago, Falk and Dierking (1995) observed that the museum field lacks the means to be specific about the extent of the benefits of museums. This critique continues to be salient with continued calls for continued development and capacity building in informal e ducation evaluation (Teasdale, 2022).    Recent reviews suggest a need for additional rigor in informal learning evaluations, including examinations of reporting (Grack Nelson & Tranby, 2015), methodological characteristics (Fu et al., 2016), and criteria building (Teasdale, 2022). In these and other research on evaluation in informal learning settings like museums, references to theoretical underpinnings of the work are unclear.

Design-based research (DBR) has emerged as a prominent methodology in learning sciences which bridges theory and practice (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012). This integration of theory and practice aligns with Miller's (2010) description of evaluation theory as conceptual frameworks that provide philosophical orientations and systematic approaches for understanding evaluation's role and making decisions throughout the evaluation process. Miller argues that theorists develop these frameworks to improve evaluation a nd leverage its impact — goals that resonate with DBR’s commitment to advancing both understanding and application. However, in a systematic review of 53 museum evaluation reports Ross Nelson and Lundgren (2023) observed that few evaluators employed explicit  theoretical frameworks to guide their work. These findings present an opportunity for the learning sciences community: museums serve as settings for understanding learning across the lifespan, and stronger connections between evaluation practice and theory could improve museum evaluation while at the same time advancing evidence of learning in informal settings. This research aims to explore this intersection through a DBR approach anchored in the question, "How might we use evaluation theory to strengthen museum evaluators' ability to provide evidence of learning value of museums?" This research applied DBR layered with co-design to develop and test a professional learning experience that supports the use of evaluation theory by museum evaluators. Webster-Wright (2010) emphasized the situated nature of learning and described the need to understand professional learning from the perspective of the professionals involved within the context of everyday professional practice. As such, DBR, an approach with the 1149 goal to “engage in the close study of learning as it unfolds within a particular context” (Barab, 2022) is a logical fit for designing – in a purposeful partnership with the intended user – a professional learning experience that supports the use of evaluation theory by museum evaluators. Reeves (2006) defines four phases of DBR: Phase 1, analysis of practical problems by researchers and practitioners in collaboration; Phase 2, development of solutions informed by existing design principles and technological innovations; Phase 3, iterative cycles of testing and refinement of solutions in practice; and Phase 4, reflection to produce “design principles” and enhance solution implementation. Following this framework, in Phase 1 of this process, six evaluators who had conducted evaluations in science museums within the past two years were interviewed. Researchers employed a set of semi-structured interviews-including discussions of artifacts in the form of their evaluation reports-to explore evaluators’ familiarity with, use of, and views on evaluation theory. Researchers then surveyed a broader sample of evaluators who had worked in science museum settings to confirm or disconfirm findings. The survey asked questions related to evaluators’ familiarity with evaluation theory, what guides their processes, and how they continue to learn about evaluation. Interview transcripts were analyzed using Braun and Clark’s (2006) guide to thematic analysis for identifying, organizing, and reporting patterns within data. Researchers looked at frequencies of responses for both open-and closedended survey responses to  understand how survey respondents' experiences differed from or agreed with interviewees’. Building on Phase 1 findings, Phase 2 will involve continued collaboration with science museum evaluators to co-design a theory-informed solution that strengthens their evaluation practice. This solution will be developed through iterative workshops, pilot testing, and reflection sessions with the evaluators. A tenuous practice-theory relationship was the overarching theme of interviews with science museum evaluators. Thinking of theories in terms of conceptual frameworks that provide philosophical orientations and systematic approaches for understanding evaluation's role and making decisions throughout the evaluation process (Miller, 2010), familiarity with and application of theory from the broader field of evaluation is minimal in the work of museum evaluators. In both interviews and surveys, a few well-known theories were sometimes mentioned, but more often evaluators cited concrete or practical tools as guiding their work. Interview and survey data suggest three themes supporting this overarching result.

The limited practice-theory relationship in museum evaluation may be due to modest formal evaluation training (theme 1). None of the six interview participants intentionally planned for a career in evaluation, and only one had dedicated evaluation coursework as part of their university studies. While three planned for a career in museums and pursued graduate programs to prepare for such, they each anticipated working in visitor-facing, education roles. In their graduate programs, if evaluation was taught, it was only summarily as part of other coursework related to understanding museum visitors. Two interview participants did not know about evaluation until they were either in an adjacent work role or searching for work and saw an evaluator job listing they felt they had the skills for. Vanessa reflected, "...Like many evaluators, I didn't come to this job directly. It was kind of a winding path and a field that I didn't even know existed really until I was interviewing for the job...." Survey respondents' paths to evaluation mirror those of interview participants. For 14 of the 23 respondents, evaluation work grew out of other museum roles such as education, development, or exhibits, or from work in adjacent fields like social science research, recreational t herapy, and formal education. While six respondents intentionally pursued evaluation training and work, others leveraged existing skills to position themselves for a job opportunity in evaluation. With modest formal training, participants credit the workpl ace for the majority of evaluation training. Sometimes this training started in internships or graduate assistantships, but most often it was permanent employment. Similarly, most survey respondents indicated “on-the-job training” (21 of 23) as not only a source of their training, but also the most helpful in their day-to-day work (17 of 23). With this applied perspective, engagement with evaluation theory and literacy around it is passive rather than active concern, and whatever theoretical knowledge museum evaluators have, practical needs take precedence in their day-to-day work. The predominance of on-the-job training is reflected in the way museum evaluators lean into theory that is pragmatic and tool based (theme 2). Results of this study suggest that museum evaluators use theory more concretely versus philosophically-looking towards it more for methodological guidance rather than philosophical underpinnings and with a pragmatic worldview. Interviewees and survey respondents commonly cited Utilization Focused Evaluation (Patton, 2011) and Culturally Responsive Evaluation (Hood et al. 2015). These evaluation theories are somewhat paradigmatic in the way they posit philosophical framings but are also practical in nature, providing models or patterns for conducting evaluation. Tara, the only interview participant with specific academic evaluation training, spoke of a practice/theory tension as she reflected on her grad school training in evaluation theory, saying, "How are we talking about the same thing for four months? How do I make this useful…for the people who are holding the questions…and then for the communities that they're trying to serve." 1150 Additionally, an interview participant shared content from an evaluation training content that included both Patton’s (2008) definition and the Joint Committee Standards for Educational Evaluation (Yarbrough et al., 2010), but without attribution. They described how the content was previously more fulsome but was scaled back to what was most relevant. This suggests a more instrumental relationship with evaluation theory-taking what works without necessarily considering or crediting the broader theoretical context. As participants described influences on their evaluation practice, it became clear that evaluation in museums involves many factors beyond evaluation theory (theme 3). In particular, museum evaluators rely on templates, client requests, peer expertise, and  theory from adjacent fields. Interview participants commonly mentioned templates for planning an evaluation, analyzing data, and reporting as  tools to guide their work. While the scope of this research did not consider theoretical underpinnings of these templates, such an examination would be valuable research in the future. As another sort of template, interview participants commonly describe referencing previous reports to guide their work. Darren described being more likely to refer to previous reports when starting an evaluation, and running through questions in his mind, including: "How have they done it in the past? How have they worked with this stakeholder? What am I building on?” and ended by saying, “I'll use that much more than like any sort of theoretical background." While on one level previous works were used as frameworks for structuring a report, they were also used as examples of instrument development and coding schemes. Similarly, in survey responses, examples of other reports  was the most frequently selected response to “What kinds of tools or job aids do you find helpful to your work?” These reports might be internal or referenced from repositories such as informalscience.org.

Two interview participants described drawing from adjacent fields—design, education, psychology, and recreation—when discussing their use of theory. Similarly, just 8 of the 23 survey respondents cited evaluation theories and theorists as influencing their  work. More commonly respondents referred to education theories and theorists as influencing their work (e.g. Vygotsky, Bandura) including some specific to museums (e.g. Falk and Simon). This makes sense considering the interdisciplinary nature of museums,  as well as the backgrounds of museum evaluators. Ultimately, we might characterize practice-theory relationship as neither avoidance nor use, but rather what serves a practical need.

Work in progress This work-in-progress reports on the first phase of this DBR project. The first step of DBR Phase 2 will be a comprehensive exploration of Phase 1 findings in conversation with five science museum evaluators. Leveraging these findings along with the evaluators’ expertise and professional experience, researchers will identify patterns, constraints, and strengths in the practice of evaluation in museums, and brainstorm solutions to foster the use of theory in it. Researchers will then design the solution. The  solution will take into account the practical nature of how science museum evaluators do their jobs (e.g. templates and peer support), while also attending to features of effective professional learning (Labone and Long, 2014). In DBR Phase 3, researchers  will invite the collaborating evaluators to pilot it and provide feedback. Feedback interviews will be structured around design research concerns described by McKenney and Reeves (2018): internal structure of a solution, such as soundness and feasibility; how it is used in practice, such as local viability and adoption; and effects, such as the extent to which the solution is meeting its objectives with no scaffolds in place. This study makes an original contribution to the learning sciences by integrating evaluation and professional learning research within informal learning contexts. These fields have been studied together;  however, research has primarily focused on formal educational settings.

While informal learning is frequently conceptualized as supplementary to formal educational systems, this thinking underestimates the potential of informal learning. Given that Americans spend approximately 95% of their lifetime outside traditional classro om settings (Falk & Dierking, 2010), informal learning environments like museums present an important context for humandevelopment and knowledge growth. These settings are uniquely positioned to facilitate lifelong learning and enable individuals to navigate evolving social, technological, and scientific landscapes. Understanding the mechanisms and effectiveness of informal learning designs like exhibits, facilitated programs, and online resources is therefore critical for developing educational frameworks that acknowledge how learning occurs across the human lifespan. Additionally, while there is a growing body of research on evaluation, our use of DBR is unique in it. DBR’s emphasis on collaborative design and testing theory affords meaningful, practice engaged research with real-world implications. The findings of this work present implications for both the museum and evaluation fields, made possible by learning science methodologies. In terms of museums, this research will also provide tangible benefits to museums by proposing a model for professional learning tailored to science museum evaluators, enhancing their 1151 ability to support impactful learning experiences. As shown in the first phase of our research, the use of prior evaluations and practical wisdom represents a pragmatic application of established strategies. However, this approach may inadvertently sustain unexamined conventions and inhibit opportunities for reconceptualizing and advancing evaluative rigor. Operationalizing professional learning for museum evaluators will advance research in evaluation by critically examining evaluation theory while considering King’s (2003) call for theory “derived from practice” (p.66) - creating further implications for the field of evaluation. While the evaluation field claims to be trans-disciplinary, considering the context-specific nature of evaluation, theory derived from practice might better serve the work of practicing evaluators, including those in informal learning contexts. In both cases, one sees that museums are “filled with complex, rich, and fascinating learning problems” (Pierroux et al., 2022) ripe for learning research.

By strengthening the relationship between evaluation practice in museums and evaluation theory, this work not only advances our understanding of professional learning in informal contexts but also challenges the evaluation field to embrace context-driven theory development— transforming how evaluation is conceptualized and conducting evaluation in the spaces where lifelong learning occurs.
