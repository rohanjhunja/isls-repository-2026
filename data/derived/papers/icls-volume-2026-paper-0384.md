# Middle School Teachers' Pedagogical Reasoning about Biomimetic Engineering Design

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We explored middle school STEM teachers' pedagogical reasoning about middle-schoolers’ biomimetic engineering design. Using case study and grounded theory methods, we interviewed four teachers after they participated in professional development and implemented biomimetic design challenges with their students. Our findings suggest that teachers conceptualize biomimicry as a set of eight sub-skills.

Biomimetic design offers a rich learning experience in the STEM field, integrating science and engineering, connecting students to nature (ISO, 2015), and reflecting authentic industry practice (Vanderbilt, 2012). With growing interest in biomimicry in STEM education (Gurjar, 2025), questions remain about how teachers learn to support students’ biomimicry design work. While PD programs for science and engineering practice are common, few focus specifically on teacher learning about biomimicry. Since prior r esearch shows that teachers’ existing knowledge shapes what they take away from PD (Allen & Penuel, 2015), examining teachers’ reasoning about biomimicry can provide insights for more effective PD design. In this study, we applied the concept of pedagogical reasoning to examine how middle-school STEM teachers make sense of biomimetic design as a learning experience for their students. Pedagogical reasoning refers to how teachers interpret subject matter, un derstand students’ needs, and transform disciplinary knowledge into teachable forms (Horn, 2005; Loughran, 2019; Shulman, 1987). Our context for studying teachers’ reasoning was a flexible PD model that we designed to offer multiple biomimetic learning exp eriences and tools for teachers to adapt. Our research question is: What is teachers’ pedagogical reasoning about middle-schoolers’ biomimetic engineering design?

## Method

Study participants were four middle-school teachers who attended our summer PD and later implemented biomimicry in their classrooms. The design challenges teachers used focused on five main functions: grasping (e.g., disability assistant grabber), flinging  (e.g., weather rescue launcher), digging (e.g., rubble remover), wind dispersal (e.g., micro package delivery), and energy absorption (e.g., helmet design). Teachers provided information about different natural organisms that are well adapted for these functions. The students' task was to take inspiration from these organisms to design their own devices to solve the engineering design problem. Data sources included teacher interviews, video/audio recordings and field notes from PD, teachers’ lesson plans, and students’ artifacts. For data analysis, we used a case study (Yin, 2018) approach informed by grounded theory (Charmaz, 2014). We conducted open coding of the teacher interview transcripts for ways the teachers described the substance of students’ biomimetic design work. We used the constant comparative method (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) to merge initial codes into categories. For triangulation, we reviewed PD session transcripts, lesson plans, and student work from classroom implementations. We found that teachers reason pedagogically about eight sub-skills that students need to learn to engage in biomimetic design. Teachers reasoned about these students’ sub-skills by noticing students’ weaknesses and strengths in carrying them out.

Describe structure: First, the teachers reasoned that during biomimetic design, students need to recognize the parts of organisms and give details about their characteristics. For example, Kate mentioned, “And then with the pomelo fruit, that was the one that everybody kind of gravitated to, because it had the layers and the air pockets and the peel.” Describe function: Second, the teachers reasoned that students need to recognize the actions of organisms or devices and give details about their characteristics. For example, Blake and Ben mentioned, “I did actually notice some of the kids talking to each other about, like the angle that you’re holding it, or pull it back farther but angle it higher for it to be more accurate cause it’s going to go up higher, and then land, you know.” 2578 Explain relationships between structures and functions: The teachers noticed that, beyond descriptions of structures and functions, students alsoneed to point out something about the cause-and-effect connection between the parts and actions of organisms or design solutions. For example, Sophie mentioned, “We were looking at the specific, for the specific purpose of seed dispersal, like how is that organism specifically dispersing its seed? Is it the wings? Is it the pollen grain sacks? Is it the, you know, large [bristle] structure of the dandelion?” Translate from biological structure and/or function to a design plan:  Connecting from biology to engineering, the teachers also paid attention to the sub-skill of taking inspiration from an organism to inform a solution to a design problem. For example, Kate mentioned her students' conversation, “I think I have to do a parachute, so to make it fall slower. So then everybody was saying, oh, dandelion, because that’s the one that we learned about that had the parachute.” Set criteria for a design:  Shifting toward considerations of engineering practice, the teachers reasoned that biomimetic design requires students to determine what counts as a successful solution to a design problem. For example, Sophie mentioned her conversation with students: “We were talking a little bit about that, like what two feet for our test would be like in the real world if we were really like delivering these packages.” Be fluent with digital and physical tools:  The sixth sub-skill that all teachers included in their pedagogical reasoning about biomimetic design was fluency with software and hardware for creating prototypes of design solutions. For example, Blake and Ben mentioned their students, “Who pretty much dove right into the [robotics] kit, and he made like a trebuchet kind of a deal where the motor would wind it back, and then, [fooo], and then fly it over the thing. We had a couple of others that …were manually operated.” Choose appropriate materials and tools:  In addition to fluency with using  tools, students’ ability to choose appropriate tools and materials was also an aspect of teachers’ pedagogical reasoning. The teachers reasoned that students need to select the objects that make sense to their design goals when constructing a prototype of a solution. For example, Sophie mentioned, “whatever tools (such as robotic sets or CAD) they want. A lot of the groups and I had like conversations about what we thought the best way to model their final prototype, you know, solution to the problem would be, some groups were a little confused on how to create something that they could show if it was not going to be working prototype...” Think visually or in 3D:  Finally, the eighth sub-skill included in the teachers’ pedagogical reasoning was the skill of thinking visually or in 3D to translate a design idea into a concrete representation of the design solution. For example, Kate mentioned, “Just being able to … look at a picture and visualize how the movement of one part affects other parts is difficult for them. And then having the time to build things like things that act like joints, like movable joints….to really think through how one  part impacts the other.” This study examined how middle school STEM teachers reasoned pedagogically as they began to implement biomimetic design challenges in their classrooms. Teachers characterized eight sub-skills by considering the abilities students exhibited during biomimetic design challenges, the challenges students faced, and the teacher-provided scaffolds that helped students progress. Teachers’ reasoning about both student strengths and weaknesses in biomimetic design demonstrates their reflective decision-making. This r eflects how pedagogical reasoning supports teachers in transforming disciplinary knowledge into learnable tasks and adapting instruction responsively (Shulman, 1987). By observing student thinking, setting goals, and scaffolding learning, teachers can support students’ growth in both scientific and engineering practices. While this study is based on the biomimicry context, the pedagogical reasoning and scaffolding design-based process can also work in other integrated STEM learning environments (Glancy et al., 2014). Teachers' identification of sub-skills about how to connect science concepts to engineering practices, or support students to use different technology tools, can also apply to broader science and engineering interdisciplinary contexts. Future work might examine how these reasoning processes work across different classroom contexts and over time. Understanding teachers’ pedagogical reasoning about biomimicry can provide guidance for designing professional development (PD) workshops. To help teachers learn to implement biomimetic design challenges, PD providers might use the set of eight sub-skills as a checklist both for teachers’ own engagement with biomimicry (as learners themselves) and for teachers’ thinking about classroom scaffolds. Hands-on design challenges conducted during the PD workshop can give teachers a chance to develop their own capacity with each of the sub-skills. Discussions and instructional materials shared during the PD can help teachers determine how to scaffold student development of the sub-skills.
