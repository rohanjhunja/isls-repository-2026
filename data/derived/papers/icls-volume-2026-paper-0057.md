# Developing K-12 Teachers’ Ethical Competencies through Cognitive Dissonance of Gameplay

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As AI literacy becomes increasingly integrated into K–12 education, teachers require robust ethical competencies to effectively teach AI ethics in classroom instruction. To address this need, we developed Responsible AI CAB, a scenario-based tabletop role-playing game designed to immerse teachers in real-world ethical dilemmas and foster collective ethical reasoning. Eight technology teachers participated in the gameplay. We employed bipartite epistemic network analysis (ENA) methods to examine teachers’ discourse. Results highlighted that gameplay-induced cognitive dissonance facilitated the development of teachers ’ ethical competencies. This was evidenced by their adoption of multiple stakeholder perspectives under role pressures, their comprehension and application of responsible AI principles in group ethical reasoning through peer challenges or arguments, and their critical evaluation of new information to challenge existing beliefs. These findings inform the design of professional development programs seeking to cultivate the ethical competencies essential for K–12 AI literacy education.

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into K-12 curricula is a global trend (Miao & Shiohira, 2022), yet the ethical dimension of AI literacy remains critically unde rprioritized compared to technical competencies (Wu et al., 2024). This gap is reflected in teachers’ limited understanding of AI ethics, which often focuses on technical, static knowledge related data-driven AI while lacking the dynamic, socio-technical moral reasoning needed for real-world dilemmas (Sperling et al., 2024). To  address this gap, this study presents  a professional learning workshop to foster meaningful ethical competencies among K-12 teachers. While UNESCO AI competency framework highlighting teachers need to acquire, use, and create ethical principles (Miao & Cukurova, 2024), a significant challenge persists in translating these high-level principles into classroom practice (Taddeo & Floridi, 2018). This translation is difficult because ethical dilemmas are inherently complex, often involving competing values and multiple stakeholders  (McIntosh et al., 2021). Thus, it requires group moral reasoning that is socially situated and contextual (Haidt, 2013; Kohlberg, 1984). To bridge this theory-practice gap, we turn to the strategic provocation of  cognitive dissonance, a psychological state known to motivate individuals to deepen their reasoning when confronted with conflicting beliefs or values (Cooper, 2019), which prompts individuals to resolve with in-depth moral reasoning  (Rholes et al., 1982). To trigger cognitive dissonance, we designed and implemented Responsible AI CAB, a Tabletop role-playing game  contextualized within China’s socio-technical environment, as the core component of for the professional learning workshop.  Previous research has demonstrated TPRGs ’ efficacy in ethics education by creating immersive environments that encourage perspective-taking and ethical reasoning through provoking cognitive dissonance  (e.g., Huang & Ho, 2018; Wang et al., 2025). By immersing participants in realistic scenarios, TRPGs compel them to confront ethical conflicts, adopt alternative perspectives, and justify high-stakes decisions. We therefore use this game to provoke cognitive dissonance and examine its role in teachers’ ethical competencies building. Specifically, our study seeks to answer the following research questions: RQ1: What patterns of ethical competencies do teachers demonstrate during gameplay? RQ2: How does cognitive dissonance arise during the gameplay experience? RQ3: What role does cognitive dissonance triggers play in the development of teachers’ ethical competencies? Theoretical underpinnings Teachers’ ethical competencies for AI literacy education Recent years have witnessed a growing consensus that AI ethics is foundational to AI literacy development (Kong et al., 2024; Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021). International policy documents and academic discourse highlight that preparing teachers to navigate the ethical complexities of AI require  more than familiarity with ethical principles (Miao & Cukurova, 2024). Rather, it demands a dynamic, reflective engagement with the social, 474 cultural, and human impacts of these technologies (Tuomi, 2018). Concurrently, a growing body of research suggests that AI ethics education moves beyond principle-based instruction toward experiential and participatory approaches involving group ethical reasoning. When learners  are engaged in authentic dilemmas, they develop the moral judgment and ethical reasoning necessary for responsible AI use (Corrêa et al., 2025). Role-playing games which simulate the real-world scenarios have been shown to promote deepen moral reasoning and foster a sense of agency in understanding AI’s societal implications (Fernández-Martínez et al., 2021). To systematically frame the development of ethical competencies, this study draws on Rest (1986) ’s four-component model of ethical decision making and  action, which identifies moral sensitivity (recognizing ethical issues), moral judgment (evaluating right and wrong), moral motivation (prioritizing ethical values), and moral character (implementing ethical actions) as interconnected dimensions. This model provides a valuable lens for conceptualizing the competencies teachers need to cultivate for AI literacy education, including: considering diverse stakeholder perspectives (Friedman & Hendry, 2019), identifying emergent ethical risks (Kamila & Jasrotia, 2025), applying ethical principles such as transparency and accountability (Miao & Cukurova, 2024), making ethically grounded decisions (Dolan, 2017), and critically evaluating societal implications  (Ali et al., 2021).

Cognitive dissonance triggers for ethical reasoning Cognitive dissonance refers to the mental state of discomfort experienced when an individual holds conflicting cognitions (Festinger, 1957). In the context of ethics education, this state of mental disequilibrium can serve as a catalyst for deeper reflection and ethical development (Rholes et al., 1982). Eliciting cognitive dissonance through authentic scenarios is particularly valuable in teacher education, where such experiences can challenge preconceptions and foster meaningful self-reflection, to build teacher resilience and reflective capacity while immersing future teachers in complex situations that enhance the knowledge, adaptability, and collaborative skills essential for effective classroom practice (Dai et al., 2023; Galman, 2009; Ledger et al., 2022). Role-playing tabletop games offer a particularly suitable context for triggering cognitive dissonance (Chiou & Wan, 2007; Wang et al., 2025). Such dissonance often manifests as a conflict between a player’ s preexisting beliefs and the values or actions required by their assigned role (Yao et al., 2021). Peer challenges during group discussion can expose flaws in reasoning, thereby revealing the inadequacy of one’ s initial justifications (Bricker & Bell, 2008). The process of weighing competing choices compels participants to consciously confront trade-offs that resist resolution through simple rules (Izuma et al., 2010). Additionally, the introduction of new information or unforeseen consequences at critical junctures candisrupt settled judgments, inducing dissonance and prompting a re-evaluation of the situation (Reeve, 2024). Game design Ride-hailing services as AI ethical dilemma scenarios Online ride-hailing services presents a real-world context for examining AI ethics due to its widespread integration into China’s daily life and the complex socio-technical system it represents (Lee et al., 2020). It brought various ethical issues, such as algorithmic control of drivers through rating systems that affect livelihoods,  dynamic pricing models that can lead to discriminatory, safety and accountability questions following regulatory interventions, and the transparency risks  of AI-driven dispatch systems  (Rao et al., 2025). Furthermore, the services involve multiple stakeholders with often competing interests  (Ma et al., 2018), providing a productive context for role-playing, making argumentation, and developing new intuitions in a TPRG  (Haidt, 2001). By grounding ethical dilemmas in this  authentic context, this scenario makes abstract AI ethics principles tangible and culturally responsive, thereby fostering deeper understanding and critical reflection among participants. A core mechanic throughout these phases is the use of ethics currency, which participants must spend to articulate arguments, support proposals, or challenge others ’ positions during both a portion of their currency. This bid is then subjected to a vote by the other participants; only if the argument is deemed persuasive by the group is the currency officially awarded. This voting mechanism, coupled with the ability for peers to challenge reasoning, transforms the dialogic space into a rigorous forum for collective sense-making. This framework cultivates a dynamic dialogic space where individuals articulate and defend ethical positions yet are prompted to reconsider them through collaborative ethical reasoning and critical reflection. Cognitive dissonance triggers As illustrated in Figure 4 (b), cognitive dissonance among teachers was primarily triggered by the co-occurrence of peer challenge or disagreement and new information that challenged existing beliefs. It is also noteworthy that strong connections were observed between role pressure s and such belief-challenging information. That is, teachers experienced tension from the dual demands of advocating for their assigned stakeholder’s position while having to respond tonew perspectives introduced through incident cards and others’ argument s. This dynamic created a context ripe for cognitive dissonance. For example, one teacher playing the role of a programmer expressed difficulty in balancing conflicting points during the analysis of Incident #2. On the one hand, she noted: “As a programmer, I need to listen to my boss; it is the CEO’s decision to develop the credit system for drivers.” This reflects a sense of role pressure to comply with hierarchical directives. On the other hand, she also acknowledged: “I agree with the market supervisor’s argument that it may reduce drivers’ motivation to work for the company, with the increasing complaints received.” It is also important to note the weaker connections between ethical principles conflict and the other three codes. Teachers recognized conflicts between ethical principles when analyzing real-world circumstances. For example, when evaluating Idea #2, one driver recommended: “I think we can use the audio recording during the ride that we usually have to identify passengers who have special needs, like elderly couples, to give them fast response services.” However, the market supervisor opposed this idea, sta ting: “But it will violate passengers’ privacy. Usually, these data are recorded for ensuring safety for both passengers and drivers. But if the company uses it for business purposes, it would be another story.” Another teacher playing passenger commented: “I found it hard to balance, like the trolley problem-hit more people or hit less.” This reflects how teachers recognized the complexity of ethical dilemmas and the importance of trading off multiple values when making ethical decisions. RQ3. Ethical competencies development through cognitive dissonance The bipartite network model (See Figure 4 (c)) reveals meaningful interconnections between cognitive dissonance triggers and teachers’ ethical competencies. Analysis demonstrates that the strategic integration of role-playing and dilemma scenarios created fertile ground for fostering teachers’ ethical competencies building. The strongest connection observed is between role pressures and stakeholders’ perspective taking. This alignment suggests that requiring teachers to advocate for specific stakeholder positions within the AI ecosystem —particularly those contrasting with their personal views—effectively induced cognitive dissonance. As one expert teacher who play passenger #1 reflected, “I don’t have a car, I must take his car, I must put my life in his hands. Then I ’m very worried. Yes, I’m a passenger, I’m an office worker who uses cars every day. Robust connections also emerged among new information to challenge beliefs, critical evaluation, recognizing ethical issues, and stakeholder perspective taking. The carefully designed incident cards and idea cards provided crucial contextual information that prompted teachers to critically examine situations through their assigned roles. The market supervisor ’s argument against Idea #2 illustrates this dynamic: “From a market supervision perspective, I might receive many complaints. Why prioritize VIPs who always get rides, while regular passengers wait extensively? This clearly raises fairness concerns.” Although the connections among peer challenge or disagreement, critical evaluation, and new information to challenge beliefs appear relatively weaker in the network, evidence from a teacher demonstrates that peer challenges introduced additional perspectives into conversations and triggered further critical evaluation. For instance, when discussing Incident #2, after the CEO defended the company policy, a peer challenged this position by stating: “But boss, you have to consider-if the driver’s complaint rate is high, your company might really have trouble recruiting drivers, because competition in the industry isn’t good right now.” The epistemic network analysis showed strong connections between stakeholder perspectives and the application of principles, suggesting that teachers were contextualizing abstract ethical concepts within real-life contexts. Specifically, when discussing th e driver acceptance-rate policy, teachers consistently raised concerns about fairness, system accountability, driver autonomy, and passenger safety. This indicates the TPRG fostered sophisticated ethical reasoning, fostering moral sensitivity (the recognition of ethical issues) and moral judgment (the application of ethical principles), as conceptualized of ethical action model by (Rest, 1986). Moreover, teachers demonstrated the capacity for ethical decision-making (moral motivation) by critically evaluating existing solutions and proposing revisions that prioritized competing values in difficult situations (moral character). This was evidence d by their ability to not only identify ethical tensions but also generate modified solutions that balanced stakeholder interests. For example, when confronted with the passenger credit prioritization dilemma, teachers moved beyond simple critique to propose tiered service models that maintained efficiency while protecting vulnerable users. This demonstrates how moral motivation emerged through 480 gameplay, enabling teachers to translate ethical reasoning into ethically grounded decisions that acknowledged real-world constraints while upholding core ethical principles. Regarding the mechanisms of cognitive dissonance, the gameplay highlighted three primary triggers that provoked ethical reasoning. The first was role pressure, which created tension between personal ethical beliefs and the responsibilities imposed by assigned roles. Teachers required to justify company policies that conflicted with their own values experienced immediate discomfort, which compelled them to engage seriously with perspectives that challenged their initial beliefs. The second trigger was peer challenge, which introduced social tension through collaborative argumentation. As teachers advocated for differing stakeholder positions, they encountered reasoned counterarguments that exposed limitations in their justifications. The social context highlighted the need to either reinforce their positions or acknowledge competing perspectives, in line with social constructivist theories that emphasize the importance of dialogic exchange in argument  (Kuhn, 1991). The third trigger was information revelation, as new data or scenario elements introduced during gameplay contradicted participants’ assumptions. When teachers encountered information about unintended consequences or overlooked stakeholders, their initial ethical certainty was disrupted. The process through which cognitive dissonance facilitated ethical competencies development unfolded along three critical pathways: perspective-taking, contextualization of principles, and integration of reasoning. As teachers worked through discomfort induced by their assigned roles, they engaged more deeply with unfamiliar stakeholder viewpoints, often moving beyond surface-level consideration to genuine understanding of competing values. This supports Festinger (1957)’s conception of cognitive dissonance as a driver of attitude and perspective change, broadening ethical perspective-taking in this context. The challenge of applying abstract principles to complex, real-world scenarios prompted teachers to develop more nuanced understandings of how ethical considerations interact in practice. For example, the concept of fairness evolved from a straightforward idea of equal treatment to a multifaceted construct that included procedural justice, distributive outcomes, and contextual appropriateness. The epistemic network patterns indicated that cognitive dissonance could prompt the rapid development of sophisticated ethical reasoning, It is also important  to note that cognitive dissonance persist ed throughout the gameplay, particularly when teachers  were confronted  with conflicts  between core ethical principles. During role-playing scenarios, participating teachers recognized fundamental tensions. For instance, they discovered that enhancing passenger safety through increased data collection inherently conflicts with protecting driver privacy, while improving service efficiency often compromised algorithmic transparency. This recognition suggests that ethical competence may encompass not only the capacity to reason through dilemmas but also the awareness of inherent tensions between competing ethical frameworks (European Commission, 2019). Rather than seeking definitive answer s, teachers demonstrated awareness in acknowledging and navigating these inextricable conflicts while maintaining their ethical commitments, which is a crucial capacity for  preparing students to engage in the rapidly evolving AI’s ethical landscape.

## Discussion & Implications

In conclusion, the patterns revealed by the ENA  suggest that cognitive dissonance, when strategically induced through role pressures, new information, ethical conflict and peer arguments, can effectively promotes teachers’ ethical competencies  development. By navigating these dissonant moments within the gameplay, teachers demonstrated their ability to recognize, evaluate, and re flect complex AI ethical dilemmas while applying responsible AI principles. Our analysis revealed that through engagement with dissonant scenarios, teachers demonstrated emerging ethical competencies in recognizing ethical tensions, evaluating dilemmas from multiple perspectives, and applying responsible AI principles in situated reasoning. The tabletop role-playing game served as an immersive, dialogic learning environment  that supported the translation of abstract ethical principles into dynamic moral  frameworks. These findings offer valuable insights for designing professional development programs that prepare teachers to guide students through the increasingly complex ethical landscape of AI technologies.
