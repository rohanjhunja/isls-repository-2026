# Conceptualizing Design Science Research for Learning Sciences: Linking Meta- Requirements, Design Principles, and Design Features

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines Design Science Research (DSR) as a resource for the Learning Sciences (LS). DSR contributes paradigmatically by framing design as a basis for knowledge generation, and methodologically by supporting clearer documentation, communication, and collaboration within design research. Th is paper proposes an MR-DP-DF framework linking Meta-Requirements (MRs), Design Principles (DPs), and Design Features (DFs) to trace design reasoning from contextual demands to intervention features and make design knowledge contributions more explicit and traceable.

Learning Sciences (LS) has a long tradition as an interdisciplinary field motivated by the goal of understanding and improving learning in authentic contexts. Educational Design Research (EDR), including Design-Based Research (DBR), has advanced this agenda by integrating iterative design, enactment, and theorization to produce use-inspired knowledge grounded in real contexts (e.g., Barab & Squire, 2004; McKenney & Reeves, 2012). LS scholars have also developed methodological tools for making design reasoning and contribution claims more explicit, including work on argumentative grammar (Kelly, 2004) and conjecture mapping (Sandoval, 2014). In view of this context, DSR provides additional support not only in terms of describing context, intervention, and outcomes in detail but, more critically, explicating the derivation of design choices from existing contextual requirements. This matters because LS design research must often address situated learning problems while also contributing beyond a single case. This paper examines DSR ’s paradigmatic and methodological applications towards design knowledge contributions in LS  before proposing an MR-DP-DF framework as one approach through which these potentials may be actualized.

DSR contributions to LS Design Science Research (DSR) can be understood as a problem-solving paradigm in which knowledge is generated through the systematic design and evaluation of artefacts that address real-world problems (Brocke et al., 2020). Although DSR has been adopted across multiple design-oriented fields, it is most prominent in domains concerned with the development of technological artefacts and systems. This is of increasing relevance to LS as technological innovations become more integrated with learning designs. Rooted in Simon’s (1996) view that the sciences of the artificial warrant systematic study, DSR treats human-made artefacts as legitimate objects of inquiry through which scientific insight can be generated. Drechsler and Hevner (2022)  further position DSR as a link through which technological innovation feeds back into scientific understanding in the form of design knowledge. In this sense, DSR offers paradigmatic contributions by reframing the tension between context-specific problem solving and broader knowledge generation as a legitimate point  of inquiry, positioning the creation and evaluation of artefacts not only as interventi ve and engineering, but also as a basis for developing design knowledge that links practical solutions to more enduring understanding. Alongside these paradigmatic contributions, DSR also offers useful methodologies for organizing and communicating design research. In fields where design work involves the development of artefacts and systems, DSR methodologies (e.g., Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Peffers et al., 2007) have supported more explicit documentation of design logic and relations between artefact development and broader knowledge contributions. Such methodologies offer useful structures to guide the documentation and presentation of design decisions, especially those involving digital innovations. This paper presents the MR-DP-DF framework as one way in which DSR can be adapted to LS, offering a structured approach for tracing how design logic is distilled, abstracted, and realized. The MR-DP-DF framework Within the DSR literature, particular attention has been given to making the justification behind design work more visible. The proposed framework presents a logical and traceable linkage among Meta-Requirements (MRs), Design Principles (DPs), and Design Features  (DFs) as one way of articulating how design work moves from a literature-grounded problem space toward a tangible solution space. While the terms in this sequence draw on existing design-research vocabularies  (Gregor & Hevner, 2013), the proposed linkages are adapted for LS to support traceability while ensuring the process is manageable for non-technical users by limiting more elaborate technical specifications, such as those proposed by Gregor et al. Within DSR, design theories use MRs to describe the class of goals to which a theory applies, and more broadly define the purpose and scope of a whol e class of artifacts rather than a single instance (Strohmann et al., 2023). In consideration of the multidisciplinary nature of LS, MRs in this framework articulate the demands that any solution must address within each contextual domain. Such MRs may be derived from extant literature, from data collected from the target populat ion, and, more tentatively, from personal or professional experience. Literature and empirical data offer two viable bases for the derivation of MRs, while personal or professional experience may provisionally surface candidate MRs that require later corro boration. As an illustrative example, a study on the design of an AI chatbot intervention for Self-Directed Learning (SDL) would involve the synthesis of MRs from the domains of SDL, chatbot applications for learning, and Artificial Intelligence in Education (AIEd). These MRs can then be reviewed across contexts, allowing complementary requirements to be abstracted into a more condensed set of DPs (e.g., refer to DP3 in Figure 1). DPs and DFs, by contrast, belong to the solution space. DPs ’ prominence in both DSR (e.g.,  Gregor et al., 2020) and LS (e.g., McKenney & Reeves, 2012; Sandoval, 2014) stems from their transferability across single instantiations and contexts. That role is made explicit in this framework, where DPs link MRs to DFs by grounding specific design instantiations in higher-level requirements and rendering those relations traceable. Here, DFs refer to the design elements through which DPs are instantiated in practice. In LS, these may include pedagogical arrangements beyond technological features. Back to the illustrative example, a DF might include an introductory in-class chatbot activity under teacher supervision prior to implementation, thereby realizing DPs concerning learner support and safe use, especially if younger learners are involved. As a whole, the MR-DP-DF framework is intended as a generative and iterative structure for design work. In the ideation phase, it offers a shared map through which different stakeholders (e.g., researchers, educators, developers) may propose the addition, refinement, or consolidation of MRs, DPs, DFs, and the relations among them. During development, the framework allows focused attention on the finalized DFs, with acceptance testing used to ensure that they adequately realize the intended DPs. Across both DSR and LS, rigorous design research relies on explicit evaluation and testing (e.g., Hevner et al., 2004; McKenney & Reeves, 2012; Sandoval, 2014). During and after implementation, evaluation can be centered at the level of DPs, as findings can be traced through the map to identify where refinement is needed. In this way, the framework supports not only the development of design proposals but also the evaluation and communication of the design knowledge they generate.

## Implications and future work

This paper has introduced DSR and presented the MR-DP-DF framework as one means of supporting LS through clearer documentation, communication, and collaboration around design work. More specifically, the framework provides a structured approach to tracing design work from MRs to DPs and DFs, making design knowledge contributions more explicit and traceable, including in small-scale studies with limited iteration. This paper acknowledges that more work is needed on how candidate MRs surfaced through personal  or professional experience may be warranted, corroborated, or revised, given the difficulty of incorporating such insights into research. As an early conceptual proposal, the framework would benefit from application across a wider range of studies so that its trustworthiness and practical viability can be further strengthened, thereby supporting broader uptake of design research in LS.
