# Teacher Attention and Improving Student Reasoning in Classroom Discussions

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research on improving reasoning practices in classrooms assumes that students will internalize the reasoning practices in which their teachers prompt them to participate. Drawing on recent advances in cognitive psychology which portray reasoning as a distributed phenomenon in which group members build new understanding out of the individual perspectives contributed by members, we present a case study of growth in student reasoning ability in a teacher’s classroom in order to shed light on how such internalization occurs. We find preliminary evidence that students internalize a teacher’s pattern of attention to the metacognitive opportunities afforded by individual discussion contributions.

## Introduction

Quality classroom discussions with a high potential  for learning are dialogic – so goes a point of a consensus in research on classroom discussion  (Applebee et al., 2003; Nystrand, 1997, 2006; Soter  et al., 2008). In dialogic discussions, students are given the opportunity to engage in substantive, reasoned explanation and arg ument, instead of merely trying to respond to teacher questions with the teacher’s expected, predetermined answer. Much research on improving teacher quality looks for way s to help teachers promote more reasoned, inquiry-centered dialogue in the discussions that take place within their classrooms. A key assumption in this body of research is that students who participate in dialogic classroom discussions will adopt as their own the habits of reasoning and dialogue which their teacher exemplifies and facilitates – “the assumption”, in other words, is “that students who participate in inquiry dialogue will eventually internalize important argumentation practices and use them without additional prompting from the teacher” (Reznitskaya & Wilkinson, 2021, p. 3). Finding evidence that this assumption is true, toge ther with a better understanding of how this internalization occurs, would do much to clarify how students become better reasoners and what role teachers can play in that learning process. We provide a case study of a  teacher to shed light on how teachers can facilitate their students’ internalization of reasoning practi ces. We draw  on recent work in cognitive psychology  that portrays reasoning as a group-level, distributed process of building new understanding i n a group out of the perspectives contributed by the group’s individual members. Through the case study, we find evidence that a key facilitator of students’ growth in reasoning abilit y is the example teachers provide, through the ques tions and responses by which they prompt discussion, of how to pay attention to the inferential structure implicit in students’ contributions to the group process  of building understanding. This perspective suggest s that future work should focus on ways to help teachers learn to exhibit these attentional patterns. Empirical work on group reasoning Recent advances in cognitive psychology portray rea soning as primarily a social activity. According to the interactionist theory of reasoning, our capacity to reason is primarily a tool for groups to build new knowledge by coordinating the differing perspectives of their  members (Mercier & Sperber, 2011, 2017). This is not to say that reasoning in groups is free from any epistemic  dangers, but rather that the proper unit of analys is for understanding how reasoning works is the group of reasoners, not the isolated individual. While mature reasoners do engage in reasoning by themselves, such individual activity should be thought of as a learned imitatio n of a group activity. One piece of evidence for this view  comes from the different roles that biases play in  individual and group reasoning. Individual reasoners are susceptible to apparent flaws in reasoning which  are epistemically disadvantageous to the individual reasoning alone b ut can be advantageous in group settings. For example, confirmation bias candisadvantage an individual re asoner by making them more likely to consider only the strongest possible version of their current perspective, walling them off from important correctives to their present state of understanding. In a group setting, however, individual members’ confirmation biases make it l ikely that the group will hear the strongest possible version of each individual’s contributed perspective, provi ding better grist for the collective cognitive mill  (Rollwage & Fleming, 2021). On the interactionist view, apparent flaws of reasoning like confirmation bias are not flaws at all: they are cases of reasoning functioning in ways that promote the building of new understanding as groups engage in a process of reasoning together. Equally important, groups of reasoners have access to a multiplicity of perspectives out of which to build a more accurate understanding than isolated reasone rs are typically capable of achieving.  This finding is well established empirically. For example, Moshman and G eil (1998) found that individuals attempting to solve a difficult reasoning problem on their own almost alw ays failed the test; however, when reasoning about the problem in groups, individuals reached the correct solution over 80% of the time. This phenomenon is well represented in classroom settings. The cognitive benefits of discussion are strongly a ssociated with dialogic conversations in which individual contributions are  taken up and integrated into a coherent flow of di scourse (Murphy et al., 2009; Soter et al., 2008). Group performance in classrooms seems to benefit from the presence of an expert member specifically when the expert uses reasoning to persuade other members of the group (Littlepage & Mueller, 1997; Moshman & Geil, 1998). Conversely,  discussions in which no participants begin with understanding of how to solve a problem can lead pa rticipants to discover partially or fully correct s olutions by discussing their (incomplete and incorrect) perspec tives on the problem  (Ames & Murray, 1982), and groups in which no members begin in a position of expertise o ften show more learning gains than those in which one member already knows how to solved an assigned prob lem specifically because members of these groups are more likely to reason together about how to proceed (Schwarz et al., 2000). In both cases, students use reasoning to build new understanding out of individual members’ perspectives. From an interactionist perspective, the upshot is n ot merely that reasoning in a group is advantageous compared to reasoning alone, but rather that reason ing is in the paradigm case a distributed phenomeno n. Group discussion is reason’s natural home, in that an individual’s capacity for reasoning is a capacity to coordinate their perspectives with others in pursuit of converging t oward new, shared understanding. This view suggests  that a dimension of teacher quality lies in the degree to which they facilitate such coordination in their classrooms. Some research on teacher facilitation of student reasoni ng focuses on the counts of various student reasoni ng moves, such as the number of arguments made, number of app eals to evidence, number of alternative perspe ctives considered, number of rebuttals, or even the number  of uses of the word “because” (Chinn et al., 2001; Reznitskaya et al., 2001; Wegerif & Mercer, 1997). Other, more recent work takes a more integrative approach, seeking to measure the degree to which students int egrate their claims, arguments, and counterproposal s into a consensus line of thinking (Nussbaum, 2021; Reznits kaya & Wilkinson, 2021). That reasoning is closely connected to the process of building understanding through group interaction is also a major theme in Bereiter and Scardamalia’s work on knowledge building  (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2005). The interactionist view of reasoning provides a cognitive-psychological basis for this latter approach and su ggests that research on student reasoning should attend not just to the reasoning m oves students make, but to the way those moves buil d on one another to construct new understanding through coordinating individual perspectives. It also suggests an approach to the question of internalization with which we be gan: whether we see students engaging in behavior which facilitates such coordination without explicit teacher prompting. In what follows, we provide a case study of a teacher’s facilitation of perspectival coordination  among their students and the new understanding students are thereby enabled to construct.

## Case study: coordinating perspectives in the classroom

Analytical framework Research on student argumentation and reasoning com monly rely on Toulmin’s (1958) argument framework, in which an argument consists of a claim, various kind s of support for that claim, and various kinds of r esponse to it (e.g. Berland & Reiser, 2009; de Vries et al., 2002; von Aufschnaiter et al., 2008). Other approaches that do not draw on Toulmin’s entire framework maintain the bas ic unit of a claim and a statement in support of it  as the basic unit of analysis (e.g. Crowell & Kuhn, 2014; Kuhn et al., 2013; Schwarz et al., 2003). We follow and extend this basic approach by choosing as the unit of anal ysis inference units: a statement offered as a claim together with one or more statements offered in support of t hat claim. Following Toulmin, we identify student claims, evidence, and warrants.1 As others have noticed (Berland & Reiser, 2009; Kelly et al., 1998), these categories are fluid: a statement can be treated as both claim and evidence (or warra nt), occupying more than one role simultaneously in  a given discussion. For example, the statement that when water is scarce, fewer plant species can thrive can be treated as a claim in need of further justification through evidence even if it was first offered as a warrant for another claimevidence pair (e.g. that we probably won’t find many plants here  because the region is experiencing drought). This fluidity represents not the fuzziness of reasoning but the important role of metacognition in coordinating the different perspectives each group member brings to a discussion (Kuhn, 2000; Kuhn & Dean, 2004). Metacognition is thinking about the support for or reasonability of candidate knowledge claims, and it is this kind of thinking which students engage in when they consider where there is support for a given statement. Treating a statement offered in support of a claim as itself in need of further explanation or support provides the group with greater opportunity for developing new understanding than merely accepting it with no further examination. Such metacognitive pressure gives students the opportunity to make sense of the relation of their contributions to each other and innovate new, more explanatorily adequate  insights. We therefore also take note of episodes in which inference units share a member in this way; we refer to such groups of two or more units, or a series of units which may not share members but do share a common topic, as inference cycles. An inference unit can be considered a basic unit of  perspectival coordination: it offers groups the opportunity to consider a member’s perspective and the support for it, i.e., the reason(s) why the gro up might adopt an individual member’s perspective as its own in their quest to build new understanding. Since students are not mature reasoners, teachers often have to facilitate this coordination of perspectives through skillful questioning in response to student contributions. We therefore also note teache r requests for claims, evidence, and warrants. We identify as claim requests explicit questions or other prompts that ask students to provide their own perspective or interpretation in a way that is arguable or could in principle be responsive to evidence (as opposed, for example, to a report of their personal reaction to a text or a guess about what happens next.). Evidence requests ask students to provide a textual basis for their claims about the text, and warrant requests are prompts for further information to help explain how evidence supports or is relevan t to a claim. Restatements of previous requests or other reinitiating moves are not counted as separate requests. In sum: on the view proposed here, reasoning is viewed as the creation of new understanding through the coordination of multiple perspectives in discus sion. The engine of this coordination is metacognition: it is facilitated by the opportunity for testing and comparison of perspectives provided by the questions and responses received from other participants in the discussion,  i.e., the questions and responses that exert press ure on group members to engage in thinking about the meaning of and support for their own perspectives. Using this analytical framing, we examine the reasoning moves embedded in  students’ contributions in an excerpt from a class room text discussion, in order to illustrate what building towards new understanding looks like in practice. By reasoning toward an explanation of a textual claim, students innovate a new (to them) concept that increases the ir understanding of the phenomenon at issue.  This reasoning is facilitated by their teacher’s at tention to the metacognitive opportunities afforded by each studen t’s contribution and their potential for building n ew understanding. Engaging in the routine of reasoning provides students practice on component skills within wholeclass discussions; meanwhile, when teachers make students more metacognitively aware of elements of th e process of reasoning, students also have opportunities to learn a dialogic schema for engaging in reasoning which is a key for internalization.

## Case study

The following is an excerpt from a class discussion about the novel A Long Walk to Water in an elementary school classroom.

Teacher A: All right. <Claim Request> So what more are we learning about Sudan? Oh, [Student 1]? Student 1: <Claim> We know that the Dinka and the Nuer tribe don't get along. Teacher A: Okay. We know that the Dinka and the Nuer tribe don 't get along. <Evidence Request> And what tells you that? Student 1: <Evidence> Because it says they've been fighting for hundreds of years. Many people in both sides have been killed.

Teacher A: <Warrant Request> So it's not just that they don't get along. It's what else? Yes? Student 2: <Evidence> That the war never stops.

Teacher A: Yeah, that there's a war and it never stops. Yes?

Student 3: <Warrant> The reason they're fighting was because they didn't know where the Nuer land ended and the Dinka land began.

Teacher A: Okay. Very good. <Warrant Request> And what's another reason they're fighting other than the land? … What's another reason they could be fighting? [Student 4]? Student 4: <Warrant> <Claim> Water?

Teacher A: Water. <Evidence Request> What tells you that? Student 4: <Evidence> “Each tribe tried to lay claim to the areas richest in water.” Teacher A: Okay. Very good. <Warrant Request> Now how does this connect with what we already know about the country? With the Dinka and Nuer tribe? H ow does this connect? What else is going on with this country? [Student 5]?

Student 5: <Warrant> They're fighting over water because it hardly ever rains over there. The discussion begins with a claim about two groups in conflict  and the teacher’s request for support for that claim. The student who made the claim responds by referencing textual evidence for their claim. But while that evidence alone might show that the claim is right, it seems to show more than the mere fact that the t wo groups are in conflict or “don’t get along”. According to the evidence provided, not only are they in conflict, but this is a conflict that has been ongoing for centuries despit e heavy losses on both sides. There must be more to  the story, more context that can explain what the evidence means.

This expansion of context  is exactly what Teacher A asks for. In response, another student provides a restatement of the evidence already provided (“the war never stops”) and a third student provides an i mportant part of the lacking context: there is some uncertai nty about the territorial boundaries of the  two groups. But note that this is not a very useful bit of context on it s own. Territorial uncertainty does not necessarily lead to conflict and there are plenty of examples of territorial dis putes that do not issue in bloodshed. For example, from 1978-2022 there was an ongoing territorial dispute between Canada and Denmark regarding Hans Island in the Atlantic Ocean, but it is hard to imagine that armed conflic t over this dispute could ever have occurred. There  must be some further bit of context which explains why terr itorial uncertainty has led to armed conflict in this case – i.e., further context which will complete the warrant. Th is is provided by a third student, who  (aided by the teacher’s reminder of the relevant textual information)  points out that the two groups are fighting not jus t over land, but over land containing rich sources of water.

The class has now collaboratively constructed a com plete inference unit: the Dinka and Nuer are in conflict (claim), the text says that the two groups have been fighting constantly for hundreds of years (evidence), and there is a territorial dispute over r egions that are rich in water (warrant). Note, however, this is not a full explanation of the original claim. Why does it matter which regions provide access to water? There are  plenty of rich sources of water all over the world which have  not resulted in armed conflict; Lake Superior is t he largest single source of fresh water in the world and yet h as never provoked military action. In what context does the claim “their dispute over water led to fighting” ma ke sense? The warrant of this inference unit thus  comes into question and the teacher now treats it as a claim b y asking for evidence: “what tells you that [the gr oups are fighting over water]?” The student who originally p rovided the warrant, now treating it as a claim, ci tes textual evidence in favor of the claim. But once again the evidence is not enough to meaningfully advance the explanation on its own: a claim that we do not understand but are compelled by evidence to believe is not very useful. A fifth student provides the crucial context: “it hard ly ever rains over there.” The reason that rich sou rces of water combined with territorial ambiguity can lead to centuries of conflict is that there are very few sources of water in this country, imbuing the few rich sources that do exist with life and death importance. The students have now constructed a second inference unit: there is a territorial dispute over regions that are rich in water (the warrant from the original inference unit, now treated as a claim), the text says this explicitly (evidence), this is importa nt because water is scarce in the area (warrant). This unit is not just a n additional, independent inference, but explains the inference u nit that precedes it. Not only is each student’s co ntribution taken up into an inference unit, therefore, but that unit is itself taken up into a cycle of inference units. What these five students have done here is thus not merely to connect two chains of reasoning together. By working together to reason about an initially unexplained claim about the text, they have collaboratively constructed a new (to them) concept, that of water scarcity. Out of a series of unconnected details the students previously grasped – there is a conflict, it has a long history, there is a territorial dispute, the conflict is over water-rich regions, and it hardly ever rains in this area – the students have constructed a meaningful, coherent explanation, building new knowledge by contributing to a discussion using text evidence to support an initial claim. This discussion excerpt thus features an inference cycle consisting of two units, both of which are facilitated by the teacher’s questions. These quest ions are not simply requests for further contributi on: each one is tailored to represent a specific metac ognitive opportunity presented by the student contr ibution to which it responds, an opportunity to reflect on what is being claimed, what the claim might mean, how it might be further supported, and so on. The teacher’s questions thus constitute an activit y of metacognitive modeling: they model to students the metacognition in which mature reasoners engage. In order to become better reasoners, s tudents need only follow the teacher’s example.

## Methods

In order to understand whether this framework is useful for describing improvements in classroom discussions as well as learn whether students in Teacher A’s class are int ernalizing their teacher’s metacognitive modeling, we analyzed data on student reasoning behavior and teacher ques tions in four classroom discussions facilitated by Teacher A. Teacher A was sampled from a study examining the main effects of one-on-one instructional coaching, following a 20-hour workshop (i.e., Online content-focused-coaching). In prio r work we have demonstrated a treatment effect for the eight treated teachers versus 23 control teachers in a sm all randomized control trial (Correnti et al., 2021). Teacher participants were all 4th-and 5th-grade literacy teachers from the same district. 61% of students in the study schools were from low-income households. 73% of students identified as Latinx, 15% as Caucasian, 7% as African American, 4% as multiracial, and 1% a s Asian or Pacific Islander.  The focus of the coaching was on improving text discussions b y promoting student-centered discussion following Questioning the Author and using Accountable Talk m oves (Zook‐Howell et al., 2020). The coaching did not have an explicit focus on argumentation, but rather  sought to develop teachers’ adaptive expertise at facilitating text-based discussions (Walsh et al., 2020). Thus, as with all of our treated teachers,  we expected them to be focused on prompting for student contributions and developing their facilitation skills to help studen ts make meaning from complex texts as was observed in the excerpt above. Classroom discussion recordings  were co llected at baseline and during  several successive coached sessions throughout the rest of the academic year. C oaching cycles were roughly one month apart  each and concentrated in the spring. Our longitudinal data allows us to examine trends over time in a single classroom case study. We coded each transcript for the parts of inference  outlined above, inference units and cycles, teache r requests for claim, evidence, and warrant. Additionally, we also coded for spontaneous inferen ce units. A spontaneous inference unit was defined as an individual student spontaneously providing a claim and its evidence or warrant without being prompted by  an evidence or warrant request. The purpose of the case study is to understand whether and how well the coding scheme d emonstrates patterns of improvement in class discussions by capturing, marking and counting the instances of  both collaborative and spontaneous student reasoni ng. In order to establish inter-rater reliability, the two rat ers independently coded for inference units and spo ntaneous inference units across random selections of 21 disc ussion transcripts from the original study  with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) =.84. The two raters then independently coded one of the four transcripts (25% of the sampled transcripts) with ICC =.78.

## Discussion

While it is obvious that the episode of reasoning in the excerpt above has been mediated by the teacher’s questions, one point of th e close analysis of the excerpt  (taken from coaching session 2) is to reflect on the teacher’s role during facilitation of text-based discussions. The teacher is neither merely asking students to co ntinue their thoughts in a completely open-ended way nor engaging in the typical closed-ended questioning typical of low-quality discussions. As the above analysis demonstr ates, their questions are in each case appropriate to the kin d of metacognitive opportunity each student’s contrib ution poses. As the students slowly build the infer ence cycle in this excerpt, at each juncture there is the possibility that the next step in the inference will not be made: without proper facilitation students could fail to see the next move, and the wrong question from the teacher could lead the students in an entirely different direction. The inferences the students make are made possible by the teacher’s awareness at each step of the shape of the potential inference(s) that could unfold with the right promp ting. The teacher in our case study is able to take in the content of  what the students said but also consider the potential structure of the inferences into which that content could be integrated.. With each student contribution, the teacher is sufficiently aware of the potential for coordinating students’ contributed perspectives into a complete inference unit. What is perhaps most impressive about  this teacher is her ability to, in-the-moment  pose question s or responses that capitalizes on exactly the metacognitive opportunity posed by what a student just contributed. This pattern of teacher attention is a  potential source of student development in learning to reason. The teacher in the above excerpt is benefiting students in two ways. First and most obviously, they are facilitating the construction of new understanding by helping studen ts to coordinate their various contributions in such a way as to build an inference. As we saw, students are dire ctly learning in the moment by being guided toward the construction of new conceptual understanding. But s tudents are also being afforded the opportunity to learn how to coordinate their own perspectives – or to put it differently, how to pay attention  to their own and their peers’ contributions.2 If this suggestion is correct and students in this teacher’s classroom are learning to pay attention t o the structure of an inference as it is distributed acro ss their own and their peers’ contributions, we sho uld be able to see evidence that students are internalizing this ability by looking to see whether they begin to produce inference units spontaneously, without teacher facilitation, as they spend more time with teachers who are modeling such attention. Students in the classrooms we observed c ontributed more spontaneous inference units as the academic year progressed. While there is not data here appropriate to making a causal claim, the observations are consistent with the hypothesis that teachers’ modeling of perspectival coordination helps students to internalize and replicate that behavior. As a next step, further work should explore whether this pattern holds over a greater n umber of discussion transcripts across a greater number of t eachers, as well as whether there are statistically  significant differences between the number of spontaneous infere nce units observed in classrooms of teachers receiv ing treatment as part of the instructional coaching study and those observed in control classrooms. The case study presented here provides preliminary evidence that teachers who exhibit a certain form of attention to their students ’ inferential contributions thereby facilitate studen t internalization of reasoning ability. Anecdotally, as we continue to examine transcripts from treated teachers, we suspect that while most t reated teachers quickly increase their number of claim req uests, there is variation in the degree to which ev idence requests and warrant requests successfully prompt students to complete an inference unit. If this is true, it suggests that teacher professional development (including instructional c oaching) should include a focus on training teachers to notice the metacognitive opportunities posed by student discussion contributions and the w ays that those contributions can be coordinated to produce new group understanding.
