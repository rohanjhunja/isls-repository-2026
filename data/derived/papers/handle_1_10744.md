# Narrative Interviews for Studying Teacher Sense-making of Equity

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While equity has been an emphasis of teacher education research for several decades, there remains a need for studies that investigate how teachers make sense of equity. In this study, I employed a narrative framework to investigate what educative experiences prospective teachers draw upon when making sense of equity. This short paper describes the methods and underlying theory of that work, as well as initially findings.

## Introduction

Equity has long been a focus of research in education, with teachers being identified as agents of change (LadsonBilling, 1993). The field of teacher education has taken up this work and as a result, there exists a body of research dedicated to the investigation of how to teach teachers to contribute to a more equit able education system (e.g., Ajayi, 2017; Davilla, 2011; Moore, 2008; Parker, Bartell, & Novak, 2015). Often, these studies take up an equity framework, provide teachers with instructional activities to support the development of understanding and practice to support that framework in the classroom, and report out on the relative uptake and change in teachers’ beliefs, attitudes, and practices.

While many of these studies focus on how to provide experiences that teach teachers equity, less work has been done that foregrounds teachers as learners of equity (Horn & Garner, 2022). Philip (2011) offe red an approach to teacher learning of equity that her ter ms “ideology in pieces” and builds on diSessa’s (19 93) notion of conceptual change. Turner and colleagues (2013) offered a hypothetical learning trajectory for pros pective teachers’ learning of how to draw upon students’ cultural a nd mathematical knowledge bases in their lesson planning. Each of these studies began with teachers  as learners, either of racial justice or of funds of knowledge (Civil, 2007). Similarly, additional research is ne eded that foregrounds teachers as learners of equity and has an emphasis on how they make sense of equity.

Theoretical framing For this study I investigated prospective teachers’ sense-making of equity. To define “equity,” I borrow from Gorski and Swallwell  (2023) to mean a broad range of “anti-oppression and justice efforts, commitments, and conditions” (pg. 20). Oppression often operates sim ilarly along multiple dimensions (e.g., race, class, gender, language status, religion) and as such, a teacher’s  sense-making of equity candevelop along different axes o f oppression, as they come to identify, understand, and address the system(s) of oppression. This broader theoretical framing allows me to consider teachers’ sense-making of equity as inclusive of classism, racism, cis-normativity, and other forms of oppression.

The study employed two complementary and intertwined theoretical frameworks: narrative and experience. At its core, narrative work elicits sto ries from research participants, with the assumption that individuals live “storied” lives and that we create stories to make sense of our experiences (Casey, 2017; Connelly & Clandinin, 1990; Creswell, 2014; Marshall & Rossman, 2016). As such, narrative framing is particularly useful for education research focused on  individuals’ sense-making. This is because “human beings think, percei ve, imagine, and make moral choices according to narrative structures.” (Sarbin, 1986; quoted in Carter, 1993, pg. 7) As individuals make sense of experiences, they do s o narratively by  organizing a story that places disparate elements in relation to one another. Carter (1993) states that a story “is a theory of something. What  we tell and how we tell it is a revelation of what we believe.” (pg. 9) As an individual constructs a story, they make choices— how to structure the story, what elements to privil ege, how each element is framed, where the emphasis  should lie, what tone to use, etc.—that all convey how the individual is making sense of these elements of the experience. The story tells us not just what happened in the experience, but also what the individual values within and about that experience, what they made of that experience,  and how it connects (or doesn’t) to other experien ces. In so doing, individuals create meaning and produce knowledge of their experiences and the world around them. In this way, narrative technique makes an individual’s sense-making visible. The way in which narrative makes sense-making visible is precisely why a narrative frame is a promising tool for the current moment in research on teacher learning of equity.

Similarly, we must consider how and why experiences come to be included in a narrative. Experiences, as related by individuals through stories, take on particular meaning—specifically, the meaning the individual attributes to it. As Connolley and Clandinin (1990) state, “Narrative is a way of characterizing the phenomena of human experience and its study which is appropriate  to many social science fields.” Those experiences that are drawn upon in building a narrative are those that h ave been productive for the individual in their sen se-making. This is similar to what Dewey (1938) describes as a n “educative experience.” In this way, experience i s complementary to narrative in that the relating of experience by individuals displays the sense-making the individual has done about the ir educative experiences and their larger implication. The lived experience can be considered the source material from which individua ls relate their interpretation of these experiences  in their personal narratives.

In the current study, the relating of experiences t hrough narrative offers us a wider view of what individuals dr ew on when making sense of equity. The view is not u nlimited, because it is confined to those experiences that the individual: 1) has lived through, 2) has remained with them such that they have interpreted it to attach meaning, and 3) that the individual continues to draw on in the construction of personal narratives. Over the course of their life, an individual may have ma ny experiences, but not all experiences are memorab le or meaningful. Not all experiences are drawn upon in considering new knowledge or topics. Not all experiences are placed in relation to other experiences in order to  make sense of the world. The experiences that rise  to the level of inclusion in a personal narrative, then, are imp ortant residue of sense-making. In this sense, the experi ences referenced in the narratives are educative (Dewey, 1938). Building on the existing research and employing narrative and experience as my theoretical frameworks, I investigated the following research questions: What experiences do prospective elementary teachers draw on to make sense of equity?

## Methods

The study was conducted at a large, public university i n the southwest region of the United States. Eight participants were recruited from an elementary teacher preparation program that had a social justice focus. Th e study occurred during the final program year. The recruitment script did not explicitly use the word “equity.” All participants self-identified as women and were between the ages of 21 and 22. Five participants self-identified as White, one as Jewish (in addition to being White), one as Mexican, and one as Latina/Hispanic.

## Data collection

The data collection spanned from the end of the fal l semester of the participants’ senior year into the spring semester (which was solely focused on student teaching). Data was collected through a series of three open-ended interviews, blending the interview techniques of Se idman (2013) and Jovchelovitch and Bauer (2000). Ea ch interview was slightly different in aim and built on the previous interview s. In this way, the structure offered an opportunity for building rapport with participants, as well as for participants to review the story/stories produced by prior interview(s).

The interviews were primarily narrative in nature (Jovchelovitch & Bauer, 2000). Narrative interviews are characterized by a prompt (provided by the rese archer) followed by ample uninterrupted talk time f or study participants. The first interview prompt was: I am interested in hearing the story of how you view equity and what caused you to view equity as you do. The story might include things you learned in formal settings like classes, but it might also include experiences you had at work, with friends, things that happened in the world, things that happened to you personally, or something I hav en’t named. I really just want to hear the larger narrative—your story. What’s most important is that you tell me the story that is true to you.

This prompt was followed by long, uninterrupted time for the participant to tell their story. When there was a clear coda, I asked clarifying questions, constraining my  questions to the content that was already raised b y the participant. This structure was intentional to offer as much freedom and space to the participants to tell their story as was feasible, and to constrain the content of the resultant data to only that which was produced by the research participant in response to the prompt.

The first interview was audio recorded, then transc ribed using software. The transcribed text was primarily a long text of the participant speaking f ollowed by interaction to clarify details. To move the oral/transcribed audio data to a written story, I a dded structure like punctuation and paragraph break s to convey where topics shifted or tone changed. I removed my questions and filler words and married the clarifyi ng responses into the relevant portions of the text. This created the participant’s story. In the second interview, I supplied the participant  with their story printed out and a red pen. I aske d the participant to please read over the story and feel free to make any edits they felt would make it more  their voice and reflect their story. The seco nd part of the second interview mirrored the first interview. I again gave the participant a prompt and ample uninterrupted time t o talk. The second prompt was: Please tell me the story of how you understand equity and teaching, as well as how you came to hold this view.  Again, the interview was audio recorded and transcribed and converted into a story using the same methods employed for the first interview. This time the story developed from the interview se cond interview was added to the first story to crea te a larger story.

In the third interview, I supplied the participant with the newest iteration of story printed out and a red pen. I asked the participant to please read over th e story and feel free to make any edits they felt w ould make it more their voice or better reflect their story. I asked any lingering clarifying questions, and then asked demographic questions—age, gender, race/ethnicity, and placement in fieldwork and student teaching. Any edits they made, or clarifying details they provided, were added to the story, and ultimately, the story was now complete and ready for analysis.

The interviews were conducted sequentially and independent of one another. They were distributed over three to six months, with a week to several months between interviews. Each interview lasted 20 to 75 minutes.

## Early data analysis

To address t he research question, I used experience as the analytical frame. Experience was conceived of  as something the individual lived through. This includ ed things that might not colloquially be considered  an experience, as they may be passive, internal, or se cond-hand (e.g., reading a book, hearing about someone else’s experience). I looked within each story for places where experiences were invoked.

Using Tesch (1990), I coded the experiences referen ced in each story. My preliminary codes included experiences with coursework, experiences in fieldwo rk, and experiences from childhood.  I made note of experiences that did not fit these preliminary codes and considered whether additional codes should be developed and applied. New codes were created to reflect the experiences referenced in the stories, and the resultant codes were added to the initial list. Through iterative coding, codes were refined, combined, or split as necessary. At the completion of iterative coding, there were ten distinct codes. To further analyze how the study participants made sense of equity in narrative, I analyzed the storie s for narrative techniques using Riessman’s (2008) st ructural narrative analysis. This analysis generated a list of narrative techniques employed by the study participants within and across the stories.

## Early findings

The narrative research method was productive for the research que stion in the study, as it kept the data close to the research participants and was generative. For example, at the two interviews where participants were presented with a draft of their story from earlier interviews and asked to edit for content and tone, very few edits were made. Further, the only edits that were made were to remove filler words or  to correct spelling of names. None of the study participants felt the need to edit the contents of the story that had been co-constructed. All participants felt that the stories produced through the methods detai led here accurately reflected their equity stories  in tone and content.

In addition, the stories created were useful for in vestigating sense-making of equity.  Across the eight stories, I found ten categories of experiences that became “experience types.” The ten experience types that were drawn on in equity narratives were:  experiences in teacher preparation coursework, expe riences in teacher preparation fieldwork, experiences as a student, experiences from childhood and/or with family, experiences with media, experiences related to the participants’ pro ximity to education p rofessionals, experiences related to relationships, experiences at work, personal experi ences with inequity, and others’ experiences with i nequity. These experience types covered educative experience s within formal teacher education, and outside of f ormal teacher education, with many experiences outside of education altogether. Lastly, narrative features were productive in  foregrounding the sense-making of study participants. For example, many of the equity narratives explicitly d escribed how a particular experience caused a chang e in their understanding of equity and therefore their equity story. Participants would say something similar to, “After that, I thought about equity differently.” These descript ions of shifts in understanding served to both high light experiences that had been educative for making sense of equity, as well as how the understanding of equity shifted through that sense-making.

## Conclusion

This study investigated the educative experiences of prospective elementary teachers in making sense of equity through use of narrative inquiry. This method allowed me to identify ten experience types that were educative for sense-making of equity. While scholars like Tuner and col leagues (2013) and Philip (2011) have begun to char t how teachers’ understanding of equity can shift or develop over time, narrative inquiry offers a tool for looking more closely at those processes. The methods descri bed here have the potential to help researchers inv estigate which experiences—both in and out of formal education settings—are educative for prospective teachers’ sensemaking of equity. Further, analysis of the narrativ e techniques employed offers opportunity to investi gate how understanding is built. While further research is c ertainly warranted, these early findings demonstrat e that this method can be generative for understanding teacher sense-making of equity.
