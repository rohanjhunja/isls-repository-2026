# Interplay of Form and Function in Learners’ Re-Designed Games within a STEM Classroom

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Re-designing games facilitates interest-driven learning and immerses learners in systems thinking. However, there are limited studies into how formal and functional aspects of designing tabletop games inform and influence learners design decisions and help them learn in distinct ways. This study delves into this by involving third and fourth-grade students in the process of re-designing a tabletop game, Triominos. We take a mixed methods approach and investigate occasions that emergence of form influences learners’ decisions on functional aspects of their re-designed games and helps them learn in distinct ways. The research was conducted within a STEM classroom in a primary school in western Canada. Data include classroom videos, images of students' work-in-progress, and final game designs. We describe the ways through which learners translate their formal ideas into functional mechanics. Outcomes show interplay of formal and functional aspects engage learners with mathematical concepts, challenges, and connections in novel systematic ways.

## Introduction

Prior research emphasizes the use of game design to  facilitate interest-driven learning and engage students in systems thinking (Baradaran Rahimi & Kim, 2023; 202 1; 2019). Games require diverse elements, such as, mechanics, dynamics, and aesthetics (Hunicke, LeBlanc, & Zubek, 2004). Players involve with the form, function, and experience  of the game  in different ways, and progress  by managing resources, making decisions, and responding to the consequences of their actions (Salen & Zimmerman, 2006). Learners, while constructing games, make numerous decisions, encounter unexpected outcomes, and address emerging goals and issues. Game design process goes beyond that to fosters unique connections between learners and the  artifacts they create, as well as the knowledge they explore  (Kafai, 2006; Kim, 2018; Kim & Reeves, 2007). Game play and design inherently involve mathematical thinking akin to everyday practices (Stevens et al., 2006). How such inherent mathematical thinking emerge in a game re-design project for STEM learners in grade three and four? Is it the form that facilitates the inception of function and emergence of mathematical thinking for such young learners?

In this paper, w e consider the emergence of  mathematical meanings  from the interplay of form and function, which influence learners’ engagement in mathematics and systems thinking. We investigate and count the choices made by learners in terms of form to flow the function and experience of their game and vice versa. We take the agentic view into the artifacts that le arners’ designs to inquire if these choices are driven by mathematics and systems thinking (Kim, Rasporich, & Gupta, 2019). We advocate learners’ designing of tabletop games —especially when the form flows function — for the potential offered to learners to think and learn with their evolving artifacts.

## Theoretical framework

Louis H. Sullivan, American architect popularized the  maxim “form follows function”  in the late 19 th and early 20th century. Since then, it has been considered as a design prin ciple suggesting the form (encompassing visual and physical aspects) of an object should primarily relate to  and serve its function (i.e., intended use or purpose) (Ching, 2023). This maxim is often associated with modernist and minimalist d esign movements, emphasizing simplicity, practicality, and the elimination of un necessary ornamentation  (Giedion, 2009). Although "form follows function" remains relevant and influential, contemporary design approaches often explore a more nuanced relationship between form and function. Some design ers prioritize aesthetics and user experience along side functionality, recognizing that the emotional and p sychological impact of design elements can also enh ance the overall usability and satisfaction (Norman, 2013). This conveys the idea that form might have an underrepresented role in the interplay of form and function depending on the context where form flows and informs certain design decisions in relation to function. One such contexts can be one where learners take agency to re-design a game in a STEM classroom. Recent literature in mathematical education acknowledges the importance of recognizing the agency and distributed nature of designing artifacts, not only for enhancing conceptual understanding but also for collaborative learning (Roth, 2016). Moreover, learners' gestures, movements, and the use of sym bols and materials serve as expressions of mathematical idea s (Alibali & Nathan, 2012; Ferrara, 2014). This age ntic perspective aligns with the notion that form and function of the artifact are part of the assemblage for meaningful mathematical activities (de Freitas & Sinclair, 201 3; Nemirovsky & Ferrara, 2009).  Taking an agentic view and concentrating on interplay of form and function throughout the game re-design project in a STEM classroom will guide our research design and discussions in this paper.

## Research design

This study took place in an urban elementary school  in west Canada during the second year of a design-based research (DBR) project (Hoadley & Campos, 2022). Th e research aimed to involve students in mathematics learning through game design, achieved by collabora tively creating lessons with teachers and engaging in reflective inquiry (Collins, Joseph, & Bielaczyc, 2004). In our joint design sessions, teachers actively participated in game playing, redesigning games, and brainstormi ng ways to involve their students in similar activities with researchers. Ms. Lennox, who taught STEM classes across different grades, decided to focus on a combined grade 3/4 class for this project. Early in the school year, students explored various games for mathematics learning. The teacher and the grade 3/4 students chose Triominos as the game to redesign in their STEM class. Triominos is a two to four player game where each player starts wi th 7-9 triangular tiles out of 56, each bearing three nu mbers (ranging from 0 to 5) in its corners. Points are ea rned by matching two numbers on one side of the tri angle, and the first player to reach 400 points wins the game.  The learners' task was to transform Triominos into  a paper-crafted game, allowing exploration of different forms (e.g., shapes, numbers, and symbols) and function (i.e., modifying the game rules). Over a span of six weeks, involving two or three 45-minute sessions per week, we gathered ethnographic data through observation notes, classroom video recordings, and photographs documenting the students' evolving game designs. During group d iscussions, Ms. Lennox occasionally used a small ac tion camera. In this study, we examined how interplay of form and function guided learners' design choices leading to diverse mathematical challenges during the iterativ e process of materializing their game concepts. In itially, we pinpointed instances where groups made formal or functional decisions. Subsequently, we analyzed how learners incorporated their formal and functional decisions  into the assemblages of their mathematical and game-making practices. We counted the number of times that formal decision s guided and flow the functional decisions and vice versa to find which way is more occurring in the context of our study.

## Findings

Initially, students generated individual concepts f or redesigning Triominos after discussing potential modifications to the game. They explored diverse sh apes, such as squares, rhombuses, circles, stars, a nd right triangles, as well as different numbers or  symbols, including shapes, letters, and symbols fro m Pokémon. Ms. Lennox then organized students into groups of three to four based on the similarities in their individual game ideas. Out of 42 learners 12  groups were created. Five groups (41.6 %) focused on formal, four groups (33.3%) concentrated on functional, and three groups (25%) centralized both aspects to redesign their games. However, we observed that those three groups prioritized form to function when they were to make decisions for their games. For example, learners had ideas to change the tile shape from triangle to rectangle  and put earning paper money as a new function in designing Number-ominos. Yet, putting these ideas together was challenging f or learners as they did not have a proper rule for connecting these ideas. So, they prioritize form and changed the tile shape and used the same function in Triominos to match the numbers  written on tiles and earn paper money as a secondary function to the matching. As such, in those situations form came first in the  interplay of form and function. As such, for eight groups from flow the function  through out the game redesign process. This means 57.1% of individual learners in the class with tendency to begin with form whereas 42.8% with tendency towards function. Formal choices had a substantial impact on the mathematical experiences of each group. In the subsequent sections, we present our discoveries from two groups wherein form was initially changed (i.e., Squareominos and Rhombinoes – Wild Edition) and one group wherein function was initially changed (Riominos). As it follows, we provide a brief overview of the prevailing patterns observed in the implementation of their redesign ideas. We observed a deepening utilization of shapes and t heir patterns in students' designs over time. When formulating rules, they considered how to arrange t heir game pieces to create various shapes (e.g., sq uares into larger squares, right rectangles into squares or rhombuses) and devised corresponding rules for these shapes (e.g., earning bonus points or paper money). The group Rhombinoes-Wild Edition (led by Kia, Dennis, Aron) utilized rhombuses in their game. Riominos (led by Kia, Dennis, Aron), one of the groups, had the opportunity to test their game multiple times and refine their rules. While exploring the rules related to shapes, Riominos opted for triangles, akin to the original Triominos rules. In this version, matching all three sides rewarded players by adding the values of all three corners of the placed tile when formin g a hexagon. However, their illustration inaccurate ly depicted seven triangles. They did not explore the possibility of creating other shapes, such as larger triangles, rhombuses, or parallelograms, which do not require matching three corners. The introduction of a larger square (e.g., 2×2) in Squareominos (led by Yara, Melanie, Albert, Tim) became a common occurrence, involving the matching of three corners. The corresponding rule stipulated that pla yers would earn points by totaling the values of th e four tiles. However, during interviews, the group realized that  they had not excluded other shapes (e.g., rectangles or 3×3 squares), allowing players to potentially score sig nificantly higher points. In the case of the Rhombinoes group, they devised rules for their rhombus pieces, reward ing players for creating two distinct shapes requir ing the matching of three corners: hexagons and hexagrams (or 6-pointed stars). These three groups' game  re-design processes intricately intertwined with their explor ation of shapes and the rules associated with them.  The initial choice of shapes influenced their decisions and spurred their mathematical exploration of various geometric forms. The groups recognized the implications of using fou r-cornered shapes (rhombuses and squares) compared to three-cornered shapes (triangles). Concurrently, they exp lored the choice between numbers and letters or other symbols, as well as the variety needed for their pieces. Following the triangular shape of Triominos, members of the Riominos group experimented with various options early in the process (Figure 1a). Initially, they aimed to create a game incorporating a mix of words, operations, and symbols. Eventually, they settled  on a combination of letters, symbols, and numbers, resul ting in a game that required fewer pieces but followed a very similar logic t o Triominos. They utilized two groups of symbols, w ith three symbols in each group being matchable (Figure 1b; clockwise from top left: fish shape, f, z, 0-zero, I-French fries, III-zebra). One group included f, fish, and French fries, while the other featured z, zero, and zebra. Points were earned by matching the symbols as shown in Figure 1b. The rightmost piece in Figure 1c demonstrates a player matching "f" with fish, gaining 8 points for matching two "f" (4 points eac h). Although the number of points they assigned mig ht relate to the difficulty of matching (e.g., having fewer p ieces with French fries symbol), they were un able to clearly explain this aspect during the interview.

Squareominos opted to use four distinct shapes as their symbols (stars, squares, triangles, circles) after encountering challenges with using numbers (0-10). They assigned values based on the number of si des each shape had (i.e., star = 10 points, square = 4 points, triangle = 3 points, circle = 1 point). Despite the limited number of symbols, they managed to create a playable game after this modification. Conversely, the Rhombinoes group faced difficulties in creating all the pieces when they decided to use numbers 1-6 on their rhombus pieces. This group had to systematize their crafts, considering the necessary number of cases, which proved challen ging due to the variations needed for four corners and six numbers, as well as the complexity of the craft work (e.g., cutting the pieces to the right size and inscribing numbers). Observing other groups' approaches, they devised the idea of introducing wild cards that could be placed anywher e during the game, instead of generating all possib le combinations. They also incorporated "Wild Edition" into the game title. While the use of symbols was a learner's choice, these choices might not have been thoroughl y contemplated. However, the learners' ongoing craf ting activities and playtesting with their partially completed game facilitated their design growth.

## Discussion and conclusions

The outcomes from this study, suggest that from flow the function in majority of the games re-designed by learners in our STEM classroom. As discussed, in addition to cases that learners explicitly prioritized form (e.g., shapes, numbers, and symbols) to accommodate function (i.e., modifying the game rules), it was explicitly valued by learners in cases that function was prioritized (e.g., Riominos). In both cases formal aspects could flow function and help learners  make design decisions. Findings offer insights into  how the interplay of form and function in the processes of re-designing tabletop games could encapsulate mathematical concepts. This was mainly influenced by the selection of the original game, leading to diverse challenges and mathematical experiences. Our research illustrates how the interplay of form and function present learners with both mathematical and practic al challenges, extending beyond the specific mathemati cal topics under exploration. Additionally, it high lights that learners' designs are shaped by their choices and the evolving forms they adopt to flow the function. We contend that engaging in the redesign and creation of table top games not only facilitates meaningful links bet ween mathematics and design but also immerses learners in distinctive mathematical problems and practices specific to their individual games. Although we observed that form flows function in ou r study, it may have been related to factors beyond the scope of our research, such as a ge range, level of familiarity with mathematics, gameplay experience, and the choice of original game. As such, future st udies may explore and contrast cases wherein different age groups re-design an original game. Moreover, one may investigate if form still flows function in a case that the same age range re-design another original game. It will be rewarding to investigate how learners with advanced mathematics competency approach the interplay of form and function in re-designing games. A future question might be: to what extends form flows function in the process of re-designing games?
