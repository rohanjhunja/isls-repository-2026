# Teaching with Representations: How Teachers’ Perception Shift Their Science Teaching

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Representations are essential to science education. However, few studies explore teacher understanding of their implementation of representations. In particular, there is little attention to how teacher perception influences their in-classroom teaching with representations. To support teachers in using representations in the elementary science classroom, we designed the longitudinal Project, Representation: Teachers as Learners (RepTaL), which includes various professional development activities. This paper explores teachers’ perceptions of science representations that influence the way they incorporate representations in their science teaching. We analyzed six participants’ interviews and classroom recordings across the 2021-2023 academic years. The results revealed after engaging with the (RepTaL), teachers started to try various representations in their science classrooms. Another major change is the teachers would like to have students be the “owners” of the representation rather than the audience. Their in-classroom teaching also showed that they integrated what they learned from PD in their teaching without coaching in the last year.

## Introduction

Representations play an important role in scientifi c practice and, thus, science instruction (Danish & Enyedy, 2007; Ainsworth, 1999; Coleman et al., 2011). Previ ous studies have shown that representations are a p owerful tool to help students develop, refine, and communic ate their ideas. (Latour et al., 1990; Ostrowdun, 2 020). However, despite the importance of representations in science learning, there is still a gap in what we know about elementary school teachers’ understanding, percepti on, and application of representations within scien ce instruction (Danish et al., 2020, Coleman et al., 2011, Patterson & Norwood, 2004). In addition, teachers’ belief in representation may also influence their classroo m teaching (Patterson & Norwood, 2004). Thus, we de signed the Representation: Teachers as Learners (RepTaL), a multi-year project that includes professional development activities and in-classroom teaching observations in the RepTaL project. We focused on leveraging a combination of Professional Development (PD) sessions  to help teachers collaboratively build their unders tanding of representations and thus influence their in-classroom teaching practices. In our paper, we focused on the data from the end of the project (Spring 2022 to Spring 2023), and aimed to explore the following research questions: RQ1: How do teachers change their perception of representations？ RQ 2: How do teachers’ perception of representations influence their in-classroom teaching?

## Literature review

Representations in science education Representations play a pivotal role in science education (NRC, 2012). Students can use representation to simplify complex concepts, make abstract ideas tangible, and visually convey information (Gilbert,  2008; Henderson, 1999; Latour et al., 1990; Pierson et al., 2023). M ore importantly, we need to explore how teachers understand the use of representations and how that  may influence their classroom instruction. In previ ous research, scholars have explored teachers’ knowledge and beliefs about the representations (Danish et al., 2020; Humburg, et al., 2023; Krist & Shim, 2023; Lee & Gail Jones, 2018; Pierson  et al., 2023). This research also demonstrated the benefits of using different representations.

In our research, we aim to support in-service teachers in the use of representations in t heir own classrooms in any form connected to their teaching goals in different grades. Therefore, we define representations as anything that stands for something else (Palmer,1977, Danish et al., 2020). This wide range of representation could include diagrams, text, computer simulation, visual representation, and embodied representation (e.g., the use of gestures to demonstrate ideas). We intend to  give these options to in-services teachers, so they can be exposed to various representation and to choose whatever fit their own teaching goals. For example, in the setting of preschool to early primary school, there was research on representation focusing on young children using physical models, drawings, and movement-based models to help them engage with concrete representations o f abstract notions as they develop their understandin g (Anning, 1997; Pierson et al., 2023; Vujičić et al., 2020; Williams, 2022; Zangori et al., 2013). For higher grades in elementary, such as fifth grade, it’s common to have students use representation as science models, digi tal simulation, and other forms of representations to simultaneously help them see new dimensions of the content, and begin to appreciate scientific practices such as modeling (Gilbert, 2008; Henríquez et al., 2015; Sung et al., 2015). In addition, there’s increasing awareness that scie nce teachers need additional support in designing lessons with a variety of representations. It is re cognized in the field that it’s important to provid e professional development or teacher preparation programs to help expose teachers to multiple forms of representation (Danish et al,2020; Krist & Shim, 2023; Zembal-Saul et al., 2002). However, much of this work focuses on proximal measures of teacher learning and has not explored how changes in what teachers understand about representations do or do not carry over into their actual classroom  practices when they are no longer  supported by those PD efforts. (Hoban et al., 2011; Zembal-Saul et al., 2000). Our prior work established a framework for teache rs’ use of representations in science classrooms in terms of 1) what types of representations teachers may use, 2) how representations are used, and 3) why the teachers c hose a particular form of representation  (Danish et al., 2020). The present analysis extends this prior work by ask ing how the teachers who had participated in this extended professional development later influenced the teachers’ use of representations in their elementary science classrooms when they no longer had the support of one-on-one coaching.

## Method

Design of the RepTaL project The RepTaL  was built  on sociocultural theories of learning, which highli ght the role of representations in transforming one’s ongoing practices, and changing what they do, notice, and understand by using representations in meaningful activities  (Danish & Enyedy,  2007; Danish et al, 2020; Greeno & Hall, 1997). Thus, there were several assumptions: 1) representations gain their meaning from the contexts in which they are used, a nd 2) the sociocultural context of a teacher’s classroom can impact how teachers and students interpr et the features and usefulness of the representations available.

## Participants

This RepTaL project is a five-year project, but the present analysis focuses on the teachers who fully participated from the 2021 to 2023 school years  and for whom we have data about their activities af ter the PD is completed. The participants in this study are six elementary t eachers from the Midwest U.S. (n=6). Deb joined in 2018 (C1_Year1) and participated for four years, two tea chers (Pete and Zoe) joined in 2019 (C2) and partic ipated for three years, and three teachers (H arper, Luna, and Beatrice) joined in 2021 (C3_Year4) and participated for one year.

## Data collection

These participating teachers participated in profes sional development activities in the 2021-2022 academic year which included one-on-one coaching (Danish et al, 2020). In Spring 2022 (year 4), we interviewed all participating teachers about their perception of representations and potential use in Year 5. In spring 2023 (Year 5), we focused on how the teacher would use the experiences they g ained through PDs in their cl assroom. Thus, we recorded a unit of 3 to 4 classes (approximately 30 minutes) for each participating teacher’s science classroom. The teachers developed the lesson plans by themselves, with no mentoring from the research team during the semester. Each participating teacher created a self-reflection video after each class, about 3-5 minutes  in length. After each teacher’s unit was recorded, one co-author interviewed each teacher individually about their understanding of representations and their reflection on their own teaching with representations. Instrument and data sources In the RepTaL, we designed a post-interview instrument after each year’s PD or classr oom recording.  The instrument covers topics such as 1) representationa l forms, 2) reflection on teaching with representat ions, 3) teachers’ emotions about the science unit, and 4) ways to incorporate different kinds of ideas. We analyzed three types of video data sources:1) Year 4 interview dat a (2022 spring), 2) Teachers’ classroom recordings (2023 spring), and 3) Year 5 Post interview data (2023 spring).

## Data analysis

We applied thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2021) to analyze teachers’ interviews. For the Year 4 postinterview, we focused on  the forms of representations that teachers were fam iliar with, and how their understanding of representation changed. Then for t he classroom recording, we focused on how many type s of representations were used, and how this representation was used. Three research graduate students' content logged in all 20 sessions of classroom recording in Atlas. ti23. Each session was about 30-45 minutes, we started by creating quotations by event, such as introduction,  switching groups, and doing science activities. Th en for each quotation, we applied the RepTaL coding framework (Danish et al., 2020) to 1) what types of representation were used, 2) how the representations were used, and 3) who was the “owner” or author of the representation (e.g., the students, teachers, textbook).

## Findings

RQ1: How do teachers change their perception of representations？ To answer our first research question, we first ana lyzed teachers’ year 4 interview data to identify h ow they felt their ideas about representations had changed. We f ound teachers’ perceptions changed around 1) forms of representations, 2) purposes of using representatio ns, and 3) applications of representations (includi ng when and how to use them).

What: when asked how their understanding of representatio ns had changed over the years, half the teachers explicitly noted changes in their understa nding. In the past, the participating teachers beli eved representations were limited to graphs, tables, and  diagrams. Now they know more forms of representati ons that can be used in classrooms. For example, Amy (a teac her who has only participated in the project for on e year), said, “I definitely have learned more about what cl assifies as representation…And like  videos, pictures can be representations too.” Why: Moreover, we found that teachers' perceptions of th e purposes of using representations in their classrooms also changed. The participating teachers  mentioned they used to think representations were to show information to students. But now, they believe that representations can serve multiple purposes in their classroom (7 teachers), and they can use a combination of rep resentations in their classroom (7 teachers). This was an important feature for our PD, as we worked closely with teachers to help develop the practice of helping students build knowledge with representations and through representing their ideas. Who is the “owner”?  In Year 4 interviews, we also found that teachers h ad reflected on the different roles that students might take up with respect to representations. Half of the participating teachers’ understanding of representations appears to have shifted to highl ight the value of having learners create or co-create representations during scientific inquiry.   In the past, they considered representations to be something teachers created before class to show key ideas to the students. But now, they indicated an interest in taking a “step back” (2 quotes) and having students lead in creating rep resentations (12 quotes). Teachers who would like t o have students take the lead also mentioned that when stu dents contribute and construct the representation b y themselves, they have the authorship, can explain the ideas, and better use this representation in later units. These ideas also indicate that teachers' application of r epresentation in their classroom has been changed to a more dynamic approach: representation is not a product  to show ideas but a dynamic process of creation in which students can participate in. In addition, we asked teachers what they meant by “students lead” or “take responsibility” in their science classroom. Harper (C3), who taught kindergarten used her bee unit as an example to explain how she gave students opportunities to try out and experience the pollination process. In her interview, she mentioned that it is important to have students interact with the content instead of the teacher showing students the content (see Excerpt 1). She has also used her Cheeto’s activities as an example. In the Cheeto activities, she let students use th eir hands to transfer the snack Cheetos. As students noticed the leftover cheese dust on their hands, they soon understood how bees accidentally pollinate flowers.

## Discussion and conclusion

In this paper, we explored how teachers’ perception s of representations and representational activitie s influence their science classroom teaching. Thus, we analyzed  interview data from 2022 to 2023, with over 20 ses sions of real classroom recording. The results reveal ed that with the RepTaL PD, teachers’ perception of 1) forms of representation to use and 2) students’ role in usin g representation in science classrooms has been cha nged. With year 4 interview data, teachers were more willing to use multiple forms of representation and have students create models. Furthermore, teachers not only said they would use more representations, but based on our video they did so in their classrooms and felt that this was productive for their learners. Furthermore, as the teachers reflected on these moments, they indicated that they valued stud ents’ ownership of the representations and their en gagement with them.

For year 5, teachers were doing class planning by t hemselves. We are glad to see that they were able t o transfer what they’ve learned from the PD sessions into their teaching. Our recording of their classro om showed that 1) teachers were able to use a combination of representations in their classroom, and 2) they designed different activities to support students in creating represen tations. This matches what the teachers intended to  do moving forward as they stated in the Year 4 interview. The se findings showed that with some support in recognizing the potential utility of representations in their class rooms, teachers appear enthusiastic about their pot ential. This suggests there is value not only in an increasing f ocus on representations in science education PD but potentially in pre-service teacher training as well. For future scienc e professional development, we consider in-classroom observation as a way to capture teachers’ takeaways  from PD sessions. In our data, we found that their teaching practice mat ches their interview data, which provides robust ev idence of how their understanding turned into practice.
