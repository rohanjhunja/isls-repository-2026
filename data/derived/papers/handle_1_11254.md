# Impact of AI Literacy on Student-AI Interactions on Academic Writing

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Advancements in Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) provide an opportunity to evolve student-AI interactions (SAI). Research studying the nature and effects of SAI on learning with students with differing characteristics, such as AI literacy levels. This study examines how students with varying AI literacy engage with AI and whether these differences impact task performance by analyzing three data sources (think-aloud protocols, screen recordings, and chat histories) produced by 36 Chinese graduate students using a GenAI writing system. Epistemic network analysis (ENA) was applied to uncover SAI patterns across students with differing AI literacy levels. Findings reveal that high AI literacy students employ collaborative approaches when interacting with GenAI, while low literacy students interact with GenAI to a lesser extent. Findings further revealed statistical differences in final task performance between AI literacy levels. This research provides insights for designing and implementing GenAI agents in writing tasks and enhancing GenAI-assisted pedagogy.

## Introduction

Academic writing is a complex process requiring pro ficiency in research, argumentation, and expression. Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) supplies students with new tools that can potentially be used to enhance their writing performance. These GenAI-powered writing tools can provide automated feedbac k on their writing to improve the overall quality of their writing (Malik et al., 2023). GenAI tools can also generate first drafts based on a learner’s ideas and provide suggestions for improvement (Črček & Patekar, 2023). However, not all students can realize these benefits on their own due to barriers to using GenAI effectively, such as a lack of guidance from educators on how to use the technology in an educat ion setting, an inability to effectively prompt AI to get appropriate results, or weak critical thinking skills that interfere with their ability to effectively assess or result in over-reliance on AI-generated content (Črček & Patekar, 2023; Malik et al., 2023). Som e students merely used AI in a linear fashion where they simply copied generated c ontent into their essays seemingly without assessin g it and, as a result, demonstrated lower performance outcomes (Nguyen et al., 2024). Previous research has shown that learners' interaction patterns and task perfor mance vary based on individual characteristics, hig hlighting the need for personalized support and interventions that account for learne r diversity (Kim et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025).

However, current research has insufficiently examined the multifacet ed dimensions of studentgenerative AI interaction (SAI), including the variance in engagement patterns amo ng students of differing characteristics (such as AI literacy levels), their  distinct approaches to task completion, the evolvi ng nature of student-GenAI relationships, and how these differential interaction patterns influence academic writing outcomes. To facilitate effective learning that acknowledges the growing import of GenAI, it is essential to understand how students interact with GenAI to complete academic w riting tasks. This will enhance our knowledge about  how learning occurs during GenAI-assisted writing, informing future GenAI-assisted instructional design to meet learner needs across diverse SAI patterns.

This study, therefore, aims to identify SAI pattern s of academic writing tasks across students with va ried levels of AI literacy and examine the differences i n academic writing performance between the identifi ed SAI patterns. This allows the researchers to unpack the SAI process in writing tasks, providing insights for designing and implementing GenAI agents for writing and the pedagogy of GenAI-assisted instruction. This leads us to pose three critical research questions to explore this phenomenon further:

1. What are the frequency distribution differences in student-generative AI interaction (SAI) amongst a

group of students with different levels of AI literacy (high/low)?

2. What are the SAI patterns related to the AI literacy levels?

3. Do writing performance differences exist between the AI literacy groups?

## Literature Review

Previous research in human-computer interaction has established that effective collaboration between humans and machines involves complex activities, including com munication, joint action, and the ability to adapt to humanaware execution (Lemaignan et al., 2017). Therefore, students' characteristics play a pivotal role in determining the effectiveness of their interaction with technology. Research exploring the outcomes of student-AI engagement in writing endeavors has progressively emphasized t he significance of students' individual traits, esp ecially their AI literacy, on writing performance (Kim et al., 2024; Kim & Lee, 2023).  For instance, studies found that students with a higher level of AI literacy tend to be profi cient in analyzing and evaluating AI content, demon strating a remarkable ability to discern the relevance and acc uracy of information retrieved from AI-powered writing assistants (Chango et al., 2021; Walter, 2024).

Moreover, these students tend to exhibit more posit ive attitudes toward AI, perceiving it as a collaborative partner rather than simply a tool (Kim et al., 2024;  Wang et al., 2023). This deeper understanding of AI enables them to utilize its features more eff ectively, resulting in higher quality  and more efficient writing outcomes. Conversely, students with lower AI literacy may str uggle to fully utilize AI's potential and even view it as a threat (Delcker et al., 2024; Hall, 2024). Against this b ackdrop, it becomes imperative to investigate SAI differences based on students' characteristics, particularly their AI literacy levels.

## Participants

Purposeful sampling was employed to recruit 36 first-year Chinese graduate students at a joint-ventured (SinoBritish) research-led international university in China that offers c ourses primarily in English. Before a one-onone experiment, all participants were asked to answer 7-point Likert-scaled questionnaires regarding AI literacy level (Long & Magerko, 2020) and assigned to either a high AI literacy (HL) or low AI literacy (LL) group corresponding to the median cutoff (5.09).

## Procedure

The SAI on a writing task was conducted via a GenAI  writing system developed by the research team, uti lizing Unity (version 2021.3.17f1), designed to operate on the Windows platform a nd employing OpenAI’s GPT-4-0613. Before the experiment, each participant was g iven instructional guides on the GenAI prompt skill s for

performing learning tasks effectively, practiced the think-aloud methods, and explored the GenAI writing systems with research assistants. Each student was then randomly assigned a writing topic. During the experiment, students were asked to think aloud and narrate their experie nces while working on a writing task with the GenAI  system. Participants took an average of 1.5 hours to complete the assignment using the GenAI tool.

## Data collection and analysis

To answer RQ1 and RQ2, we collected students’ trans cribed think-aloud protocols and video-recorded their interaction behaviors with the GenAI writing system. Consequently, the data were analyzed to examine SAI patterns in academic writing using a coding scheme grounded in the literature review  which included three dimensions: Writing activities (WA) including ideation, drafting, and interaction with AI (IA) (Malik et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2024); Meta-cognitive activities (MC) including planning, orchestration, and evaluation (Molenaar, 2022); and socio-emotional activities (SE) including positive (PE) or negative (NE) emotional responses (Chan & Hu, 2023; Kim & Lee, 2023). To answer the SAI differences between different levels of AI literacy (high/low) groups, the Chi-square test was first employed to discern the frequency differences in SAI codes (RQ1); then, the epistemic network analysis (ENA) was conducted to investigate whether and what differences during SAI process occurred in completing a writing task between the t wo groups (RQ2).  For RQ3, we collected the participants’ final written essays and conducted expert evaluatio ns using a standardized rubric to assess task perfo rmance differences between the AI literacy groups.

## Discussion and conclusion

Analysis revealed distinct Student-AI Interaction (SAI) frequency patterns  between HL and LL groups. The HL group demonstrated higher engagement across most in teraction activities, with notably higher frequenci es in planning (HL=173, LL=74), orchestration (HL=5, LL=3), and evaluation a ctivities (HL=297, LL=147). While the LL group showed greater engagement in reasoning and critiquing AI suggestions (IA4: HL=2, LL=5), rather than interacting with AI,  the HL group exhibited substantially higher frequencies in  other interaction activities (IA1: HL=78, LL=12; IA2: HL=74, LL=12; IA3: HL=48, LL=26; IA5: HL=2 8, LL=23). This pattern indicates that the HL group demonstrates collaborative skills with AI, activ ely taking time to understand its capabilities, including both strengths and limitations. They refi ne their perspectives and knowledge to gain valuabl e insights from GenAI’s suggestions, recognizing the benefits of engaging with GenAI. While this study provides valuable empirical eviden ce regarding the relationship between AI literacy levels and GenAI-assisted writing performance, contributing to both theoretical frameworks and practical AIED applications, several limitations warrant considera tion. The study's generalizability is constrained b y its sample size (n=36) and context (single graduate-level course). Future research should expand to div erse educational settings and task types while considering additiona l variables beyond AI literacy, including gender, p rior GenAI experience, academic discipline, and attitudes toward AI technology.
