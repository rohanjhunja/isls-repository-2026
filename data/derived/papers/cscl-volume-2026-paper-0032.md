Research on collaborative discussion has highlighted how individuals integrate ideas distributed among participants with their prior knowledge, thereby deepening understanding and generating new insights into a shared meaning  (Sawyer, 2014; Stahl, 2002). In CSCL contexts, collaborative  discussions are incorpo rated in diverse forms, including online scaffolding for collaboration (Zheng et al., 2022)  and holographic agents that promote group regulation (Nishimura et al., 2023). Analyses of collaborative  discussions are often grounded in the framework of knowledge convergence, which examines how a group’s understandings become shared over time (Jeong & Chi, 2007; Weinberger et al., 2007). K nowledge convergence involves recurrent movement between concrete and abstract concepts; learners construct shared knowledge by abstracting from episodes and by returning to examples to test and refine those abstractions  (Roschelle, 1992; Schwartz, 1999). Studying collaborative discussions through the lens of knowledge convergence reveals how learners transform individual understandings into shared knowledge  by abstracting from concrete experiences and jointly refining those abstractions (Dillenbourg, 1999; Jorczak, 2011). However, there remains a widely recognized need to quantify such processes in ways that connect theories of learning with computational approaches to dis cussion analysis (Jeong et al, 2014, Wise  & Schwarz, 2017). Prior work often relies on qualitative analyses that categorize utterances into a small set of predefined states, such as concrete or abstract (Wilmont et al., 2012).  Therefore, there is a lack of insight into how to determine appropriate state counts and how to systematically capture the dynamic widening or narrowing of abstractness in the discussion, rather than merely tracking directional changes between concrete and abstract states.

This study introduces a quantitative approach to analyzing knowledge convergence  by applying change point detection to lexical abstractness sequences, building on prior work that quantified the convergence based on the abstractness of words in utterances  (Nishimura & Egi, 2025). In this study, knowledge convergence is operationalized at the level of group discourse as temporal shifts in the abstractness of words used in the understanding. Our objective is to provide quantitative insights into knowledge convergence, focusing on capturing dynamic changes in both the mean and dispersion of  the abstractness throughout the discussion. As an initial validity check, we examine how the data-driven patterns align with the actual content of group discussions, thereby validating of the quantitative insights into knowledge convergence.

## Literature review

Knowledge convergence in collaboration Knowledge convergence refers to the gradual integration  of shared knowledge  in interaction, a process that oscillates between concrete experiences and abstract formulations (Roschelle, 1992).  Building on this definition, 214 research on knowledge convergence has developed along two complementary views: a process-oriented view that analyzes how convergence unfolds during interaction and an outcome-oriented view that examines convergence in post-hoc learning products (Fischer & Mandl, 2005).

From a process-oriented view, researchers examine how participants adjust the level of the abstractness in their utterances, shifting between concrete experiences and abstract concepts during interaction (Weinberger et al., 2007). Accordingly, knowledge convergence is understood as invol ving both abstraction and concretion, rather than focusing on one over the other. More specifically, prior work characterizes knowledge con vergence as an iterative, back-and-forth movement between concrete and abstract ideas, including both the generalization from concrete examples and the re-grounding of abstract knowledge through concrete instances (Wilmont, 2012; Chaparro-Moreno et al., 2023). Consistent with this view, abstract external representations such as concept maps and argument diagrams have been shown to guide collaborative knowledge convergence (Janssen et al., 2010; Kolloffel et al., 2011; V an Boxtel et al., 2000). In addition, concrete external  representations such as heuristic worked examples also promote abstraction and thereby foster knowledge convergence (Kollar et al., 2014). However, high-quality processes do not guarantee high-quality outcomes: groups may display sophisticated abstraction and still fail to share understanding across members (Barron, 2003). Related perspectives also say that the process does not ensure learning outcomes (Kreijns et al., 2003; Kirschner et al., 2009). While the importance of abstractness in the process of knowledge convergence has been recognized, researchers hold divergent views on its influence on learning outcomes. This uncertainty partly stems from the fact that most previous studies have relied on qualitative coding, without a consistent understanding of what is considered concrete or abstract. To address this issue, it is essential to establish a quantitative measure of abstractness applicable across discussions. In particular, quantifying abstractness as a continuous variable allows for capturing its mean level and dispersion over time, thereby complementing insights obtained through qualitative research. To summarize, although concretion and abstraction are known to occur throughout the process of knowledge convergence, quantitative insights into the magnitude and dynamics of shifts in abstractness remain limited. This lack of quantitative understanding makes it difficult to describe how abstractness change s and develops during collaborative interaction. Therefore, quantifying the abstractness of utterances within the flow of interaction can provide a clearer view of how learners construct and refine shared understanding during collaboration.

Recent advances in information technology have broadened the scope of quantitative analyses of how learners engage in collaborative discussions. Within this research domain, studies have primarily focused on utterances and observable actions rather than on  learners’ internal understanding processes. This focus arises because utterances and actions are directly observable and can be readily converted into digital data suitable for computational analysis. Although their interpretation may differ across indivi duals, these behaviors are consistently measurable and therefore well suited for quantitative investigation. In contrast, understanding during comprehension (Shirouzu & Miyake, 2002; Cang et al., 2024).  Therefore, most quantitative approaches to the knowledge convergence have tended to focus on externalized knowledge (Suthers et al., 2007; Dehler et al., 2011; Zheng et al., 2014) or observable actions (Zottmann et al., 2013; Xiang et al., 2024). Among quantitative studies on knowledge convergence, research on abstraction typically employs qualitative coding of utterance abstractness and analyzes transitions across abstract ness levels to examine how sessions were coded into two states, concrete and abstract, and their temporal transitions were examined (Antoniou et al., 2019). The results indicated that concrete utterances dominated during divergent phases, whereas both concrete and abstract utterances alternated during convergent phases. Subsequent research proposed a three-level coding framework comprising concrete, first-order abstraction, and second-order abstraction (Wilmont et al., 2020). In this framework, first-order abstraction refers to identifying shared patterns across concrete examples, whereas second-order abstraction involves deriving higher-order principles underlying those patterns.  Their subsequently to second-order abstractions; however, this process is cyclical rather than linear, characterized by recurring shifts between abstraction and concretization. Using the four-level model of abstraction as a framework (Blank et al., 1978),  later research likewise demonstrated bidirectional transitions among abstraction levels in preschool conversations (Chaparro-Moreno et al., 2023). Although these studies have established detailed frameworks for coding abstraction levels, three major limitations remain. First, the quantification of abstractness has not been automated, which makes it difficult to conduct cross-group comparisons or to quantify discussions in real time.  Second, because the unit of analysis is 215 confined to individual utterances, these approaches cannot capture the abstractness dynamics that unfold across multiple utterances. Third, while these studies describe hierarchical levels of the abstractness, they do not examine how the abstractness becomes more concentrated or more dispersed as the discussion progress. This distinction matters because knowledge convergence is theorized as repeated movement between concrete grounding and abstraction; a discussion phase may differ not only in its average le vel of the abstractness but also in whether concrete and abstract expressions co-occur within it. In short, previous studies have measured how high or low the level of the abstractness is, but not how wide or narrow its range becomes.  To address the first two of these limitations, our previous research introduced methods for visualizing the abstractness of words in utterances and quantifying collaborative discussions using the moving average of the abstractness (Nishimura et al., 2024; Nishimura & Egi, 2025). These methods made it possible to observe gradual shifts in the abstractness over time. However, the analyses primarily focused on mean-level trends. The dynamic concentration and dispersion of the abstractness, reflecting its dispersion, have not yet been systematically investigated. In summary, previous studies that quantified the abstractness in knowledge convergence  have mainly focused on its mean level, overlooking how it becomes more concentrated or more dispersed over time. Thus, a new quantitative framework is needed to capture both the level and variability of abstractness and reveal a finer structure of knowledge convergence.

## Quantifying the abstractness in participants utterances

Since the participants were Japanese, the abstractness of words in their utterances was quantified using the AWDJ EX dictionary (Nara Institute of Science and Technology, 2019), a large-scale lexical resource containing approximately 437,000 Japanese words. The AWD-J EX was designed for research on cognitive processing and assigns every word an abstractness rating from 1 to 5, where smaller values represent more concrete meanings and larger values indicate higher abstractness. For instance, in this lexicon, Tokyo has a rating of 1.3, city 2.0, and optimization 2.5. Notably, words with abstractness ratings above 3 often include nouns, adjectives, and verbs that are not commonly used in everyday language. Each utterance was thus converted into a numerical sequence representing the abstractness of the words it contained. The discussion corpus used in this study was drawn from sessions conducted at a Japanese university with undergraduate and graduate students majoring in science and engineering. Each of the six groups, consisting of three participants, engaged in a ten-minute collaborative discussion on the question “What is happiness?”. This topic was selected because it was familiar to all participants and encouraged them to share concrete experiences and express their ideas in their own words  (Nishimura et al., 2023). To facilitate knowledge convergence, all groups were instructed to reach a shared conclusion by the end of the discussion. Figure 1 provides an overview of the data processing pipeline used in this study.  Each participant’s speech was recorded using an individual microphone connected to a single-board computer. The resulting audio files were automatically transcribed with the Whisper API (OpenAI, 2024) and were subsequently reviewed manually to correct recognition errors. After transcription, morphological analysis was applied to the text to identify parts of spe ech. From this analysis, nouns were extracted as the primary target for abstractness measurement because nouns most directly index the conceptual objects and provide a relatively stable unit for tracing topic-level shifts in the abstractness across utterances. Each noun was then assigned an abstractness using the AWD-J EX dictionary, which rates words on a scale from 1 (concrete) to 5 (abstract). The resulting values were arranged in order of occurrence to form a numerical sequence for each utterance (e.g., [2.0, 2.2, 2.4, 2.1, 2.7, …]). These sequences provided the foundation for the subsequent quantitative analyses. 216 Figure 1 Data Processing Pipeline to Quantify the Abstractness of Words in Utterance Change point detection of the abstractness To capture changes in both the mean and dispersion of the abstractness during discussions,  we applied change point detection to numerical sequences representing abstractness.  Change point detection is a statistical method that identifies points in a time series where the underlying data distribution, such as the mean or variance, changes significantly (Aminikhanghahi & Cook, 2016). This method  has been applied in a variety of research fields, including finance, climate science, and biomedical signal analysis, to detect transitions in underlying processes or behavioral patterns (Truong et al, 2019). Its flexibility and reliability make it suitable for identifying transitions in temporal data, such as changes in the abstractness during collaborative discussions. In this study, we used the Dynp method implemented in the Python library ruptures (Truong et al., 2019). The library provides modular implementations of several algorithms for segmenting time-series data. The Dynp method requires two parameters to be speci fied: the model  parameter, which defines the assumed statistical distribution of the data, and the min_size  parameter, which sets the minimum interval length between adjacent change points to prevent over-segmentation caused by small fluctuations or noise.  In our analysis, t he model parameter was set to a normal distribution because the abstractness values were continuous and unimodal, which made this distribution appropriate for capturing gradual variations in the data. The normal model is commonly adopted in change point detection for continuous time-series data (Aminikhanghahi and Cook, 2016; Truong et al., 2019). The min_size parameter was empirically set to 20 % of the total number of nouns in each discussion. This setting was chosen to avoid detecting change points within very short segments such as individual utterances, which would make interpretation unreliable. Because the total number of nouns varied among discussions, the value of min_size  was adjusted for each dataset. To assess the robustness of this empirical setting, we repeated the analysis with min_size  values varied by ±10% and examined whether the number of detected segments remained consistent. The Dynp algorithm also requires specifying the number of change  points, denoted as K, in advance. To determine the optimal segmentation, we tested a range of K values, starting from zero and increasing up to the theoretical upper limit, which corresponds to the total number of data points divided by min_size, minus one. The optimal number of change points was selected according to the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). As a result, each abstractness sequence was divided into several segments that differed in their means or variances. Comparing these segments allowed us to examine how discussions evolved over time, for example, from concrete to abstract expressions or from narrower to broader ranges of  the abstractness. It reflects the way speakers illustrate general ideas through concrete examples: one might say they feel “happiness” when they read a “book” or drink a “coffee”. This pattern aligns with previous findings suggesting that abstract concepts are often anchored in internal states and tend to elicit utterances drawing on concrete or experiential content (Villani et al., 2022).  Figure 2 also provides  the basis of the first quantitative result, concerning the number of segments inferred from the detected change points. The optimal segmentation differed across groups: Groups 2, 3, 4, and 6 were divided into three segments, whereas Groups 1 and 5 were divided into four segments. For each discussion, the minimum segment length was set to 20% of the total number of words, allowing at most one long segments in theory. Yet, none of the series collapsed to two or fewer segments. Thus, every group showed at least two shifts in the mean or dispersion of the abstractness. This pattern reflects the iterative nature of knowledge convergence, where participants alternate between examples and generalizations rather than moving steadily in one direction. The variation in segment count across groups further suggests  that analytic interpretations should account for group-specific phase structures instead of assuming a single uniform progression.

For example, an English expression such as “time limit” may correspond to a single Japanese noun. With this caveat in mind, the following examples illustrate how participants alternated between concrete and abstract references. Group 3 initially discussed two broad categories of “happiness” (2.71): “material” (2.16) and “mental” (2.78), while also mentioning “volunteer” (1.95) as an illustrative example. In the middle segment, participants referred to the fact that one’s “stomach” (2.02) becomes empty in the “evening” (1.55), using this concrete experience to explore whether happiness has a “time limit” (2.21). In the final segment, they reached agreement that “material” (2.16) happiness is “temporary” (2.73) whereas “mental” (2.78) happiness is “lasting” (2.93), concluding that happiness is the “resolution” (2.57) of “desires” (2.94).  Group 6 followed a similar course. The middle part, the participants considered an “elderly person” (1.81) recalling the “past” (2.56) and examined how reinterpretation or idealization of the past might alter the feeling of happiness. By the end, they organized their view around the two interrelated dimensions of “value” (2.52) and “time” (2.56), proposing that happiness is shaped by how people assign value to the past and present.  Group 2 also began with an abstract discussion on how the duration of “time” (2.56) spent under “difficulty” (2.57) relates to happiness. In the middle segment, the group anchored its reasoning in Maslow’s “pyramid” (1.86) of needs (Maslow, 1943) and examined the minimal “condition” (2.65) required for happiness. Finally, they converged on the view that happiness corresponds to a “state” (2.76) in which “life” (2.44) is materially secured and stable.  Group 4 started from the fulfillment of “desires” (2.94) such as the satisfaction of “appetite” (2.38). In the middle phase, participants drew on “Instagram” (2.03) “comparison” (2.39) to discuss desires beyond the basic physiological ones. By the end, they concluded 219 that happiness requires not only the fulfillment of fundamental needs but also satisfaction with one’s “status” (2.57) and overall “state” (2.76).

In contrast, Groups 1 and 5 showed four segments rather than three.  Group 5 largely followed the Ushape pattern for its first three segments but exhibited an additional downturn in the final phase, reflecting a late re-grounding of abstract ideas in concrete considerations. At the outset, participants proposed that “happiness” (2.71) consists in the absence of “stress” (2.49), defining it as the opposite of “unhappiness” (2.73). In the middle phase, the discussion turned to more concrete examples, using “club activity” (2.02) as a reference point to compare the degree of “satisfaction” (2.73) when the “result” (2.47) of a competition was favorable or not. In the third segment, participants abstracted these situational examples into a temporal framework, comparing happiness experienced in the “present” (2.54) with that anticipated in the “future” (2.76), and concluded that being satisfied at this very moment constitutes happiness. Finally, the discussion returned to the example of “club activity” (2.02), where participants reflected on their earlier points. Thus, the final phase of Group 5 represented a reconfirmation of its conclusion, consistent with the theoretical notion of knowledge convergence. Group 1, by contrast, showed an inverted trajectory. It reached its conclusion relatively early. In the final phase, participants exchanged remarks unrelated to the discussion itself, such as checking the remaining time (two” (1.8) “minutes” (2.36) left?). Taken together, these characteristics suggest that the interpretive weight of the segments should be considered with caution.

These examples indicate that the processes of concretization and abstraction during knowledge convergence, along with  the dispersion and concentration of abstractness, can be quantitatively captured as segment-wise shifts in the mean and dispersion of abstractness identified through change-point detection. In our data, four groups exhibited a common structure characterized by a U-shaped trajectory in the mean and an inverted U-shape in the dispersion. The mid-segment, marked by lower mean and higher standard deviation, corresponded to a phase of knowledge convergence in which participants anchored their reasoning in concrete examples while collaboratively integrating more abstract conceptualizations. This pattern is consistent with prior results that knowledge convergence proceeds through iterative movement between concrete grounding and abstraction rather than a linear shift in a single direction (Roschelle, 1992; Wilmont et al., 2020; Chaparro-Moreno et al., 2023). Group 5 added a late re-grounding phase, whereas Group 1 included several remarks unrelated to the discussion. Thus, in response to the research question, the knowledge convergence process of converging abstract ideas through concrete anchoring is reflected as a decrease in the mean and an increase in the standard deviation of abstractness within the segmental structure defined by detected change points.

## Limitations

This study has some  methodological limitations.  Because the analysis dealt with only one discussion topic, the observed patterns may not fully extend to other domains of discourse, particularly those characterized by more concrete reasoning processes.  Another limitation involves the fixed parameter settings used in change point detection. Although the robustness of the results was verified within a ±10% range of the min_size  parameter, examining a wider range of parameter values or alternative segmentation criteria could further clarify the stability of the detected transitions. In addition, although AWD-J EX contains ratings for many words, the present analysis was restricted to nouns; including verbs and adjectives may yield somewhat different patterns of abstractness. The representative excerpts were used as an initial interpretive check on the detected segments, but the study did not include an independent human-coding reliability analysis. Therefore, the present results should be read as a proof of concept rather than a full validation against human coding. T he abstractness is  also language-dependent to some extent, since the lexical units used for annotation differ between Japanese and English. This means that an English expression may correspond to a single Japanese noun, or vice versa, potentially introducing small cross-linguistic differences in the computed abstractness. Future research should also include multiple discussion topics to evaluate the generality of the present findings across different collaborative contexts. We proposed a quantitative framework for analyzing the process of knowledge convergence through temporal changes in the abstractness of words in collaborative discussions.  Specifically, we modeled each discussion as a time series of abstractness and applied change point detection to identify shifts in the mean and dispersion of the distribution. Data were obtained from six triads who engaged in ten-minute collaborative discussions designed to elicit knowledge convergence.  The analysis revealed that knowledge convergence unfolds through alternating phases of concretion and abstraction, observable as distributional shifts in the abstractness. In particular, the process of converging abstract ideas through concrete anchoring was reflected as a decrease in the mean and an increase in the dispersion of the abstractness within segments defined by detected change points.  This pattern indicates that participants temporarily grounded abstract reasoning in concrete examples before re-integrating 220 them into shared abstractions. The segmentation relied on fixed parameters, such as the minimum segment length set to one-fifth of the total nouns. Although the robustness of the results was confirmed through sensitivity checks, adjusting these settings or applying adaptive criteria could enable finer-grained analysis and broaden the interpretability of the detected transitions.

Overall, our method reveals discussion intervals characterized by a wide range of abstractness, that is, intervals where abstract reasoning is anchored in concrete examples. Because the method requires only transcript data and lexical ratings, it can be used to compare phase structures across groups at relatively low annotation cost and to provide quantitative evidence for qualitative analysis. By jointly examining both the mean and dispersion of the abstractness, the approach show s patterns that would remain hidden in analyses focusing on either dimension alone. This dual focus not only extends quantitative frameworks of knowledge convergence but also deepens its qualitative interpretation, providing an integrated analytic perspective on knowledge convergence in collaborative discussions.
