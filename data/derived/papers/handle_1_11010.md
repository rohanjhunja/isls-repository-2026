# An Interdisciplinary Perspective on Student Engagement during Collaborative Science Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaborative learning is known to be effective, but not all students engage and benefit from it. Here, we applied video coding in conjunction with electroencephalography (EEG) recordings to examine how students engage in collaborative learning. Our findings indicate that students frequently alternated between on-task, off-task, and idle states. The duration of continuous on-task engagement was associated with better learning. Finally, students’ EEG brain activity during idle moments resembled off-task brain activity.

Collaboration with other students typically has a positive impact on student learning, but the effectiveness of Collaborative Learning (CL) depends on students’ level of engagement (Hijzen, Boekaerts, & Vedder, 2007). Engagement varies not only across students but also within individual students: students might be on-task in one moment and off-task in the next. However, very little is known about how frequently these fluctuations occur because engagement is an implicit process that is difficult to measure. Further, engagement consists of different dimensions, such as behavioral and cognitive, and these dimensions do not always align (D’Mello, Dieterle, & Duckworth, 2017). For example, during CL, students might appear disengaged while being cognitively engage d, and vice versa. When students are demonstrating neither on-task or off-task behaviors, it is difficult to know how engaged they are cognitively: they might be thinking deeply about the problem or mind wandering. Neuroscience methods could potentially provide insight on the underlying cognitive engagement of students in CL. R ecent advances in portable brain technologies, such as portable  electroencephalogram (EEG), now allow for the collection of brain  data in real-world classrooms (Davidesco et al., 2021). One specific metric of interest is EEG activity in the alpha band  (8-12Hz), which has been associated with lapses of attention in labbased studies (e.g., Boudewyn & Carter, 2018). In the current study, we explored student engagement in CL using a multimodal approach consisting of EEG recordings and video coding. Our research questions were: (1) How often do students switch between on-and off-task states? (2) How do engagement fluctuations relate to student learning? (3) How does students’ brain activity vary between on-task, off-task, and idle states? 36 high school students were recruited and invited to participate in a research  study that took place at the school after regular school hours. One week before the study, students completed a pre-test, where they were asked to list up to 16 cell organelles and describe their function. On the day of the study, students were assigned to groups of four and equipped with portable EEG devices (32-channel Neuroelectrics Enobio 32 5G gel sensor system). The students were instructed to collaboratively make a model of a cell  using a set of craft supplies. CL sessions ranged from 11 to 22 minutes and were followed by a post-test. The post-test was identical to the pre-test. Each group of students was captured by a single video camera. Two members of the research team watched the video recordings in 5-second segments and assigned one of the following codes to each student: (1) Idle (not engaged verbally or physically); (2) Physically on-task (quietly working on the cell model); (3) Verbally on-task (speaking with other group members about the model); (4) Physically and verbally on-task (working on the model and talking about it); (5) Off-task (either speaking or doing something not related to the task). EEG power in the alpha band was computed for each 1-sec segment of data using a fast Fourier transform and averaged based on the video codes.

Students were graded separately on listing cell organelles and describing their function. Overall, students’ grades were low, but there was a significant pre-to-post change in students’ ability to list cell organelles (from 25% ± 10% in the pre-test to 33% ± 13% in the post-test; t(35) = 2.72, p =.02). The change in describing the function of cell organelles was not significant. Analysis of video data revealed that students remained on-task for about 60% of the time, either verbally, physically, or both ve rbally and physically. Students appeared to be off-task 11% of the time and idle 13% of the time.

## Significance

Our findings suggest that students frequently alternate between on-and off-task states during CL. Off-task states are typically assumed to be detrimental for student learning, but prior work suggests that these periods can help students refocus on the task and disrupt the power dynamics in student groups, allowing more equita ble participation (Langer-Osuna et al., 2020). Yet, we found that continuous  on-task engagement is important: students who were able to stay on-task longer, benefited more from CL. Our findings also shed light on what is happening in students’ brains when they are idle. The EEG data suggest that, on average, student s’ brain activity during idle moments is very similar to being off-task. However, future research should further investigate how brain activity varies within and across idle periods.
