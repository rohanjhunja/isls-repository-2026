# Designing for Climate Justice in a Social Studies Methods Course

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper reports on a community-based design research partnership involving community-and university-based educators and researchers who came together to design for environmental justice in a Social Studies Methods course for preservice teachers in an Elementary Teacher Education Program. The goal of the design work was to develop a process that centers Black and Indigenous voices and sense making in understanding and connecting climate justice to elementary social studies. Through design circles, relationality emerged as integral to the ways the group thought about their relationships and responsibilities to each other and the environment. Further, the design work led to a relational, as opposed to a scientific, approach to climate justice and held racial justice as central to understanding one’s responsibility to care for the natural world and those most impacted by the climate crisis.

Purpose Amid rapidly intensifying climate change and persis tent racial injustice affecting the daily lives and  livelihoods of humans and more-than-human kin, educators must confront these challenges  and design for transformative learning that centers Black and Ind igenous communities and knowledge systems. The real ity of climate change and its disproportionate impacts on Black and Indig enous communities (Akom, 2007; Hernandez, 2022) has largely gone unmentioned in educator training and e lementary social studies curricula. Black and Indig enous communities offer significant perspectives on the c limate crisis because of  their experiences of violence and displacement due to colonization, and have located environmental justice conversations within racism, capitalism, and imperialism (Thomas, 2022).

Prompted by concerns about the climate crisis and its disproportionate impacts on Black and Indigenous communities (Akom, 2007; Hernandez, 2022), our coll ective of community and university-based educators and researchers came together to design for environment al justice in a Social Studies Methods course for p reservice teachers in an Elementary Teacher Education Program. Together we questioned: How do we design for lear ning about climate justice at the edge of movement space s where a Social Studies Method’s course and community spaces join? As a collective of Black, Indigenous, White, Queer, women, and gender non-conforming people, we aimed to develop a perspective of climate justice r ooted in lived and felt experiences of climate and racial (in)justice. Thr ough design circles that centered Black and Indigen ous experiences, relationality emerged as integral to the ways we think about our relationships and responsibilities to the environment.

## Theoretical framework

We understand learning as a powered and cultural process where we learn in and with the human and more-thanhuman communities that inform our values, knowledge, and practices (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Bang et al., 2018). Learning in the context of ongoing social movements regarding the future of the planet requires that people work together, “to critique, re-imagine, strategize, design, and re-make how we can engage with one another now and in the future” (Curnow & Jurow, 2021). This type of  learning for a future we cannot fully comprehend or predict involves sensemaking across intellectual, emotional, spiritual, and felt experiences (Simpson, 2014), and contending with injustices, uncertainties, and disp roportionate outcomes across communities who did no t cause the global crisis (Engestrom & Sannino, 2021).

Aligned with this definition of learning and the wa ys that our collective thought about our relationsh ip to the environment and environmental justice, Indigenous and Black feminist conceptions of relationality were an integral part of our design process and the course redesign. Relationality acknowledges the interconnectedness and interdependence of all lifeforms on earth (Delo ria Jr., 1988; Wilson, 2008). Key to this process i s building trusting and accountable relationships with each ot her, the land, the cosmos and ideas (Brayboy et al., 2012; Wilson, 2008). Dillard’s (2006) endarkened feminist  epistemology also centers relationality and uses t he notion of “research as responsibility” to describe researcher’s responsibility to be “answerable and obligated to the very persons and communities being engaged in inquiry” (2000, p. 663). Research as a responsibility validat es “multiple ways of knowing and doing research” for t hose “serious enough to interrogate the epistemolog ical, political, and ethical level of  their work” (Dillard, 2000, p. 663). These understa ndings of relationality were embedded and practiced throughout the entirety of our research and redesign process. In an academic environment that privileges western ways of knowing and engaging in research, Indigenous and Black feminist conceptions of relationality are central to designing new visions of teaching and learning on climate justice (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016).

Relationality offers a way into making meaning of “ runaway objects” or crises that are not solvable within a single classroom or community (Engestrom &  Sannino, 2021).  The promises and challenges of transforming knowledge production and valued practices within social studies education to address climate justice can begin relationally. Our process became a practice of considering our personal histories in relation to the natural world, listening to others’ histories and experiences, and considering interlocking systems of oppression in order to see new ways to address ongoing challenges with young people (Combahee River Collective, 1977).

## Methods

Our team of three University-based educators and researchers (a Black woman, Whi te woman, and Indigenous woman) from a University in the Pacific Northwest and three community-based educators (a Black woman; Black, queer, non-binary person; and a Guyanese woman) from The Root of Us (TRU),  a Black woman-led community organization focused on racial justice and healing with Youth of Color, engaged in a community-based design research partnership (Bang et al., 2016; Bang & Vos soughi, 2016). The goal of our collaborative work w as to develop a process that centers Black and Indigenous  voices and sense making in understanding and conne cting climate justice to elementary social studies. Our d esign-based research team studied our design process of incorporating climate justice into an existing Social Studies Methods course taught by the first author. Our collaborative work included eight design meetings/circles in the summer of 2023 lasting two to four hours. The two lead researchers took copious fieldn otes to capture our conversations and the design pr ocess. To prepare for each meeting we read/viewed multimedia resources on environmental and climate racism and (in)justice. We started each design circle meeting with a semi-structured agenda to allow for shifts and changes based on input from everyone in the group. Our community partners often took the lead and engaged the collective in grounding activities, time to reflect on and sha re our lived and felt experiences learning with and  from the environment, and we often ended with what was sitting with each of us from our time together. We began our design process by sitting with and the orizing the impact of climate justice and the sense of loss, grief, and embodied connections to land and water. Centering Black, Indigenous, Queer, and wo men perspectives on the topic of climate change and climate justice in our group meant listening, holding, and learning from our community of seasoned elementary, community, and university educators. Our group also took part in a two-day Climate Justice design workshop for teacher educators during the summer of 2023 that primarily focused on teaching climate justice in STEM classrooms whil e allowing time each day to meet with our design te ams to continue work on our individual projects.

Data analysis: We used grounded theory of inductive  and deductive coding to analyze the data and develop findings (Glaser & Strauss, 2017). These co des included educators’ developing definitions of c limate justice; forms of relationality; community histories of land, water, and all living things as kin; and learning through remembering histories of relationships with more-than-human kin. Centering Black and Indigenous perspectives in reorganizing for climate justice kept us  mindful throughout the  design and  data analysis stages about how expertise was positioned in coursework and how stud ents incorporated new sources of expertise with exi sting knowledge and interest in teaching environmental justice in the context of an elementary classroom. Centering Black and Indigenous ways of knowing and doing in design research Embodying Indigenous and Black conceptions of relat ionality and Dillard’s (2000) perspective of resear ch as responsibility, our design work over the summer of 2023 pushed back on notions of white western ways o f knowing and engaging in research. The research team developed a flexible agenda that we shared at the beginning of our design meeting, offering it as a suggested p lan for our time together. As was our practice, we asked for input from community partners and their suggestions shift ed the way the rest  of the summer design unfolded. Fernell, the lead community partner, suggested moving away from the items on our flexible list to going outside, connecting with our bodies, our environment, and our purpose for doing shared work together. The list was gone. Drawing from circle practices used with young people in The Root of Us (TRU), Fernell led us in a circle for each design meeting, often changing our location from an  indoor to an outdoor space. Our design circle meetings included a check-in, a grounding activity to help us connect with nature and our emotions, and a time to share our past and present experience with the natural world and how we were defining climate justice. We were invited to share stories and memories that connect us to our e nvironment including our more-than-human kin. We were prompted to slow down, reflect, and share our learn ing from the land, tree, plants, etc. This process set the tone for developing a more relational approach to the topic of redesigning a Social Studies Methods course to cent er climate justice.

Defining climate justice as relationality Through this process we developed our initial and growing perspective of a kind of climate justice rooted in lived and felt experiences of our community. As one Indig enous educator in our group described, climate just ice is a new word, but it is not new to  Indigenous communities. Climate justice from this p erspective, is to be a good relative and understand how we are interconnected t o all things and fulfilling our different responsib ilities to all things. She went on to say, “the issue of climate j ustice is a newer term, but the web of life and the impacts  that happen when you mess with that web of life and the impacts of the land through the decimation of the b uffalo nations, we’ve been talking about this for a long time but with different language” (fieldnote, 7/19/2023). A Black, queer, non-binary community educator in our group added that w e need to make these connections to land and water personal. Land-based is a relationship that must be cultivated. They stated, “there is no Indigenous practice without Indigenous people” (fieldnote, 7/19/2023). From the perspective of Fernell, a seasoned Black elementary and community educator, climate justice is racial j ustice. She also described the root of the problem in this way, “Whiteness is causing the hostile environment. We should not be afraid of our environment. We are talking about justice because of the hostile environment” (fieldn ote, 6/27/2023). She suggested that defining climat e justice meant that we need to humanize our environment. The se discussions h elped to decenter definitions of climate justice that started with white narratives about th e science of carbon dioxide emissions without namin g who has been responsible for creating these harms and who i s most directly affected by these decisions (CarlsonManathara, 2021; Hernandez, 2022). Relationality became a central tenant for redesigni ng the Social Studies Methods course. Our design collective spent considerable time defining environmental justice with a central focus on how our majority Black and Indigenous experiences of nature intersected with both joy and institutional racism and colonialism. The group went on to discuss the joys of gathering with three  generations of family in local public parks, spend ing time noticing nature and convincing family members to appreciate small wonders in nature, and connecting with their grandmother on their homeland. There were also disc ussions of harms connected to broken treaties and s tolen land and redlining that places Black and Brown communities in environmental disaster zones. As a result of these conversations, we considered how preservice elementary educators could learn how to teach social studies where climate and racial justice were central to their work.

## Redesigning the social studies methods course

As a result of our design work together, the Social  Studies Method s course was expanded to include environmental/climate justice as a key component of teaching social studies for racial and intersection justice (an original course focus). Course objectives now inclu ded teaching teacher candidates (TCs) how to teach about environmental racism and (in)justice in elementary classrooms. For course assign ments, TCs were expected to reflect on their positionalities on Indigenous land s and to develop an environmental and racial/inters ectional justice-oriented stance for teaching social studies. Studen ts were also required to create a culturally and historically responsive social studies lesson plan centering environmental/climate justice. Last but n ot least, Fernell and TRU served as guest instructors and led  a circle that engaged  TCs with the following questions that came from our design discussions from the summer: ● What is our relationship to/with the lands, waters,  peoples, plant life, and animals where we live, st udy, and work?

● What are our lived and felt experiences engaging wi th and learning from the environment or natural world?

● What do our relationship with the natural world rev eal about our relationships (or lack thereof) with Indigenous, Black, Brown, and Womxn people?

## Discussion and conclusion

So often university-based educators and researchers have rigid plans and timelines that inhibit true design work. Our commitment to engaging in “research as a respon sibility,” acknowledging “multiple ways of knowing and doing research” (Dillard, 2000, p. 663) caused us to slow down, step back, and lean on the experiential knowledge and practices of our community partners. Our design work led to a relational, as opposed to a scientific, approach to climate justice, which held racial justice as ce ntral to understanding our responsibility to care f or each other and our natural world. As conversations led into planning for the Social Studies Methods class, we sought to bring preservice teachers through a similar experience of connecting climate justi ce to lived and felt experiences with nature, people, and more-than-human kin. This relational understanding and teachi ng of climate justice in the context of the Social Studies Methods course felt well aligned with the content area and furthers Callison’s (2014) argument that “even though climate change may have begun as a scientific concept, it has flourished as  it’s been adopted, torqued, politicized, paired…it’s been fil led with meaning through its interaction with belie f systems, practices, and other systems of knowledge” (p. 247).  Lastly, our process of designing for climate justice in social studies education has defied “any ‘framing’ of clim ate change in a static, solely scientific, or progr essive/liberal environmentalist fashion” (Callison, 2014, p. 247).
