# Students’ Prioritization of Initial and Direct Causal Mechanisms During Model Evaluation

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Scientific modeling is supported by epistemic criteria to evaluate the “goodness” of scientific products. A central epistemic criterion is causal mechanism which means identifying the underlying causes which generate changes within a system. During a five-week model-based inquiry unit students identified causes of the phenomenon, eutrophication, but it was unclear whether student understood the causes hierarchically, i.e., whether some causes led to others, and whether some were more critical to include in a model than others. In a model evaluation task, students prioritized a model that was complete and had all the causes, deprioritized the model missing the direct causal mechanism, and placed models missing the initial causes in the middle. The results indicate that students thought about mechanisms in terms of chaining which means students thought about how causes led to outcomes within a system.

## Introduction

Scientific modeling has been increasingly emphasize d in scientific inquiry learning environments (NGSS, 2013; NRC, 1996). Scientific modeling involves constructing, using, evaluating, and refining models to und erstand a system as a whole and the relation between parts, to develop theories, and make predictions (Kenyon et al., 2008; Schwarz et al., 2009. Through modeling, students organize and represent their sensemaking about phenomena in the natural world (Gouvea & Passmore, 2017; Schwarz  et al., 2009). Modeling helps students to make the ir thinking visible, build explanations, and test idea s (Schwarz et al., 2009). We define scientific mode ls as, “A set of representations, rules, and reasoning structures  that allow one to generate predictions and explana tions” (Schwarz & White, 2005, p. 41).  Examples of scientific models are the Copernican and Ptolemaic models of the solar system, the billiard ball model of a gas, and the Bohr model of the atom (Frigg et al., 2020). Scientific models are created and evaluated based o n shared epistemic criteria for what count as good models in science (Kuhn, 1977; Longino, 2002).  Epistemic criteria are shared norms that guide enga gement in scientific practices as an aspect of practice in the scientific community (Lave & Wenger, 1991; L ongino, 2002). Examples of epistemic criteria are fit with evidenc e, explanatory power, fit with established theories, conceptual consistency and coherence, and parsimony (Kuhn, 1977; Longino, 2002). The epistemic criterion which is the focus of this article is causal mechanism. By mechanism we mean the “entities and activities organized such that they are productive of regular changes from start or setup to finish” (Machamer et al., 2000, p. 3).  By causal mechanism we mean the underlying causes which generate changes within a system. Understanding c ausal mechanisms is central to scientific inquiry (Koslowski, 1996),  domainspecific theories (e.g., Carey, 1995), and aids oth er aspects of scientific inquiry, such as control o f variables (Schauble, 1996), and aids understanding of the cau sal structure of the world (e.g., Ahn & Kalish, 200 0). Articulation of causal mechanisms in a system is more often readily provided by experts than novices because they identify and understand the u nderlying causal relationships that main tain and disrupt a system’s balance (Hmelo-silver and Pfeffer, 2003). Causal mechanisms are also often hierarchical, not linear, which can be difficult for science learners to disentangle (Craver, 2002).  Understanding of causal mechanisms is therefore  significant for science learners because it can help them to understand the interconnected nature of complex systems (HmeloSilver & Azevedo, 2006). Prior research has shown that students’ use causal mechanisms to explain natural phenomena (Andersson, 1986; Brewer et al., 1998; diSessa, 1993; Vosniadou & Brewer, 1994) and that the sophistication of their mechanistic reasoning evolves throughout inquiry units (Metz, 1991; Ryan et al., 2023). In a study by Ryan et al., (2023) students’ mechanistic reasoning evol ved from describing simple cause and effect relatio nships and towards more complex relationships within a system (chaining). Metz (1991) found three phases of development with mechanistic e xplanation being the most complex. Our investigation into students’ prioritization of caus al mechanisms within a systems model  was sparked by several students’ statements during the five-week modelbased inquiry unit about the multiplicity of causes  within their own models that they developed during a five-week model-based inquiry unit to explain the phenomenon of eutr ophication. Students’ statements  made us wonder what students thought about the various causes within a model, whether students thought that all causes were equal or whether students thought that some causal mechanisms to be more central than others. For example, fertilizer is a greater cause of an algal bloom than  sunlight. Students’ pr ioritization of some causal mechanisms over others would reveal not only that they can ide ntify causal mechanisms, but that they understand t he hierarchical causes and effects within the system. Students mechanistic reasoning would therefore mirror experts understanding about mechanisms within a complex system. To that end, our research questions were which causal mechanisms, if any, do students prioritize within a  scientific model that explains the process of eutr ophication? What do students’ justifications reveal about why they might prioritize some causal mechanisms over others?

## Context and participants

Data from this study came from an implementation of  a five-week model-based inquiry unit in a public charter school in the Northeast of the United States. Two f ifth-grade classes (termed Class A and Class B) participated, with a total of 50 students. The demographics of the school were 60% White, 22% Asian, 9% African American, 7% Latinx, and 2% other; 5% of students were eligib le for free and reduced lunch. The school performs above average on standardized  state tests on mathematics, language arts, and science. In the unit, students developed and iteratively revised models to explain the pheno menon of eutrophication. The students’ investigation was led by a guiding question: why are fish dying in a local pond?  Eutrophication occurs when an excess of nutrients i n fertilizer causes an increase in plant growth which  depletes the oxygen supply and results in fish dea th by suffocation. Students investigated eutrophication by analyzing reports of empirical studies and interacting with a simulation (Figure 1).

Figure 1 The Dissolved Air and Fertilizer Evidence Based on these two types of evidence, students crea ted models in a new modeling software called  Model and Evidence Mapping Environment [MEME] (Danish et al.,  2019) (see Figure 2). The models consisted of components (parts) that were linked by mechanisms (processes) to create a causal account of why the fi sh were dying. Here, “mechanisms” denotes the arrows that connect the components (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2016). The evidence consisted of scientific reports of empirical research and NetLogo simulations (Tisue & Wilensky, 2004). Students sought to account for both types of eviden ce as they developed the ir models. The evidence was stored in MEME in a resource library. Students linked the evidence to the parts of the model to support the components and mechanisms (see green circles on the model in Figure 2). Examples of reports given to students were a graph showing that decomposers use up dissolved air, graphs showing the amount of sunlight and nutrients in the pond, and a fertilizer label to show the chemical ingredi ents accompanied with reports of how fertilizer increased plant growth in a pond ecosystem (see Figure 1). The agent based NetLogo simulations supported investigations of how the variables sunlight, fertilizer, and oxygen impacted fish death on a micro and a macro scale. Figure 2 A Student’s Final Model and a Section of the Evidence Library Model creation and evaluation were guided by a list  of epistemic criteria that was developed by each c lass. Students made the class criteria list by analyzing pairs of models about scientific processes and coming up with a list of agreed upon and shared criteria. Half-way through the unit the students revised their class criteria lists based on observations and critiques to their models durin g a gallery walk  (see Murphy et al, 2023 for more thorough analysis). The final class criteria list featured the criteria “models should make sense,” “ components and mechanisms should be clearly ordered and labeled to  show a start and finish,” “each component and mechanism should have a clear explanation supported by reason s and evidence, “the mechanisms should be understandable sentences, and “models should have a main focus that includes all components and mechanisms” (see Murphy et al, 2021 for a deeper analysis). However, the class criteria list did not contain a criterion about a causal mechanism. The mechanisms mentioned here refer to the arrows that connect components (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2016).

## Data collection and analysis

The data for this study was taken from interviews with students from one of the participating classes, Class A (16 total students). Each interview lasted approximatel y 40-60 minutes. The data for this study are taken from an interview task which assessed students’ evaluation of a series of scientific models. Students were given a scientific model about eutrophication which approximated the m odels that students had made in class (see Figure 3). Students were asked, what do you think about the model? If students identified any specific features of the models, follow-up questions were asked such as why is that feature important/a problem? How do you  know it is important/a problem? Students were then given other models that were the same as the first model, but they were missing parts (the subsequent models were either mi ssing dissolved air, fertilizer, or sunlight). As students were given the subsequent models, they were asked the same questions as when they were given the first model. Once students had reviewed all models individually, they were asked which one is the best model? Why? Which model is the worst? Why? Compare the model missing fertilizer and the model missing sunlight. Which is worse? Why? Students ordered the models from most to least important and explained why they picked the order that they did. Students’ ordering of criteria is presented in Table 1 (see below) with the frequencies showing how many students ordered each model in that position. For example, a ll students placed model 1 first, so the frequency of 16/16 is given, 9/16 placed Model 3 in second place, and so on. Some students disagreed with their partner about the model order and thus spoke separately, so to keep consistency i n results across dyads, students’ answers were anal yzed individually. In instances in which students agreed, the intervie wer checked with both students, thus ensuring consensus, and assigned the model order to both student in the dyad. Figure 3 Model 1 (the complete model) The interviews were videotaped, audio recorded, and  later transcribed. The transcripts were reviewed i n two rounds of analysis. First, we analyzed the transcript of each dyad to understand  the model order that students provided and to note the justifications that students in each dyad gave for the placement of each mode l. Then we looked across transcripts to see all the justifications that students gave for the placement of each model in a crosscomparison method (Glaser, 1965. For example, Table 1 shows all the justifications that students gave for placing Model 1 first, Model 2 last, and for placing Models  3 and 4 in between. A students’ statement was coun ted as a justification if it provided a reason for why a Model had been prioritized as it did. For example, “Model 1 is first because it is the most accurate,” was counted as a justification because it provided a reason for the prioritization of model 1 as most important. We then grouped simil ar kinds of justifications by each model. For examp le, students’ justifications that Model 1 was important  because it explained what is happening and answers  the question were grouped together because answering th e question means explaining the phenomen on. The two rounds of analysis (within students and across stud ents) helped us to see patterns within students, such as why they prioritized models in relation to each other, and across students, such as whether all students would highlight the absence of a particular causal mechanism while evaluating the series of models. After we analyzed students’ justifications, we cate gorized them according to whether they contained mechanistic reasoning. To guide our analysis, we used the  mechanistic reasoning coding scheme that was developed by Russ et al., (2008). The coding scheme  specifies 9 categories of increasingly sophisticat ed mechanistic reasoning: (1) describing the target phenomenon (DTP), ident ifying (2) setup conditions (SC), (3) identifying entities (IE), (4) identifying activities (IA), (5) identifying properties of entities (IPE), (6) organization of entities (IOE), (7) chaining (C), analogies (A),  and animated models (AM). The categories are assumed to increase in sophistication because they progress from identifying entities, to thinking about how entities are related to produce an effect. This coding scheme was useful to guide our analysis because it highlights the ways in which students thought about mechanisms within a modeling  system. Students’ statements were counted as chaining if they, “…used(ing) knowledge about the causal structure of  the world to make claims about what must have happened previously to bring about the current stat e of things (backward) or what will  happen next given that certain entities or activities are present now (forward)” (p. 513). The justifications that did not contain mechanistic reasoning were marked with an x in Table 2.  Any issues that ar ose were resolved by presenting the data to the larger research team, reviewing the data, and discussing interpretations until we reached consensus.

Initial causal mechanisms After students ranked Model 1 the highest because i t was most complete and Model 2 the lowest because it was missing the causal mechanism, most students ranked Models 3 and 4 (without sunlight and fertilizer) in the middle (see Table 1) because they did not have the initial causes (see  Table 2). Here, students are exhibiting forward chaining because they are thinking through how the fertilizer and sunlight kick start the process of eutrophication (Russ et al., 2008). Further, s tudents thought relationally about how missing the direct causal mechanism (dissolved air) was worse than missing the initial causes (fertilizer or sunlight) because the direct causal mechanism was needed to adequately explain fish death.

Students reasoned that fertilizer was needed becaus e that is where the nutrients came from (5/16) and started the entire process (4/16), again exhibiting forward chaining (Russ et al., 2 008). The following statement from Tara shows why missing fertilizer was a problem.

Tara: I think it’s because the fertilizer started a whole  algae bloom. If you don’t have no fertilizer, you don't know what started it in the first place. Students (8/16) reasoned that sunlight was needed because i t caused the algal bloom and two additional student s stated that sunlight and fertilizer were equal causes, again exhibiting chaining. However, although sunlight does cause plants to grow, it was really the fertilizer that caused the algal bloom. There are two explanations for students’ prioritizing models that include sunlight. We think that the form of the model (sunlight leading directly to the algal bloom) influenced students’ reasoning about sunlight as an initial cause of the algal bloom. We think that students saw sunlight as a part of the model and assumed it was an initial cause on par with fertilizer. This is supported by students’ actions during the unit because only two students linked sunlight to their model. Students’ justifications are therefore useful to highlight students mechanistic reasoning, and instructional m oves, such as the inclusion of sunlight on a final model, that may hinder students understanding of scientific content.

## Discussion and implications

Students consistently valued Model 1 the highest and gave the justifications tha t it was the most complete and contained that causes that led to fish death. Almost all students deprioritized Model 2 because it was missing the dissolved air and therefore did not adequately expl ain how the fish suffocated and dies. Most students placed Models 3 and 4 (missing the initial causes fertiliz er and sunlight) in the middle because even though they were each missing an initial cause, they still contained  the direct cause of fish death. Students’ articulation that the causal mechanisms were important because they led to the outcome of the system, which was fi sh death by eutrophication, indicates mechanistic reasoning in terms of thinking through how entities in a system produce outcomes (Russ, 2008). Students’ mechanistic reasoning therefore reveals “ chaining” which  is the most sophisticated form of mechanistic reasoning accordi ng to Russ’s coding scheme because it indicates one ’s thinking about how entities in a system interact to produce outcomes. Other forms of mechanistic reasoning, such as identifying entities, may still be important to help students be able to chain causes because they first need to identify causes because they can consider how they interact within a system. The results indicate that students did identify causes, such as fertilizer and sunlight, b ut they used these to explain how they resulted in fish death. Students’ reasoning about how causes impact outcomes are significant because learners can struggle to articulate how hierarchical causes can produce outcomes, which  experts more readily explain than novices (Craver,  2002; Hmelo-silver and Pfeffer, 2003).

The results helped to make students’ thinking salie nt about the scientific models that they developed during the five-week model-based inquiry unit. Throughout the unit, students o ften identified causes within a system, for example, they needed to add fertilizer or low dissolved air because that led to fish death, but it was unclear what students thought about the causes them selves. Students may have seen all causes as equal,  which isn’t the case. For example, fertilizer has a greater impact on fish death than sunlight. The results point to the need to clarify aspects of scientific content during mod eling units to clearly identify how aspects of a sy stem work together to produce outcomes. Conversely, most students deprioritized Model 2 which was missing dissolved air which shows that many students grasped the direct causal mechanism within the unit. The results also have implications for instruction. Now we have realized that students exhibited chaining we would have liked to discuss this more throughout  the modeling unit. We share this point to highligh t a limitation of the study but also to point to potential areas of future work. First, neither class criteria list contained a criterion about causal mechanism, yet this criter ion came up in students’ conversations in both clas ses. Since we have now seen that understanding the causal mech anism was a part of students’ sensemaking we could have had a class discussion to name this criterion and place it on the list, or, perhaps mor e meaningfully, help them to make this criterion explicit in their work. We also could have had conversations about what different causes mean, and which causes are more important than others, th us providing a scaffold to help make chaining more explicit in their modeling work. With explicit naming throughout the unit, students could have articulated whether they were identifying causes during model development or  whether they were incrementally exhibiting chaining throughout the unit.

In future work we would like to trace students’ mec hanistic reasoning and provide an analysis of what might have led to students’ chaining mechanistic re asoning, but at an initial glance, it seems that students continually reviewing evidence and updating their m odels, which included evidence links, may have help ed students to incrementally understand the phenomenon, and thus develop an understanding of how causes l ed to the outcome of the system. If our initial guess is correct, the results could point to curricula sugge stions on how to support students’ mechanistic reasoning throughout modeling units.
