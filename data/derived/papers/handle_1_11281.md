# Evaluating Language Models Using Linguistic Variations in Multilingual Learners’ Writing: A Teacher Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper investigates teachers’ perceptions on linguistic variations in bi/multilingual learners’ (MLs) writing to evaluate the (in)effectiveness of Multilingual Large Language Models (MLLMs), which are artificial intelligence (AI) models that generate texts in multiple languages. Due to their inherent linguistic biases, these models often struggle to interpret MLs’ linguistic variations. To address this gap, we elicit teacher feedback on prevalent linguistic variations in MLs’ writing and assess how Meta Llama 3.1, a state-of-the-art MLLM, responds to these variations. Using translanguaging as a lens—the fluid use of multiple languages to convey meaning across social contexts—we propose a new approach to evaluate MLLMs in multilingual learning contexts. With the increasing prevalence of AI in K12 classrooms, this paper advocates for the inclusion of bi/multilingual educators to better align the use of AI with progressive pedagogies such as translanguaging.

## Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs), which are artificial intelligence (AI) tools trained on extensive text d ata to process language, have been introduced into K12 classrooms to support tasks such as automated grading (Henkel et al., 2024), personalized feedback (Fung et al., 2024), and question generation (McNichols et al., 2 023). However, these AI tools risk exacerbating educational inequities due to their English-centric training and inability to process multilingual learners (MLs) language pra ctices (Hyung et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024). Prio r research (Syamkumar et al., 2024) shows that LLM performance  declines significantly when assessing student writ ing in Spanish compared to English, with further reduction s in accuracy when processing translanguaged text s uch as Spanglish (English-Spanish). Such biases not only impact grading accuracy but also limit AI’s potential to support MLs’ language practices. Past efforts to address li nguistic bias in LLM training have led to multiling ual models (MLLMs) trained on diverse language data. Despite the recent advances of LLMs, however, model development often treats multilingualism as a set of separate, switchable languages rather than an integrated system of meaningmaking. This lack of alignment with real-world linguist ic practices, where speakers fluidly alternate betw een languages, presents a major methodological gap. Fur thermore, the absence of mainstream MLLMs trained o n authentic bi/multilingual writing limits their effe ctiveness for linguistically diverse educational se ttings (Armengol-Estapé et al., 2021).

Using translanguaging —a framework that values students’ complete linguist ic repertoire —we collaborate with educational stakeholders to determ ine prevalent linguistic variations in K12 classroo ms. Given that translanguaging is a common practice among MLs, supports learning, and promotes identity developm ent (García & Lin, 2016), we choose this approach to address the diverse linguistic realities present in heterogeneous learning environments. Moreover, we evaluate Llama 3.1, an open-source MLLM known for its robust language capabilities (Dubey et al., 2024), on its ability t o effectively assess translanguaged (English-Spanish-Spanglish) sentences seen in MLs’ linguistic repertoires. Given the focus on linguistic variations as a source for investigating MLLM effectiveness, we ask: RQ1: How do teachers perceive the prevalence of linguistic variations in MLs’ writing? RQ2: How sensitive are MLLMs to the prevalent variations when assessing MLs’ writing? Why linguistic variations?

Translanguaging pedagogy, as outlined in the CUNY-New York State Initiative on Emergent Bilinguals (CUNYNYSIEB), recognizes that MLs fluidly integrate ling uistic resources across languages rather than adher ing to rigid, monolingual standards (Hesson et al., 2014). CUNY-NYSIEB provides educators with strategies to leverage students' full linguistic repertoires, emphasizing the importance of fostering inclusive, asset-based learning environments that validate translanguaging as a legitimate communicative practice. The guide contains linguistic variations (i.e., features that individuals use based on region, social context, and communicative intent) that support meaning-making in bi/multilingual education. Research in ap plied linguistics emphasizes MLs do cognitively separate languages but instead leverage a unified linguistic repertoire (Otheguy et al., 2018). However, despite theoretical advancements in translanguaging, most MLLMs continue to be developed within monolingual or rigidly bilingual frameworks, limiting their ability to accurately assess and support ML writing. Despite efforts to improve MLLMs, evaluating their effectiveness re mains challenging due to the diversity of MLs in la nguage proficiency, cultural background, and learning cont ext (Heineke & Giatsou, 2020). Additionally, MLLMs often demonstrate highly context-dependent performance. For example, if an M LLM is tested in a science classroom focused on concepts like height and energy, the model’s performance may be highly specific to that context. To bridge this gap, we propose linguistic variation s as a core evaluation metric for assessing MLLMs. By identifying how these models process different variations in student writing, we establish a scalable framework applicable across subjects, grade level s, and educational contexts. This study disaggregat es Spanglish into linguistic variations representative of authentic M L writing. Furthermore, we identify prevalent lingu istic variations through teacher interviews and assess ho w MLLMs respond, highlightin g a systematic evaluation approach grounded in translanguaging frameworks and authentic classroom communication practices rather thandecontextualized text processing.

## Participants

Eight K12 teachers from various U.S. regions, refer red by colleagues at our institution’s research cen ter, participated in two virtual 1-hour sessions. Participants also came from a previous list of educators who completed a survey the previous summer and whom we reached ou t to for further participation. Their teaching expe rience ranged from 2 to 25 years, and all used both English and Spanish in instruction through various subjects, including: language arts, ESL, Spanish, literacy, math, science, and social studies. We applied Think Aloud Modeling (Philippakos, 2021), which involves verbalizing thought processes to highlight problem-solving and strategy use, in focus groups and individual interviews to gather detailed teacher reflections and classroom examples with ML s. In focus groups, small groups of 2-3 teachers met virtually with researchers for a collaborative discussion on lingu istic variations seen in their students’ writing. W e presented teachers with a list of nine linguistic variations (see Figure 1) and aske d them to identify whether they observed the same variations in their MLs’ writing. After in terviews, all participants completed a post-survey designed to capture their feedback on the linguistic variations  categorized earlier and offered suggested linguis tic variations perceived through their own experience teaching MLs.

Identifying linguistic variations To assess how MLLMs process MLs’ writing, we analyz ed teacher interviews using Thematic Analysis (Brau n & Clarke, 2006), applying both deductive and inductive coding. Deductive codes aligned with nine pre-identified variations from translanguaging pedagogy  (see Figure 1), while inductive codes captured addi tional features raised by participants, such as phonetically influenced orthography (i.e., the influence that sounds in spoken words have on letters in written words) and noun transfer  across languages. To ensure trustworthiness, we fo llowed Nowell et al.'s (2017) thematic analysis guidelines  by maintaining an auditable decision trail, record ing and systematizing coding steps, and providing transparency for readers to evaluate the credibility of the process. We then selected six linguistic variations —dialects (default Spanglish and Mexican), spelling errors, PIO, cognates, and noun transfer—for Llama 3.1 model assessment. These reflect both translanguaging pedagogy and current bi/multilingual teaching practices, and allow us to examine how the model handles cr oss-lingual and dialectal similarities vs. dissimilarities. Non-dialectal variations were grouped into twonested p airs: spelling errors with PIO, and noun transfer with cognates, w here one is a subset of the other (e.g., PIO within spelling errors). These pairs differ in transferability, wit h spelling errors and cognates being more cross-lingually consistent due to shared grammar and direct word ma ppings in English and Spanish. We included both a d efault and a Mexicandialect of Spanglish to compare how ML LMs handle a broader range of linguistic variations. Mexican Spanglish was selected due to its higher usage and greater digital availability (España-Bonet & BarrónCedeño, 2024), while default Spanglish allowed us t o test a more generalized form. This comparison add resses the significant imbalances in multilingual training data, particularly for less-digitized language varieties (Joshi et al., 2021).

Each data point, consisting of a generated question and a single-sentence answer, received a binary grade from GPT-3.5: 'acceptable' if the answer was correct and 'unacceptable' otherwise. We modified each answer using GPT-3.5 to introduce one of six selected linguistic variati ons: dialects (de fault and Mexican Spanglish), spelling errors, PIO, cognates, and noun transfer. To ensure authent icity, we validated the Mexicandialect subset with  two bilingual Spanish-English annotators using translation accuracy and n aturalness criteria (Yong et al.,  2023), achieving strong agreement (97.5% accuracy and 92% naturalness). We also reviewed data points to confirm that each variation (e.g., noun transfer vs. cognates) remained distinct.

Using the 1200-instance dataset (200 examples across six variation  types), we tested Llama 3.1’s pretrained (8B) version and three LoRA fine-tuned models, each trained on a subset of question-answer-grade triplets in English, Spanish, or Spanglish. Fine-tuning aligned model outputs with realistic classroom grading use cases, allowing us to examine how linguistic variat ions affect model predictions. We defined bias as v ariance in scoring due to linguistic differences, and testing focused on cross-lingual (di s)similarity. Therefore, our hypotheses include: H1: Llama 3.1 will perform better on linguistic variations that are more cross-lingually similar in nested pairs, including cognates and spelling errors.

H2: Llama 3.1 will perform worse on linguistic variations that are less cross-lingually similar in nested pairs, including noun transfer and PIO.

Using off-the-shelf Llama 3.1 as our baseline, we found strong pe rformance for English answers (AUC =.88), confirming effective assessment of standard English. For the first nested pair (i.e., spelling errors and PIO), spelling errors yielded comparable results to Engli sh (AUC =.87), supporting H1 and suggesting model adaptability to minor orthographic changes. In cont rast, PIO scored lower (AUC =.79), aligning with H2 and indicating challenges in processing phonetically influenced spelling, likely due to its scarcity in training data. In the second pair (i.e., cognates and noun transfer), cognates scored relatively high (AUC =.83), again supporting H1, while noun transfer dropped to (AUC =.73), consistent with H2. These results suggest that Llama 3.1 performs better on cross-lingual elements with shared orthography (e.g., cog nates) than on less predictable structures (e.g., noun transfer). Among dialects, t he model handled Mexican Spanglish moderately well (AUC =.77) but performed slightly worse on Default Spangl ish (AUC =.74), likely due to its generalized stru cture and lack of specificity in generation.

Fine-tuning revealed notable improvements. The Spanglish  fine-tuned model achieved the highest average performance (AUC =.94) and narrowed the pe rformance gap across nested pairs: spelling errors (AUC =.93) versus PIO (AUC =.92), and cognates (AUC =.94) versus noun transfer (AUC =.93). In contrast, English fine-tuning maintained the spelling-PIO gap (.11) but reduced the cognate–noun transfer gap (.03). Spanish finetuning showed similar patterns (.10 and.03, respec tively). These findings suggest that  targeted fine-tuning (i.e., Spanglish-specific training) is most effective in mitigating MLLM bias across linguistic variations.

## Discussion

Our analysis shows that linguistic variations in ML s’ writing offer a critical lens for evaluating MLL M bias. Variations with cross-lingual similarities, such as spelling errors and c ognates, yielded stronger model performance, likely due to shared orthography and etymology. In contrast, dissimilar variations like PIO and noun transfer led to decreased accuracy, likely due to limited presence in training data. These findings align with prior research highlighting MLLMs’ limitations in process ing translanguaged text (Syamkumar et al., 2024; Hy ung et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024). Teacher feedback affirmed the importance of these variations and reinforced the need for educator perspectives in MLLM development. Addi tionally, fine-tuning with linguistically diver se data improved performance, underscoring the value of incorporating underrepresented linguistic features. Methodologically, we introduced a scalable framewor k that uses both synthetic and educator-informed data to assess MLLM biases, in turn complementing c lassroom-based data collection while enabling audits at scale. This approach supports translanguaging-aligned development (García & Lin, 2016; Hesson et al., 2014) but challenges remain with some dialects. For instance,  our Puerto Rican Spanish dataset had low naturalne ss and accuracy scores, despite native speaker involvement. Future work should refine data collection and annotation to better represent less-digitized dialects (Joshi et al., 2021). By framing linguistic variations as part of a fluid translanguaging process rather than “informal” or “nonstandard” writing, our study advances both AI and b ilingual education research. This perspective refle cts how MLs leverage their full linguistic repe rtoire in learning contexts (Otheguy et al., 2018) and responds to calls for educational equity in Learning Sciences (ISLS, 2025). While our dataset was synthetic, the iterative collaboration with bilingual teachers and annotators improved cul tural and lin guistic authenticity. Collecting real-world classroom data will be critical in future efforts to reduce MLLM bias and enhance support for bi/multilingual K12 learners.
