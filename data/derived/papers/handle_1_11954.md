# Comparing Collaboration Patterns in Virtual Reality Content Co-Creation Activities between High- and Low-Performing Secondary School Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study analyzed system logs and written reflections from 111 secondary school students engaged in collaborative Virtual Reality (VR) content creation. Twenty-six student groups of 4–5 members were classified into 13 low-performing and 13 high-performing groups based on their final VR artifacts scores. Results showed that high-performing groups exhibited superior collaboration quality and more proactive problem-solving approaches, than low-performing groups.

## Introduction

Collaborative maker education involves groups of students working together to create tangible artifacts and solve problems during learning processes, emphasizing tea mwork and collective participation (Wang et al., 20 24a; Miyata et al., 2009). Collaborative virtual reality (VR) content creation, as a specific form of collaborative maker activities, requires students to collaborate in building VR artifacts, such as digital stories or models, while applying technical skills and effective collaboration strate gies (Wang et al., 2024a). Despite the growing intere st in VR content creation, most research focuses on university-level participants (Ng et al., 2023; Quintana-Ordorika et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a; Wang et al., 2024b), leav ing a gap in understanding how secondary school stu dents engage in collaborative VR creation (Miyata et al., 2009). The reason we chose to study secondary school students is because early adolescence is a critical period f or developing social-cognitive skills and emotion regulation (Blakemore & Mills, 2014; Ladd et al., 2014), which plays an important role in collaboration (Ladd et al., 2014). Group performance has been shown to be closely link ed to collaboration patterns (Zheng et al., 2021). High-performing groups, for example, tend to demonstrate more metacognitive interactions than low-performing groups, showing positive correlations between  the frequency of metacognitive interactions and lea rning achievement (Zheng et al., 2021). However, research  on how high-and low-performing groups differ in collaboration patterns during maker activities rema ins limited, and this study seeks to address t his research gap. Specifically, we aim to answer the following resear ch question: How do high-performing and low-performing students differ in their collaboration patterns dur ing collaborative VR content creation? This paper p resents a preliminary analysis  of log-based measures of collaboration quality, alongside text-analytic findings from students’ written reflections on collaboration chal lenges and their solutions, with a comparative focu s on high-and low-performing students. Theoretically, this study cont ributes insights on shared regulation in collaborat ive maker activities, particularly among secondary school students who are undergoing the development of cognitive abilities, social skills, and emotional regulation. Practically, this study identified patterns associated with varying performance levels, based on which we can offer evidence-based recommendations for enhancing group learning.

## Methods

We designed a learning program including training workshops and collaborative in-class and after-class activities focusing on co-creating VR content. Introductory training was prov ided in four workshops held in school, each lasting for two hours. The project spanned three weeks from June to July 2024 as an extra-curriculum activity. To facilitate these activities, we developed an online  platform, the Collaborative Learning Environment for VR creation (CLEVR), where students could collaboratively edit compone nts (e.g., text, images, audios, or background music) within VR artifacts/stories (Wang et al., 2024a). One-hundred and eleven Grade 7 students from the same s econdary school in Hong Kong worked in groups of 4–5 to develop VR artifacts based on themes such as local community landmarks, natural environments, or heritage sites. These VR artifacts were evaluate d using scales assessing four dimensions: theme con sistency, content richness, multimedia usage, and artifact qu ality (Rizvic et al., 2019). Two researchers in lea rning technology independently coded one-quarter of the VR artifacts, achieving excellent agreement (kappa = 0.846), with the first rater scoring the remaining artifact s. The 26 groups were then classified into 13 high-performing and 13 low-performing groups based on their total scores of final VR artifacts.

To address the aforementioned research question, we  employed the following data sources collected in the VR co-creation activities, along with the corresponding data analysis methods.

(1) Logs: All student actions performed on CLEVR during VR content co-creation were recorded in the

system and retrieved afterwards for analysis. We ca lculated log-based collaboration quality using a learning analytics-based framework consisting of four dimensions: Cont ribution, Participation, Transactivity, and Social Dynamics (Hu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024a). “Co ntribution” (termed as “Creation” in this study) wa s operationalized as the frequency with which participants add new scenes, objects, or music during the experiment. “Participation” refers to making changes to already-existing content in the VR story by a creator inclu ding heror himself, labelled here as “Update”. “Transactivi ty” was defined as the events when students modifie d the objects “created” by their peer students, reflecting the extent to which they built upon each other’s contributions. “Social Dynamics” refers to the behavior of sending chat mes sages on CLEVR or adding sticky notes to the VR stories for intra-group communication. The log-based collaboration quality metrics were aggregated at the group level to provide a holistic view of collaborative engagement. Higher occurrences of creation, update, transactivity, and social dynamics can indicate a stronger sense o f collaboration, as they reflect increased contribu tion, engagement with peers’ work, and communication. Usi ng independent sample t-tests (two-tailed), we compared the log-based collaboration quality metrics between high-and low-performing student groups.

(2) Written reflection: The content of what students wrote in their reflections on collaboration challenges

and solutions at the end of the program was analyze d. We extracted the bigrams each student’s reflecti ons on collaboration challenges and solutions.  To measure the importance each bigram in the reflec tions, we calculated the term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) value and summed them across students in each group (Ng et al., 2023; Setiawan et al., 2024).

## Results and discussions

Results showed that across all four log-based dimensions (i.e., creation, update, transactivity, social dynamics (Hu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024a)), the collaborati on quality of the high-performing groups surpasses that of the low-performing groups. To  be more specific, the high-performing groups had a significantly higher number  of “Creation” (t(24) = -2.1352, p = 0.0432, Cohen’s d = 0.8375), “Update” (t(24) = -3.5161, p = 0.0018, Cohen’s d = 1.3791), “Transactivity” (t(24) = -2.5439, p = 0.0178, Cohen’s d = 0.9978) and “Social  Dynamics” (t(24) = - 3.6392, p = 0.0013, Cohen’s d = 1.4274) than the low-performing groups. The results suggest that members within high-performing groups possessed a stronger sense of col laboration, actively engaging in editing each other ’s contributions and collaborating to craft VR stories  together. Moreover, the high-performing groups were more willing to communicate and interact with their peers as indicated by the “Social Dynamic” measure. As for the most frequently used bigrams of reflections on solving collaborative problems, “ask question” appeared at the top for the high-performing groups, indicating that high-performing students might actively search for other’s help to solve the problems, which showed their proactive approaches to problem solving and regulated learning. The low-performing groups, on the other hand, did not seem to have demonstrated such agency. Instead, the bigram “discuss problem” is among the top for the low-performing groups but is not for the high-performing groups, indicating students in low-performing groups were more focused on the problem itself. This might have caused their communication to stay more at the disc ussion level and lack efficient execution, which po tentially resulted in their poorer performance (Brewer & Holmes, 2016) in final VR creation artifact. Summary This paper presented preliminary results from log-based and text analytics for understanding the coll aboration processes and assessing the collaboration patterns of secondary school students involved in collaborat ive VR content creation activities. The log-based results indicate that students in high-performing groups had a stronger sense of collaboration and actively engaged in edit ing each other’s contributions, as well as a greate r inclination towards communication than those in the low-performing groups. The text analytics of student-written reflections reveals that students in high-performing groups exhibited more proactive problem-solving, compared to those in the low-performing groups. This study evaluated collaborati on quality based on system logs ag gregated across entire collaboration processes and student summativ e reflections collected at the end of collaborative  learning. Future research could analyze student formative ref lections along the collaboration process and employ  timeseries analysis to examine both log and reflection data in a more fine-grained manner.
