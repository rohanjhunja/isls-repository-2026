# Leveraging CSCL for Social Emotional Learning: Early Insights from a Large -Scale Classroom Study

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Social and Emotional Learning (SEL) helps students to develop healthy identities, regulate emotions, build relationships, and make responsible decisions. Effective SEL instruction often relies on group-based activities; however, traditional classrooms without technological support make it difficult to capture the fine-grained interactions among students. As a result, teachers struggle to provide timely feedback and interventions. This paper reports on an ongoing project that leverages a computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) environment to implement 36 SEL lessons with fifth and sixth-grade students over the course of a semester. We describe the design and implementation of the curriculum and present preliminary findings from a pilot study. Early results indicate positive feedback from both teachers and students, along with a negative correlation between students’ off-topic chat behavior, characterized by nonsensical or “gibberish” messages, and their learning performance.

Social and Emotional Learning (SEL) is a fundamental component of education that helps students develop essential life skills, including emotional regulation, empathy, relationship-building, and responsible decisionmaking. These competencies are crucial for developing healthy identities and navigating the complexities of social interactions both in and outside of school  (Bierman et al., 2008; Durlak et al., 2010; Durlak et al., 2011; Durlak et al., 2022; Greenberg, 2023; Nelson et al., 2003; Domitrovich et  al., 2017; Taylor et al., 2017). Teaching SEL presents various challenges due to the skills being rooted  in interpersonal engagement and collaboration. The CASEL framework (Borowski, 2019; Jagers et al., 2019) proposes that students develop five competencies: selfawareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making. Assessing students’ SEL competencies has traditionally relied on self-report questionnaires and observational rating scales. While these methods provide useful summaries, they offer limited insight into the dynamic processes through which students interact, communicate, and collaborate to develop SEL skills in real-world contexts (McKown, 2019).

To address this challenge, we adopted a well-established, evidence-based Social Skills Improvement System Classwide Intervention Program (SSIS CIP; Elliott & Gresham, 2007; Elliott et al., 2022) and integrated 36 lessons into a computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) system, the ETS Platform for Collaborative Assessment and Learning (EPCAL; Hao et al., 2017), see Figure 1  for the teacher and student interfaces. More detailed design considerations can be found in (Guo et al., in press). CSCL has been extensively shown to enhance both students’ academic learning outcomes and social skills by engaging learners in joint problem-solving processes (Stahl, Koschmann, & Suthers, 2006; Jeong, Hmelo-Silver, & Jo, 2019).  Within each class, students use Chromebooks to log in to the EPCAL system, where they are assigned by the teacher into small teams of two or three to collaboratively engage with the curriculum. The platform delivers the learning tasks and collect s student responses and their communication process data. Using advanced analytics, the system provides teachers with actionable insights into each student’s learning processes,  enabling targeted interventions and continuous feedback. Moreover, team composition changes between lessons  by design, providing a unique opportunity to examine how students’ collaboration patterns change when working with different peers. The full study is a n efficacy study designed to evaluate the differences in learning outcomes between students participating in the computer-supported collaborative SEL curriculum and those in a control group. To fulfill this goal, we  will implement the 36-lesson curriculum to 400 students over a semester, using a multi-site cluster-randomized trial with treatment assigned at the classroom level. Ten schools are participating, each contributing eight classrooms—four assigned to treatment (tw o 5th-grade and two 6th-grade) and four serving as controls, resulting in a total of 80 classrooms. Within each classroom, approximately 10 students with parental 668 consent will participate in data collection, resulting in 400 students in the treatment group and 400 in the "businessas-usual" control group. Prior to the full study, we conducted a pilot from February to June 2025 in Michigan (U.S.) schools with 23 students and two teachers who completed 54 lessons. The pilot aimed to gather teachers’ and students’ perceptions of the online collaborative SEL curriculum and to identify areas for improving the system and its implementation. In this short paper, we present the overall curriculum design, summarize teacher and student feedback from the pilot, and highlight an interesting finding that gibberish chats  are negatively associated with student learning—consistent with prior evidence that off-task or disruptive talk in collaborative learning environments can hinder engagement and learning outcomes (Carpenter et al., 2020; Park et al., 2021). Figure 1 Teacher (left) and Student (right) Interfaces In addition, both teachers and students have access to dashboards that provide summaries of the learning activities. The teacher dashboard displays students' communication quality (e.g., on-topic discussions vs. off-task chats), responses to questions, and patterns of interaction within teams. It also enables teachers to provide personalized feedback to individual students. The student dashboard presents information, including their own contributions compared to those of other team members, teacher feedback, and badges awarded by teachers.

## Preliminary findings from pilot study

Reception by teachers and students Teachers reported positive perceptions of EPCAL, highlighting ease of use, engaging content, and smooth implementation, with minor concerns about some role-play scenarios. Most students found the activities enjoyable and beneficial, especially for collaboration, while a few suggested improvements such as adding a back button, refining chat features, and simplifying certain tasks.

Gibberish message and learning performance We analyzed 2,587 chat turns from the pilot, coding each message as gibberish (1) or not (0). We defined a Gibberish Index (GI) combining (a) the fraction of gibberish turns and (b) cross-session prevalence (proportion of sessions with any gibberish), computed as their product.  Student performance was measured using two embedded questions for each lesson: The first was a self-reported rating, in which students evaluated their grasp of the targeted skills on a scale of 0 to 4 after completing each lesson. The second was a situational judgment question, which assessed whether students could select the most appropriate response in a scenario-based situation based on what they learned in that lesson, scored as 0 or 1.For each student, we averaged scores across completed sessions and summed them into a total score.  Pearson correlation showed a significant negative relationship between GI and performance (r = -0.419, p = 0.0467), suggesting more gibberish is associated with lower learning. Given the small pilot sample, results are preliminary and will be validated with full data. Summary Preliminary findings indicate positive responses from both teachers and students, as well as valuable insights from the process data captured by the EPCAL platform. Initial analyses of the interaction  process data suggest that meaningful patterns exist linking students' communication behaviors to their learning outcomes.
