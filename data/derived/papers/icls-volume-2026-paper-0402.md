# Superpowers and Fears: International School Teachers' Beliefs about AI in Multilingual Classrooms

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines international school teachers’ beliefs about AI in multilingual classrooms through qualitative analysis of 103 survey responses. Teachers primarily desired AI’s pedagogical support, especially for curriculum and instruction and language support. However, they raised concerns about misinformation, limited cultural understanding, and student overreliance on AI. The findings highlight a tension between AI’s benefits and risks, emphasizing the need for ethical implementation, safeguards, and teacher training to support equitable, linguistically responsive AI-integrated learning environments.

International schools are multilingual and diverse, challenging traditional definitions centered on English-medium education (Bettney Heidt & Olson-Wyman, 2025). In this paper, international schools are defined as independent institutions that adopt an international approach to students, faculty, curriculum, and language. While this diversity can enrich learning, it also requires teachers to navigate unfamiliar linguistic and cultural contexts. However, many international schools still privilege English, often overlooking students’ home languages and their multilingual assets (Bettney Heidt & Nordmeyer, 2023). While some teachers may still hold hegemonic beliefs about language superiority (Bettney Heidt & Olson-Wyman, 2025), others aim to build on students’ linguistic and cultural experiences. AI tools, particularly LLMs, offer potential support through translation and generation of teaching materials like multimedia content (Donley, 2024; Yang & Lin, 2025) and teaching plans (Hu et al., 2025). However, AI tools raise concerns such as accuracy of information (Saeidnia et al., 2025), cultural sensitivity (Godwin-Jones, 2025), and teacher roles, leaving many educators unsure how to integrate such technologies responsibly (Felix, 2020; Ng, 2025).

Understanding teachers’ perspectives is essential as they determine how AI tools would be applied (Akram, 2022; Shifflet, 2025). This is especially relevant in international schools where the multilingual and multicultural diversity could create greater opp ortunities. Exploring both teachers’ desires and fears around AI can guide researchers and technologists in developing ethical, practical, and empowering integration of AI tools for multilingual classrooms. In this study, we aim to answer the following research questions: RQ1: What superpowers do international school teachers wish they have for supporting multilingual learners? RQ2: What fears do international school teachers have when using AI when teaching multilingual students?

## Method

We conducted a survey of international school teachers, distributed via UW Madison’s MLRC (Multilingual Learning Research Center) mailing list and social media (Guha et al., 2025). The survey covered topics like beliefs about multilingualism and translanguaging, classroom practices, attitudes towards AI, and desired areas of support. Participants were experienced teachers, with over half of them having more than 11 years of teaching experience. Over 50% of their students were multilingual and were 5 to 19. In this work, we focused on two questions: 1) “If you had any superpower to help you support your multilingual students, what would it be?” and 2)“When you hear “AI support for multilingual students,” what concerns, fears, or constraints come to mind?” We collected and analyzed 103 responses on desired support and 94 on AI concerns. Through qualitative analysis, each response was divided into lines based on their content, resulting in 267 superpower and 247 fear-related lines. The first coder developed a codebook, grouping subthemes into broader categories and iteratively refining them (Miles et al., 2014). Then the second coder coded a portion of the data using the themebook. The initial Cohen’s kappa was 0.63 for superpowers and 0.66 for fears. The discrepancies were resolved through discussion (Krippendorff, 2011). In subsequent coding rounds, reliability was increased to 0.72 and 0.80, indicating substantial agreement (Landis & Koch, 1977; McHugh, 2012).

2635 For superpowers, we identified the major themes of Pedagogy, Language, Telepathy, and Community Impact. Within Pedagogy (40%), teachers especially desired to master curriculum and instruction (15%) by designing meaningful curricula, activities, and teaching materials. They wanted to foster inclusivity (8%) by creating a welcoming learning environment, and change students’ mindset (6%) to recognize multilingual abilities as assets. Other subthemes included supporting personalized learning (5%) and independent learning (2%), assessing students’ performance fairly and accurately (2%), and persevere through challenges  and varying learning paces while supporting students’ growth (2%). The second most wished superpower was Language  (35%) which comprises language fluency and translation. Language fluency (25%) refers to mastering more than one language, particularly the home language of students. Translation (10%) on the other hand is the ability to automatically convert a language into another so that individuals can communicate with each other. The third theme is Telepathy (17%). They believed that both empathy  (9%), being able to understand students’ emotions and react appropriately, and knowing students’ needs (5%), being able to understand exactly where and what kind of support students need for their learning, are crucial for supporting multilingual students. They also expressed a desire to understand students’ cultural backgrounds (3%) like values, traditions, and social norms. Finally, international school teachers desired to have a Community Impact (7%). This includes influencing educators and policymakers (5%) by supporting teacher professional development and shaping policies, and empowering students’ family (2%) by engaging with parents and caregivers to help them understand teachers’ pedagogical goals and foster homelanguage use. On international school teachers’ concern for bringing AI to multilingual classrooms, 16 themes emerged. Teachers feared the limitations of AI itself, including its potential for misinformation  (15%), lack of understanding cultural context (12%), and the lack of privacy (8%) measures. Teachers also had concerns around communal/systemic support such as the lack of teacher’s knowledge of AI (1%), lack of parents’ knowledge of AI (1%), AI policy (3%), and the lack of AI education (8%). Teachers also believed AI could more directly impact learning in a negative way. Students could develop overreliance on AI (16%), and lack critical thinking (9%), language fluency (6%), creativity (2%), and social interaction (3%). Teachers said AI could also make classrooms less inclusive  (4%), foster plagiarism  (3%), and limit learning  (6%) altogether. Lastly, teachers expressed concerns on the barriers (5%) they would face during teaching. Teachers’ emphasis on pedagogical and language-related “superpowers” highlights a need for integrated approaches that support both content and language development. This suggests that effective instruction in international schools requires tools and strate gies that simultaneously address language proficiency and subject understanding. The strong focus on communication also underscores the importance of fostering interaction in linguistically diverse classrooms. In this context, AI has the potential to address these needs by enabling real-time translation and providing language scaffolding (Donley, 2024; Yang & Lin, 2025). Regarding teachers’ fears about AI: first, teachers hope that AI systems improve overall, particularly in issues of misinformation, bias, and data privacy. They emphasized the need for AI tools with clear safeguards to ensure that educational content remains accurate and appropriate and called for AI tools specifically designed for educational purposes and diverse learners. Second, the findings highlight the need for AI education based on the reported lack of AI education among teachers and students. Both teachers and students need to understand how to use AI critically, including recognizing what it can and cannot do, learning how to craft effective prompts, and how to critically evaluate AI-generated information rather than blindly accept it. Teachers also  expressed a lack of knowledge and uncertainty about AI and how to apply it in their teaching, which led to anxiety about allowing students to use AI. This suggests that teacher professional development opportunities (e.g., AI training or workshops) should precede student AI education. Such preparation would enable teachers to integrate AI tools more meaningfully and effectively into their pedagogical practices. Considering both teachers’ desires and fears, the goal is to explore how AI can enhance inclusive classroom practices. For instance, AI-supported translation, discussion, and writing tools can facilitate communication for multilingual students, potentially  increasing participation and interaction. While some teachers feared that AI might reduce social interaction, others recognized its potential to bridge linguistic and cultural gaps. Thus, the discussion should move toward how educators can leverage AI’s advantages while critically addressing issues such as bias, misinformation, and lack of cultural understanding. Developing policies that promote appropriate and meaningful AI use, even among students, is essential for responsible integration. Equally important is communicating with parents and the community to help them understand the role of AI. Building mutual trust among teachers, students, and families will be essential for creating an equitable and meaningful learning environment in the age of AI.
