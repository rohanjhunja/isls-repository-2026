# Constructions of Feasibility within Expansive Designs for Justice with Communities

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Design-based research (DBR) has served as an important methodological building block for researchers interested in working with communities to envision equitable possibilities for learning, particularly ones that differ from the status quo. Especially within these expansive design contexts, feasibility is a thorny issue precisely because it has historically been leveraged as a rigid, objective means to delimit what is possible in the world as it is, as opposed to what it could be. At the same time, we cannot ignore issues of feasibility if our goal is to realize expansive designs in real learning environments. This symposium brings together five papers across different phases of DBR and collectively illustrate a conception of feasibility that is dynamic, contextual, and contested. We design this symposium specifically to engage the learning sciences community about how such conceptions of feasibility can open up expansive possibilities for design.

## Introduction

This symposium contributes to an emerging line of d esign-based research (DBR) in the learning sciences that is shifting the orientation from the given to expansive new possibilities in designing for learning and equity (Garcia & Mirra, 2023). DBR as world-making towards just, expansive ends is a community-oriented learning practice, one that requires iteratively molding wide-ranging desires, wishes, and fears into tangible de sign possibilities. During this transition, expansive possibilities – liminal and fragile in the initial stages of imagining – often lose their edge as they become easily blunted into dominant practices (Rajala et al., 2023; Chang et al., 2024). Central to this convergence to the status quo is the issue of feasibility, a concept conventionally deployed to determine whether a proposed transformation is achievable wit hin the world as it is: technically, economically, socially, or politically (Patterson et al., 2024; Ackerman, 2000). The underlying question is often: why waste time considering something if it is not feasible?

Given this conventional understanding of feasibilit y, in spaces of expansive envisioning, feasibility is accompanied by a negative connotation. As designers, researchers and educators, we often hear learners voice the glimmer of a radical possibility but b efore it is given time to breathe, it is quashed by  even the mention of feasibility (Chang & Philip, in press). Feasibility is a powerful construct that can be efficiently deployed to delimit sharp boundaries between which proposals are wishful fantasy vs. educated hopefulness (Levitas, 1990), and who is a “dreamer” vs. “doer.” However, dismissing feasibility altogether is also no desirable option in design contexts. After all, wishful thinking with no intent to change also only reproduces the status quo. In this symposium, we bring together five technolog y-engaged projects that engage with students, teachers, and community members. Collectively, thes e papers seek to reform our understanding of how we consider feasibility in DBR, from imagination to im plementation. We start from the idea that because the world as it is is contested, so too must be a conception of feasibili ty. Feasibility is far from objective, but is activ ely contested and being constructed, closing off and op ening up new world-making possibilities. All world-making projects are imbued with social, cultural, politica l, ethical, and technical dimensions, and we requir e an understanding of feasibility that captures the dyna mics and complexities of those context-dependent constructions.

The learning sciences, as a field, is well-positioned to provide nuance to these discussions b ecause (a) increasingly interested in addressing systemic inst itutional issues in education and beyond, (b) well-versed in designing for and studying human activity in organic learning spaces instead of laboratory environments (i.e., the sites of enacting world-building), (c) developed novel theoretical and meth odological approaches for embracing the speculative, utopian aspects of world-making (Garcia & Mirra, 2023; Philip et al., 2018). In this symposium, we ask: in the process of envisioning, implementing, and sustaining new technologysupported worlds outside the status quo, how do com peting notions of what is real and attainable precl ude or enable the emergence of radical new possibilities?

The five papers in this symposium will explore different temporal phases of DBR as world-making. The first three papers address the goal formation/imagi nation phases of designing for worldmaking. The las t two papers address the enactment of such designs. We or ganize the papers in this way in order to illustrat e a key contribution, namely how participants operationalize feasibility of their desired outcomes depending on the phase of DBR. Specifically, during formation and imaginat ion, interest holders often took up opportunities t o suspend immediate material questions of feasibility, thereb y creating expansive spaces where they could grappl e with enduring and naturalized political narratives – a massively challenging task in its own right. On the other hand, in our papers which more carefully study the enactment of such designs, interest holders were tasked with grappling with overwhelming sets of immediate material issues  and doing so blunted some of the initial goals.  H owever, those material issues also res ulted in unforeseeable, emergent possibilities. Eac h paper raises a different context that introduces many nuances; below, we briefly introduce each of the papers and the context under study. Envisioning: Goal formation and imagination of new worlds Chang and Philip  examine a three-year co-design project that engaged high-school aged youth of color in envisioning AI-supported equitable possibilities for learning that  are outside the conventional possibilities of K-12 education. Their study sheds light on how constructions of feasibility are co-constituted with identity and social practice.

Cortez examines a pedagogical practice that drew from video game play to engage educators in collective imagination for equity. The study identifies refram ings of feasibility that shaped opportunities for r e-imagining video game play to address social dilemmas related to equity. Shaw focuses on the practice of restorying in and throug h which nondominant youth interrogated and reimagined alternative worlds to challenge dominant imaginaries and their notions of feasibility within computer science.

Enacting: Implementing and sustaining new worlds Rajala, Lehtinen, and Taimela examine expansive uses of digital communication technologies to promote utopian pedagogy and students’ civic activism in a rural hi gh school in Finland. They show how such digital communication tools enabled the students and their teachers to organize the ir transformative activity and expand its scope beyond boundaries of activity systems and across geographical distance and time. Garcia, Vishwanath, and Pea examine unforeseen challenges of feasibility that emerged in their attempts to engage globally and culturally distant geographi cal locations and communities in their research. Th e research drew from digital storytelling and immersive panoramic video to center globally under-represented communities and voices on the topic of climate change.

Structure for symposium Across our studies, we illustrate how the two phase s of DBR described here afford different constructi ons of feasibility. We therefore structure our symposium t o elucidate these differences and elicit conversati on in the learning sciences community about how a multifacete d, contextual understanding of feasibility can info rm DBR efforts across all the phases. The symposium co-chairs will provide some initial framing (5 minutes), before each speaker briefly describes their project (7 minutes each, 35 minutes total). Next, we will separate into two small groups: Envisioning and Enactment. While symposium participants will join the group most connected to their experience, presenters will join the opposite group (e.g., Cortez’s paper is on Envisioning but t hey will join the Enactment group). Together, the small group will di scuss for 20 minutes about 1.) how feasibility has been constructed and/or contested in participants’ resea rch and 2.) how different co nstructions of feasibility across Envisioning and Enactment may come to shape existing work in DBR. After a 10 minute share-out, our discussant Nicole Mirra will reflect on the collective learnin gs from the presentation (10 minutes). Dr. Mirra ha s written extensively about radical dreaming alongside and with communities and has a long record of applying speculative approaches across the phases of DBR laid out in thi s symposium. The remaining time will be allocated f or audience questions and whole-group discussion.

Growing into expansive futures: Constructing feasibility as a long-term, proleptic activity Michael Alan Chang, Thomas M. Philip

## Context and setting

Cooperative and participatory design has become inc reasingly accepted as an important practice in the design of Artificial for Education (AI-Ed) tools as a means to meaningfully incorporate st akeholder voices (U.S. Department of Education, 2023). Acknowledging the constraints of schools and the multiple demands on schools, researchers have explored the possibilities of usin g AI tools to support collaboration and relationali ty (Chang et al., 2024; Tanksley, 2024). In denaturalizing the s tatus quo of schoolin g, researchers have created co-design contexts that scaffold learning across multiple tim escales, primarily by attending to the broader hist orical, political, and ethical dimensions of schooling/technology (Benjamin, 2019) or interactional sensemaking through facilitative moves that opens up or closes off poss ibilities (Chang & Philip, in press). However, what  is missing here is an exploration of how “recent time” (Daniel et al., 2023) comes to shape one’s identity, and consequently, what is desirable and possible (Dreier, 2008) for schools and AI. Here, we share a three year co-design effort that engages primarily high-school aged youth of color in envisioning AI-supported equitable possibilities for learning that are outside the conventional possibilities of K-12 education. We focus on the envisionings of two youth, Karl and Mango, who participated in our workshops across the three years and ask: how do recent experiences of youth in K-12 settings shape their envisioned worlds within multi-year co-design contexts, and how do those experiences change their views on what possible worlds are deemed feasible, necessary, and feasible-by-necessity? Identity and future-making have been connected in the learning sciences  through prolepsis, where the past informs the future, thereby guiding actions in  the present (Gutierrez, 2018). Proleptic activity is intertwined with telos, or the existing and shifting goals of an ongoing activity (Sengupta-Irving et al., 2021). To understand how the recent past guides the interleaving of prolepsis and telos, we draw on social practice theory which elucidates how learning unfolds across context and time (Penuel et al., 2016), all while political and social institution of schooling itself is evolving (Lave & McDermott, 2002). Outside of education, critical organizational studies help describe how students and their local schools co-evolve. Dennis Mumby characterizes th e co-evolution of controlling organizations and social a ctors as a dialectical process, where  “social acto rs are neither romanticized nor viewed as unwitting dupes but rath er are seen as engaging in a locally produced, disc ursive process of self-formation that is always ongoing, always tension filled” (Mumby, 2005, p.39). Contradictions and tensions between locally produced discourses (“recent past”) and globally-produced discourses (“historical time”) thereby become the seeds of new worlds within the interactional space of a co-design environment.

## Methods and analysis

Our data includes video recordings and design artif acts collected over all three years of our Learning  Futures Workshop, totalling around 200 hours of data. Addit ionally, we conducted exit interviews with particip ants who attended multiple workshops over those three years,  including our two focal youth Karl and Mango. We c reated content logs for each discussion unit of the data a nd noted similarities and differences in participan ts’ hopes and dreams regarding schools and AI. Next, we deductive ly coded the video data looking in particular for opportunities and barriers described by youth in th eir efforts to envision or critique new schooling p ossibilities (Penuel et al., 2016). Starting from these codes, w e then added additional labels that identified loca l experiences as being resistive or belonging to the status quo, and then documented how those local experiences opened up or closed off opportunities for world-building.

## Findings and implications

Our findings demonstrate that over the three years participating in our workshop, Karl and Mango had m any similar wishes in schools (i.e., wanting to feel cared for, heard,  and respected). However, when it came to conceptualizing how to realize those wishes, they p roposed and justified those wishes in considerably different ways, e.g., from eliminating poorly performing students from their collaborative work in year one, to creating an AI that supports teachers in advocating for their struggling students in year three. Our findings suggest that these new possibilities emerged from (a) the students’ recent experiences in schooling which they described as clamping down on their freedom as the schools reopened durin g the COVID-19 pandemic (b) their local ways o f resisting these clamp downs in the school, and (c) a change in their perceptions on the goals of schooling, from individually performing well on standardized tests to developing  skills “applicable to real life.” We show how thos e recent local practices and shifting goals inform new visions of schooling, thereby creating the context for justifying what possibilities for schools and AI are feasible, necessary, and feasible-by-necessity.

Our findings indicate that the construction of feasibility in co-design spaces is co-constituted with one’s identity developed within the supports and obstacles of their local practices and shifting hopes for the future. This understanding of feasibility contributes to how we build speculative co-design contexts; rather than consider feasibility as something to be suspended or avoided, future work on co-design should support participants on speculating on multiple futures for themselves and consider feasibility as a byproduct of those envisionings. The moral, ethical, and socio-political dimensions of collective dreaming: Dream scoping as pedagogical practice Arturo Cortez

## Context and setting

This paper examines educators’ learning as they developed dream scoping, a futures-oriented pedagogical practice that leverages the moral, ethical, and socio-political dimensions of speculation. Within the con text of an after-school setting, educators routinely engaged and ref lected on their video game play with young people. In particular, as part of the design of the after-school program, educators and young people drew upon readings from socio-technical and gaming studies to re-imagine inequities they en countered in their game play. During these reflections, educators and young people were expect ed to identify and resolve dilemmas that were part of the game design or emerged as a result of how they play ed the games. Over the course of this semester-long afterschool program, educators learned to support youth in imagining beyond today’s injustices (Garcia & Mi rra, 2023), especially as they tinkered with and represe ntations of race, gender, and sexuality in the cont ext of game play (Cortez et al., 2024). As the semester unfolded, educators collectively reflected on their work with youth and they began to identify pedagogical practices that enabled and constrained resolutions to the dilemmas that emerged in game play. In particular, they identified discou rse practices that leveraged young people’s multi-faceted understanding of complex social dilemmas. Often, th eir collective dreams involved plausible, fantastic al, and imaginative scenarios, and these served as resource s that educators would leverage to identify “possib le futures and using them as tools to better understand the pr esent and to discuss the kind of future people want, and, of course, ones people do not want” (Dunne & Raby, 2013, p. 2-3). In this regard, this paper identifies how educators learned to explore the moral, ethical, and socio-political contours as they collectively identified resolutions to social dilemmas in video gameplay. While educators frequently leveraged frames of feasibility as part of their collective dreaming with young people,  they did so in order to identify if a dream should be manifested and brought to fruition.

## Methods and analysis

In this social design-based experiment (Gutiérrez, 2018), educators’ learning was iteratively designed and studied as part of the naturalistic collective process that  resulted in their reflections on video gameplay. T his study examines approximately 35 hours of video recordings  and the collectively written field notes of 4 afte r-school educators. Video data were transcribed and all data  were reduced and coded across themes and using ind uctive processes (Bogdan and Bilken, 1997). Video analysis processes were used in order to examine the specific micro interactional moments of collaborative inquiry as e ducators identified and generated pedagogical proce sses that enabled and constrained collective forms of dreaming with young people.

## Findings and implications

As such, this study reports on how educators indivi dually and collectively learned to engage in a spec ulative process of dream scoping. In particular, this paper identifies three speculative framings that shaped opportunities for re-imagining video game play. In particular, the pedag ogical practices are: shaping, delimiting, and reproducing resolutions to social dilemmas across (1) moral dimensions, (2) ethical dimensions, and (3) and sociopolitical dimensions. These framings were developed  as part of thei r collective reflection on their pedagogical practices. While these frames served to delimit soc ial dreaming, in some cases, the feasibility of the se dreams were shaped according to a set of commitments that the educators felt were necessary when identifying new social futures.

As attention to equity and justice-oriented pedagogies increases, the importance of de signing for equitable relationships between educators and learners should become central in how we prepare educators across formal and informal learning environments. As discu ssed above, collectively imaging beyond today’s ine quities requires intentional design in relationship buildin g between adults and young people. In this regard, educators have the power to shape how young people imagine and the resulting dreams that emerge as they try to r esolve everyday dilemmas. I argue that educators can either delimit, reproduce, or shape imaginings of the future. While efforts often exist to delimit young people’s dream s based on frames of pragmatism (i.e., feasibility), this study argues that educators engage in practices that intentionally shape how young people dream. Through the practices of dream scoping, or princip led collective imagining —educators and young people often ask whether a drea m should emerge in the future based on a set of moral, ethical, and socio-political commitments. Navigating tensions between reality and restorying with adolescent youth of color in an informal CS workshop Mia Shaw Efforts to critically reimagine computing education  have promoted rethinking the past and present in o rder to “create more equitable and just futures” (Morales-Navarro & Kafai, 2023, p. 534). However, what happens when our abilities to imagine become suffocated under the weight of living each day not only through the escalation of global crises but also by dominant imaginaries and their notions of feasibility that paint resisting computing’s role in such crises as futile? Through exploring moments  when adolescent youth of Color in an informal comp uter science (CS) workshop struggled with visualizing alternative worlds beyond current realities, this paper hopes to illustrate how integrating storytelling, literacy, and design by means of restorying through design offers a context for interrogating and reimagining new worlds with CS. As a speculative literacy practice, restorying describes the ways youth from nondominant communities have levera ged digital tools to create new stories from perspe ctives that have been  marginalized or silenced (Stornaiuolo & Thomas, 201 8). Throughout workshop activities, participants applied CS concepts and practices in the form of programmable paper circuit materials to the creation of interactive quilt patches that restoryed dominan t narratives related to the field (Shaw, Coleman, T homas, & Kafai, 2023).

I conducted inductive qualitative analysis of parti cipants’ quilt patches, design worksheets, and interviews, as well as researcher field and debrief  notes. Subsequent findings will illustrate (1) how  participants negotiated the task of imagining new CS st ories while contending with present CS realities and existing power structures and (2) the role the learning environmen t played in shaping participants’ ability to restor y alternative pasts, presents, and futures with CS. Through analyzing how notions of what is feasibility or possibility impacted youths’ restorying through design  experiences, scholars can better understand how soc iotechnical imaginaries—”collectively held, institutionally stabilized, and  publicly performed visions of desirable futures, animated by shared understandings of forms of socia l life and social order attainable through, and sup portive of, advances in science and technology” (Jasanoff & Kim, 2015, p. 19)—become unveiled and embodied during the process of imagining stories and realities about CS. Further, this study invites learning scientists and practitioners to critically reflect on the foundations and mechan isms of dominant sociotechnical imaginaries in the field, and how who gets to decide what is considered feasible rooted in capitalism, racism, heterosexism, and ableism. Through naming the present CS realities of our worl ds and historicizing those realities in historical and cultural contexts (Stornaiuolo & Thomas, 2018), we can begin  to resto ry alternative imaginaries with emer ging technologies.

Using digital communication technologies to promote a utopian pedagogy and students’ civic activism over contexts and time Antti Rajala, Jarmo Lehtinen, Ilona Taimela Aim This paper examines a successful implementation of a utopian pedagogy in a Finnish rural high school. A utopian pedagogy in this high school sought to support the students in collectively challenging established no tions of possibility and to reimagine and enact ecologically sustainable ways of organizing a ctivities within their communities (Rajala et al., 2023). The paper focuse s on Climate Warriors, which is a large climate act oin group of around 100 students and several teachers of the school. The group is guided by a pedagogy of concrete utopias, which seeks to harness students’ capacity to reimag ine new visions for the future and contribute to pr actically enacting these future visions in local settings. A concrete utopia leverages potentials for change i n an ongoing, existing activity (Bloch, 1986). Here, the term con crete utopias refers to young people’s visions of a lternative futures and enactment of such visions in terms of p edagogical projects planned by youth in collaborati on with their teachers and other stakeholders, such as city planners, NGOs, and other community partners. These projects can be characterized as concrete utopias because th ey involve an orientation to learning as reimaginin g and changing in the existing ways of living and organizing activities in their local communities. The aim of this paper is to understand the role of creative uses of digital technology and social media in the project for supporting the feasibility of a uto pian pedagogy as it was implemented in and beyond t he high school. We pose the following questions for our inquiry: How does digital technology and social media platforms help to organize and expand the civic engagement of  the Climate Warriors group across contexts and ove r time? How are tensions, dilemmas and possibilities manage d in the digitally-mediated activity, and what are the implications for the viability of the utopian pedagogy? Theoretical frameworks Digital communication technologies are transforming  the boundaries of learning, and connecting informa l and formal settings in unprecedented ways, with profoun d implications for young people’s learning and enga gement across contexts and time (Sefton-Green & Erstad, 2017). These new technologies have become  a significant infrastructure for youth’s civic engagement and pol itical mobilization (Ito et al., 2015; Picard et al. 2020). However, harnessing the learning potential of digit al technologies and social media for pedagogical pu rposes is not easy. For example, tensions and conflicts are likely to emerge when diverse identities, interests, and discourses intersect in hybrid informal/formal spaces (Lantz-Andersson et al., 2013). School organization of time and space can also pose pedagogical challenges (Kumpulainen & Rajala, 2017).

## Methods

The data include the Climate Warriors group’s inter nal communications in Whatsapp between 2018 and 202 1 (altogether 320 pages), as well as texts, pictures and videos publicly available in the group’s Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, and Youtube accounts. In addition, the dat a include ethnographic field notes and student and teacher interviews. In the data analysis, we first created a timeline for the project, which mapped the spatio-temporal aspects of the projects, their major events, the pe rsons involved and  division of labour,  as well as digital tools used in the project. In the second phase, we conduc t a systematic thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2 017) on the group internal Whatsapp chat over three years, as well as, the group’s social media posts in and across different platforms. This analysis is contextualized with the help of the other ethnographic data.

## Findings and implications

The findings show how the digital communication too ls contested established notions of what is feasibl e to achieve in a formal education setting – for example, related to social and physical boundar ies; time constraints; curricular constraints; student and teacher roles – by providing varied platforms for expansive civic action for the Climate Warriors group and its members in their sch ool, in the municipality, and in the society at lar ge. The analysis unpacks the variety of functions that the digital communication tools served the group, and how their use developed over time. These tools, for example, helped the group to coordinate their activity, organize their activity in time and space, overcome boundaries between diff erent activities, reach to different  audiences and collaborators, and create spaces for student-initiated actions to envision and enact change. The findings also shed light on how the digital communication tools created tensions and dilemmas as well as new communication spaces for addressing those. In all, the study makes an important contrib ution by shedding light on creative pedagogical uses associated with digital tools to support the implementation of a utopian pedagogy across contexts. It offers a rare developmental perspective on the topic by trac ing the development of the activity over the course  of three years.

Tendencies and latencies: Negotiating multiphasic viability in global youthcentered design based virtual field trip climate change study Jorge Eduardo Garcia, Aditya Vishwanath, Roy Pea Purpose This study examines globally-situated students’ concepts and practices of agency, collaboration, design, and affect while creating Virtual Field Trips (VFTs) informed by and responding to climate change. We centered th ese elements to better understand the roles of new medi a technologies to make meaning, build empathy, and foster agency in a global crisis. We encountered unforesee n obstacles challenging us to either pivot or sacri fice core commitments. Our study participants aren't only global, from countries such as Egypt, India, and Kazakhstan, but U.S. participants are all students of color —seldom heard voices in climate change discourse. Ch anging our research partner, a global student-led organization, to a local partner may have made aspects of the study  more convenient, but would also de-center leadership from global youth voices. We began to theorize about these challenges and the  very idea of viability. Design-based research is by its nature iterative and responsive to socio-historical realities. We sought tonegotiate these challenges by questioning their tendencies, and attuning to the potential for latencies (Greenaway, 2024) in the Blochian sense— tendencies being the pressures the historically-informed present time exert on what’s possible, whereas latencies are the distinct possibilities for the future actua lly available and at hand now. These questions surfaced how participants themselves encountered notions of viab ility while experiencing climate change. This pivot  offered a revealing lens into the research pressures  the socio-historically informed present exerts, and highlight ed opportunities to not only refuse the default of who  are research partners and how studies are conducte d, but how participants encountered viability as they thought about climate change. Emerging questions include: 1) how to contend with the global te ndencies to mute particular voices and approaches t o research? 2) how to center participants’ viability notions, not only of study participation, but the larger existential questions  climate change presents in their world region?

## Methodology

Digital storytelling fosters learners’ agency, iden tity narration, and develops multimodal literacies (Lambert, 2002; Ohler et al., 2013). Virtual reality (Bailenson, 2018) and 360° panoramic video (VFTs: Pea, 2006; Peterson et al., 2020) promise a new authoring medium for es tablishing empathy (De Wall, 2010). Media viewpoint  and motion create “fields of potential action” to captu re viewers' attention and promote conditions for en gaged learning and knowledge construction (Lewis et al., 2018). This research empowers globally-situated high schoolers (14-18 yrs) to become storytellers by creating panoramic VFTs augmented by generative AI tools. We ask: ● How does project collaboration shape participant le arning processes, outcomes, agency, and notions of viability?

● How do researchers identify tendencies, latencies, and agency in design-based research? Participants are working in 50 nearby-located pairs in diverse global community contexts,  each loaned a 360° camera kit. Participants are engaging in Zoom works hops on storytelling, topic selection, VFT producti on, and sharing. We advance the connected learning research community, “for broadened access to learning that is socially embedded, interest-driven, and oriented toward educational, economic, or political opportunity” (Ito et al., 2013). Thematic analysis of qualitative data will identify patterns and themes re: student agency, learning processes, and affective engagement with process and subject. Quan titative data for comparing outcomes between the AI  and non-AI groups will be analyzed using statistical methods.

## Findings and implications

We encountered varying viability in every phase of research design and implementation. This project has unique ambitions in utopian underpinnings, scope, and tech nical depth. It centers non-dominant voices in Western education research on a complex and enc ompassing global topic, and requires methods to ren der global participants’ meaning making, ideations, and vision s visible via an immersive panoramic VFT. There wer e so many layers of potential project blunting, a myriad  of obstacles pushing towards defaulting to study t he usual participants in the usual way; so many ways current research tendencies (Greenaway, 2024) could have thwarted our hopes for a study centering globally non-dominant voices. These obstacles included language,  technical expertise, cultural dimensions, socio-historical contextualization of “climate change”, political restraints in world regions that might challenge learner engagement wit h the topic, but also access to the internet or oth er necessary tools like AI, equipment apps, and quo tidian challenges like shipping. This paper proffers a provocation for researchers implementing design-based research frameworks in global contexts. As re searchers concerned with proleptic research approaches, how can we identify and de-center the tendencies, whilst simultaneously attuning to not-yet-visible latencies to expand our work?
