# Youth Navigating Educational and Social Contexts Through Micro, Meso, and Macro Positioning: A Study of Memoir

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study focuses on how women who have endured difficult childhoods are positioned within micro, meso, and macro level scales in educational and social contexts. Findings suggest that although the women experienced unwanted positioning within the three levels, they used improvisation to seek persons or contexts in which they experienced desired positioning to promote their agency.

## Introduction

Holland et al.’s (1998)  description of a person’s “history-in-person” captures the experiences vulnerable youth encounter when navigating educational and social contexts, noting it is the “sediment from past experiences upon which one improvises, using the cultural resources available, in response to the subject positions aff orded one in the present.” (p. 18). Children and young adults from chaotic and dangerous family situations must reconcile their histories to make sense of unfamiliar expectations, processes, and practices in  school and social systems. They improvise their approach to schooling with their le arned ways of doing while negotiating positional id entity through classroom discourse with teachers and peers; transactions with schools and social institutions; and interactions within a larger context of gender, race, class, and power laden societal structures. Through the lens of Holland et al.’s (1998) concept  of figured worlds and positional identities, I am seeking to answer, how positional identities for ch ildren and youth coming from unsafe home environments intersect with their learner identities and how imp rovisation disrupts the undesired social positionin g so that the actors can have greater access to desired spaces, activities, and genres. The subjects of this study are two women who document their struggles in school and life thr ough the writing of memoir. The common thread for t hese women is a traumatic upbringing in which  they endured physical, emotional, or sexual abuse from family members or family friends  and endured parental neglect  in which their basic needs, like food, clothing, and education were unmet. Despite these obstacles, the subjects were able to improvise the making of their lives to later become college educated professionals.

## Conceptual framework

Figured worlds according to Holland et al. (1998) are social and cultural phenomena where people construct their identities through features that are distinct to persons who participate in the same contexts. For example, Holland describes the cultural world of romance where romantic encounters occupied the thoughts of its actors. Individuals share common roles or characteristics,  activities, and understandings of how events should unfold. Positio nal identities are offered or self-negotiated in figured worlds and are defined as “a person’s apprehension of her social position in a lived world: that is, depending on th e others present, of her greater or lesser access t o spaces, activities, genres, and through those genres, autho ritative voices, or any voice at all” (Holland et al., 1998, p. 127). Individuals’ own understanding s of how they fit in the world in relation to others and considering the contexts in which they live, then, can give them the agency they require to make desired change. Figured worlds have been used to describe identity production and the various ways people are positioned in society in various educational contexts. Urrieta (2007) describes Mexican Americans who were drawn into the figured world of Chicana/o activist educators throu gh positioning themselves in conscientious raising efforts. Bartlett (2007) uses th e figured world of an educated person where its mem bers contest the social position of being illiterate by positioning themselves to “feel ” literate through cultural artifacts. Finally, Rub in (2007) explored the availability of learner identities in the figured world of competence in an urban high school.  In this paper, the subjects, Tara and Liz are members of the figured world of survival in childhood in which they are actors in the cultures of extremist survivalism and parental addiction respectively. Micro, meso, and macro scales of positioning Micro, meso, and macro level scales exist within fi gured worlds and represent the conditions the actor s must negotiate in their daily lives. These levels within ecological systems have been used to describe how children and youth are positioned in educational and social contexts. For instance, Anderson (2009) described how micro (lived) and macro (ideological) scales of social practice in classroom activities mediated meso (categorized) level activity. Stroupe (2022) demonstrated how epistemic injustice occ urs in moment-to-moment interactions, micro (within a site), meso (sites withing communities), and macro (sites within national/international syst ems) in curated sites of learning.  These multiple leveled interactions give us insight into the struggles tha t vulnerable youth face in educational and social contexts.  How they overcome those struggles lies in how they improvise through the negotiation of the positions they are placed. Improvisation as agency Holland et al. (1998) discuss improvisation as agen cy by conceptualizing instances in which people face problematic situations that prohibit them from reaching desired goals, and they  are not able to navigate  the problem through conventional means. They must come up with impromptu solutions through cultural resources that are available to them. Holland et al’s (1998)  iconic story of the woman who climbed the house tel ls of a woman in Nepal who considered herself prohibited fr om entering the home of a higher-caste person. When she needed to get to the second story balcony of the home  for a meeting, she climbed the outside of the house. Her sensational improvisation enabled her to take contr ol of a situation that she was not  able tonegotiate before. I consider the entwining relations between and within micro, meso, and macro scales as possibility for children and youth to remake the situations of their lives. Thro ugh improvisation, they seek to find something new while reinventing their lives in response to their changing worlds.

## Methods

I draw upon two memoires as my primary data source to explore positioning vulnerable youth navigate educational and social contexts at micro, meso, and macro levels. Walker (2017) indicates how autobiographical stories and the recounting of one’s own feelings, r eactions, and motives influence what they do or thi nk in situations within context of cultural settings. I c hose memoir because of the rich narrative in which the authors recount the phenomena of their lives that shape the ir identities. Data for this  paper comes from the memoires of two women who overcome traumatic circumstances in t heir childhoods to attend elite universities.  These two memoires were selected for their rich content in reflecting on personal experiences in their K-12 educational and social contexts.  Breaking Night by Liz Murray (2010) is a personal account of her survival in a home of parents addicted to cocaine which includes description of her time being homeless to avoid being placed in foster care as well as her later attendance at Harvard University. Educated by Tara Westover (2018) accounts her upbringing in a family of survivalists isolated from mainstream s ociety where she endured sibling abuse, inadequate medical attention, and only periodic schooling while later completing college at Brigham Young University then graduate school at Cambridge. Both women exist in a figured world i n which they are forging identities of resilience a nd survival to later move to a figured world of a college student.

## Analytic approach

As with an earlier related project in which I examined the improvisation the women used to navigate educational contexts, I again use the framework of interpretive phenomenological analysis which focuses on how individuals make sense of their significant life experiences (Smith et al., 2022).  This framework has been used in identity work which utilizes in-depth interviews and diaries of participants. For t his study, however, this traditional analysis was modified to using published memoires to capture the experien ces and reflections of the authors in their journey from childhood to college. The steps involved are 1) engagement with the d ata; 2) initial notes and comments to explain what the researcher is observin g; 3) forming “experiential statements”; 4) making connections between experiential statements; 5) nam ing emerging themes and organizing them; 6) working with larger samples emerging from the group (for this st udy, a comparison is made between the two cases); a nd 7) looking for patterns across cases (Smith et al., 2022). As I show in my findings, I developed the theme of positional identities in education through the lens of figured  worlds and micro, meso, and macro l evels of positioning. I organize my presentation of the theme through discussing each participants’ evidence of this theme.

## Findings

The data reveals that Tara and Liz are positioned  in micro, meso, and macro processes within the figured  world of survival in childhood (see Table 1). For this study, I used the following definitions of these scales to d escribe this complex changing participation of the subjects  withing each level of the systems. First, m icro-level transactions are the lived experiences of a child that occur in the settings such as the home o r school (Brofenbrenner, 1977). These transactions are commonly seen in interactions between two people such as between a student and a teacher. Wortham (2008), for example, follows a high school student whose identity changes from that of a “good” student to “outcast" through positional interactions with her teachers and peers. Second, Brofenbrenner (1977) defines m eso-level transactions as the relations that occur among syst ems such as home and school. There may be, for example, conflicting behavioral expectations between a child’s home and school. Allen and Eisenhart (2017) found women of color in STEM were positioned by school discourse with defic it views in academic achievement. Last, m acro-level systems refer to dominant  beliefs and ideologies of social institutions within cultures such as race, gender, class, and religion  (Brofenbrenner, 1977). Spencer et al. (2020) discuss how Peruvian youth were positioned through historical narratives of inequality as citizens of their country. Tara and Liz experience both desired and unwanted positioning in each of the systems (see Table 1). For instance, the unwanted examples positioned the actors as outcasts (Tara-micro; Liz-micro and macro) or resistant (Tara-meso). They are often expected to adapt to the spaces they are required to be without the necessary resources (Tara-meso; Liz-micro, meso). Conversely, the subjects also experie nce desired positioning within the different levels. They were empowered and validated through individuals, organizations, and cultural ideology (Tara-micro, meso, macro; Liz-micro, meso, macro). Through micro, meso, and macro positioning, the actors’ identities developed from a deficit stance to one of agency and potential. The findings also suggest that Tara and Liz improvised the navigation of problematic situations to reach desired identities by seeking desired positioning in different contexts. When Tara’s parents, e.g., position her as someone who does not need school, she seeks the help of a sibling who positions her as deserving of an education When her family is positioned as nonconformist by her dance school,  Tara seeks a different approach to become a performer  through singing  in her church  where she is positioned as someone who is talented. When Liz is positioned by the school system as a dropout and discipline problem, she improvises by seeking a different means to receive a high school education. The instructors in an alternative high school position her as someone who has potential. Liz’s social class positioned her as someone who does not belong, she seeks a community where she is positioned as someone who does belong and can achieve. Tara and Liz disrupt unwanted micro, meso, and macro positioning through improvisation by using existing  resources to address conditions that prevent them from reaching desired identities.

## Discussion and significance

Research customarily analyzes human problems either  within micro, meso, or macro  systems but rarely view these issues through the parallel intersection of all of them. Brofenbrenner (1977) suggested research should move away from focusing on existing institutional ideolo gies and systems previously isolated from each othe r and instead strive f or interconnecting and innovative ecological system s by rethinking goals, roles, and activities within the structures. Viewing chronic trauma of children and youth, then, through a complex ecological systems perspective can help inform schools, communities, a nd social and educational policy makers to address the complexity of problems children and youth experience. In addition, trauma experienced through ongoing exposure to abuse or neglect can cause significant problems with learning and it affects the way children and youth act in a classroom (Miller, 2024). Understanding how Liz and Tara were able to navigate educational and social contexts through improvisation despite their chronic trauma,  then, helps schools support children better and he lp them to promote student agency and resiliency for all child ren. This agency fosters increased engagement, moti vation, connection, self-worth, and hope with students (Parameswaran,  2024) which benefits their overall growth, well-being, and development.
