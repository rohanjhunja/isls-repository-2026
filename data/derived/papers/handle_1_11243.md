# Interdisciplinary Teaching and Learning in the Humanities: How it Might Go Wrong

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
There are relatively few empirical investigations of interdisciplinary teaching and learning (ITL), mostly focusing on the affordances of ITL for teachers and learners. In contrast, this study identifies and examines two critical challenges within ITL classrooms, focusing on coherence and the need for cross-disciplinary expertise. Ethnographic data we collected from two schools revealed (1) that students struggled with fragmented understandings when subjects were not effectively integrated, and (2) that teachers faced limitations in addressing topics outside their disciplinary training. These findings can serve educators and policymakers interested in implementing ITL, as well as scholars interested in understanding and conceptualizing it.

## Background

Definitions of interdisciplinary teaching and learning In 2024, the Journal of the Learning Sciences dedic ated its annual special issue to interdisciplinary teaching and learning (ITL), indicating a growing interest in th is topic among learning scientists. Yet with the exclusion of STEM and STEAM, empirical investigations of interdisciplinary teaching and le arning (ITL) are surprisingly scarce. A recent systematic review of the  scientific literature on ITL in the humanities, arts, and soci al sciences, which had broad inclusion criteria, found only 85 empirical studies published in ranked academic journals on this topic. Moreover, this small body of research is quite di vergent in its conception of what ITL  is. ITL has been framed in cognitive and sociocultural terms, and can translate into at least four different pedagogical approaches:

(1) knowledge from one discipline that is employed to expand the boundaries of another discip line; (2) multiple

disciplinary perspectives that are brought to bear upon a single issue; (3) focusing on fundamental knowledge that underlies multiple disciplines (e.g., critical social theories, such as feminism); and (4) new disciplines that are created through the combination of multiple existing disciplines. The dearth of scientific literature on ITL and the conceptual ambiguity  surrounding it  both suggest that more empirical work is required to advance our understanding of what ITL is and what potential it holds (Cohen et al., 2024). Research focus Most studies of ITL have foregrounded this approach’s potential affordances, which include positive outcomes in terms of learners’ knowledge and character development, as well as opportunities for teachers to collaborate and hone their professional knowledge. For instance, Duatepe-Paksu and Ubuz (2009) asked whether integrating drama into a geometry curriculum might mediate better learning outcomes; Schuitema et al. (2009) asked whether ITL can enhance learners’ moral awareness; and Coutts et al (2009) showed how the adoption of  an ITL-based curriculum contributed to teachers’ ability to collaborate across subjects. In contrast, in this study we are primarily interested in identifying and describing some key challenges facing learners and teachers as they engage in ITL, and the detrimental effects these challenges  might have. In so doing, we hope to make a dual contribution to theory and practice, by advancing our theoretical understanding of ITL, while simultaneously alerting educational practitioners and policymakers to some important issues that ought to be considered.

## Context

The data for this paper was collected as part of  a four-year government-funded study  on K-12 ITL in the humanities. The purpose of the study was to inform a n overhaul to the standardized national humanities curriculum in Israel. Previously, the curriculum was almost exclusively  disciplinary, but the Ministry of Education’s leadership  believed that their overall policy goals would be better served by an interdisciplinar y approach in the humanities  (for more on this project see Authors, 2024b). Ten schools were recruited for this study. Teachers from each school participated in professional development over the c ourse of two years. As part of these courses, they developed interdisciplinary lessons in the humanities and taught them in their classrooms.

## Data collection and analysis

In this paper, we focus on ethnographic data collected from two schools. At Tikvah middle school we collaborated with Lia, a first-year history teacher who had been involuntarily ass igned to teach in an interdisciplinary environment, although she preferred to teach  history in a more traditional disciplinary fashion.  At Tel Nof high school we collaborated with Anna, an experienced teacher who led an interdisciplinary humanities program that was established at the school  several years before our study  began, and was upgraded during the professional development courses we led. As participant-observers we immersed ourselves within the social f abric of the schools. We observed, video-recorded, and transcribed a total of 10 45-90-minute lessons from Anna and Lia’s classes (5 lessons each) and conducted three interviews wit h each of them. To understand how classroom discourse mediated ITL, we analyzed the lessons’ transcripts three times. First, we segmented the data, dividing it into episodes according to the general topic of speech, noting the topic, participants, and activity (e.g., giving instructions) for each episode. Next, we coded each episode to identify types of knowledge b uilding and identity work teachers and students engaged in. Finally, we drew on well-established discourse analysis methods to conduct a turn-by-turn analysis of the classroom discourse. These met hods involved asking questions about the meaning of interlocutors’ discourse in relation to various social contexts  (Gee, 2014). In addition, we mapped narrated and narrating events for each episode and asked how interlocutors used discourse to shape their identities and positionalities (Wortham & Reyes, 2015).

## Findings

Challenge #1: The purpose of ITL can by vague Lia’s interdisciplinary unit was dedicated to the European Renaissance. To teach it, she assembled an ad-hoc team of teachers including herself, a history teacher by training; Shira, the science teacher; and Neta, an art student who volunteered at Tikvah. In science, students conducted inquiry into important technological innovations during the Renaissance, such as the telescope. For the rest of the unit, the class split into two groups. One group, led by Lia, studied the history of European explorers such as Columbus and Magellan. The other group, led by Neta, created an art project  inspired by Raphael’s masterpiece The School of Athens. One of the lessons we observed was dedicated to an exploration of the notion of humanism through religious texts. Students worked in small groups on a worksheet with sources from various periods ra nging from antiquity to modernity. At a certain poi nt, Lia called for everyone’s attention, and explained: The general idea of the texts [in the worksheet] is  the topic that developed most during the Renaissance: humanism. It means putting humankind at the center and we a re going to learn through religious sources and through other sources about what humanism means. You need to understand that the basis for these discoveries – the basis for all the expeditions across the globe, for the scientific discoveries, the basis for the development of art, the basis for all of this – was humanism. The understanding that people, their lives, and their happiness are at the center. Lia offered this explanation because she was concerned that the interdisciplinarity of her curriculum was invisible to students, and she wanted to help them rise above the  different subjects in a coherent and meaningful way. Yet to her dismay, this goal was rarely achieved and our field notes indicated that students found it difficult to connect the different subjects. To illustrate, consider the following conversation between Neta (the art teacher) and several students, in which she explained how the art project fit into the broader curricular unit: 101 Neta: So what Raphael did was to insert all of the figures that he thought were most important and influential for that period… 107 Neta: I shadowed out some of the figures [in the original painting]. Like, you’re going to invent it now. 108 Neta: A kind of school, actually define who are the most important people of this period. 109 Student: And what about Raphael…?

The interdisciplinary unit that Lia designed was me ant to connect art, history, and science to advance  students’ understanding of the Renaissance and mediate learni ng that was relevant and interesting. However, beyo nd this general pedagogic aim, Lia had failed to articulate to herself, to the students, and to the other teachers on her team, precisely how the subjects were meant to complement one another, which left students confused. To illustrate this finding, we employ Gee’s (2014) Connection building tool, which calls on analysts to ask how interlocutors use language to draw some connections but not others. I n line 101, Neta drew a connection between (1) Raphael, a 15th century Italian artist, (2) the figures populating his painting (most of wh om lived in ancient Greece), and (3) the renaissance period. She suggested that Raphael chose to paint these figures because they had a pro found impact on his era. In line 107 Neta drew another connection, between Raphael and the students, explaining that just like he chose and painted the influential figu res of his day, so too will they choose the influen tial figures of their time to add to their art. The students, in turn, focused on other connections. For example, one student wanted to know how the figures they were choosing needed to connect to  2022? Were they to choose figures whose impact could be felt today (even if they lived long  ago) or did the figures they chose need to be cont emporary (line 124, 132)? What constituted a meaningful connection between a potentially influential figure and the present era? Could they choose a singer, or were they limit ed to scientists and philosophers (140)? Were they limited to figures whose influence was positive, or could they also choose someone whose impact was negative (145-147)? Neta was baffled by the students’ questions. This was because the answers to students’ questions depended on another, more essential, connection that undergirded the entire project – the connection between art and history. How exactly these two subjects were meant to complement one another in the curricular unit that Lia designed was never made clear to the students or toneta, even though this was of crucial importance. For example, art could be harnessed as a method to support the study of a specific list of other humanities subjects, and then students might be asked to choose a historian, a ph ilosopher, and a politician to populate their new p ainting. Alternatively, the focus might have been history, and in particula r students could have been invited to grapple with the questions of how individuals shape history, and how they are shaped by it. The possibilities are endless. However, because the connection between art, history, and other subjects remained obscure, students had a hard time understanding the purpose of the interdisciplinary lens, and how to employ it. This deep uncertainty was best captured by the question “what is the project itself?” (114), which remained unanswered throughout  entire the school year.

Challenge #2: ITL requires expertise in multiple disciplines One issue that we identified as central to ITL discours e was identity. In Anna’s class, questions surrounding gender and sexuality were salient, while other teachers foregrounded other identities (e.g., nationality and cultural heritage; see Novis-Deutsch et al., 2024). In an interview that we conducted with Anna, she highligh ted that identity-focused discourse was one of her primary aims: [I want] students to encounter age-appropriate characteristics of gender, sexuality, stereotypes, and we find this in the curriculum [that we designed]. For example, if I talk with them about the emergence of the medieval feudal society and I discuss the role of women, who were responsible for educating knights for instance, so [I ask] how are women perceived in society in general and feudal society in particular, according to the material we teach? Considering such statements, it was surprising  to discover that  in our observations, Anna seemed to be intentionally avoiding identity-focused discourse, even when it was specifically fo cused on gender roles in the feudal age. We identified the avoidance of meaningful identity discourse as a recurring theme in Anna’s lessons, and thus as a rich point in our data that was highly informative. To illustrate, consider the fol lowing interaction between Anna and a keen, 8th grade, female student regarding the role of women in feudal society: 201 Anna: The lord is basically the nobleman 202 Student: So, women can’t be lords?

203 Anna: Right, women were sidelined. They had no rights at all and in terms of gender - [hesitates] OK, they were the ones to actually raise the children w ho grew up to be knights like we said. They taught them etiquette and how to dance and-how should I say? To fulfill their roles in various ceremonies, and of course they [the knights] were w arriors. So, women in this case were sidelined. They had no rights.

204 Student: But couldn’t they also be [noble]?

205 Anna: It wasn’t even an issue there. As you can see, this ceremony speaks for itself. We’ll discuss. 206 Student: But Anna, couldn’t they at least fulfill the role of serf? 207 Anna: No, what do you mean, the role of serf? They were, um, they did labor like the men. There was no difference.

This student was troubled that Anna hadn’t addressed wome n’s gender roles  in feudal society. Her questions seemed to be motivated by a contemporary feminist d iscourse, meaning that she was concerned by the fact  that women and men weren’t treated equally in medieval t imes. Her questions in lines 202, 204, and 206 were performed as a break in the flow of the lesson, exp ressed in her tone of voice, which clearly indicate d she was changing the subject. Surprisingly, this was also h ow Anna responded to the string of q uestions, despite them being so precisely aligned with the goals that Anna herself articulated in the interview cited above. Had Anna seized the opportunity presented by the student  to address identity-centered questions head-on, she could have taken advantage of the affordances of ITL to  create a “third space” where teachers and students meaningfully encounter one another (Gutierrez et al., 1995). But because Anna clearly indicated to the class t hat she did not want to dedicate any time to discussing these issues, this entire interaction was treated as an off-topic interruption, by teacher and students alike, and the class moved on. The most plausible explanation that we could find to explain Anna’s behavior was that she was ill-equipped to answer the questions because she was tr ained as literature teacher, and her knowledge of women’s rights in the  Middle Ages was limited, a limitation expressed in her answers in lines 203, 205, and 207. The student presented Anna with three relatively simple yes/no questions, hoping to move beyond a conversation of history to discuss a contemporary value-laden issue, but to each of these questions, Anna seemed unable to provide a simple y es/no answer. Seemingly, this was because she lacked the necessary historical knowledge.

## Discussion and conclusion

ITL has been popular among educational practitioners for many years (Wineburg & Grossman, 2000). However, among scholars, ITL is not well-understood, and it remains under-theorized. Most empirical efforts to conceptualize ITL to date have focused primarily on its affordances, seeking to show how the shift from disciplinary to interdisciplinary learning environments might mediate desirable learning processes and outcomes. In this study we take the opposite track, asking what pedagogical challenges ITL can pose. Analyzing discourse from two ITL classrooms helped us identify and describe two  key challenges that can be traced back to interdisciplinarity.

In Lia’s class, several different subjects were brought to bear upon a single theme. However, this turned out to be a connection that students didn’t find meaningful enough, and they failed to understand how the different subjects were meant to complement each other, and why. As a result, students in this class tended to overlook the interdisciplinarity of the curricular unit the y were engaged in and treat each lesson as a disciplinary one. When the question of how the subjects were meant to compleme nt one another came up, it left learners and teachers confused. In Anna’s class, the goal was to look beyond the discipline (in this case history) and to engage learners in discourse that was interdisciplinary in the fundamental sense, in that it was related to gender, which arguably cuts across subject matter. However, as our findings showed, Anna failed to m ake the most of  the pedagogical opportunities that arose, apparently due to a  lack of disciplinary expertise, which  prevented her from delving deeply into a body of knowledge that she was not well-versed in. In this paper, we are neither advocating for, nor against, ITL. Rather, we hope to make a humble contribution to theory and practice. Theoretically, we have pointed towards two issues that directly relate to ITL: coherence of purpose and disciplinary expertise. Our findings suggest that both constructs are fundamental to ITL, and that when they are lacking, ITL can manifest itself only partially, at best.
