# Fostering Inclusive Practices in Educational Policy Reform

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, we examine how a group of diverse stakeholders in a predominantly White school district expanded their practice of inclusion through an intentional co-design process. The Aleph School District (ASD) in New England, U.S., co-designed a protocol for addressing discrimination and harassment, a persistent concern affecting historically marginalized students with and without disabilities. The school district partnered with the local university and employed the Learning Lab intervention (Bal et al., 2016; 2018), an inclusive, process-oriented methodology to enable the local stakeholders most impacted the chance to better understand and address their concerns. Our analysis of data suggests that, in addition to producing a needed and useful protocol, this process expanded authentic inclusion of student voice in the school district. Further, we identify practices that fostered meaningful inclusion within the Learning Lab and tangible and intangible outcomes that extended beyond the Learning Lab space.

## Introduction

In this paper, we present a school-family-community-university coalition aimed at addressing systemic injustices in schools, particularly issues of discrimination a nd harassment in  Aleph School District (ASD; pseudonym),  a predominantly White school district in the U.S. We utilized the Learning Lab  (LL) methodology—a formative intervention (Bal et al., 2016; 2018) grounded in Cultural Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) (Engeström, 1987; Vygotsky, 1978) —to expand inclusive education to incorporate studen ts in  policy collaborative design (co-design). CHAT recognizes historical practices of th e exclusion of students and families in educational  decision making. It also embodied principles of solidarity-driven co-design, in which diverse stakeholders collectively identified issues and designed solutions (Ishimaru et al., 2023). The purpose of this paper is to identify the practices and principles that fostered meaningful inclusion of students and parents from historically marginalized backgrounds during the LL intervention. Examining inclusive practices is essential because educa tional decision-making has traditionally been dominated by educator s, especially administrators, or privileged parents and community members. Including voices that have his torically been excluded requires the creation of new spaces, norms, and structures that facilitate inclusion (Bertrand et al., 2023; Mitra, 2008). Such spaces often exist within broader, non-inclusive systems imbued with power imbalances and knowledge hierarchies. In these contexts, creat ing collaborative spaces functions as a “Thirdspace ” (Soja, 1996)—a space of resistance and innovation (Mawene et al., 2024) where students, parents, community members, educators, administrators, and other stakeholders to collaboratively develop a district-level policy from the ground up. The guiding research questions for this paper are:

1. How did the Learning Lab foster inclusive space for  its participants while designing a

protocol for addressing discrimination and harassment for the district?

2. What were the initial outcomes of the Learning Lab?

Theoretical backgrounds This paper is grounded in the theoretical framework s of inclusive education (Slee, 2010; Waitoller & K ozleski,

2013) and Cultural Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) (Engeström, 1987; Vygotsky, 1978). While the conc ept

of inclusive education in the U.S is of ten limited to special education and disability (Sl ee, 2010), the core principles of inclusive education comprise a transformative agenda that extends beyond assimilation or curriculum design for students with disabilities (Artiles, 2020). We understand inclusive education as both a pro cess and the end goal to address broader systemic injustices across educational settings (Artiles, 2020; Slee, 2010).  This paper advances inclusive education by highlighting inclus ive processes where intentional actions are taken t o address barriers that hinder students and parents from mean ingfully involve in decision making and transform d ecisionmaking cultures at the local school district level.  The values of inclusion need to be represented and  articulated through the school's decision-making policies. Second, this paper addresses the r estorative agenda of inclusive education: redistribution, representation, and reco gnition (Fraser, 2007; Kozleski, 2020; Slee, 2010; Waitoller & Kozleski, 2013). This restorative work of inclusion  involves directly representing those whose voices have been marginalized in a democratic process that is intent ionally designed and treated seriously. The restora tive agenda of inclusive education aims to “build the confidenc e of less powerful agents at the table.” (Slee, 201 0, p.169). Lastly, we advocate for a shift in educational reform towards incorporating inclusive education as a fundamental goal, integrating curriculum, pedagogy, assessment, and organizational practices within general education settings (Slee, 2010). Such integration seeks to foster sust ained systemic transformations that dismantle historical discriminatory practices and promote inclusive cultures within schools and broader educational frameworks (Slee, 2010; Artiles, 2020).

The CHAT framework complements inclusive education in two main ways: (1) to examine the social, cultural, and historical processes within schools t hat perpetuate exclusion of students and families in educational decision making. For example, educational de cision-making is typically seen as the responsibility of administrators (Bertrand et al., 2023), with studen ts and families often regarded as “guests” in schoo ls. CHAT enabled us to uncover these exclusionary practices.  (2) to establish a Thirdspace —a space of resistance (Soja, 1996; Mawene et al., 2024) —thatchallenges normative exclusionary practices in decision-making. The transformative power of inclusive education lies in  its ability to confront the deep-rooted socio-historical and political factors tha t perpetuate exclusion, discrimination, and oppress ion within educational systems (Artiles, 2020). Addressing these issues, inclusive education  becomes a framework not just for access, but also for meaningful engagement and equitable outcomes (Artiles, 2020; Slee, 2010).

## Methods: Data collection and analysis

Contexts: Aleph School District Aleph School District (ASD) in New England in the U nited States served approximately 5000 students fro m six different towns. In recent years, the district has seen a rise in students from diverse racial/ethnic backgrounds, economic status, and gender preferences. However, t he district struggled to serve historically margina lized students, particularly pertaining to discrimination  and harassment. For example, a Black student was r epeatedly subjected to racial slurs, a Jewish student was har assed with raciali zed images, and a student who uses a wheelchair was often told that their life was “wort hless.” These issues become more complex when consi dering the U.S. context: the nationwide call for racial equity in policy and the counter-rise of White supremacist groups. Despite expressing a commitment to social justice, district leaders lacked a systematic method to report, much less address, these injustices. Consequently, many incidents of discrimination and harassment wen t unreported or were miscategorized as minor disciplinary issues. This perpetuated cycles of distrust, harm, mental health challenges, and a lack of belonging among hi storically marginalized students. To address this g ap, the school district collaborated with the first author of this paper to design a protocol for systematically reporting and resolving incidents of discrimination and harassmen t. During the academic year 2022-23, the researcher and ASD’s Director of Diversity and Equity (DDE) (Pseud onym) launched a Learning Lab (LL) intervention. Th e first author and DDE joined forces to facilitate the cr eation of the protocol for addressing discriminatio n and harassment. In the 2022-2023 academic year, Learning Lab members were able to create the protocol, and it is now being implemented in all schools in ASD.

## Learning Lab methodology

The Learning Lab (LL) methodology (Bal et al., 2016; 2018) used in this study is a formative intervent ion (Engeström, 2011; Sannino et al., 2018) designed to foster agency by identifying contradictions within collective activity systems and using them as catalysts for tr ansformative change (Engeström, 2011). Rooted in de mocratic participation (Bal et al., 2012; Freire, 2008), LL brought together students, parents, community membe rs, and school professionals from diverse backgrounds to co llaboratively develop a system—in this case, a protocol for addressing discrimination and harassment.

The Learning Lab in ASD met biweekly for 75–90 minutes in a high school meeting room, co-facilitated by the first author and the district’s DDE. Over th e course of 18 sessions, LL followed seven phases (see Figure 1), excluding the implementation phase. In the first phase of the LL, we formed a team that included key decisionmakers as well as students and parents of color—those most impacted by the anti-discrimination policy. LL started with six members, with the DDE suggesting the distr ict’s existing Equi ty Committee as a starting point. This committee consisted of educators and administrators  committed to justice-oriented policy work. Leveraging existing resources was a priority. The first two meetings focused on expanding the team, with facilitators asking, “Whose voice should be at the table?” Membership co ntinued to grow until session #8, reaching a total of 16 members, though two later opted out of the research (see Table 1). In formative interventions, participant selection is guided by the team’s objectives rather than proportional representation, ensuring a balance between inclusivity and manageability (Mawene et al., 2024).

During the questioning and analyzing phases, member s identified key problems —such as the underreporting of incidents—and examined existing school practices to uncover systemic gaps that allowed biasbased incidents to go undocumented. They engaged with both primary and secondary stimuli, using empirical data on bullying and harassment alongside their lived ex periences as conceptual and material artifacts (Bal, 2018). These discussions surfaced tensions and conflicting  perspectives among members, but rather than avoidi ng discomfort, the team collectively explored these tensions as part of the learning process. During the modeling phase, LL members mapped out an ideal protocol and its place within the school’s system through creative visualization tools such as  diagramming. In the examining phase, they assessed  its feasibility, asking critical questions such as: “What is missing from this protocol?” and evaluatin g its cultural responsiveness. Finally, in the planning phase, mem bers identified the institutional support necessary  for successful implementation.

## Data collection

Data for this paper comes from two resources with e ach source corresponding to each research question:  1) The meeting transcript of 18 LL meetings; 2) Follow up individual interviews with L ab members. Due to transitions of members’ roles in the school district, we were a ble to conduct interviews with 10 members. Each mee ting lasted for an average of 60 minutes.

## Data analysis

We analyzed our data together via Dedoose Software.  One of us led the coding of the LL meetings, while the other led the coding of the interviews. We met biweekly to discuss our coding process, adding to the interpretation and understanding of data. Data analysis consisted of three steps. First, we conducted open coding (Saldaña, 2021) guided by the research questions and the theoretical framework. W e created 103 initial codes/subcodes based on our initial understanding of data and theory. Second, the initial codes were then consolidated (Saldaña, 2021) into potential themes such as: Modeling Inclusion, Practicing Inclusion, Axiological Commitment, Describing Hopes, Describing Fears, Describing Barriers to Inclusion, and Identifying Outcomes. For the scope of this paper, we organized relevant themes to answer both the research questions.

## Findings

The findings were organized based on the research questions below. RQ1: How did the Learning Lab foster inclusive space in designing a protocol for addressing discrimination and harassment?

We identified several practices and principles that fostered a culture of inclusion during the design process. In this paper, we will focus on two practices: 1) Leveragin g axiological commitment to center students as legi timate policy actors and 2) Building a community of learners.

Leveraging axiological commitment to center student as legitimate policy actors This theme particularly focuses on the inclusion of  students’ voices in the Learning Lab (LL). One of the critical factors in fostering inclusion is the axiological commitment from the facilitators, in this case,  the researcher and the ASD’s DDE. Prior to  embarking the LL intervention, the researcher and t he DDE have extensively met and discussed the LL methodology. We both agreed that t he LL would not simply be a technical tool to get b usiness done. Rather, the LL should address the existing hi storical practices that hinder the participation of  those from historically marginalized backgrounds, especially s tudents, parents, and community members. Our collec tive commitment to social justice was translated into the way we structured prompts or questions. For example, in the “forming the group” phase, when the initial members  brainstormed the compositions of the team, the fac ilitators asked questions such as: “Who is the protocol for?”  and “Who do you think should be included in the de sign of the protocol? and “Have you thought about the roles  and demography of the group?” During the first cou ple meetings, LL members struggled with the idea of sha ring space with students. However, this tension bec ame a catalyst for learning. For example, Lizzy, a parent, shared her apprehension during the first meeting: What is the vision for student participation? Will they be in every meeting? Will they be receiving all the information and, from a confident iality standpoint, carrying that with them? What are they carrying in terms of what they hear about their peers and family? (LL #1) Lizzy’s concerns, focused on important issues like confidentiality and student protection, reveal bure aucratic barriers that may limit the perception or participa tion of students in educational decision-making. In response to Lizzy’s struggle to understand the importance of student participation, Mr. Juan Pablo, the DDE, emphasized the importance of including student voices. His commitm ent as a district leader made centering student per spectives a non-negotiable priority: We hope that if they [students] come here, they're part of creating the space. So they would stick to those [meeting] norms. Again, we can't control w hat anyone knows, but I think having the students here and treating them like they are part of the committ ee. (LL #1) Here, Mr. Juan Pablo positioned students with the same rights, responsibilities, and agency in decision making as adults, legitimizing their roles as policy actors. Similar to Lizzy, Darius, another community member at the same meeting, was apprehensive about whether or not students should be a regular part of the LL team. He asked “Can students join the meeting occasionally when necessary?” As a facilitator with an axiological commitment to social justice and inclusion, the first author responded w ith emphasis that since the protocol is for student s, we need to have voices and involvement from the beginning. The  initial LL members agreed to recruit students to j oin the LL team and brainstormed preliminary “inclusion and  exclusion criteria” of potential students to recru it. For example, Ms. Virginia, a high school teacher stated her criteria as: “Not students senate. They have always had a chance to voice out their voice. We need to find students whose voices haven't been heard.”  The empha sis on students' voices, especially those who have been ma rginalized, signified LL members' learning and comm itment to creating meaningful inclusive space. By LL meeting #3, three students started to attend Lab meetings regularly and their presence changed the course of discussion around the protocol, and therefore the district. Challenging exclusion and advocating for inclusion was one of the moral purposes (Fullan, 2018) both facilitators held during the LL. Inclusion of student voices was both the means and the goal to challenge systemic exclusion of student voices in educational decision making (Ko et al., 2022), and we continued to model inclusion throughout the LL process. For example, we used the  last 10 minutes of each meeting to collectively br ainstorm the agenda and goal for the next meeting and asked members to collectively create meeting norms. We also asked questions that positioned LL members' lived experie nces as knowledge that shaped the collective unders tanding about the problems of discrimination and harassment  in their district and how to address them via desi gning the protocol.

Building on each other’s lived experience: Everyone was a learner LL members extended the modeling of inclusive practices by gradually engaging in collaborative learnin g structures (Fullan et al., 2018; Mitra, 2008). They invited one another to share their lived experiences as a source of data to understand the complexities of discrimin ation and harassment. For example, during the “ques tioning” phase, members mapped out their current practices r egarding discrimination and harassment. They noted a tendency to protect Whiteness by refraining from re porting or addressing discriminatory incidents. Som e described instances where bystanders, teachers, and other members of the district avoided reporting the incidents or labeling incidents as harassment or discriminato ry, fearing it might harm a student's reputation. A dditionally, some members observed a tendency to dismiss certain behaviors as “kids being kids,” particularly when involving White students. RQ 2: What were the initial outcomes of the Learning Lab? There are two initial outcomes of upholding inclusi ve processes throughout the LL. The tangible outcom e was a student-centered Protocol for Addressing Harassment and Dis crimination. The intangible outcome was a germ cell of increased meaningful inclusion at the district-level, as well as self-reported shifts in individual perspective that students can be capable policy actors.

Tangible outcome: Student-centered protocol Not only did this diverse group of stakeholders suc ceed in designing a protocol, the protocol they cre ated was intentionally student-centered. In the semi-structured follow-up interviews, LL members indicated how the LL process, particularly the inclusion of students in the team, drove them to put students’ well-being and lived experience at the center. Centering the students resulted in the following key components of the protocol: repairing and educating rather than punishing, considering the long-term experiences and growth of all students, and making the process clear and transparent for those involved.

Centering the students resulted in the core goal of  the protocol: to educate students instead of punis hing them, which meant addressing the harm experienced by the victim while also providing learning opportunities for the perpetrators. For example, Ms. Virginia, one of the teachers stated: My favorite part is the fact that there's attention to students on both ends of an incident, and how not only do we have to care for the student who fel t harmed, but also the student who, whether it was intentional or not intentional, caused the h arm, and looking out for both sides and those students and their families. And I think for me, maybe that's something I hadn't thought quite as much about. Ms. Virginia illustrates the strength of the protocol designed with a student-centered approach by shifting from a purely punitive response to one focused on education and support. Similarly, another parent, Ms. Lizzy, who was apprehensive about the inclusion of students at the  initial LL meeting, echoed the strength of the pro tocol as educating and supporting students “with the long-term in mind:” I'm proud that as a committee the protocol really r eflects the centering of the individual or individuals harmed. This shift away from punishment, a visible response  that centers administrators, to the goal of studen t learning and growth, centers the experience of all students–present and future–within the protocol. ## Discussion

This paper emphasizes how inclusive education can t ransform school practices by challenging traditiona l power hierarchies within organizational and educational d ecision-making processes. We acknowledge that inclusive education does not emerge naturally. Instead, it re quires intentional creation, driven by deliberate a nd collaborative efforts among all stakeholders, inclu ding researchers, facilitators, and participants. T his effort involves actively addressing and dismantling power dynamics, hierarchies, and privileges through inten tionally including marginalized voices in policy design. Though the impacts of the protocol are currently being measured, we found that simply the work of designing the prot ocol in an inclusive manner resulted in germ cell t ype shifts in school culture. These findings illustrate that i nclusive spaces are not only instrumental in produc ing tangible outcomes, such as policies that meet the diverse ne eds of students, but they also spark broader systemic change. These spaces encourage schools and districts to resist structures that limit student participation and reimagine new spaces where students are empowered to be policy actors and decision-makers. This transformative approach lays the groundwork for a shift toward equity and inclusivity in educational practices and governance. With this paper, we contribute to the learning scie nces by highlighting ho w inclusive spaces to address injustices can be co-designed and how they can foster meaningful cultural changes. This paper also contributes to the fields  of educational leadership, policymaking, and inclus ive education by advancing both theoretical understanding and practical applications. We ground  our theoretical framework in inclusive education l iterature and CHAT and offer actionable strategies for rese archers, administrators, and practitioners to creat e inclusive spaces. We address the gap between theory and practice to offer a framework for all policy actors to t ransform their lived experiences together.  One limitation of this paper is we did not include the voice of those who opted out from the research. We also were not able to interview all LL members.
