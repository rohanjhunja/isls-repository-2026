# Designing for Disruption: Two Teachers’ Culturally Responsive Design Journey in Co-Creating a Land-Based Curricular Unit

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Expanding understanding of teachers as designers of learning environments is critical for youths' educational experience. As part of a design-based implementation research study, we trace two teachers’ Culturally Responsive design journey designing and (re)designing a land-based curricular unit over five years. Findings illustrate an ontological shift as teachers design for disruption and move toward culturally responsive design. Insights expand current understandings of culturally responsive design and teachers’ role in supporting students’ engagement with diverse cultures.

## Introduction and background

The design of the learning environment is not neutral because designers operate from deep cultural values that are embedded in the designs themselves (e.g., Bang et al., 2012). As such, learning environment s can prescribe and privilege certain ways to know, do, and be (Nasir e t al., 2006). In the field of learning sciences, a growing body of work has enriched the notion of design for disruption (Stamatis et al., 2023), as researchers and educators work to innovatively expand and liberate learning environments. As a multi-year research-practice partnership (RPP; Coburn & Penuel, 2016), we join this conversation b y sharing our work on disrupting learning environments as acultural spaces. Our partnership is a multicultural team of educators, researchers, and  designers with the shared goal of supporting sixth graders’ cultural developm ent and awareness. In this paper, we share stories about how teachers work to design culturally centered learning environments by “making culture visible” (San Pedro, 2018) through their design and peda gogical approach. Specifically, we ask,  how do teachers engage with design for disruption in creating culturally centered learning environments ? We share two teachers’ design journeys as a case study to illustrate how teachers engage in culturally responsive design (Lee, 2003) through their pedagogical decisions over time.

Our work is built on evolving theoretical and pract ical experiences of centering culture with a series  of evolving pedagogical approaches (Ladson-Billings, 1995; Gay, 2010; Paris, 2012; McCarty & L ee, 2014) that inform the design of learning environments, as outl ined by Lee (2003) in her rationale of Culturally R esponsive Design. Lee illustrates four rationales for Cultura lly Responsive Design: (1) building on prior knowle dge, (2) cultural models as socially  constructed ways of knowing, (3) supporting engagem ent and motivation, and (4) supporting social, civic, and political empowerment (Lee, 20 03). These  rationales not only ground our work theoretically but also provide a practical frame th rough which we trace the teacher partners' design j ourney as presented in this study.

## Methodology

To align with the goals and values of our RPP, we employed a design-based implementation research methodology (DBIR; Fishman et al., 2013), which includes an ite rative process of design, implementation, and evalu ation of the curriculum design.  As a team, we develop growth in recognizing ourselv es as cultural beings and gaining awareness of others’ culture s through engaging in culturally disruptive research (Litts et al., 2020). This multiyear work takes place at The Experiential Learning School, a K-6 elementary public charter and Title I school in the western United States, where Authors 4 and 5 co-teach the sixth grade

## Data collection and analysis

Data collected for this paper spans four implementations of the Bears Ears unit that took place across the 2019 to 2024 school years. Data collected include partnership meeting audio and video recordings, written fieldnotes and memos, pedagogical design work, and ongoing individual and collective reflection interviews and discussions. In our analysis, we trace the design journeys of Authors 4 and 5 across iterations of the Bears Ears unit to construct a longitudinal case study (Stake 2008) of the DBIR process of this unit.

## Findings

We trace Author 4 and Author 5’s, as teachers and l ead designers, journey of culturally responsive des ign and cultural competence through co-creating a land-based curricular unit centered on  a school trip to the Bears Ears in southeastern Utah, USA. This unit focuses on the Bear’s Ears land controversy to engage students with multiple perspectives of the land, particularly cultural sig nificance to Indigenous communities. Guided by Culturally Responsive, we align design choices, reflections, and personal growth with each rationale Lee (2003) presents as an illustration of these teachers’ culturally responsive design journey. Cultural awareness and models of ways of knowing The culturally responsive design journey was teache r-initiated in recognition of the limited perspective s of students' prior knowledge; thus, they wanted to engage sixth graders with In digenous perspectives of the land in a formal learning environment. When coming to this design work, Author 4 and Author 5 articulated thei r awareness of the boundary of their knowledge system, particularly as their teaching training does not focus on “the content knowledge” (Author 5, Written Contribu tion, 1/2020) that they need to engage students wit h land and culture. This awareness has been developing thr oughout the partnership, which prompts the teachers  to a cultural checking in on how things were being taught. For example, As Author 5 shared, they started recognizing the stories as cultural models and, thus, are socially constructed ways of knowing.  As their awareness increased, they recognized the importance of sharing different cultural perspectives as they teach the Bears Ears unit. Supporting engagement and motivation while meeting curricula and standards As the teacher partners’ cultural awareness grew, they engaged and continue to engage in ongoing learning about different cultures to support the culturally responsive design. The culturally responsive design for the Bears Ears unit is also driven by an ob jective to engage students and motivate them to understand multiple perspectives of land use, including Miners, Ranchers, and Native Americans, while supporting their learning to meet the curricula objectives and standards. To support students’ deep  engagement of perspectives, Author 4 and 5 have be en iterating on the form of learning by exploring a ra nge of learning arrangements including: small-group debates, class-wide debates, public-facing political-style rallies promoting particular perspectives, and even engaging AI to invite students to practice perspective taking. Over time, Author 4 and 5 became able to adapt the forms of the unit to meet the needs of their classroom. Author 5 shared the importance of “being creative in designing lessons to meet standards” and not “using the standards only as a top-down approach” (Author 5, Collective Reflection, 07/2024).

Pushing the boundary of the classroom for social and civic empowerment The initial motivation of the Bears Ears unit was t o connect the students’ experiences of the San Juan trip with their classroom learning, disrupt ing the boundary of classroom spaces. Over time, Author s 4 and 5 identify different roles as they co-teach to expand classroom experience: Author 4 focused on the place-based experience of the Bears Ears and Author 5 focused on preparing  students to explore d ifferent perspectives. As the teachers' cultural awareness and knowledge grew, they started  to try out different cultural competence skills and teaching these to the students. Notably, after the first implementation, Author 4 and Author 5 modified the unit to the form of an outdoor rally, which supported social and civic empowerment  by sharing different perspectives with the broader school community and the public. Their disruptive move further pushed the boundaries of learning out of the classroom.

## Discussion

We traced teachers’ culturally responsive design journey and evolutions over five years. Their journey began with disrupting the embedded dominant knowledge systems and making visible the teachers’ role as designers of learning environments. Our work expands current und erstandings of both culturally responsive and cultu rally disruptive pedagogical design frameworks by illustr ating the generative nature of the disruptive moves  both frameworks invite. Moreover, our study further contributes to reimagin ing the  dynamic design process as negotiation with culture, identity, and lived exper iences from teachers’ perspectives. In particular, across the iterations, Author 4 and Author 5 became more cohes ive and confident in their engagement with cultural ly responsive design through the Bears Ears unit, which illustrates a possible ontological future where developing cultural competence becomes a way of being-a designer, a teacher, a student, a human.
