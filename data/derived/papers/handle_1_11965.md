# "Memorize the Source!" — Does Focusing on Learning Partners Improve Source Memory Without Impairing Content Learning?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Remembering which learning partner shared certain information (source memory) helps learners to identify whom to approach for more information (academic help-seeking). Research suggests that while explicit tasks to remember sources can improve source memory, they may also impair learning the content. Our experimental pseudo-collaborative study (112 participants) applied a 2×2 between-subjects design (with vs. without source-related and content-related tasks), testing beneficial or detrimental effects of tasks on source memory and learning. Participants received information from three learning partners with different knowledge levels (high vs. medium vs. low). Source memory and learning correlated positively but were not influenced overall by tasks. However, content-related tasks improved source memory for high-knowledge partners. Supplementary analyses of two real collaborating groups revealed that source-related tasks promote self-regulative strategies (taking notes who said what). Our complementary results indicate that learners might need tasks with more guidance targeting group-level processes to support source memory in collaborative learning.

## Introduction

Collaborative learning offers several benefi ts, enabling learners to engage in discussions about the  learning content while using each other as valuable sources of information and academic help. Learning collaboratively is especially beneficial if learners are aware of their learning partners’ knowledge (e.g., how much they know about the learning content). Such group awareness can be particularly enhanced in computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) using group awareness tools, which can collect, transform, and present knowledge-related information of learners (Bodemer et al., 2018). Acquiring adequate group awareness facilitates learning (Sangin et al., 2011) and effective social strategies: learners can regulate their collaborative learning processes by applying strategies such as  targeting questions regarding their learning partners’ high expertise or offering much needed assistance regarding learning partners’ low knowledge areas (Dehler Zufferey et al., 2010). While these social strategies are beneficial during  collaboration, some strategies  may occur after the collaboration session has ended. As in everyday life, when we rely on the expertise of colleagues or friends for different tasks, the ability to identify the right person for additional help is also crucial for stud ents: (academic) help-seeking often involves asking previous learning partners for more informat ion. This can become relevant after formal and informal learning sessions, for example, when instructors distribute complementary information among learners (e.g., Oshima et al., 2017) or when students form self-organized repeated exam preparation groups in which each of the learners focuses on different learning contents. During such sessions, learners typically share unique information. Some time after collaborating, learners may partially remember certain information but need to seek additional information or clarification from the learning partner who  originally shared the information. For such self-regulated academic help-seeking to be effective, learners must remember not only th e content but also the source of information  retrospectively. Memory for the origin of information, known as source memory (SM, Johnson et al., 1993), is therefore essential to  identify the appropriate learning partner for furth er help. Indeed, SM can be crucial for academic help-seeking because deciding whom to ask is an integral part of (successful) help-seeking (Makara & Karabenick, 2013). Academic help-seeking in turn  can positively affect academic achievement (for a review, see  Martín-Arbós et al., 2021). Given the importance of SM for effective help-seeking, this leads to the question: how can SM be enhanced in collaborative learning settings? One straightforward approach to foster SM could be to explicitly direct learners’ attention to the sources of information with a task by instructing them to memorize and remember the learning partner who shared certain information. Such a task can be beneficial: for example, in a series of experiments by Cook and colleagues (2006), participants had to learn word pairs which were presented by one of two sources. While some participants were told to pay attention to the sources and were made aware of the upcoming SM test (intentional learning), others’ attention was not guided to the sources (incidental learning). Unsurprisingly, in all experiments, learners’ SM was better under intentional learning conditions  (see also Popov & Dames, 2023, experiments 10 and 11). However, the simplest solution can also have drawbacks: for example, in some experiments by Cook and colleagues (2006), instructions to intentional ly learn the sources led to poorer memory for the word pairs themselves. A similar tradeoff was also observed in other contexts, such as advertising psychology: in some settings, better memory for sources of product statements was accompanied by  poorer memory for the information itself  (Bell et al., 2022, experiments 1 and 2). These findings align with the theory of an  item-source tradeoff, which proposes that in some conditions, focusing on one aspect—either the source or the content—candistract from the other (Jurica & Shimamura, 1999). This raises a critical question: (how) does this tradeoff play out in collaborative learning settings, where both—SM and learning the content—are essential for the long-term success of learners? It needs to be experimentally tested whether the task to memorize the sources might unintentionally impair learning. One potential explanation for the  tradeoff between learning the content and its source lies in cognitive load, which refers to individuals’ mental load and effort while performing a (learning) task (Paas et al., 2003). A fundamental assumption is based on the  limited capacities of human working memory: when mental load is too high (e.g., due to poorly designed instructional material or certain learning tasks), cognitive resources cannot be allocated for learning-relevant processes (mental effort), thereby impairing learning (Sweller et al., 2019). This assumption can also explain the item-source tradeoff effect: cognitive resources required for the learning task and those needed for tracking  the source may be limited and compet ing (Jurica & Shimamura, 1999). Such competition can arise during tasks that involve intentionally focusing on sources (Cook et al., 2006) or even when humans subconsciously focus more on sources without  an explicit task (Bell et al., 2022). However, such a tradeoff is not always found: for example, in the experiment of Symeonidou and Kuhlmann (2021), lower source similarity improved memory for sources and information. L earners may be able to process both types of information effectively if mental capacities are sufficient, allowing them to benefit from both: source-and contentrelated tasks. When learners can attend to the source of information while also grasping the relevant content, they may become more adept at recalling not only what th ey learned but also who provided that information, thereby facilitating more effective help-seeking behavior later on. Still, in a (collaborative) learning context, the main task usually focuses on the content and not on tracking the source, which raises the question: does combining sourceand content-related tasks impede learning of the source and the content by overstraining mental capacities? Examining the effects of instructions on source memory and learning An experiment al study  provides answers to the question s raised. It  evaluates the interplay of  tasks guiding learners’ attention to the source of information (w ith vs. without a source-related task) and/or tasks directing learners’ attention to learn the content (with vs. without a content-related task). We assume learners’ SM to be better with source-related tasks than without such tasks (H1a, see also Cook et al., 2006). Furthermore, we expect an interaction between both task-types in so far  that with source-related tasks, SM should be  better without content-related tasks than with such tasks (H1b) because the combination of both tasks might strain  mental capacities. Correspondingly, we also expect learning outcomes to be higher with tasks guiding learners’ attention to the content than without such tasks (H2a). Again, we expect an interaction between content-related tasks and source-related tasks in so far that  content-related tasks should improve learning more in the absence of sourcerelated tasks (H2b) because of limited capacities of working memory. Building on this, we will f urther exploratively consider  the influences of learning partners’ knowledge levels. C ollaborative learning  can occur in groups where  learning partners differ in their knowledge level s regarding the learning topic at hand (e.g., high vs. medium vs low). Indeed, learning in such a heterogenous group can be beneficial for knowledge acquisition (e.g., Zhang et al., 2015). While information from learning partners with high or low credibility or expertise is learned equally well (Andrews & Rapp, 2014), different effects on SM can be found: for example, in collaborative learning, learners’ SM is best for high-knowledge partners (Ülker & Bodemer, 2024). Thus, we want to test whether (combinations of) tasks affect learning from and SM for partners with differing knowledge levels differently. Lastly, studies finding a tradeoff between memory for the information and SM have not always directly measured mental load: therefore, we further want to consider effects of  both tasks on such load, as well as relationships between mental load, SM, and learning. In our attempt to gain a deeper understanding of the effects of tasks, we additionally conducted a real collaborative study in which two groups collaborate d (with and without a source-related task). With this mixedmethods approach, effects of such instructions can be examined not only on individual s’ SM and learning, but also on potential beneficial or distracting (collaborative) learning processes. This complements the investigations of our  controlled experimental study  and bridg es the gap between controlled research settings and re al-world collaboration. Testing effects of (combinations of) source-related and content-related tasks on SM, learning, and collaborative processes can inform the development of instructional designs that foster learning and long-term academic help-seeking behavior in different CSCL environments. Such tasks could (or should not) be applied by educators in different educational contexts or implemented in group awaren ess tools supporting learners ’ knowledge exchange, ultimately enhancing learners’ ability to utilize their peers’ knowledge.

## Design and participants

To test our main hypotheses, w e applied a 2 × 2 between-subjects design with the factors source-related task (with vs. without) and content-related task (with vs. without).  We randomly assigned participants to the four experimental conditions. Participants received information from the discussion of three (alleged) learning partners differing in their knowledge level  (high vs. medium vs. low)  regarding the learning topic  and were instructed to imagine learning collaboratively with them. In  later phases, participants had to answer learning a nd SM tests. Figure 1 depicts key phases of our experiment. Our final sample consisted of N = 112 participants (77 female, 35 male), which allowed to detect medium-sized effects (f =.27) for our main hypotheses, given α =.05 and a target power of 1 – β =.80. The mean age was 22.64 years (SD = 3.55) and participants were mostly students (103). Figure 1 Illustration of the Procedure with Short Descriptions of the Key Phases.

## Material and procedure

Material and procedure w ere similar to the experiment of Ülker and Bodemer  (2024). The learning material covered information on the extinction of dinosaurs. Prior studies have shown that learners typically possess low prior knowledge regarding the topic. Texts were generated from various online sources and adapted for the study.

(1) Partner descriptions: After filling out demographic information, participants were informed that

information from the learning texts were (allegedly) summarized by three students  which had the task to gather and exchange information regarding the extinction o f dinosaurs. The learning partners would further be represented by three gender-neutral names (Alex, Luca, Jona) and randomly assigned avatars (see Figure 2). Also, the three learning partners allegedly performed a validated knowledge test regarding the learning topic and the results (high vs. medium vs. low) would be displaye d. For each participant, names and avatars  were randomly assigned to the learning partners with high, medium, and low knowledge levels to control for potential biases.

(2) Task-instructions: The main manipulation occurred in this phase. All participants received the task

to provide the learning partners with feedback  regarding their discussion. We needed this task as a baseline for the condition without a source-and content-related task. In the conditions with a source-related task, we informed participants about the SM test after the learning phase and instructed them to memorize which learning partner presented each piece of information. In the conditions with a content-related task, we informed about the learning test and instructed to learn the content of the tex ts. In the condition with both source-and content-related tasks, participants were informed about both tests and instructed to learn the content and remember the source.

(3) Learning and discussion phase: We presented 27 texts (three texts regarding nine topics, e.g., “impact

location”) covering unique information regarding the extinction of dinosaurs. The texts had similar length (MWords = 50.40, SD = 1.42) and were presented on single pages in a fixed order. First, partner information (Name, Avatar, knowledge level) appeared on top  of the screen and afterwards, the text appeared  (Figure 2, left). Mental load, which is disruptive for learning, was measured with two subscales of the second version of the naïve rating questionnaire (Klepsch et al., 2017): extraneous cognitive load (α =.77), which is the mental load caused by the instructional design of the material (e.g., “During this task, it was difficult to recognize and link the crucial information ”) and intrinsic cognitive load  (α =.84), which measures the mental load evoked by task complexity (e.g., “For this task, many things needed to be kept in mind simultaneously”). (4) Learning test: After some questionnaires, we applied the learning test, which consisted of 27

(5) Source memory test: In this phase, we presented participants with (paraphrased) pieces of information

Participants indicated for each information whether  it was new or by which of the three learning partn ers it was presented. This test comprised 81 trials: 54 pieces of information were old (2 paraphrased information from each of the 27 texts) and 27 information were new (3 distractors for each of the 9 topics). In each trial (Figure 2, right), at the top of the screen, the piece of information was presented first.  The answer options were represented by avatars, names, and knowledge levels (the last option was “no one”, indicating new items). We presented the information serially in a random order. After 81 trials, we debriefed the participants. We complemented our experimental study with a real collaborative study, testing the effects of a sourcerelated task on (collaborative) learning  processes. In this follow-up study, we analyzed and interviewed t wo groups who worked with the same material  as in the experimental study, offering deeper insig hts into authentic individual and group-level processes. Procedure and results will be described in the results section. Statistical analyses Learning outcomes were operationalized as the number of correctly solved questions in the learning test (0 – 27). For explorative analyses, we separately measured learning outcome s for information presented by the three learning partners with differing knowledge levels (high vs. medium vs. low). For example, Learning Low (0 – 9) represents how well information was learned that was presented by the partner with a low knowledge level. We computed SM measures by dividing the amount of correct source judgements (e.g., “high” responses to information which was originally presented by a partner with high knowledge level) by the sum of “h igh”, “medium”, and “low” responses. Thus, SMHigh, SMMedium, and SMLow reflect participants’ SM for the three learning partners with differing knowledge levels. These so called Conditional Source Identification M easures allow to estimate measures without confounding SM and old-new-recognition, however, thes e measures can confound memory and guessing processes (Bröder & Meiser, 2007). For example, a guessing strategy such as “If I ca nnot remember the source, I will rather guess that the p artner with medium knowledge presented the informat ion”— which can be conscious or subconscious—candistort SM scores for the medium knowledge partner and one would wrongly assume that memory for medium knowledge partners is exceptionally good. Multinomial processing tree (MPT) models offer a solution, as they  disentangle memory and guessing processes by calculating separa te parameters for each (for an overview, see Erdfelder et al., 2009, for a recent application in CSCL, see Ülker & Bodemer, 2024). Model-based approaches often require larger sample sizes for stable parameter estimation s (sometimes especially for SM parameters). Our SM scores  are readily compatible  with traditional statistical methods (e.g., correlations). We therefore combined the strengths of MPT models a nd SM scores and used the MPT model of source monitoring (Bayen et al., 1996) to identify potential guessing strategies. The parameter gMedium (in all conditions >.40) reflects the probability of guessing the medium knowledge partner as the source of information in the absence  of SM. Since all three partners shared the same amount o f information, we constrained gMedium to the constant.33, which results in significant m isfit in all experimental conditions, ΔG² > 18.51, p <.001. The parameter gHigh (in all conditions  between.48 and.53) reflects the probability to guess “high” when it was not guessed to be medium before and was restricted to the constant.50 in all conditions, as high and low knowledge partners shared the same amount of information. There was no significant deviation from.50, ΔG² < 2.17, p >.140. Consequently, given the significant bias in over-guessing the medium-knowledge partner, we will omit SMMedium in our analyses because this measure confounds memory and guessing, while SMHigh and SMLow do not seem to have underlying and distorting guessing tendencies.

Source-related tasks in practice: Insights from real collaborative groups We conducted a supplementary small-scale study (N = 6, MAge = 20.17, 3 female, 3 male)  with two learning groups. The same 27 texts as in the main study were printed out and divided among learners. For each topic (9), each learner received a text. Participants were told that the purpose was to find out how students learn with texts from other students. They were again represented by (printed out) avatars with stars depicting the knowledge level of the alleged students who created the text.  Participants should first learn their information individually (15 minutes) and then collaborate (30 minutes). While collaborating, participants had to find similarities among their 9 topics and exchange their information. They were informed that a following knowledge test would contain questions regarding their own and their learning partners’ te xts. While one group was additionally instructed to memorize which partner shared certain information, the other group was not. After collaborating, participants conducted the learning test and then the SM test. Finally, participants were interviewed. A source-related task did not seem to affect group processes (e.g., learners with a source-related task did not discuss the task to remember the sources). However, the task affected some self-regulative strategies. In both conditions, learners were allowed to take notes (but not to use them during the tests). Two learners (A and B) in the group with  a source-related task recorded in their notes who has shared certain info rmation. SM of these learners (SMA =.97, SMB =.75) was higher than SM of C who did not take notes (SMC =.72). In the interview, learners were asked to indicate whether they used c ertain strategies to memorize the sources. Learners A and B (with a source-related task) mentioned note-taking as their primary strategy (“I always wrote down who said what on my sheet beforehand and noted down the most important points”, B), while C noted that he did not focus on the source of information but rath er on the content. All learners without a source-related task (D, E, F) did not mention any strategies. When being asked ab out the SM test, learners A and B indicated that they found the test easy, while C noted that he often guessed. All learners without a source-related task noted that they struggled while remembering the sources of information, e.g.: “Sometimes a certain technical term was mentioned by both of the m and I could not remember who said the most about it ” (F), or “For me it was really hard to notice whether it was said at all […]. I think I rather ruled out that certain information was not contained in my texts in the beginning ” (E). Interestingly, while learners without  a source-related task reported that they struggled more, descriptively and on average, they had better SM (SMD =.75, SME = 1.00, SMF =.86). Learners were finally asked what could support them  in remembering the sources better. Learners with a source-related task did not have any ideas. Those without the task noted different ideas, for example, that it would have been easier if they did not share certain information (“I think it would have been helpful if we did not share information […] for example if my text is about megatsunamis and other texts did not contain the word tsunami”, D). E suggested to use specific roles, with F building on  that idea: “Maybe one person could focus on aspects like numbers, […] while another on technical terms”. Overall, our real collaboration study complements the experimental study  by demonstrat ing that tasks can influence self-regulation strategies of some learners. However, it also highlights that not all students follow through with the tasks and instructions given.

## Discussion

Academic help-seeking is a social self-regulated strategy where students seek out  their peers as sources of information to improve academic success. However, this process can rely on accurate source memory  (SM), as selecting an appropriate source of help is essentia l for successful help-seeking (Makara & Karabenick, 2013). In our experimental study, we examined the effects of source-related tasks and content-related tasks on SM and learning in CSCL. SM was overall not influenced by tasks, contradicting our hypothesis that SM should be better with a source-related task (H1a): simply instructing participants before collaboration to learn the sources for a SM test was not sufficient. One explanation for the missing effect  may lie in the role of group awareness tools, which here presented the (differing) knowledge levels of the learning partners. In a recent experiment, Ülker and Bodemer (2024) demonstrated that learners in heterogeneous groups with partners with differing knowledge levels use such expertise-related information when remembering the sources.  Simply presenting expertise-related information via group awareness tools may facilitate SM to a level where the explicit task to remember sources adds no additional benefit. This explanation aligns with the results of Engelmann and Hesse (2010): the authors applied a group awareness tool which provided learners with concept maps of their learning partners. Learners with access to the tool could better identify which learning partner had information about certain learning contents (implying better SM). Group awareness tools can aim to present both the content and its source more  explicitly, fostering collaborative processes during their use and supporting academic help-seeking after collaboration. Learning was also not influenced by instructions, contradicting our hypothesis that content-related tasks should improve learning  (H2a). Learners seem to naturally  learn shared content of others in a (CS)CL context, regardless of explicit tasks. However, regarding SM, explicitly learning for a test (with a content-related task) can guide learners ’ attention to different learning partners: while overall SM did not differ, learners remembe red partners with high knowledge better as sources than  those with low knowledge  when learning for a knowledge test. Similarly, Ülker and Bodemer (2024) have shown that when learning for a knowledge tes t, SM was highest for high knowledge partners. Our results support the idea that SM is context dependent and differs based on the context in which information is learned  (Nadarevic & Erdfelder, 2019). Further, o ur results indicate  different academic help-seeking tendencies in different learning contexts: assuming that experts can provide better answers, learners may particularly attend to high knowledge partners when learning for an exam  to make use of their partners’ information in retrospect. This is in line with the  expectancy-value model of source selection (Makara & Karabenick, 2013), which explains that the probability of selecting certain partners as sources of help depends on the perceived quality of the help. In contrast, in a collaborative setting without specific test preparation (without a content-related task), partners are remembered equally well as sources. Our results highlight how SM (and thus academic help-seeking strategies) can vary  based on the context collaborators are learning for  and underline the need for consideration of such variables when designing instructional support in CSCL context. We further assumed a tradeoff between learning the sources and learning the content (e.g., Cook et al.,

2006) and proposed that source-related and content-related tasks would work better if applied separately and not

combined. However, both hypotheses (H1b and H2b) could not be supported by the data because SM and learning were overall not influenced by tasks. Quite the contrary, we observed  a positive correlation between SM and learning. In some contexts, deeper engagements with an item (here: the learning content) can also boost memory for contextual information (here: the source/learning partner) and strengthen source-content-relations in long-term memory (Tsuboi & Francis, 2025). Thus, the positive correlation between SM and learning implies that supporting SM might not only improve learners ’ long-term academic help seeking but also improve immedia te learning. Moreover, combined tasks did also not increase mental load: only source-related tasks lead to higher mental load. We found no link between mental load and SM, which is in line with prior research (e.g., Hanway et al., 2024).

## Limitations, future directions, and conclusion

One limitation of our study is that we did not consider individual differences. For instance, in our case study, one learner with a source-related task revealed that he did not focus on the sources, while the other two applied strategies considering the sources. This raises the question why some learners focus on  the source  and apply strategies (like note-taking) while others disregard them intentionally.  We propose two variables worth considering. (1) In our study, a source-related task led to higher mental load. However, humans differ regarding their working memory capacity, which could influence how effectively they proces s source-related information in collaborative learning. Learners with higher wor king memory capacity may be better able to handle b oth: learning the content and learning the sources simul taneously. (2) R esearch in the area of multiple document comprehension suggests that SM candepend on factors such as prior knowledge regarding the (learning) topic: in some cases, higher prior knowledge is associated with better SM (Stang Lund et al., 2019). Our real collaborative study revealed that  instructions before collaboration  to remember the learning partner who presents certain information can target self-regulative processes: some students applied strategies to regulate their own behavior and engaged in cognitive offloading (i.e., taking notes to externally store information). However, this spontaneous strategy might not necessarily be the best to support SM. While cognitive offloading can be beneficial and counteract the limitations of memory capacities, it can also impair how well information is stored in long-term memory and noted information is sometimes remembered worse  (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). Additionally, in the study of Stadtler and Bromme (2007), participants with the task to indicate the source when gathering information from different internet sources did not have better SM than participants without such a task. Considering that students with source-related tasks engage in note taking and that such behavior might even impair memory, we propose that source-related tasks should be applied with further guidance and that tasks should focus on (regulative) strategies regarding the interaction between collaborators. For example, instructions that encourage frequent questioning could strengthen mem ory for source-content-relations and can then be incorporated into educational tools —if proven useful. With a shared goal  to collectively support each other in learning source-content-relations, such tasks could foster shared regulation, learners can aid each other, and a positive interdependence might emerge (for an overview of tools prompting shared regulati on, see Järvelä et al., 2016). Since partner modeling (mental models about learning partners’ knowledge) candepend on the quality of interactions between learners  (Dillenbourg et al., 2016), it is plausible that improving the quality of peer interactions can also shape SM and holistic source-content-relations in learners’ long-term memory. However, it remains unclear whether such tasks could also increase mental load, as observed with our source-related task. Therefore, such interventions and potential strategies to foster collaborative learne rs’ SM should be carefully applied under certain conditions, for example by considering individual differences we pointed out earlier. (1) Individuals with higher working memory capacity can better  utilize strategies to encode information more effectively  (Streitberger et al., 2024)  and might be able to fully utilize certain instruct ions. In contrast, individuals with lower working memory capacity might particularly benefit from implicit guidance (e.g., group awareness tools providing knowledge-related information of learning partners). (2) Especially learners with low prior knowledge might need support, while interventions could be redundant or even disruptive for learners with high prior knowledge. Group awareness tools, for example, could assess learners’ working memory capacity or prior knowledge and adaptively provide targeted support. Identifying further potential learner attributes that might affect whether and how students make use of instructions can help to derive more implications for CSCL. Drawing on research on multiple document comprehens ion can offer valuable insights, as it often addresses the question of how sources and content are integrated in mental models of readers (e.g., Stang Lund et al., 2019). Our controlled experimental study isolated the effects of tasks systematically, ensuring that confounding variables were minimized and that SM could be  carefully analyzed without influencing  guessing processes, thereby contributing to rigorous research on SM in CSCL.  Despite the small scale, our real collaborative study revealed qualitative insights into  the practical application  of tasks, allowing to derive holistic implications for CSCL, instructors, or educational technologies. Taken together, our mixed-methods approach and studies answer questions raised earlier. Simply instructing learners to remember the sources  appears ineffective, potentially due to interindividual differences of learners. While a source-related task induced more mental load,  it did also not impair content learning and no tradeoff between learning the source and learning t he content occurred. Instead, the positive relationship between learning and SM suggests that it is worthwhile to identify effective interventions on how to scaffold SM. Supporting learners in achieving better mental representations of which learning partner shared certain information can help learners to better utilize  their peers’ knowledge, ultimately promoting sustainable collaborative practices, fostering mutual growth, and building foundations for academic communities.
