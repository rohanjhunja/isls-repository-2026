# Student Assessors’ Engagement with Online Peer Feedback

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We investigated how two EFL students of different writing proficiency engaged with online PF behaviorally, cognitively, and affectively as assessors by multiple data sources. Key findings include: the HWP (high writing proficiency) student had high behavioral, cognitive and affective engagement; the LWP (low writing proficiency) student displayed high affective engagement but limited behavioral and cognitive engagement. The findings suggest the complex relationships among the three dimensions of EFL student assessors’ engagement with online PF.

## Introduction and research purpose

Peer feedback (PF) has been widely used for formati ve assessment purposes in second language  writing classrooms. As assessees, students reflect on the w eaknesses in their own writing and make revisions b ased on the feedback they receive (Y u et al., 2019). Therefore, PF promotes learning when students actively en gage in it. However, peers, as novice writers, might lack the necessary expertise to offer meaningf ul feedback; alternatively, students may not provide negative feedback because of concerns a bout interpersonal relationships (Topping, 2009). P rior research has shed some light on how students b ehaviorally, cognitively, and affectively engage with receiving PF as assessees (e.g., Fan & Xu, 2020). A paucity of extant research has investi gated student engagement with PF as assessors. This work presents a multi-case study of two students of different writing proficiency levels engaging in online PF in an EFL writing classroom. Their behavioral, cogn itive, and affective engagement was described and compared, and insights for EFL writing instruction were discussed.

## Methodology

The participants of this study were two third-year English majors (female, years old = 20) at a STEM university in China. The instructor conducted PF training seminar at the beginning of the semester. Using Paulus’ s (1999) criteria, assessors provide written comments on six  dimensions  of both content and form: organization, development, cohesion, structure, vocabulary, and m echanics. By analyzing the peer feedback received, the teacher showed the students how to provide high-quality feedback on the online PF system according to the criteria. ## Results

Behavioral engagement Regarding behavioral engagement with online PF, both the HWP and LWP students  provided more negative feedback on content-focused issues. The HWP student identified and expl ained more problems, while the LWP student gave small number of helpful comments. In this sense, the HWP student seemed to engage with providing feedback at a deep level, while the LWP student’s e ngagement was limited. The LWP student’s lower writ ing proficiency and self-efficacy might have limited her ability to effectiv ely give helpful information. Another explanation for the LWP student’s superficial engag ement is related to Vygotsky’s ZPD (Zone of Proxima l Development). For a student with lower writing proficiency, providing feedback to peers is  more likely to be far above the level of their writing proficiency. Thus, they tend to provide more praise comments. Cognitive engagement The cognitive engagement of the two students differed in the feedback features. The HWP student provided much more explanations, suggestions and solutions and had more cognitive engagement in providing feedback. Writing proficiency might explain the difference between th e two students, which echoes previous studies’ find ings that assessors’ writing proficiency is related to provid ing helpful feedback (e.g., Wu & Schunn, 2023). The HWP student’s deep-level cognitive engagement explained her high behav ioral engagement. T he LWP student had more difficulties in reviewing others’ work, perhaps because the work and revisions are beyond her ability. Lack of effective strategies (e.g., revision avoidance) might decreased her cognitive engagement although s he used some positive strategies (e.g., evaluating assessors’ intention). It’s therefore recommended that interaction should be included in PF processes to facilitate meaning negotiation (Fan & Xu, 2020). Affective engagement The study found positive affective engagement in bo th students, despite their different levels of behavioral and cognitive engagement with online PF. They believed in the value of online PF and acknowledged its worth because online PF enabled them to provide more honest and critical feedback. The positive affective engagement of the HWP student was consistent with her high behavioral and cognitive engagement. However, a mismatch was observed between what the LWP student expressed in the interview and what she did in peer review. LWP students may lack strong motivation to learn to wri te or participate in peer review, which may hamper their benefits from PF. Another reason might be that LWP students have difficulties  in providing feedback and therefore need more assistance to improve their ability of evaluating peer feedback.

## Conclusion

This study investigated how students of different English writing proficiency engaged with online PF as assessors in an EFL writing classroom. Consistencies were obs erved across the three dimensions of engagement of the HWP student; she actively engaged with online PF by  providing comprehensive and helpful feedback, demonstrating high engagement. However, discrepanci es were observed between the high affective engagement of the LWP student and her low behavioral and cogni tive engagement. The findings of this study contrib ute to extending the knowledge of EFL students’ engagement  with providing online PF, and provide insights int o how HWP and LWP students differ in their behavioral, co gnitive, and affective engagement. Future research could include more participants and conduct a quantitative study to expand the generalizability of the findings.
