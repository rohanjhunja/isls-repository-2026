# Acknowledging Power Structures in the Design of AI-Based Orchestration Tools

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Classrooms are historically situated within hierarchical power structures. In traditional K-12 classrooms, teachers are positioned as the experts who makes decisions about learning, and students follow their instruction. This contrasts with approaches wherein teachers position students as having agency in how they learn and with whom. One way to disrupt more traditional power structures and support mutuality in the classroom is to leverage technology to provide an avenue to communicate and make decisions about learning. In this paper, we examine teachers’ views of shared control while designing an orchestration tool which it aimed at distributing power across teachers, students, and technology while students are moving between individual and collaborative activities. We discuss two case studies that highlight teachers’ nuanced views of how AI can be leveraged to support dynamic transitions for learning. Our contribution highlights a need to account for the range of teachers’ views about power structures when designing AI-based orchestration tools.

## Introduction and background

In an effort towards understanding power structures in classrooms we take  a socio-cultural approach to consider how social structures affect relations of power (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Masse y & Wall, 2020). Esmonde and Langer-Osuna (2013) describe, “power is made visible in the ways in which people are positioned in relation to one another, … and in the ways in which issues of status and hierarchy are c onstructed.” Power is therefore mediated by the interactions between individuals and the tools within a social struc ture (Lasky, 2005). While power is not inherently good or bad, it can lean one way or the other based on how it is operationalized in classrooms, for instance how individuals position themselves to one another and what actions they take to uphold or share that power (Holland & Leander, 2004; Learning in Places Collaborative, 2020). In US classrooms, teachers are the ones who have the power to make decisions about how students will learn and with whom moment to moment, as they are positioned as the experts in the  classroom who most often get to make decisions about how collaboration happens. However, there is a gr owing movement for students to have agency in their learning (Gustein, 2007; Massey & Wall 2020) and a push to le verage AI to support these interactions (Holstein & Aleven, 2022). While researchers argue that AI can complement what humans are able to do, we argue that introducing technology has the potential to help disrupt historical power structures and share control but can also hinder this type of interaction based on how it is designed and used. In this paper, we explicitly attend to how technology intersects with structures of hierarchy in the classroom, and how in our case, teachers’ views’ can both compliment and challenge the goals of the technology. In this article we highlight case studies of two teachers with contrasting vi ews on human-AI complementarity in the design process of an orchestration tool. This study pre sents part of the design process toward creating an AI-based orchestration tool that supports dynamic pairing in the  classroom. Dynamic pairing refers to students transitioning between individual and collaborative learning activi ties based on students’ needs in real time (blinded; Olsen et al., 2021). This learning scenario assumes that a class of students can be working on different assignments, either collaborative or individual, and that they are  moving between these modes of learning fluidly throughout a class period. This form of learning has been hypothesize d to have positive effects by prompting one student to help another to improve individual accountability, peer inte ractions, and positive interdependence among peers (Mullins et al., 2011). In this paper, we report on the stage in the design process where we worked with teachers to design the orchestration tool and discuss th e challenges when designing with teachers who have a range of perspectives regarding shared control in the ir classroom. We use teachers’ views about sharing control as a proxy for examining the power structures at play. We ask the question, how do teachers’ views about sharing control inform the design of an AI-based orchestration tool?

## Methods

The goal of this project was to create a dynamic pairing orchestration tool that distributes power between teachers, students, and AI while students are moving between individual and collaborative activities. The orchestration tool is situated in the context of two AI-based tutoring systems, Lynette, an individual problem-solving tutoring system (blinded), and APTA, a collaborative tutoring system that provides a space for mutual peer tutoring (blinded). CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 174 Both tools are used to teach linear equation solving for middle school students and have been shown to improve learning (blinded). While these tools are typically used separately, we h ave explored the feasibility of using the two tools to support dynamic switching (blinded). Twelve middle school math teachers (10 females) participated in the three design sessions in the spring and summer of 2021. All teachers were in the United States. Each teacher consented to take part in the research and was compensated for each ses sion. We conducted three co-design sessions; each one had a specific focus and activity. Throughout all sessions we were focused and iterating on our design conjectures, including how and when students should be paired and what the roles of the teachers, students, and AI are within this process so that it can support students’ collaborati ve and math learning. All sessions were conducted virtually with one teacher at a time and recorded through a video conferenc ing platform. We had approximately 21 hours of video data, which were transcribed using an online ser vice. During each session, we collected observation notes, and documented the artifacts or prototypes that tea chers interacted with and created. We analyzed session recordings using Affinity Diagramming (Hanningt on & Martin, 2012), where two members of the research team reviewed the videos, transcripts, observati on notes, and prototypes from sessions to separate design ideas into data points. In this paper, we highlight two case studies from this analysis to illustrate how teachers’ views about sharing control in their classroom inform the design of the orchestration tool.

## Case study 1: Technology and teachers retain power

Parker was a 7th grade teacher who had been teaching for 14 years after s pending several years as a software developer. Although having experience in the tech industry, she had not leveraged many digita l tools in her classroom before the COVID-19 pandemic. She joined us for her first hour-long  co-design session in April of 2021 to discuss the project and begin building a prototype of the orchestration tool. Parker had a very set perspective on the technology. She almost immediately expressed that technology could support learning in the classroom but that it also has limitations. She describ ed wanting to receive suggestions from the orchestration tool about who might benefit from being paired to gether, but always wanted the final say. She explained, “ The socio-emotional part. The thing is you can’t put that in an algorithm.” She elaborated that while the technology is great for sharing context of the learning, such as skills and progress, it always needs to be used by the teacher to account for everything else that is taking place in the clas sroom that is not and should not be included in the technology. “It can’t [do everything].” she said, “I’m sure some people want it to do everything but there would definitely be a red flag for me.” Parker positioned the technology as something that she used in her classroom to inform learning, yet when we probed into what the students’ roles might be in this process, she had a different perspective.

Early on in our conversation, Parker brought up issues of student motivation to engage in peer tutoring. She proposed, “What if [the technology] says, Lisa you’ve been asked to tutor, do you accept? If s he does, she gets a star or something or whatever it is… to motivate them to say yes instead of ‘no, I’m working’.” We discussed what it would look like to get a student to engage in tutoring and how to incentivize the process. While our discussion on motivation assumed an affordance that allowed students to accept or deny being a tutor, when asked if a student should be able to respond to a potential pairing by confirming and denying, Pa rker's answer became more nuanced. “ That’s a good question. But… Um… no.” she elaborated, “Because if I determine that Greg is struggling and uh, I can tell that he's only answered one out of ten questions in 45 minutes  then something is happening. Either he’s sleeping or something is happening. So uh. No, I don’t want him to be able to deny.” While Parker did not want students whoneeded help to be able to accept or deny a pairing, she thought that if they were doing well and in the tutoring role, it would be different. She explained, “Now on the flip side. If I say, hey Lisa, you're going to be acting as a tutor. They might not feel comfortable in that role. So, I would give them the option. I would give them the option to say no ‘I’m not comfortable tutoring’ or ‘I don’t think I can help’. And ya no, some people just don’t have a knack for it. Or some people might know it but not know how to explain it very well. But ya know, before we even talked about this I might model how to do this.” She went on to say that she would model what tutoring looks like before even starti ng an activity like this or give students a space to practice peer tutoring on a simple ass ignment so that when they get to more challenging content, they have had some scaffolding to support their interactions. We continued this conversation and made an alternative suggestion – what if a student could suggest who they are paired with? Parker was quicker with her response – “No. I don't want them to have that control. ” When asked to explain she shared, “ Same reasons. Because they’d want to pair up with their friends. And the messages  would be ‘heyyyyyyy!’ ‘So awesome!’ And it would be [nonsense].” Parker explained that her ideal scenario in the software, “I would keep [students] anonymous, no suggestions, no denying, except those who will be tutoring, I would give them the option to say ‘I'm not comfortable’ or ‘I don't know this well enough’.” CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 175 Parker’s perspective of who should have power in the classroom was both fin ite and nuanced. She was firm throughout the co-design session that she should have the control to pair stude nts based on information she had in the classroom with the data and suggestions from the algorithm in the orchestration tool. She thought students should not have a say in this process unless they are being placed in a tutoring role. Even here, Parker was sharing power only with students who were doing well with the content, as she explained early on in the session that tutors should be higher achieving students, for instance pairing a  student who is excelling in the content with someone who is struggling. Some students could inform their learning, w hereas others could not, and Parker justified not being able to suggest or confirm and deny pairings based on learning issues.

## Case study 2: Technology having the most knowledge

Jess was a middle school math teacher with 20 years experience, afte r years of tutoring middle and high school students. He regularly used technology in his classes. Jess participated for the first time in the second round of co-design sessions in July of 2021. In this session we iteratively refine prototypes of the orchestration tool based on feedback from teachers in previous sessions.

Jess overall thought that deciding who was paired together was best to be done by the algorithm in the orchestration tool. He explained,  “I like the idea of having the computer set up [pairing and unpairing]. Because guess what, I have my own personal bias, and so does every other teacher. And just like a statistic, I want this to be unbiased. And so, I think a system that would just randomly, based on whatever me trics you're going to be using.” Jess shared that he thought that the algorithm in the orchestration tool was an objective voice in the pairing process. He described that the technology could make less biased decisions compa red to him, but he would still want to be involved to consider other factors that the technology cannot. Throughout our work together he continued to emphasize the importance of interpersonal re lationships in the classroom that inform collaboration and learning. He explained, “ Personal relationships are always important [when pairing]. … Those are decisions the teachers have to make, not  the tech or the students. ” Jess discussed at length the role of students in this process because he thought students should have some kind of voice in the pairing process. Jess proposed a method for the orchestration tool to r ecord and act on students’ feedback about their collaboration. He explained that students should be able to document how the collaboration went and share if it wasn’t a good fit,  “Because there's a couple of factors. It goes back to what I was saying about bein g accepting of all students. Like, maybe, you know, I just wanted to know if it was a communication issue? Was it somebody not following our core values? We get kids that are on the autism spectrum, kids that have anxiety, we have kids, I mean… you st art throwing in the emotional intelligence learning. There's, there's more to th is than just learning math. And sometimes math is not the objective. Maybe it's, you know, did you learn something? Can you collaborate with others? And that's all part of learning, too.” He continued to reiterate that learning to work with everyone is a skill he wants his students to have. He discussed how the  technology could support these interactions, especially in cases where a student was uncomfortable or was being harassed a bout their identity or learning, “If there's a situation that comes up that Student One would prefer not to have this  particular student again [because something happened]. Yeah, I would think the system would need to remember that or know that so that's fine to randomly assign a different tutor.” Jess described scenarios where he would want the technology to document the collaboration and allow students to flag partners they would not want to work with again. But he reiterated that while there were scenarios that students might not want to work together, the teacher should have the c ontrol to mark them as resolved and that a student should never be able to deny a pairing. Since he views being able to work with everyone as a core value in his classroom, he wants everyone to try it out and then reflect on thin gs that did not go well and communicate it to the teacher and the technology. When asked if the students should be able to suggest pa irs, he again discussed how the technology was an unbiased voice compared to students and reiterated the need to work with everyone. “And besides that, [students] are biased too because they'll only pair up with their friend or their buddy or whatever. And I think sometimes it's nice to, you know, in this world, you got to work with everybody, you gotta get out of your area.” Jess had a unique perspective about the use of the orchestration tool in his classroom, valuing the data in the pairing algorithm as being highly knowledgeable and claiming it as an unbiased per spective. Jess wanted to work closely with the orchestration tool to inform pairing decisions, but still wanted students to have say to inform the process but no significant control in it beyond voicing problems. Even in instance s where Jess discussed the importance of social interaction in collaboration because math is not an objective process, this did not translate to the algorithm, which he saw as an objective party.

## Conclusion

These two cases highlight the differences in how teachers conceptualize how  algorithms might inform their teaching. Where Jess was willing to rely heavily on the AI, assuming objecti vity in the data and viewing AI as CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 176 checks and balances for his own biases, Parker was skeptical and recognize d the limitations of the technology. These scenarios give AI different levels of power in the classroom. Teachers who rely on the AI are giving it more power over the pedagogical decisions about dynamic transitions, compared to teacher s who are informed by the AI but question and interrogate its validity retain that power. Just like power is not fixed in the classroom (KayiAydar, 2015), interactions with technology are not either. Making it complex when designing technologies to redistribute power. Power is informed by the interactions between individuals, technologies, and social structures that exist within it (Lasky, 2005). Yet even if designed in a way that is expected to distribute power, the range of the teachers’ views regarding shared control and technology use poses challenges that may inadvertently reinforce traditional power structures or give more power to the AI. Therefore, these findings are critical for those designing AI-based orches tration tools because we need to understand and account for the context they are situated within (Schindler & Saxena, 2022). This includes how power structures are at play to inform who can participate when, and to wha t extent. Designers of these systems need to reflect on the realities that even under the best initiations, AI systems are not always taken up as designed. Checks and balances and transparency need to be embedded so that AI does not hold the power in pedagogical decisions, regardless of the teachers’ trust in the technology.
