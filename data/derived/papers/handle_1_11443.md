# Reimagining EdTech: Designing Tools for Joyful Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This short paper previews ongoing research exploring joyful learning as a construct of well-being-centered learning experience design. It examines industry-based interpretations of joyful learning and how designers embed it into educational technology (EdTech) products. This study uses Likert-scale surveys (Boone & Boone, 2012; Tanujaya et al., 2022) to assess participants’ beliefs about their design practices, and structured interviews to understand how they conceptualize and promote joyful learning through their products. Descriptive statistical and thematic analyses are in progress. This research aims to contribute to the learning sciences by promoting discourse on theories of well-being at the intersection of learning experience design and human-computer interaction. It also aims to develop actionable strategies for scaffolding joyful learning in the design of EdTech.

## Introduction

In our rapidly evolving world, technology is a powe rful catalyst for innovation. It impacts virtually every aspect of our lives, blurring the lines between the possible and impossible, and presenting seemingly limitless capabilities to imagine new realitie s. This capacity of technology to shape our world's  social, economic, and environmental values and traditions is awe-inspiring, with far-reaching implications for individual and collective  governance, sustainability, and engagement with the current and  future world systems we are designing. Defined as “the generation or use of knowledge and processes to sol ve problems and extend human capabilities” (Shackel ford, 2007; Technology for All Americans Project, 1996), it is easy to understand why there is growing  attention directed toward how technology can be used more str ategically in educational practice. Leveraging its various technological affordances, the potential of technol ogy has been translated into numerous digital produ cts and platforms—educational tec hnologies—aiming to address some of the education industry’s great challenges, supporting the facilitation of more expedient and impactful solutions. While educational technology (“EdTech”) is often celebrated for its potential to “automatically” enhance learning experiences, it is important to remember that technology alone does not make for good design. As EdTech products continue to be integrated into learning en vironments, it becomes increasingly important to en gage with the design and use of these tools to cultivate more  equitable and empowering learning experiences, in ways that align opportunities with the envisionings of transformative learning we hold for 21st-century learners and beyond. As such, this study emphasizes the design of EdTech  for the intentional purposes of supporting users i n more holistically meaningful learning. It assumes that, like educational policies and practices, EdTech too ls can be designed with mechanisms that work to promote student well-being. Academic scholarship discusses the impact of well-being on learning, suggesting a positive correlatio n between overall well-being and academic performance. Empirical studies h ighlight well-being’s positive association with student motivation, satisfaction,  and achievement (Jones, 2015). Education scholars s imilarly emphasize the significance of affect and emotional well-being in educational contexts, particularly for the ir role in supporting students who have faced challenges in  academic settings, suggesting that emotional suppo rt is key to fostering resilience and increasing these studen ts’ likelihood of success in school (Nasir et al., 2014). Simply put, well-being is foundational to learning, and given this significant relationship and the role of education systems in cultivating students’ ongoing positive development, it is apposite for educational environments to be designed to nurture learners’ emotional, social, and cogniti ve well-being. While the significance of well-being in educational settings is increasingly recognized, the question of how to design for it is less clearly understood. This implies a need for more explicit scholarship and practical strategies to help support its integration into educational design, to reshape institutions and the experiences they offer to successfully cultivate environments where students can accomplish their academic goals while also flourishing holistically. Aligned with this need, this exploratory study aims to contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of how to design for these objectives, and consider s the opportunities for technology’s role in the fa cilitation of the pursuit of well-being. The research question guiding this study is:  How do EdTech designers conceptualize, perceive, promote, and practice joyful learning in their products? Highlighting joy as a descriptor, or outcome, of well-being and a key construct in positive learning experience design (Bowen et al., 2020; Desmet & Pohlmeyer, 2013), the study investigates EdTech designers’ per ceptions and practices of joyful learning to contri bute to a shared vision and theory of well-being in educational technology design, and to help shape the design of EdTech tools that might work to cultivate joy in future hu man-technology interactions. Establishing a shared visi on of joyful learning in EdTech design helps make the con cept more explicit and actionable within industry while also supporting the broader integration of well-being practices across educational settings. Theoretical and conceptual frameworks Primarily rooted in sociocultural learning theory (Vygotsky & Cole, 1978), this study acknowledges an inextricable, reciprocal relationship between learn ing and culture. As Markus and Conner (2011) articu late, learners are shaped by the cultural contexts in which they learn, even as they actively contribute to and [re]shape these contexts. In this recursive process, learners  “create the cultures to which they later adapt,” i nfluenced by their surroundings while simultaneously influencing  the culture that will shape future learning. Conce ptualizing learning through the sociocultural perspective exte nds an ecological focus to examining cognitive deve lopment, considering the individual, interpersonal, and institutional contributions (Rogoff et al., 2002) within learning and development pathways (Barron, 2010) that mediate engagement while learning. These assumptions imply that the design of education systems be attuned to the inher ent, experience-dependent nature of learning, as learning is enabled and supported  by the various resources (Sawyer, 2005; Brown, 2019) employed and operationalized within learning environments. This has significant implications for reframing the utility of resources  in learning environments, leveraging the affordances of content  and contexts (beyond instructor knowledge and curr iculum) to facilitate positive engagement with learning and include opportunities to nurture student well-being and overall development.

The Learning Ecology Framework emphasizes the multi ple settings, relationships, and resources involved in the production of opportunities cultiva ting learning (Barron, 2006). This framework, theor ized by Brigid Barron, helps articulate the distribution of  resources existing within “dynamic ecosystems” shap ing and adapting to learners’ experiences and interests. Th is ecological perspective articulates the significa nce of “interdependencies between [person-level] and environmental variables in development,” calling attention to the varied opportunities enabled by engagement between these variables. Emphasizing learning in this way acknowledges opportunities to engage with resources  as catalyzing cognition and interest progression, with learning as the product of the relationships we exp erience between multiple agents and tools. Barron u ses this framework to progress the idea that youth development is dependent upon the various community assets in which youth participate, building synergy between engagem ents to construct interest and knowledge. This ecol ogical perspective of learning further supports the idea t hat people grow and progress in relation to the res ources they access. Framing learning as an ecological process emphasizes the confluence and combination of things working together in producing individual engagement mediated by collective means. Distributed intelligence refers to the distribution  and operation of intelligence across time, tools, space and place, and people, embodying the idea that “intelligence is accomplished rather than possessed” (Pea, 1993). This view shifts away from the traditional notion of intelligence happening within and confined to an individual, broadening its conceptualization to include the int eractions among social, material, and cultural comp onents. Conceptualizing intelligence in this way affords a view of humandevelopment as co-constructed between and contingent upon the human, technical, and metaphysi cal resources available within the environments we inhabit. This framing underscores an assumption of availabil ity, accessibility, and imaginative reconfiguration  of resources to support cognition and learning, and in vites a “rethinking of learning and educational goa ls and practices” (p. 57) to better scaffold the means by which intelligence is created and learning is foste red. It also supports the idea that well-being and emotional states, like joy, are, too, situat ed, relational, and co-constructed through the affordances of the environments in which we engage. Design can be conceptualized as the imaginative exp ression of intelligence towards the innovation of solutions. An influencer of emotion and behavior, good design enhances the quality and functionality of environments, experiences, and products, and mediat es encounters that are thoughtful and serviceable t o the purpose of the producer (Kim, 2020). This prospect of design as a catalyst of engagement positions  it as a generative solution for positive change, potentiall y strengthening and revitalizing the personal and i ndustrial systems that shape our lives and societies. The rel ationship between learner outcomes and the institut ions that shape the culture in which learning takes place hig hlights a powerful relational development between l earning and the “resources and practices made available across the spaces  in which learners move” (Penuel et al., 2016, p. 31). Just as learning is impacted by experience and environment, our capacity for ingenuity is enab led by the pluralistic entanglement of the principles we  embody, the relationships with people in which we e ngage, and the enactments of the rituals we practice across contexts (Hutchins, 2000; Pea, 1993). Intersecting the aforementioned theoretical perspec tives—design, the distributed nature of intelligence, ecological models of personal development, and the sociocultural foundations of learning—affords an integrative [re]conceptualized approach that leverages engageme nt among people, cultural tools, and practices we h ave at hand to imagine bold innovations in education. With in a joyful learning model, well-being is positioned as a conduit for more enjoyable, empowering learning. Wh en applied to the design of resources and various a ssets within learning ecosystems, joy becomes distributed  through interactions and engagements, resulting in  joyful learning as a meaningful, emergent outcome. Alongside other resources present and activated within the learning communities we traverse, envisioning the design of technology—educational technology specifically —with a well-being-centered lens, we might operationalize these tools as agents of re lationship building, identity development, and personal growth. This approach crosses traditional borders that limit focus of engagement with technology to the teaching and practicing of techno logical skills, repositioning students' relationshi ps with technology as salient opportunities for imaginative “creative hustle” (Sobomehin & seidel, 2022) and speculative ideation beyond technology’s traditional intended a nd actual uses (Garcia & Nichols, 2021). This proje ct encourages the examination of EdTech design for gen erative, restorative purposes, aligning with more h olistic goals of education and 21st-century learning (Noddings, 2003; UNESCO, 2021), and reframing their utility for the purposes of curating joyful learning experiences.

## Research methods

This research aims to use insights and generative t hemes to contribute to conversations in the learnin g sciences about the intersection of positive design with learning experience design, and to develop actionable strategies for scaffolding joyful learning in the design of EdTech. Therefore, this study explores industry-focused interpretations of joyful learning as well as how designers embed it into the design of educational technology products. Engaging with EdTech designers to understand their personal and professional perceptions and practices of joyfu l learning provides a behind-the-scenes glimpse of design industry approaches to operationalizing joyful learning as a design practice. Fifty-one participants (n=51) were recruited using purpos ive and criterion sampling (Douglas, 2022) from a national network of designers. All participa nts who chose to participate in this study self-identified as working in EdTech and EdTech adjacent occupations, and they represented various roles and responsibili ties across the field, designing for a wide range of users including youth-centered educators and learners in formal and informal learning settings. Participants had the right to decline participation at any stage of the recruitment, survey, and interview process, and they were not paid for their participation in the study. Employing mixed methods, the first phase of the study used Likert-scale surveys (Boone & Boone, 2012; Tanujaya et al., 2022) to gather a quantitative measure of participants’ beliefs about their current design practices. Surveys were hosted online via the Qualtrics platform and presented five prompts designed to provide a composite score representing how they rate  themselves at designing for joyful learning. Descri ptive statistical analysis of survey results is intended to provide a quantitativ e assessment of how respondents indicate the degree  to which they agree or disagree with each prompt and the broader category of joyful learning. In phase two of the study, each designer scheduled and participated in structured interviews facilitat ed over the Zoom platform, sharing “how they conceptualize joyful learning” and “how they promote it in the design of their products.” Utilizing an iterat ive process of deductive and inductive analysis, in terview texts are being examined line-by-line to identify the primary subjects of each respo nse and categorize them under generalizable themes representing how participants interpret joyf ul learning versus  how they design for it in their products. Examined together, the surveys and interviews aim to provide a quantitative and qualitative representation of how participants believe they prioritize and practice j oyful learning in the design of their products, offering potential comparative insights across various EdTech industry roles.

## Preliminary findings

While the data collection of this research is compl ete, statistical analysis of survey results and the matic analysis of interview data are currently underway. Though fi ndings and suggested implications are pending, prel iminary insights provide demographic information about part icipants’ professional roles, years of industry exp erience, access to corporate-sponsored training opportunities, and an early indi cation of participants’ perceptions of their efforts to intentionally support well-being in their products. The data reveal a range of design responsibilities within the participant pool, with the majority of designers primarily occupying industry roles in product design (n=14). The remaining participants hold positions in user experience (n=9), product management (n=8), curriculum design (n=6), research (n=3), operations/strategy (n=3), and founder/executive positions (n=8). The d istribution of these professional roles suggests a range of design responsibilities and the potential for diver se organizational perspectives on how designing for  joyful learning is manifested in their various products. M ost participants reported that they have worked in the EdTech industry between 1 and 5 years (n=27), with fewer participants having 6-10 years of experience (n=12), or 11-15 years (n=7). The two smallest groups consist of par ticipants who have between 16 and 20 years of exper ience (n=4), or more than 21 years (n=1). These data high light a predominantly early to mid-career participant pool included in this study.

Initial analysis of interview data also reveals lim ited job-sponsored training opportunities specifically focused on the topic of equity-centered design. Equity is related to joy in learning, as equitabl e design creates conditions that are necessary for learners to experience sustained joy. Thirty-three of the 51 participants reported receiving no formal training from their companies o n this topic, however, 11 of these 33 designers did  note receiving funding for discretionary professional de velopment related to their personal design interest s. These findings suggest opportunities for more formal, ind ustry-led conversations supporting the integration of equ ity and equity-related dimensions into individual and company desi gn practices. Note that while not dire ctly asked, no participants mentioned receiving job-sponsored training opportunities explicitly discussing the topic of joyful learning when answering questions about how they co nceptualized and practiced joyful learning, though some did allude to having explicit conversations about how to experience more joy at work on the job or for the sake of their users.

Preliminary review of interview transcripts suggest s a range of interpretations for joyful learning, w ith emerging themes including mechanics related to gami fication, participation incentives, and positive em otional association. These themes are still unde rgoing analysis, however, there are early indicatio ns suggesting a shared appreciation and recognition of the value of joyful learning, but variability in how designers implement or perceive it in practice. The varying interpretations of joyful learning within the EdTech designer groups reflect the diverse nature of the topic and suggest the need for more s tudies exploring how practitioners conceptualize and operationalize joyful learning in order to contribu te to actionable strategies for integrating it into  future EdTech design.

While the data need to be thoroughly analyzed to ma ke more definitive statements about possible thematic conceptual design dimensions of joyful learning, these early engagements with participants and the data have encouraged excitement about exploring this concept and its integration in educational technology. Examples of criteria for joyful learning and well-being-centered design must be more widely examined to enc ourage and explicitly support their employment in educational practice through codified frameworks and applications. Deeper exploration of this study’s participants’ joyful le arning interpretations aims to inspire future ideat ions, conceptualizations, and specific use cases for revi talizing student engagement, learning, health, and wellness across PreK12 and Higher Education learning contexts, in and out of school.
