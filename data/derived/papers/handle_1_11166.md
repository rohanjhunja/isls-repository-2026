# Looking Back to See Myself: Analepsis as Tool to Deepen Proleptic Notions of Identity Development

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Studies of identity development have a long history in educational research; yet there remains a need to deepen understandings of ways communities who have been historically marginalized build identities across cultural contexts. Following scholars who have used the notion of prolepsis to explore youth identity development, we examine the potential for additional tools, such as analepsis, to understand the ways youth draw from family histories to inscribe themselves into the world. This study took place in a local history museum with Latine youth who co-designed a museum exhibit to represent their familial and cultural stories. Findings illustrate the potential for theoretical tools that expand upon studies of identity to unpack the ways youth draw upon the experiences of their ancestors, family members, and cultural figures to make sense of themselves in relation to their experiences as youth with cultural histories both within and outside of the U.S.

Major issues Calls to further understand learning as inherently cultural (e.g., Nasir et al., 2020) suggest the nee d for new theoretical tools understand how youth develop identities in relation  to cultural stories. Following scholars who have used the notion of prolepsis to explore the wa ys that youth develop identities relative to the expectations, identities and imagined futures of their elders, we question what tools might allow further unpacking of the ways youth draw from cultural and family histories to inscribe their own identities i nto the world. In this paper, we explore the potential for expanding notions of prol epsis to include analepsis (Genette, 1980; Phelan, 2016). A sister literary term to prolepsis (which has traditionally meant flashforward), analepsis refers to the embedding of past events within present tense narratives  (or flashback). We ground this theoretical inquiry in the empiric al findings from a participatory design project that engaged 42 Latine youth in community curation at a local history museum. Drawing from participatory design methods (Bang & V ossoughi, 2016) and sociocritical theories of learning (Gutiérrez, 2008), we seek to understand the ways young people engage in practices of community curation to build identities as historical actors (Gutiérrez, 2019). Emergent findings illustrate a ne ed to better understand the ways youth drew upon the experiences of their ancestors, immediate family members, and cultural figures to make sense of themselves as Latine youth living in the United States. Our approach in expanding notions of prolepsis to include analepsis addresses gaps in  research that points to learning and identity as  inherent to, rather than related to culture (Nasir et al., 2020). With this work, we seek to move toward reimaginin g the possibilities for understanding the learning pathwa ys youth take as they develop historicized identiti es across cultural contexts.

## Theoretical perspectives

Studies of identity development have a long history  in educational research (e.g., Cole, 1996; Polman & Miller, 2010; Nasir & Cooks, 2009; Wertsch, 1997); yet there rema ins a need to deepen understandings of the ways communities who have been historically marginalized build identities and leve rage agency across their cultural contexts. Recent scholarship has particularly called for examining young people’s identities in relation to histories of marginalization (e.g., Gutiérrez et al., 2019), and as “learning pathways” that are simultaneously social, cultural, and historical (Nasir et a l., 2020). Nasir and colleagues (2020) describe a g ap in the way studies in the learning sciences have approached learning as “rela ted to culture, rather than a necessarily cultural process” (p. 195). These authors call for new tools to build understandings of learning and identity as inherent to culture. Heeding this call, we take a sociocritical (Gutiérrez, 2008) approach to learning. We understand learning as ontological —a process of developing identities in relation to pow er and alongside cultural and historical materials, tools, and activities across time (Polman & Miller,  2010; Cole, 1996; Lemke, 2000; Nasir, 2000; Vygotsky, 1978; Wertsch, 1991). We draw from proleptic analyses focused on young people’s development of identities through the evocation of a future self a nd build upon the ways this research  layers young people’s identity development with adults’ expectations for their children’s futures based on their own lived experiences (e.g., Polman & Miller, 2010; Cole, 1996; 1998; Vossoughi & Gutiérrez, 20 16). While accounts of the past are inherent in the ways that prolepsis has been used t o describe identity development, we see analytic po tential in disaggregating the “nascent experience of the futur e” (Vossoughi & Gutiérrez, 2016, p. 139, citing Col e, 1998) from the ways youth story their identities in the present based on the experiences and stories of their ancestors. In literary theory, the sister concepts of prolepsis and analepsis offer ways to conceptualize both “flashforward” and “flashback” in narrative.

In this paper, we engage with the ways young people  “flashback” from their current experiences to past familial and cultural histories. We follow Sannino’s (2020) notion of transformative  agency, grounded in collective moves to transform circumstances in light of “pressing social needs” (p. 9; see also Engeström, 2015). Within moves toward transformation, youth may devel op critical consciousness (Freire, 1970) to histori cize themselves, their positioning by others, and their lived experiences within oppressive societal struct ures (e.g., racism, ethnocentrism, classism, ableism, etc.) As youth reimagine their own stories and experiences in the context of these structures, they may come to see themselve s as historical actors, understanding the “historic al meaning of their lives” and enabling “consequential action” (Gutiérrez et al., 2019, p. 293). Building from Nasir et al.’s (2020) learning pathwa ys framework, we see analepsis as a useful tool to clarify the ways development occurs along culturally defined pathways that “are not infinite” but are historically, geographically, and politically defined (p. 197). W e also recognize that if we want youth to engage as  historical actors with potential for transformative agency (Gutiérrez et al., 2019), then we must create learning environments where they are invited to question histories of oppressio n to reimagine their future identities and rewrite the possible pathways within which they learn. This paper integrates the notion of analepsis with a proleptic analysis of the ways that Latine youth expressed their identities in relation to place-based experiences, family stories, and cultural histories within a museum youth program. G uided by theorie s of the cultural process of learning (e.g., Nasir et al., 2006; Rogoff, 2014) and trajectories and pathways as apt metaphors for understanding how learners’ narrative identities shift and grow alongside their understanding of themselves and their histories (Polman, 2006; Polman & Miller, 2010; Nasir et al., 2020), we examined the questions:

• How do youth leverage family stories and cultural histories to articulate their own identities?

• How does engaging in museum community curation and exhibit design contribute to youths’

identity development as historical actors?

## Methods

The Viviendo Aquí community curation project took place in a history museum in the mountain west where 42 Latine teens were asked to create an exhibit that t old the story of their lives and cultures in the ci ty. The project drew from participatory design research methods (PDR; Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) to explore the ways young people incorporate individual, familial, cultural, and communal stories into museum spaces.

## Context and participants

Over the course of the 2020-2021 school year, two white researchers, a white mu seum educator, two Latine teachers, a Chicano undergraduate researcher, and 4 2 Latine teens collaborated to design a museum exhi bit focused on the teens’ lives and cultures  in the mountain west. The project began in August o f 2020 during the Covid-19 pandemic. Three of the teens, Uriel, Telema, and  Maricella (all names are pseudonyms) acted as collaborative designers (co-designers) alongside the researchers and museum edu cator, developing activities for the other participants and leading the design of the exhibit.  Mateo, the project’s 21-year-old undergraduate researcher, also acted as a participant and co-designer across all aspects of the exhibit design a ctivities, often creating models for the rest of the youth  to examine based on his own experiences as a Chican o living in the mountain west (participants’ ethnic, racial, and ge nder identities were self-ascribed). Because he was both participant and researcher, Mateo’s experiences inf ormed our understanding of the youth’s participatio n and are featured as part of the data analyzed in this paper.

Over the course of the 2020-2021 school year, the design team met for two hours weekly across 24 design meetings. The design workshops took place both in person at t he museum and online depending on the shifting restrictions in place in the city due to the pandemic. In design meetings, the design team engaged in the designed activities. We then implemented the activities with two secondary school classes for heritage Spanish speakers. The high school class was comprised of 15 students ages 14 to 18. These students met online for an hou r each week beginning in February 2021 and participated in  the designed activities for six weeks during their  language and culture class. The other class was comprised of  24 middle school students ages 11-13. These students participated in a two-day online workshop with the design team. All participants identified as Latine and self-selected into the study after being recruited by te achers and the museum educator based on these ident ities. Additionally, all participants contributed to the museum exhibit by first examining the stories they felt were most common in the public spaces of their city, then creating a shadow box that featured artifacts and images depicting the stories of their lives and experiences that the teens wanted to add to the public record. In addition to the shadow boxes, the lead designers  created interactive exhibit features including a m ap of the Americas where visitors could map their own stories, a recipe book where visitors could record their favorite family recipes, and videos and audio recordings of family stories they wanted included in the histories of the city (Figure 1). The exhibit opened in April of 2021 and  drew more than 1,500 visitors in eight weeks durin g the pandemic. The exhibit focused on the question: How do I tell my story of this city? To do this, we completed initial d ata analysis through iterative rounds of etic and emic coding of artifacts and interviews  to understand how youth participants positioned the mselves individually and collectively within the design process, as well as within the stories they chose to include in their museum exhibit. We added the notion of analepsis to our analysis as we examined these case studies and noticed patterns in the stories our participants chose for their exhibit. The emergent findings in this paper are drawn from our secondary analysis of the data, using analepsis to seek themes around ways that young people engaged family stories to describe their own identities.

## Findings

Our analysis illustrated that when young people were as ked to interrogate their own stories in relation to  the city where they live, they rarely began with their own experiences but instead drew from the experiences o f their families. Following Delgado Bernal (2002), we used this analysis to see our participants as “holders and creators of knowledge” (p. 105) and consider ed the ways Latine youth wove their identities with threads drawn from the historicizing of their familial immigration experie nces. In this paper, we present our four youth designers’ cases to examine the ways that our youth co-designers characterized their own identities and experiences by describing intertwining stories of self and family.  We examine these intertwined stories of self and fa mily as analepsis – drawing from the past to understand the present – and see this kind of identity development as an essential part of recognizing oneself as an agentic historical actor (Gutiérrez et al., 2019). “I choose to take my story from my parents.” Mateo, our undergraduate research assistant, was born in the most diverse metropolitan area in the state. He moved to the city where the project took place to attend the local university and during our first meeting a rticulated he had struggled to make sense of himself in the context of the majority white population in the city. Though the city had a history of Latine activism, that history was only recently visible on the university campus. Mateo explained he had been hesitant to work on the project because while it “asked the Latino heritage speakers of Boulder share their stories…the two research leads were white.” He went on to explain that he chose to join the project because when he voiced these concerns, the research team asked him how he would change the project. He said, “I’ve never really liked museums because I felt they weren’t for me…they didn’t show my history, and I couldn’t really translate the stories for my parents.” When he recognized the project was about telling his stories and creating an exhibit that was accessible to “my people,” he became invested in the project. During the first design meeting, Mateo suggested he act as both participant and researcher, collaborating with the youth designers while adding his own stori es to the museum exhibit to model the possibilities he saw inherent in the co-designed exhibit activities. Mateo began by sharing  stories with our youth co-designers about his experiences moving from the most diverse part o f our mountain west state to the mostly white city where the study took place. The more Mateo spoke about his ow n identity, the more he articulated that he had bui lt his notion of self in relation to his parents’ experiences. In a design meeting, he explained: I don’t have my personal immigration story, I choose to take my story from my parents because I was born in America, but my parents’ story of immigration really inspires me…I’m constantly inspired by the hardships that my parents had to go through to end up here. Mateo went on to describe the reasons that his fami ly left Mexico, a place where they wanted to stay, and the ways that he and his older brother always felt a se nse of longing for somewhere they had never been. Mateo’s story of himself was deeply connected to his parent s’ stories, even though his parents insisted that h e build his own identity as a U.S. citizen. Mateo explained that he felt his parents had given up so much of themselves to be in the U.S. that his  identity was entirely dependent upon what they had given up – their family, their land, and their own potential educations. For example, when d iscussing how he chose a combined major in ethnic s tudies, history, and education, Mateo explained that his mo m had to leave school much earlier than she hoped, which drove him to strive for his own educational success. In a discussion of his model shadow box he said, “My mom really wants to continue her love and passion for h istory. And so, I decided to take that up. I want to learn more about the history and our culture of our people.” F or Mateo, regardless of the success his parents wan ted him to have in the United States and the ways they project ed their own dreams for him into the future, his id entity was deeply rooted in their experiences leaving Mexico t o escape violence and oppression. Despit e that these experiences had occurred before he was born, they w ere central to the stories that he adopted to expla in who he was in relation to the world.

“My mom has always talked about our culture like ghosts.” Telema turned fourteen the year of the study. A nin th grader, she often talked about the way she had i mmigrated in early childhood with her brothers and mother fro m a small city in Mexico. Telema explained that her  mother had enrolled her siblings in the m useum’s programs because she wanted them to find wa ys to feel like they belonged in the city. Telema voiced feeling like sh e knew the city well and had some ownership over th e ways she navigated her experiences there. However, she also explained that she thought of herself as an ac tivist, someone who fought for social justice, particularly  for people who could  not fight for themselves. When asked about this identity, Telema explained that she and her siblings had always seen themselves as activists because it was part of their family. Their mother had been an organizer for Indigenous women’s rights long before  Telema was born. Not only had her mother projected activis m into Telema’s identity by sharing her own stories  with Telema, Telema had adopted those stories as her own. She said, “I brought what I knew [from my city in Mexico] and my family values to [the city]... I think I’m from both places.” Telema went on to share the ways that she saw her mom as an activist and how she thought of herself as an activist because of those stories. When describing thinking about her family histories  within the context of the museum, Telema also explained, “My mom has always talked about our culture like ghosts and taught us to talk about ourselves and to share stories and stand up for ourselves.” After further conversation, we took this to mean th at Telema saw the ways that her ancestors walked with her throughout her experiences. She identified deeply as Mexican, and she recognized the ways that she lived alongside the “g hosts” of her ancestors, even as she walked through  places they had never seen. She went on to explain that ev en though it felt like their family history and cul ture was far away, she thought of herself not just in terms of h er own experiences, but in terms of the work her mo ther had done advocating for Indigenous women’s rights that had connected them to those communities. “We still do that work here,” she added. Telema and her brothers attended marches and rallies alongside their mom in the U.S. They considered themselves activists and artists, a ble to navigate the city’s complexities and advocat e for their rights and the rights of others. Although Telema ex plained that her mother wanted her to build her own  identity in the U.S., she saw herself as living a legacy of activism that engaged her Mexican identity and that was based deeply upon her mother’s values and experiences. This perspective was echoed by her older brother, Uriel, a tenth grader who was also part of the design group. He explained that the things the Indigenous women experienced as they worked with his mom had become part of the way he himself thought about the world. Both Uriel and Telema had identities as activists for social justice because of their family experiences. “I enjoy my family and my culture and the way it doesn’t have borders.” Uriel, Telema’s older brother, was 15 years old at the beginning of the project and had been engaged with the museum’s teen programming for the previous three years. Uriel’s mom, Rosalía, had approached the design team early in the project and explained that they had em igrated from Mexi co to the U.S. because Uriel had learning needs that their local school was unable to support. Rather than enroll him in a school that could not support him, Rosalía had homeschooled Uriel. She chose to move t o the U.S. as Uriel approached adolescence beca use she wanted him to have every chance to succeed and thou ght that the U.S. education system might have more resources to accommodate his learning.

While Rosalía saw Uriel’s identity as deeply situat ed in the context of their move from Mexico to the U.S., Uriel often referred to himself as existing across borders. Often the stories he told about his time in Mexico centered on his experiences with his mom. He freque ntly told the story of traveling three hours by bus  to meet with the Indigenous communities Rosalía supported. On this journey, he described how he and Rosalía wo uld stop and buy crepes from the vendors and then savor them throughout their trip. His telling of this story was often punctuated with stories from Rosalía that Uriel had not experienced. For example, he described the ways that she had marched for Indigenous women’s rights prior to his birth. When asked to explain the ways that he s aw the stories shaping his experiences in our city, Uriel said, “I enjoy my family and my culture and the way  it doesn’t have borders.” For Uriel, the places that he had lived were deeply intertwined. When asked to contribute a story to the exhibit that would characterize his experiences in the city, Uriel chose to create an audio interview of Rosalía accom panied by more than fifty photos she had taken duri ng her time as an activist in Mexico. Uriel ask ed Rosalía about his experiences with her, but then  used the interview to probe into the ways that her own experiences had em erged as part of their lives. In the recording, she  told him a story about arriving to the Indigenous town late on e night after their bus had been delayed. She expla ined that they had built family that night when the people they stayed with had invited them into the kitchen and fed them, despite the late hour. Later, when reflecting on this story, Uriel explained that he believed in social justice because the people he worked with always treated him as tho ugh he were family. Their stories became part of hi s own story because of their interactions. His own identities were inscribed not only through his experiences but through his interpretation of the ways that others had lived.

“I don’t really know my story yet.” Lisa was the youngest member of the design team and  the one with the least experience in the museum co ntext. Although her grandparents and then her parents had immigrated to the U.S., she was born in the city wh ere the study took place. Lisa was quiet throughout many of  the design meetings, often contributing only after  her peers had stated their ideas, and then primarily in agree ment. However, she was fascinated by the movement o f her family and the ways they had brought recipes from M exico with them wher ever they lived.  One of her contributions to the exhibit was a handwritten reci pe of pozole that she had collected from her mother  and grandmother. Lisa explained that the recipe, while common in other families, was something that her family only used during the Christmas season. Her family was Ca tholic and deeply devout. Throughout the project, L isa appeared to be working to make sense of her own fai th alongside issues of documentation that her paren ts were facing. For example, during the winter, when the pandemic had once again required that sc hools and our project move online, Lisa explained that she and her older sister were going to Mexico to visit their family f or the first time. However, they had to travel without their parents. Lisa explained that while she was excited to go, especially to see the churches and the ways her family celebrated in Mexico, she felt strange leaving her parents behind. While Lisa was much less vocal than the other participants on the design team, her shadow box featured artifacts and images that tied her own experiences and sense of self to the values and experiences of her family (Figure 2). Lisa, a sixth grader, had begun the wor kshop telling stories of her grandmother, who lived  several states away. Although Lisa had not visited her grandmother often, she distinctly remembered the banana candy her grandmother had given her each time upon her ar rival. She also included a Mexican flag and a small map of Mexico. Next to her model of the candy, she had a tiny scul pture of the Nuestra Señora de Guadalupe alongside a rosary and another image of the virgen. Although she said that she didn’t “really know [her] story yet,” she included her favorite candy, a chili mango sucker that she said she always had at school with her friends. We saw this move as Lisa’s navigation between the parts of herself – the young person who traded candy with her friends and the diligent Catholic daughter who valued her family’s heritage despite having never been to visit her country. Lisa took on her family’s stories and identities to make sense of herself, illustrating the process you ng people go through to inscribe their own identities alongside and within the stories of their ancestors. ## Discussion

Notions of analepsis have been used in literary analysis to make sense of the ways that authors draw upon the past to create a narrative present (Phelan, 2016). In studies of i dentity, researchers have frequently used notions of prolepsis to explain the interaction between young people and  the adults that support them to shape their future identities (Cole, 1996; Polman & Miller, 2010; Nasir 2009). We seek to better understand t he ways that this relationship between youth and their elders is reci procal. While adults often support young people to project themselves and their possible identities into the future, young people also draw on the experiences of their adults to make sense of themselves in the present  and past. This kind of analepsis disrupts notions of identi ty development in that it offers a means of better see ing the development of identities across the timesc ales that impact both lived experiences and the experiences we draw upon from our familial and cultural pasts. Throughout the project, many of the young people  who engaged in the exhibit design  echoed similar ideas about the ways they thought about themselves and their families. As they worked to characterize themselves and their experiences within the city, they frequently drew on the immigration s tories of their families to make sense of themselves in their present worlds  (Holland et al., 1998). While these young people never stated it blatantly, their exploration of self in relation to  the city appeared to surface questions of historic ization in the United States (Vossoughi & Gutiérrez, 2016). The young people in our study chose to participate in a project that allowed them to voice their identities to a public audience. Navigating these identities in this setting meant that they were faced with also conceptualizing themselves as Latine in relation to the museum’s mostly white audience. As Nasir et al., (2020) explain, identity is intrinsic to culture. Culture, however, is always relational. In this study, the young people who designed the museum exhibit lo oked not only at their own experiences but at the experiences of their families to make sense of and to voice these cultural identities  in the face of systemic whiteness. Their historicizing was done on the shoulders of their elders; to define their identities, they drew not only on their own lived stories or possible futures, but also on the experiences of their families to make sense of their present selves.

This study was conceptualized with the expectation that educators and family members would share their own experiences with our designers. We expected that those narratives might shape the disciplinary identities that young people took forward in relation to their participation and their identities as historical agents within museums in the future. However, we noted that not only were they engaging in prolepsis and taking up their own experiences or relying on the adults in their lives to carve pa ths for these identity trajectories (Polman & Miller, 2010), they were also engaging in analepsis building notions of  their identities in the present. In attending to race, class, gender, etc. alongside their elders and their stories, our participants had opportunities  to better understand themselves as historical actors in their present worlds (Gutiérrez et al., 2019).

## Significance

While learners’ identities have been frequently examined as connected to trajectories or pathways toward possible futures, less is known about how identities are als o developed in relation to stories grounded in comm unal histories. This paper adds the notion of analepsis to the literature around identity development (e.g., Nasir et al.,

2020) and youth as historical actors (Gutiérrez, 2019) to provide deserved attention to the complexity of the ways

identities are tied to cultural and familial storie s. As the research community increasingly points to th e need for understanding identity development in the context of culture, the notion of analepsis can support observations of the historicizing that young people do in relation to their families. This is pa rticularly important as we seek to understand the ways that young people of color engage in writing themselves into the world. Analepsis, as a tool alongside prolepsis, allows for a more nuanced desc ription and understanding of the ways that young pe ople contend with their own experiences and contextualize  the everyday in relation to their ancestral pasts.  In our current reality, while children are being repeatedl y torn from families  via bombings and border walls, this kind of understanding can lead us toward the creation of  educational spaces that not only honor young peopl e’s identities but engage them in imagining their presents and futures as deeply connected to their familial pasts.
