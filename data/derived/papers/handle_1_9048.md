# Infrastructuring for Justice: Reframing Scale Through a Rightful Presence Framework

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
A challenge inherent in infrastructuring for justice lies in how existing structures and foundations that anchor education and educational reform enterprises are rooted in White Supremacy and heteropatriarchy, given their historical dominance in the disciplines, schooling and society. Drawing from justice-oriented studies of reform, and rightful presence centered participatory design-based research in long-term research+practice partnerships (RPP), we argue that infrastructuring for justice-oriented reform is contingent on scaling allied, sociopolitical activity systems in schools. Infrastructuring for justice requires on-going resistances, disruptions and transformations of existing structures and foundations that anchor educational research and reform. The objects of reform need to shift to the scalability of new relationalities from the scalability of products, tools, and ideas. Such new relationalities should be viewed as the new foundations for infrastructuring reform, with attention paid to the political clarity necessary for the conditions that make such justice-oriented reform possible.

## Introduction

Justice-oriented studies of educational reform crit ique how current organization of educational infras tructures erase the experiences and practices of children/you th and communities in favor of schooling and discip linary knowledge. A challenge inherent in infrastructuring for justice lies in how existing structures and foundations that anchor educational research/reform enterprises are rooted in White Supremacy and hetero-patriarchy, given their historical dominance in the disciplines, schooling and society. While it might be possible to infrastr ucture by building on technologies and practices that already exist (Bødker et al., 2017), the work entailed in infrastructuring for justice lies in the “redesign of components, relations, and routines” (Penuel, 2019, p. 659) towards disrupting injustices reproduced by existing systems and struc tures. We contend that systemic injustices are wide spread, historical and enduring. Systemic injustices are not homogenous but rather, are made manifest via contextualized, local practices normalized as benign. Before we can infrastructure for justice, identifying such normalized unjust practices and structures – what they are, how they work, how they might be disrupted, whoneeds to be involved and in what ways – is necessary. Building on theories of critical justice and the rightful presence framework for teaching and learning (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020), in this paper we argue that infrastructuring for justice is contingent on engaging in sustained, allied, sociopolitical activity systems in schools. Infrastructuring Educational Innovation What and when is infrastructure?

Infrastructuring for educational innovation has become an important focus of reform efforts. As a theo ry useful for helping the field make sense of educational innovations – including their design, enactments, spread and scale

– infrastructuring has become a useful lens for designing for conditions necessary for reform to take hold (Penuel,

2019). Penuel (2019), drawing upon Star (1999), describes infrastructuring in education as the coordination of the components of systems of practice, such as standards, curriculum, assessment, policies and organizational routines, that educators, school leaders and researchers coll aboratively use to accomplish their work. Such coor dination brings these varied components into relation with each other with directionality or intention. Thus, it accounts for the systems that are already in place that shape practice, and the possibilities for sustaining it. Infrastructuring, or a lack thereof, also helps to explain why, even when innovations are successful, they often do not last beyond the “hothouse” conditions under which they are typically introduced (Penuel, 2019). A critical part of infrastructuring for sustained i nnovation involves redesigning infrastructure in wa ys that support the goals of innovation. For example, Hall et al. (2021), in their efforts to design for coherence in the implementation of the Next Generation Science Stand ards, across three crucial networks – mentor teache rs, district specialists and assessment specialists – show that infrastructuring redesign involved not only identifying useful resources and frameworks related to the NGSS, but also fostering collegial workspaces for shari ng experiences, challenges and needs as forms of productive professional learning. Such redesign work can be fraught with tensions, however, especially when innovations  seek to disrupt inequalities. Studies have shown t hat this friction often results from both the efforts to des ign for and insert missing components of infrastruc tures, ICLS2022 Proceedings 353 © ISLS especially in contexts historically underserved by society’s social structures, and also in the negotiation how these new components work within coordination with other components. Equitable implementation of innovation requires a more “proactive stance toward infrastructure redesign,” by “allocating more and targeted resources to schools and teachers than might be provided to schools with more privileged students” (Penuel, 2019, p. 670). Prost et al. (2019) report on one community-univers ity partnership sought to infrastructure for food democracy. They demonstrate how the partnerships efforts initially focused on designing a food hub, a missing components of the local food infrastructure, then shifted to redesigning how the food hub worked in coordination with other structures, through establishing new coalitions and new spaces for connecting with community around the food hub, and designing new practices for how the food hub use could expand in the community. Like Hall et al., (2021), relationship building across networks, including spaces for doing so, were critical to integrating new components into existing structures. However, integration efforts were disrupted when differences arose over the direction of the project between the baker, who wis hed to focus on expanding their food hubs, versus t he community center, who wished to improve the prototypes’ operation through engaging the local community more deeply. These differences reflected different priorities and social positionings within the infrastructuring efforts. This kind of tension is similar to the challenges that teachers in a participating school faced when their reform-oriented science lessons were observed by district leaders using a routine observation framework (Penuel, 2019). This framework did not recognize the inquiry  work required of such reform, and some participati ng teachers received low scores. This friction made vi sible the different priorities of different stakeho lders as they sought to integrate educational innovation into existing structures. RPP members redesigned the infrastructure to make space for new visions of what classroom teaching could look like, for example, by creating new resources that linked the routine observation framework to inquiry and by creating new routines involving district leaders to leverage these resources in coaching science teachers. Such negotiations involved developing new components of infrastructure, such as the new tools, as well as practices for when/how such components are enacted within a coordinated ensemble, such as how district leaders leveraged these tools in support of instructional improvement. Thus, infrastructuring is also about asking “ when”, in tandem with asking “ what” in the sense that infrastructures are dynamic interactions (Penuel, 2019). Of concern is how these components serve educators and students well more so than only whether such compon ents exist. When infrastructures are working well, they largely go unnoticed (Hall et al., 2021). However, since existing western schooling infrastructure is built to work for students of the dominant culture, there has bee n little need historically to trouble existing, fou ndational infrastructure, even as minoritized students have s imultaneously been historically underserved by said infrastructure. While the limited resourcing that make up the components of an infrastructure may be critical, the nature of the infrastructure itself should be called into question. We thus further conjecture that a central question should be “who” is infrastructure, and to whom is infrastructuring useful? Whose interests does it serve? Who is infrastructure?

Asking the question of “who” calls into focus how i nfrastructuring is both embodied and historical. Wh at we mean is that teachers and students experience how systems of practice are coordinated toward particular goals and outcomes in the present, with the materials, tools and approaches legitimized by a system that is root ed in Whiteness and heteropatriarchy. Justice-oriented re form cannot succeed if the very structures in which  such reforms are enactment are themselves not called into question.

We find urgently important that infrastructuring shifts attention from the portability of materials, tools and ideas for the sustainability and scale of innovation to “a kind of mangling of innovations within varied educational contexts.” Yet, mangling of innovations within entrenched structures, even when such efforts seek to redesign components of these structures, do not explicitly or even necessarily call into question the histories of practice and of people that live within, sustain and benefit from existing structures. To seek social innovation without the transformation of existing structures leads to bounded justice (Creary, 2021). We came to ask this question of who is infrastructure, through our decades-long RPP involving schools, community organizations, and universities. As we sought to infrastructure for social innovation in classrooms and schools, we were confronted with the same kind of f raught tensions noted by Penuel and others, making visible fundamental differences not only in the priorities of the social innovations and the existing schooling structures, but also in the underpinning ideologies. Competing priorities tied to differentially powered stakeholders produced injustices that unfolded in micro-moments, played out through normalized school practices. Surfacing such root causes of injustices is challenging because they ar e hidden as the supposed benign structures of schoo ling. To make visible such structurally normed, unjust practices, we turn to the rightful presence framework. The rightful presence framework was informed by emp irical data from long-term research-practicepartnerships (RPPs) in middle schools and local com munities across two states. We sought to have a dif ferent way to make sense of when and why innovation led to  more just and sustained outcomes, and when it did not. ICLS2022 Proceedings 354 © ISLS Three, imbricating tenets undergird this framework:  1) Political struggle is integral to disciplinary learning through asserting the right to reauthor existing ri ghts (mired in injustices that are normalized); 2) Exposing injustices and imagining new, just, possibilities are germane to rightfulness; 3) A collective disruption of norms are necessary and contingent on allied political struggle demanding engagement from the powered and oppressed. In short, we learned that new sociopolitical activity systems are needed in order to infrastructure for justice. They involve a range of stakeholders who collectively acknowledge unjust powered dynamics that have always been at work in schooling, who work together to perturb such dynamics towards more just ends. With the rightful presence framework, infrastructuring for justice brings into focus not only the when of infrastructuring, but the who of infrastructuring, as related to troubling established practices in schools related to teaching, learning and being. Such modes of transformation necessitate taking up new practices, with stakeholders (teachers, students, school staff, administrators, community members) taking on new roles, that has implications for how existing roles and practices might shift to transform who and what schooling is for. Infrastructuring for justice Justice-oriented infrastructuring seeks to redress injustice by creating new conditions to support inn ovative and political organizations, resistances, and transformations within systems (Bang, 2020). Deepening the d efinition of infrastructure in this way questions the dominan ce of the power-holders who built structures of pra ctice and interaction (Dantec & DiSalvo, 2013). Infrastructuring to redress injustice must create new conditions to support innovative and political organization and disruptio ns within systems (Bødker et al., 2017). To take se riously a political view of infrastructuring questions the dominance of the power-holders who built structures o f practice and interaction. It opens up possibilities for cons idering porosity, movement, and transformation to a llow for democratized actions and redistributions towards ideals of justice. Infrastructuring, thus, does more than mangle new innovations with existing infrastructure of systems. It offers new tools and access points into the system for critiquing foundational systemic assumptions and designs. Infrastructuring for justice provides key insights into how scale can be sought for with justice-oriented reform interventions. We present two key points.

1. A focus on the sustainability and scalability of new relationalities

New relationalities Infrastructuring for justice, with its focus on conditions necessary for scale and sustainability, calls into question the object of reform. Infrastructuring for justice requires on-going resistances, disruptions and transformations of existing structures and foundations that anchor educational research and reform. We suggest that susta inability and scalability should focus on new relationalities – anchored in the Who and When of infrastructure – rather than on products, tools, or even ideas. Such new re lationalities should be viewed as the new foundatio ns for infrastructuring education innovation, with attenti on paid to the ethical and political clarity necess ary for the conditions that make such justice-oriented reform possible. By new relationalities we mean a practice of how we understand ourselves, others, and things, such as s tructures, in relationship to each other and to con text, in the present and historically, as mediated through power and possibility. This work requires careful attention to the power-m ediated tensions that arise as people, ideas, and histories come into contact, along with the critica l awareness and reorganization of power relations t hat necessarily results. It is a kind of political work  imbued with political clarity and relationship bui lding for cultivating critical awareness on racial and social  injustice (Madkins & McKinney de Royston, 2019), b ut also oriented towards action and accountability. This ki nd of justice-oriented praxis of infrustructuring r efuses separation of the past from the present and the possible future (Tuck, 2009). It also demands the kind of critically aware action taking that is linked to accountabilit y in the sense that local actions make history, and  have social and political consequence (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2019). New relationalities, anchored in rightful presence seeking allied sociopolitical struggles, are critic al to re-imagining how infrastructuring for justice shape s the trajectories of reform. Such a mode of engage ment reframes the work of infrastructuring away from the scalability of ideas, tools or products towards relationalities, which form the new foundations for building justice-oriented infrastructures. Scalability often hinges  on “something” – a tool, model, product (e.g., recommended “best practices”) – rather than on the interactions and relationships these products mediate among people and the broader organizational, political and social contexts in which reform happens. What goes to scale is desi gned to be flexible enough to translate across sett ings with the goal of broad reach. Scalability is deemed successful when such products are taken up by large numbers with fidelity. However, when a product is streamlined for transferability and scale, even when replicability is the goal, some features are reduced while others are amplified. What gets amplified inadvertently hews to the established norms operating in society, across institutions, as  teachers are inclined to approaches most familiar to their ICLS2022 Proceedings 355 © ISLS established practices, foregoing deeper and disrupt ive pedagogical shifts for surface enactments (e.g., bounded activities, classroom materials) of reform (Coburn, 2003). In school settings, such established norms are typically undergirded in White Supremacy and heteropatriarchy. Given the systemic nature of these norms, the “common denominator” to which scalability goals adhere are the established powered norms across settings. When scalability is dependent on maintaining unjust powe r dynamics across settings, scalability becomes a m eans by which unjust power dynamics are maintained and reproduced. We build on Coburn’s (2003) notion of the depth of scaling reform. We echo her assertation that bey ond taking up discrete reform products, belief syst ems that inform norms and practices need to be likewise shifted. When lensed with rightful presence, we contend that such deeper shifts occur via allied sociopolitical struggles as legitimate classroom and school activity systems. Infrastructuring is a long-term and future-orient ed process. It needs to account for the relational work that unfolds in participatory processes, as projects shift in direction, involve new people, and develop over time, as new power dynamics are struggled for collectively through the reauthoring of rights that amplify community voices and foster community agencies. Relational work takes shape in efforts to disrupt and transform institutional relations and practices, where the locus of power/o wnership shifts towards community. This happens, in  part, through evolving and expanding involvement of stakeholder in an RPP partnership, especially participants who have typically not been included, such as parents, staff from community partner institutions, youth, teachers, and the range of roles undertaken. Widening the range o f stakeholders and expanding the roles within stake holder profiles are the processes through which unjust power dynamics that have historically been at work in society and across institutions can begin to be disrupted. To identify such, researcher-embedded research practice partnership work is a robust way to unearth the forms and functions of normalized (and thus hidden) unjust practices. Enacting in practice: Vignette from our RPP Consider how middle school science teachers learned  how to become critical witnesses of youths’ STEM-related lives outside of the classroom, thus f orming new relationalities with youth and with STEM. A design decision we made as an RPP at the beginning of a 5-year project focused on designing tools for middle grades engineering, and that built groundwork for n ew infrastructuring work, was engaging in co-design  of reform-based classroom teaching and learning tools with teachers, youth and community educators in community spaces, using pedagogies of being with (Villenas, 2019). As we developed a teaching framework for engineering for sustainable communities with engineering design challenges, we spent time together, being with, weekly, after school, in STEM club spaces at partnering community  centers. We foreground being with  as justice-driven embodiments of mutualism and trust that occur along the powered-boundaries of race, class, gender, education, age, and geopolitical locations. Being with  is conceptualized as intentional social activity, involving critical relationalities and practices of witnessing that se ek to understand/disrupt normative power hierarchie s that precipitate inequitable processes of partnering, both in the here-and-now and towards imagined futures. Sessions took place 2-hour blocks, weekly, after school at the participating clubs, and involved activity structures that supported teachers, youth, communit y educators, and researchers in engaging STEM toget her, sharing ideas/stories, giving critical witness to others’ experiences, expertise, and hopes for STEM in their lives and communities, especially across difference, and co-designing teaching and learning tools that drew upon insights from dialogues. We spent time collaborativ ely enacting and reflecting on co-designed tools, w ith stretched out enactments in these afterschool spaces, allowing for maximal time for in-the-moment and ongoing refinement of tools from multiple perspectives. Thi s process supported collaborative construction of t eaching tools that were co-owned by teachers, youth, and community, and created new spaces for teachers and researchers to build relationships with youth and community mem bers, over time and through shared activities, host ed on community turf, thereby engaging in a version of intergenerational relations and learning. Two teachers, Ms. D and Ms. L, reflected on how the y saw their students “in their element” at a community-based STEM club instead of in their schoo l classrooms. Both teachers spent time as community members participating with youth at a community-bas ed STEM club that focuses using STEM as a tool and resource to address injustices in youths’ everyday lives. Examples of prototypes included a 3 foot, 50  piece geodesic playdome with solar-powered LED “reading lights” that two middle school girls built for their  friends in response to the lack of play structures at the c ommunity center. The teachers were “blown away” by the sophistication their students displayed through in building engineering prototypes to address community identified challenges. Ms. D marveled at “how creative the stu dents are when they have time” and their understand ing of community issues, which she opined she “would have never seen or known from interacting with them just in the classroom”. Both teachers asked, “How do we do this  in the classroom?” Explicitly inviting social conc erns as legitimate school-science concerns was not the norm  in Ms. D and Ms. L’s middle school. The lack of pl ay structures at a community club serving children as a STEM issue, a community issue and a justice-related issue (one youth said, “kids need to play, kids need to move!”) became apparent to the teachers as the project unfolded. It emphasizes teacher and youth participants as people who contribute to reform by bringing concerns to bear, situates knowledge production and practice within local contexts in ways that give witness to teachers’ and youth’s experiences, and contributes to the improvement of conditions for teachers and youth. By integrating the ideas and experiences of school and community educators and researchers with youth, more relevant and susta inable results arise for research, practice and loc al communities. Oliver (2001) writes, “We are obligate d to witness beyond recognition, to testify and to listen to testimony, to encounter each other, because subjectivity and humanity are the result of witnessing” (p. 90). 2. New political allyship towards rightful presence

Political allyship Infrastructuring for justice requires attention be paid to how innovations support new political allys hip towards rightful presence through making visible particular local injustices and possibilities. Infrastructuring work, then, is about making potential futures visible, debated, and acted upon. This includes focusing on how participants at various organizational and political levels (youth, parents, teachers, school leaders) are afforded ever expanding opportunities to act both in the present and in future activities, across spaces and scales of activity. From a rightful presence framing, scalability needs  to be twinned with sustainability, not replicabili ty, given the very local and contextualized nature of u njust practices that require disruption and reimagi nation. By sustainable we mean how insights and processes, as well as design results, may be continually activate d and expanded across scales of activity – time, settings (horizontal) and levels of power (vertical). If we consider how, in a justice-oriented RPP, sustainability needs to rest on communities, once justice-oriented infrastructuring is put into place, then communities should be sustainably supported to solidify new hybrid practices that cen ter community voice and desires. For example, when parents and teachers find a way of collaborating productively together (with solidified logistical and activity-b ased infrastructure), the “new practices” the RPP h ave seeded have a higher probability of becoming the new norms that are more justice-oriented and locally-grounded. Sustaining social innovation relates not only to how the intervention itself is appropriated in use, but also how participants at various organizational and poli tical levels (youth, parents, teachers, school lead ers) are afforded opportunities to act both in the present a nd in future activities. As new people work togethe r, across communities, and intergenerationally, and people no t ordinarily involved in reform design work togethe r, they expand their understanding and opportunities for action in relation to both people (e.g., the experiences, resources and understandings others bring and how they relate to them) as well as the design context itself (e.g., what the problem space is, how we work together to address i t). Penuel notes, “Interventions themselves are con tested spaces, filled with tensions and resistance from a range of stakeholders” (Penuel, 2019, p. 21). Foreg rounding relational design work as integral to scalability is important because it centers the messy, political, and sometimes contentious work of participatory reform processes, especially those processes meant to disrupt system ic forms of power. We see such relational design work – anot her form of allied political struggle between resea rchers, teachers and youth – as equivalent to building new justice-oriented foundations as a part of infrastructuring that disrupts White Supremacy. It is through such strugg les that ownership of the more just and new practic es is cultivated and deepened within the insiders of the community with power (e.g., teachers, school staff and administrators) that sustainability of the reform might develop and settle. However,  several weeks after implementing this unit, Ms. B discovered that some of her students were frustrate d that their designs were not actually being used t o address their community concerns. Students’ designs were meant to make their school happier, healthier and mor e just, while also leveraging green technologies in support of the environment. Mrs. B was an expert in teaching this unit she helped co-develop, and had taught it during three consecutive academic years. Consider for example, two groups who shared with Mrs. B and their classmates that their designs were not being used as they hoped. The Joy Box group cre ated their original design to be used in the school ’s social workers office as tool to support students navigating hard emotional times. The students worried their design was “useless” when they realized the school social worker could not use it because her office was too small. Another group wanted to support other students in having chances to have more ways to calm down when they created the Marble Drop game. This design was an innovative one-person game that supported players in completing a marble maze and lighting up the board to celebrate. However, the students noticed that it was not kept in the common library space like they imagined but was in a closet instead. While Ms. B could have considered the engineering d esign unit a success if she only evaluated the designs based on the required STEM goals, she inste ad gave critical witness to their concerns. She hos ted a conversation in STEM class on why the projects were  not working, and what they students wanted to do a bout that. First, she supported students in defining what the success criteria would be for their engineering designs by asking questions such as: “H ow can you tell if your engineering design is meeti ng your goal?”, “Who should be using the design?”, and “How often should the engineering design be used?”. Ms. B extended the unit so that students could refine their goals and designs. In response to their conversations, they created written directions for students to follow which included these two tips: “Fact: Hand crank can go both ways.” and “Fact: Do not take marbles.” For the Joy Box, Mrs. B supported the group to collaborate with school personnel to better address their goals. Students worked with the social worker to develop a social contract on how and when the joy box  would be used. The school community put the Joy Box to use as a result. Both students and staff placed positive mes sages in the hand crank-powered light-up Joy box we ekly. While in use, the group delivered the messages to each classroom while lighting the Joy Box. Students described having their “hearts feel warm” as the schoolwide positive message delivery system was used by the community. ## Discussion

Engaging in longitudinal, community-based RPP work has led us to three insights on infrastructuring for justice, imbricated with one another, and anchored in and contingent upon allied political struggles as core practices. First, we suggest there is a need to reconsider th e nature of infrastructure connectivity. Western schooling practices have artificially siloed what counts as legitimate school epistemologies and ontologies, reflecting White male, heteropatriarchal axiologies. Even when positively regarded by the field of STEM education, communitybased STEM experiences are positioned as adjacent to school STEM teaching and learning, in a supportive role to students where positive outcomes from these out-of-school experiences might be “supplementary” to students of color but the gains from such out-of-school STEM experiences, whether epistemological or axiological, fail to accrue mobility between the out of school infrastru cture into the school STEM infrastructure because o f conflicting axiologies. While “alternative” infrast ructures, such as out-of-school STEM programs house d in community clubs, are necessary for seeding new rela tionalities between youth, STEM and communities, infrastructuring symbiotic connections between yout hs’ STEM experiences out of school and in school it self, requires allied political struggle. Ms. D and Ms. L  were compelled by the expansive STEM epistemologies and ontologies they witnessed at the community STEM pro gram. They were simultaneously concerned about how differently school STEM is set up. What was most apparent to the teachers were the incommensurability, as they perceived, between in school and out of school STEM. This calls into question the nature of STEM educa tion infrastructure pertaining to what is valued and sanctioned in which activity systems that youth engage in STEM; and discerning the kinds of allied political struggles, involving which stakeholders, where and when, need to take place to support mobilities of resources and gains made between in school and out of school STEM infrastructure. Second, friction serves as indicators of progress when infrastructuring for justice. Aligning with th e rightful presence framework, infrastructuring for justice through allied political struggles is necessarily disruptive.

When students are reauthoring rights with the suppo rt of more powerful allies like teachers, they are disrupting normalized practices, replacing them with new ones that shift the epistemological, ontological and axi ological nature of school STEM. As described earlier, dissat isfied with the mere completion and tested function ality of their engineering prototypes, students’ insistence on buy in from the school to put their prototypes to use as they intended necessitated Ms. B’s recognition that she needed to do more to advocate for students’ hoped-f or outcomes with their projects. Such friction made visible the limitations of redesign, since “infrastructuring efforts must always wrestle with what is already there in place in systems—that is, existing infrastructures and practices that are products of past design efforts and that p ersist in their influence on practice” (Penuel, 2019, p. 662). At Ms. B’s school, none of the prior reform efforts we re concerned with student projects that impact comm unity identified problems, nor prioritized the potential of shifting social interactions as a result of the afterlife of these projects, as an important STEM learning outcome. The friction manifested in students’ frustrations to Ms. B, made ICLS2022 Proceedings 359 © ISLS visible such limitations and lent directionality and impetus to Ms. B to engage in further allied political struggle with the students that involved other adult stakeholders in the school and her RPP. Understanding that systemic oppressions in schools  operate through local practices calls into questio n the feasibility of scaling context-independent products. Traditional notions of scaling such supposedly applicable and thus highly mobile products therefore becomes a ntithetical to the justice reform agenda and in fac t, most likely works to reproduce injustices. Infrastructuring for justice flips the script on scale in its grassroots origins – when local, unjust, normalized practices are collec tively identified and interrogated via allied polit ical struggle through new activity systems as inherent to reform, the possibilities of enacting new, more just practices are higher and become an accountability measure of justice-oriented scale. Such a grassroots mechanism of scale has a higher and more authentic potential to “build in” ownership and therefore, sustainability.

## Conclusions

Infrastructuring work takes place in the complex in teractions arising from resistance and accommodatio n, disruption and revision that make up the relational  work of design. This relational work does not focu s on the design of the intervention itself, but on the conditions that make possible the sustainability of outcomes tied to the intervention, such as the very kinds of transformat ions of institutional relations and practices mentioned above. Time spent, in both quality and quantity, building new foundations is necessary for the political clarity needed for the relational work of sustained reform. Our stance  on justice-oriented infrastructuring with communit ies described here is informed by our decades of entrenched, empirical participatory design work. Being embedded thickly in context across time is a robust and ethical way of making sense of the necessary infrastructuring work at the different vertical and horizontal levels of power/political authority in schools/communities.
