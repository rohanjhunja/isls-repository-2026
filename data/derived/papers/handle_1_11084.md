# Capturing Futures: Mapping Youth Perspectives through Landscape Biographies

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Utilizing ArcGIS's QuickCapture app, we enlisted 16 high schoolers from Chicago's South Side to map their daily lives, creating landscape biographies intertwining spatial-physical environments with memories. This poster outlines our phased asset-mapping approach, embracing system-oriented and youth-centric perspectives. As we explore how landscape biographies influence youth engagement, our findings highlight the importance of amplifying young voices, paving the way for future exploration of the interplay between a community’s affordances and opportunities for learning within it.

## Introduction

“The central problem of an education based upon exp erience is to select the kind of present experiences that live fruitfully and creatively in subsequent experiences.” (Dewey, 1938, p. 17) Stemming from Dewey’s (1938) belief that all experiences are not equally educative, alongside Pinkard’s (2019) view of schools as one of many nodes in a community’s educational system, there emerges a need to make visible and accessible all of the educative nodes and meani ngful learning experiences in communities. In Chica go, Illinois, United States, communities’ learning opportunities are made visible through platforms like My CHI. My Future. Still, for residents, especially youth, there remai ns a mysterious essence about Chicago challenging t o capture, make sense of, and convey (Ewing, 2020). Seeking meaningful connections, many  youth turn to social media platforms to navigate and make sense of their realities. Through social media, young people are constantly employing the autobiographical act of posting their lived experiences, engaging in a form of digital storytelling that contributes to collective narratives and can shape one’s perception of themselves and their community (RubioHurtado et al., 2022). The desire to make communities’ out-of-school learning opportunities visible, paired with this embedded social media practice of youth capturing their everyday activities, prompted our design-based approach to explore how young people’s perception of their community affects how they experience it. Focusing our efforts in Chicago, where there has been a deca de-long commitment to ensuring equitable access to out-ofschool opportunities, we assembled a group of sixteen high schoolers across the South Side to map their daily experiences using ArcGIS’s QuickCapture (QC) app. T heir efforts culminated in creating StoryMaps of th eir lifestyle activities. We use this poster to advoca te for using landscape biographies as a vehicle for  showcasing youth perspectives and illustrate how capturing their present experiences can be an educative catalyst empowering fruitful engagement in learning opportunities in their neighborhoods.

## Methods

Sixteen African American youth, ages 14-15, from eleven neighborhoods across the South Side  Chicago participated in a study on how their understanding of their landscape influences navigation and engage ment in their communities outside of school. Recruiting primarily occurred through My CHI. My Future., with additional participants joining through a referral system that  rewarded existing participants with a gift card fo r each successful referral. Starting with 23 participants,  16 completed the program. Consent was obtained fro m parents and guardians, and data collection involved field notes, geo-captures, photographs, surveys, and artifacts over the six-week study. The aim of the program was to support y outh in understanding themselves through their use of their communities. This poster outlines our methodol ogical framework for developing landscape biographi es, which we define as representations of individuals' spatial-physical environments intertwined with personal and collective memories of those living or deceased. Construction of the landscape biographies was facilitated using geo-captures, making the use of a geospatial data collection app (i.e. ArcGIS’s QuickCapture mobile app) a critical component in our methodology.

Youth used the QC app to document where they spent t ime outside of school. We designed the QC app so that youth could categorize places of significan ce using one of the following predefined lifestyle verbs: play, make, practice, buy, work eat, hang out, feel, rela x, volunteer, learn, and other. After selecting the  verb that best represents their experience in a place, users captu re a picture or video  and annotate their geo-capture with reflective notes. Each saved capture gets etched in to their personal and the group’s collective map of  the city, which served as a referential and interpretive tool when later creating their landscape biographies. Our landscape biographies methodology was divided into three phases, using the following questions as guides:

• How can communities support youth in capturing and understanding the assets within them?

• Where do youth spending their out-of-school time and does this affect how they perceive the

opportunities available to them within their communities?

• How can youth create and showcase meaningful representations of their communities?

## Findings

Phase 1: Starting with a system-oriented perspective, we spent the first week havin g youth reflect on how they used their communities while taking them to places on walking tours and using bus routes  to improve their wayfinding skills and practice capturing places using the QC a pp. After two weeks of mapping as a group and becoming comfortable capturing places with the app,  youth were prompted to only use the app to capture  places they frequented outside of our structured programmi ng hours, Mon-Thurs, 9 AM-1 PM. Soon after, we began receiving more geo-captures beyond a three-mile radius of our daily meeting site. All youth we re provided with free public transportation passes to help alleviate freedom of movement barriers. Phase 2: Shifting to a youth-oriented perspective, we devoted our days to having youth reflect on places they captured, engaging in peer discussions to make  connections across different neighborhoods, and co-constructing stories that narrate these findings  using moments from their own lived experiences. However, we noticed that youth still weren’t capturing as many places as we expected they would have by the third week. To understand why not, we asked them to identify the Q C app verbs they found “confusing”. The verbs ident ified were: make, feel, relax, and volunteer. Whereas verbs that elicited no confusion were: play, practice, buy, eat, and hang out. To help youth define these verbs for themselves and their neighborhoods, we worked alongside them to create capturing challenges that were reflective of  their realities. We also intentionally organiz ed field trips to places they could have new experiences. For example, the most captures we received for “relax” was at a Stat e Park we went for a field trip. Pivoting to a model that allowed youth to capture places in their natural environments and expose them tonew ones, this methodological sh ift empowered them to share their  stories, fostering a new, collective devotion to capturing as many places as possible to honestly reflect their realities. Phase 3: Engaged in examining how they and their peers participated  in out-of-school activities across their communities, youth gained valuable insights into their lives by reflecting on their daily experiences captured on the QC app. Their diligent efforts to document their lifestyles allowed them to use their geo-captures to create landscape biographies, exemplified by their engagin g ArcGIS StoryMaps: 3 sisters navigat ing public transportation to go shopping at their favorite malls; 2 best friends highlig hting the culturally-relevant eateries around the South Side; 2 cousins crafting double date plans that send them all around Chicago; and 3 ladies from different neighborhoods collaborate to write a poem celebrating the "Soul of the South Side”.

## Implications

As we begin to make sense of how youth's perception of their landscapes influenc es their engagement in out-ofschool learning and leisure activities, our next st eps will focus on designing and refining how young people capture, reflect, and reimagine the learning experiences that unfold within their landscapes. By examining the complex patterns of individual learning experiences and community interactions, we aim to uncover nuanced ways youth navigate their neighborhoods and recogni ze and seize opportunities for learning outside of school. This progression towards documenting learning biographies emerges as a logical next step in our commitment to ensuring equitable access to learning opportunities, promising deeper insights into the dynamic relati onship between environment, freedom of movement, and lifelong learning experiences.
