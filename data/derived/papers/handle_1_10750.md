# The Practice of Mathematical Restorying to Foster Healing for Prospective Elementary Teachers

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research has documented the prevalence of math anxiety and negative emotions related to school mathematics both in the wider population and specifically for elementary teachers. While scholarship has often treated math anxiety as an individual problem, we consider mathematical experiences to be socially and culturally constructed and intertwined with dominant storylines about mathematical competence. In this paper, we explore storytelling and restorying as a way to investigate and support the developing mathematical identities of elementary teachers. We analyze the stories a prospective elementary teacher tells about herself with respect to mathematics, how her stories shift over time as a result of her math methods course and the impacts of the practice of restorying on her views of themselves and of children. We find the process of rewriting their own mathematical stories can support prospective teachers to begin to imagine new mathematical possibilities for themselves and for children.

## Introduction

Mathematics is a human activity that involves emoti on, creativity, social interaction, personal histor ies, and relationships as well as concepts, representations,  and strategies. K-12 mathematics classrooms have not always welcomed the full human experience of mathematical activity, often valuing and rewarding traits such a s speed, accuracy with paper and pencil procedures, and memo rization, and thereby narrowing opportunities for s tudents to be seen as mathematically competent (Schoenfeld,  2014). Further e xacerbating this narrowing, the discipline of mathematics tends to be equated with intelligenc e, so that those who are positioned as mathematical ly competent in academic settings also gain status in society more broadly (Guti érrez, 2012). For those individuals whose ways of knowing and being fall outside the na rrow conceptions of mathematical competence that te nd to be valued in K-12 schools, mathematics classrooms can be spaces of marginalization and exclusion. The experience of being marginalized with respect to school mathematics can leave imprints that endure and are carried forward into people’s adult lives, sometimes leading to ongoing aversion or anxiety wi th respect to mathematics (Chang & Beilock, 2016). Research has documented the lasting consequences elementary teachers may suffer from their own past negative experiences with mathematics (Skultety et al., 2023). How teachers relate to mathematics themselves has consequences for what  they choose to do in  the classroom with children and for how they conceptualize mathematical competence (Hadley & Dorward, 2011) as well as for their own wellbeing. When researchers have investigated past negative ex periences with school mathematics for elementary te achers, the focus has tended to be on their anxiety (e.g. Ha dley & Dorward, 2011) or their mathematical content knowledge or mathematical knowledge for teaching (e.g. Hill, 2010). We take a different approach in th is paper, investigating the stories prospective elementary teachers tell about their own mathematical journeys and how their stories change over time as they explore dominant s torylines and begin to situate their own experience s within these storylines. We focus on how, with support fro m the design of their math methods course, prospect ive teachers revise their stories as they engage in new mathematical experiences and develop a deeper understanding of mathematical learning as socially and culturally  constructed. We find that opportunities to engage in this practice of story revision can foster a sense of he aling in terms of beginning to imagine new mathemat ical possibilities that had seemed out of reach.

## Theoretical perspectives

We conceptualize mathematical learning as taking pl ace within nested layers of social activity that af ford and constrain possibilities for individuals (Louie & Zh an, 2022; Zuniga-Ruiz et al., 2022). We see individual classroom experiences as shaped by local learning e nvironments, dominant storylines related to mathema tical learning, and the broader sociohistorical context (Gresalfi & Hand, 2019). For example, narrow concept ions of mathematical competence that emphasize speed and ac curacy remain prevalent in school mathematics (R. Gutiérrez, 2012), and are often carried into local learning environments with teaching practices such as giving timed multiplication tests. These types of practice s influence definitions and measures of competence as well as who is recognized as mathematically competent withi n the school context, shaping and constraining poss ibilities for who young people can become within a mathematics classroom. One way of examining mathematical identity is throu gh the stories people tell about themselves with respect to mathematics (Sfard & Prusak, 2005). Thes e stories are collectively shaped and dynamic, deve loping and shifting in response to experiences. We are particularly interested in the role of stories in limiting or expanding a sense of possibility; “when people only have acce ss to a single story —one that simplifies and flattens the complexity of human experience and excludes many pe rspectives from being r epresented—they can become constrained in what they imagine to be possible” (Thomas & Stornaiuolo, 2016, p.313). Dominant storylines that emphasize narrow conceptions of mathematical compet ence can function in this way, flattening the compl exity of mathematical activity and oversimplifying the stories peop le tell about themselves to binaries such as good a t math/bad at math (Yeh & Rubel, 2020). In this paper, we examine the practice of restorying as a process of “rewriting the dominant narratives that define us” (Penoi, 2023) to tell more complex and nuanced stories that are situated within the sociohistorical context. We are  interested in the ways restorying is intertwined w ith healing, investigating how the process of rewriting personal  stories about mathe matics can open new possibilities for prospective teachers’ views of themselves and their emerging teaching practice.

## Methods

This paper focuses on a math methods course (taught  by Mallika) that provided opportunities for prospe ctive elementary teachers to reflect on and reconstruct t heir own mathematical stories. Drawing from princip les of social design-based research (K. D. Gut iérrez & Jurow, 2016), the design of this teacher l earning environment attended to the ways the entire learning ecology me diated learning toward fostering expansive views of mathematical competence and cultivating positive re lationships with mathematics. C entral to the design was a focus on mathematics as a human activity and an emp hasis on relational aspects of learning. In additio n, prospective teachers had repeated opportunities to critically reflect on their own mathematical experi ences and identities a nd the ways these were shaped by dominant storyline s related to mathematics and mathematical competence. Throughout the course, they reflected o n the complexity of their own historias (Zuniga-Ruiz et al.,

2022) to deepen their understanding about who they are and who they seek to become as educators. Some key

elements of design included reflecti ng on personal experiences with mathematics, using videos  and readings to connect these experiences with dominant mathematica l storylines, doing mathematics together with a foc us on learning from each other’s mathematical strengths, and learning to notice and n ame students’ mathematical strengths. The aim across these design choices was to immerse prospective teachers in joint activities  grounded in dignity for all pe ople, providing teachers with new resources and too ls to disrupt dominant deficit-oriented discourses, structures, and practices and to rewrite their own mathematical stories.

## Data analysis

The primary data sources for this paper are reflective journals prospective teachers kept during their mathematics methods course, a reflective paper prospective teachers wrote at the end of their course, and follow up interviews during the first year of teaching. In taking an ass et-based perspective toward prospective teachers, we c hose to approach analysis from the lens of seeking to inves tigate and learn from prospective teachers’ develop ing narratives about who they are and who they might be come with re spect to mathematical teaching and learning (San Pedro & Kinloch, 2017). We first identified and discussed themes related to the stories teachers told about their own relationship with mathematics. Because of  our interest in restorying as opening new possi bilities, we focused on the stories teachers told that included negative mathematical experiences. A recurring them e across these stories was the ways collective mathematical experiences, alongside readings and discussions, he lped teachers question and revise their stories about themselves and about mathematics. We then selected one case for deeper analysis. This case was selected because it contained explicit discussion of mathematical traum a, suggesting a need for healing. We examined evidence of the practice of restorying, how the emerging stories this teacher told about herself related to dominant mathematical storylines, and any new possibilities that were created through restorying.

## Findings

During the math methods course she took as part of her multiple subject credential program, Lauren ref lected regularly on negative experiences she’d had with ma thematics in K-12 schools and thought about how these experiences impacted her educational journ ey. She wrote her final course reflection paper, in  which prospective teachers were invited to reflect on any aspects of their learning that were most impactful, on the imp ortance of teachers’ own mathematical identity. She began her paper by writing: How a teacher feels about math and themselves as a math learner will inevitably shape how they teach math in their classroom. I not so much learned, as dug back into and analyzed some trauma that I have experienced as a math learner th roughout my life and have reflected upon what that trauma has led me to believe about myself  and my own math abilities, as well as my criteria for being “smart” in math.

Lauren then discussed her past struggles with memorizing mathematics facts, describing her memories of “being laughed at by a peer during a 4 th grade timed multiplication tables test for using my  fingers to count.” She wrote about the impact this had, explaining that she woul d “shut down whenever I saw something with numbers, ” that she “even dropped pre-calculus my senior year of high school and took acc ounting instead” resulting in her “options for college applications being severely limited,” and the ways her math anxiety and avoidance continued into college and graduate school. Lauren described how she “realized in a discussion in this class tha t I did not consider the statistics that I did to analyze my un dergraduate research study math. I had categorize d them as science! Such is the depth of my trauma.” Throughout her writing, there is evidence both of t he negative impacts of Lauren’s past experiences (being laughed at, shutting down, dropping a course) and of her starting to rewrite her mathematical j ourney, beginning with naming that she “dug back and analyz ed some trauma” and going on to reflect on how her own negative perception of her mathematical abilities l imited her options and even influenced what she cou nted as mathematics. In this revision process, there is evi dence that Lauren is beginning to s ituate her own experiences within a larger sociohistorical context of narrow v iews of mathematics that are perpetuated in schools. Lauren then reflected on some shifts within herself and began to consider some new possibilities for her teaching: Throughout this course through the experiences of p erforming a strengths inventory on my students, reading articles describing different types of math “smarts”, digging into the interplay between procedure, conceptual understanding, and pr oblem solving, and doing math with my peers that involves many strengths, I have come to see and value many more forms of math intelligences and strengths than I did before the class, and even to see myself as a fairly capable learner and doer of math. […] The experience o f inventorying my own and my classmates’ strengths in math, and connecting them to my own students has been eye opening. I realized how narrow my unconscious classification of “strong” math students had been previously. This realization made me sad, because it made me think that if teachers go into the profession having a narrow vision for what it means to be smart at math, they will project this onto their students, identifying and praising in unspoken or spoken ways, only a few students’ math strengths, alienating and traumatizing students who have other strengths to the point that they might, like myself, label themselves as not smart at math and t hus limit their future academic and career pursuits.

Lauren’s reflection shows the ways she was in the process of revising her own mathematical story, coming to see herself and her students in new ways. She tied her practice of restorying to multiple aspects of the l earning environment in her methods course (strengths invent ory, readings, doing math together etc.). She quali fied her own mathematical abilities, describing herself as “ fairly capable,” but this still represents a revisi on of herself from “not smart at math” to someone who has some capabilities. We view this as evidence of an ongoing process of healing from her past mathematical experiences.

Throughout this reflection, Lauren linked her exper iences with dominant mathematical storylines that emphasize narrow measures of achievement. She also explicitly linked her own expanding ideas about mathematical competence with potential negative impacts on students of “alienating and traumatizing students” and limiting “future academic and career pursuits.”  She went on to write at length about different stra tegies she planned to use in her future classroom to create a “collabo rative and trusting math comm unity” where students “recognize their individual and group genius” as a way to counter dominant mathematical storylines tha t tend to emphasize narrow and individual conceptions of mathematical competence. Lauren closed her reflection paper by stating, “all of my ideas and ideals for teaching math are in t he pursuit of a bigger goal that I have, which is t o prevent and/or undo some of the same trauma about math in my future students that I experienced. I feel inspired and empowered by this course to do so.”  Throughout this paper, Lauren connected her trauma to narrow conceptions of what it means to be mathematically c ompetent, helping to shift her trauma from an indiv idual problem (e.g. of struggling to memorize facts) to one that was socially and culturally  constructed (e.g. mathematical competence being associated with speed). With the support of the design of her methods course, her past experiences became a resource to help her begin to disrupt dominant mathematical storylines that marginalize and exclude. For example, during her first year teaching, Lauren found ways to disrupt dominant deficit discourse by implementing new teaching practices such as conf erring one-on-one with her 5th grade students to help them identify their own mathematical strengths. We do not mean to suggest that disrupting entrenche d storylines is simple or straightforward, but instead that the pro cess of restorying can provide support toward this type of disruption.

## Conclusions and significance

In choosing to share Lauren’s story, we seek to ill ustrate the importance of attending to the ways pas t negative experiences can become resources for developing a critical mathematical consciousness and help to foster a sense of healing when prospective teachers have opportuni ties to rewrite their own stories in supportive lea rning communities. While there is evidence that Lauren is beginning to rewrite her story, we do not think of this process as complete. Just as developing critical consciousness is an ongoing process of interrogating dominant ideologies and critically reflecting on the ways those ideolog ies are carried through systems and structures and individual actions, we see mathematical restorying and healing as an ongoing process of learning that is never complete. For Lauren, being part of a community that invited crit ical reflection and offered resources for that refl ection (including new math experiences, math teaching strategies, readings and videos about dominant storylines related to mathematics, and reflective discussions with colleagues) was an integral part of the practice of restorying. This community was designed intentionally to offer space s of affirmation that could foster the rewriting of  dominant mathematical storylines toward more expansive and c ollective views of mathematics and mathematical competence.

We see mathematical restorying toward healing as a complex process that is rooted in personal histories and identities as well as in the broader sociohisto rical context. While in this paper we did not exami ne how restorying was connected to intersecting aspects of  identity such as race/ethnicity, class, gender, and linguistic background, we hope future research takes up some o f this complexity. For example, we think that gende r and gendered narratives about mathematical competence m ay be an important aspect  of the restorying process for Lauren, but since she did not speak directly about gender, it was difficult to see more than traces that raised more questions than answers. We intend to explore restor ying from an intersectional perspective in our futu re practice as teacher educators as well as in our research about teacher learning.
