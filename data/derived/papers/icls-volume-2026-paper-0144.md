# Building Groupwork Moment by Moment: A Preliminary Typology of Social Bids in Secondary Mathematics Classrooms

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Groupwork is often touted as a tool to support mathematics students to build on their own thinking rather than learning concepts by rote, yet supporting robust cooperative sensemaking is a difficult problem of practice. As part of a study on how teachers l earn to facilitate groupwork, we explored how students build a foundation for successful collaboration. Using the sensitizing concept of bids for connection, we analyze how cooperation is established and maintained by students in secondary mathem atics classrooms. We see this as adjacent to work on belonging and rightful presence, while bringing notions of sociomathematical norms into conversations about trust and social cohesion. Importantly, however, we focus on the interaction order rather than on social structures to see what we might add to anal yses of status and authority at an alternate scale. Preliminary findings include an initial typology of bids, and possible implications for facilitating groupwork in mathematics classrooms.

This study is part of a larger design-based research program in partnership with university researchers and a professional development organization aimed at providing career-long learning opportunities for secondary mathematics teachers in the Western U.S.  As part of this partnership, the research team interviewed and videotaped teachers in their classrooms and used the resulting video data to debrief with focal teachers and their colleagues. This analysis centers the recordings of focal lessons, including teacher PoV cameras and 360 cameras from select student tables. The broader research program centered teacher learning, with the most recent visits narrowing in on groupwork facilitation as an example of how teachers learn to teach responsively. 1 Relevant literature Part of answering questions about teacher facilitation requires understanding what students are doing in small groups. While significant research on small groups describes how broader social structures —and racial ized, gendered, or positional identities—mediate groupwork, for this level of analysis, we focus on the interaction order (Goffman, 1983)—details of face to face interaction that have their own internal logic at least partially analyzable independent of macro structures and timescales. Consistent with other scholars (e.g., Vanderstraeten, 2001), we argue that there are several reasons to consider the interaction order of the classroom in general, and a cooperative mathematics class in particular, an especially interesting case, largely because they call into question long-held assumptions about more generic interactions. For a start, before students can begin to work on math together, they must establish that they are  together. We generally assume that coming into physical proximity is enough to establish copresence (Goffman, 1963). In classrooms set up for groupwork, by contrast, students are seated facing each other as if ready for conversation. They are also discouraged from physically leaving the table. Thus, in this context something other than creating an F-formation (Kendon, 2010) must signal the start of an interaction. Furthermore, trust and social cohesion are established through myriad small choices (Garfinkel, 1963; Gottman, 2007), whereas classrooms generally dictate who you sit with and talk to, as well as topics of conversation. Our analysis thus focuses on microanalytic details. This focus on the interaction order — how students signal the start and end of an interaction, how topics are negotiated, and ultimately how smooth social functioning in keeping with a teacher’s expectations is accomplished—builds on work from the fields of sociology and gesture studies that considers how humans accomplish the groundwork for meaningful interaction (e.g., Kendon, 1985; Kendon & Ferber, 1973; Goffman, 1955; Goffman, 1963). For example, efforts consistent with preframe change negotiations (Kendon, 1985, p. 242) are captured in our coding as “pre-bids.” We also see students leaning heavily on other types of behavior, such as civil disattention  (Goffman, 1963) tonegotiate moving in and out of each other’s response presence (Goffman, 1983, p. 2).

To remain close to the data, we take a grounded theory approach (Charmaz, 2006), moving iteratively between coding data, developing emerging theory, and returning to the corpus to check preliminary findings. In the 1202 following sections, we describe our sensitizing concepts and initial rounds of coding. We are not yet in the phase of comparing our grounded theory to existing work in the field, though where appropriate we identify strands of literature that we expect wil l become relevant. Our ultimate goal is to make an argument about how  the work of social cohesion around collaborative mathematical reasoning is accomplished moment by moment between secondary mathematics students. The current task is to build grounded theory about what the students are doing. Further work will compare classroom-level snapshots to teacher-level analyses and broader timelines. Theoretical sampling The corpus from which these data were taken included 61 videotaped lessons with follow-up debriefs across ten years. After concerted reduction, we ended up with three focal lessons to represent a range of fluidity with groupwork across the corpus, listed here by teacher’s name 2 from least-to most-fluid: (1) Kasey3, who describes this class in particular as having a hard time with groupwork; (2) Sandy, who says in her interview that she only does “real” groupwork (by which she appears to mean something mor e like project-or problem-based learning lessons) a few times a year, but does encourage her students to work together repeatedly throughout her focal lesson; and (3) Lizette, whose debriefs routinely focus on getting the students to work together, to rely on themselves and each other as much as on her, and whose class is predominantly devoted to students working in groups. From three focal lessons, in this preliminary analysis, we focus on Lizette’s because her class was broadly agreed to be the most cooperative, with students working successfully in small groups for a significant percentage of class time. Based on this observation, we judged that an inductive codebook built from these groups was more likely to be able to cover the phenomenon than one from  the less successful classes. The next phase of the study will endeavor to describe the less successful groups using this taxonomy as a starting point. Preliminary coding Bids for connection as a sensitizing concept While debriefing her class with the original research team, one of the focal teachers (Sandy) alluded to John and Julie Gottman’s framework for communication between romantic couples, saying that maybe her students needed help understanding how to have a conversation with another person—a common refrain among teachers after the acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic during which many students were isolated from their peers. We took this to mean that for many teachers, students’ foundational social skills were  as much if not more in question than mathematical ones (cf. Mercer & Sams, 2006). Rather than beginning with education research, we chose to honor our participant’s contribution and followed Sandy’s line of reasoning. Specifically, we took up “The Science  of Trust: Emotional Attunements for Couples” (2011), and focused on the concept of bids for connection. Bids for connection worked as a sensitizing concept (Blumer, 1954) because the research team agreed that the metaphor, if not the specifics of intimate relationships, seemed apt for the social struggles of high school students. While the Gottmans’ framework does not engage with the literature from sociology and gesture studies described above, it echoes Goffman and Kendon’s work in looking more foundationally at conversational moves. Further, it has the advantage of being intuitively simple

4. People in relationships regularly make bids to connect

with one another, and those bids are responded to in one of three ways: the partner can turn toward, away, or against (Gottman, 2011). A partner’s response need not be enthusiastic to count as turning toward—all that is required is that the responder go with whatever the bidder was offering. A turn against is generally hostile, actively and explicitly shutting down a bid. In between, the turn away might be only subtly dismissive, as when the partner ignores a bid, changes topic, or responds unthinkingly (Lisitsa, 2012). Open coding This preliminary report focuses on all three student groups for which we had video records from Lizette’s focal class. As mentioned, Lizette’s class was chosen for initial coding because her focal groups were more consistently successful in working together cooperatively. Each group included three or four students who were present for the full 55-minute class. Each video was assigned to one author at a time. We built our codebook iteratively, comparing notes or points of confusion in regular meetings until we reached consensus, then combining codebooks, swapping videos, and re-coding until the coding stabilized. In general, we treated a bid for connection as an interactive accomplishment (Cobb et al, 1992), and did not code something as a bid unless it was taken up by someone else on camera, or repaired (e.g. if a participant apologized for taking the conversation in a different direction, we took that as evidence that they recognized there had been a bid to go another way and coded the previous utterance as such). What follows is a report of the codes we developed, with illustrative descriptions of the individual groups. Based on a single classroom, we conjecture that bidding for copresence 5 is an ongoing 1203 process in cooperative secondary mathematics classrooms, involving several layers of attunement to one’s classmates, and that this kind of bid is foundational to bidding for mathematical recognition. Unit of analysis In addition to the codes themselves, a preliminary finding of this work is that the unit of analysis in classroom interactions is far from obvious. We contend that table groups in mathematics classrooms experience multiple contacts (Goffman, 1983) within a single period, often when the teacher signals a change of focus (e.g., groupwork vs. whole class vs. individual think time), but also when the social momentum of the conversation has flagged and requires re-establishing. Number of contacts does not directly reflect copresence, however—both livelier and quieter groups may experience fewer separable contacts, the former because individual contacts have longer duration (better social momentum) and the latter because the group fails at establishing contact. In general, every contact began with some kind of bid, and was judged to be over only when a new contact was initiated. We used Episodes of Pedagogical Reasoning (EPRs) (Horn, 2005) and “situations for justification” (Cobb et al., 1992) as models for bounding a contact, though conversational topic  was not enough to provide continuity—often, copresence needed to be reestablished after two students had been collaborating and then split to work on their own papers, even if they later came back to discuss the same topic. Occasionally two contacts overl apped, such as when one participant started a new contact before the previous one was finished, though this was taken to be the exception that proved the rule as evidenced by social repair (e.g. apologizing/absolving) when this happened. Pre-bids Many, if not all, students engaged in a behavior that was outside of our original sensitizing concept, which we came to call “pre-bids.” These were nonverbal gestures or postural changes that appeared to offer readiness to engage without being true bids. Examples include stretching while looking past group members, tapping a pen while looking around, or regularly applying lipgloss or drinking from a water bottle —gestures that were not directed at another person, but nevertheless brought the gesturer into a posture where they would most certainly notice someone trying to start a conversation. Pre-bids were outside our coding rules because they were not interactively accomplished — many did not receive recognition from their peers, and peers never apologized or otherwise attempted social repair in these instances. Thus the category is still ill-defined. Even so, these gestures were clear to us as being “on the way” to a bid and their presence or absence may begin to explain why certain groups connected more or less smoothly. Future rounds of coding will further refine this category, though we are thinking of pre-bids as a type of facework (Goffman, 1955) and expect to relate them to work from gestural studies, including “preframe change negotiations” (Kendon, 1985, p. 242) mentioned earlier. Overt bids The most unambiguous category was overt bids—an explicit address, usually phrased as a question and aimed at a specific person or group, such as asking a tablemate what they got on #2. Certain students produced more or even most of these types of utterance; one student was even described by researchers as sounding like he had been fed sentence stems about how to do groupwork.  Interestingly, overt bids appear to be more common in groups that are compliant but less copresent (cf. Chapman & Metts, 2025). Implied bids More commonly, however, students relied on what we came to call implied bids —turning a notebook or laptop toward another student, or leaning over to look at someone else’s paper. Implied bids were largely gestural or postural in nature. This surface similarity might suggest that implied bids are merely an extension of pre-bids. On the contrary, in these preliminary groups, implied bids are only employed once a group has gained social momentum—students are less likely to slide a laptop to a peer, or lean over to look at someone else’s paper, in a group that has not established a base level of sustained cooperation. Provocations While implied bids were subtle and p re-bids did not appear to require a cknowledgement, explicit address — especially when phrased as a question—generally did provoke a response. In between was a category we called a provocation—not quite a direct address, but clearly something that elicited  a response (and was likely meant to)— such as when a student  said “Three! Oh, you need to do like, oh. So what is that? 13 minus 10” at a groupappropriate volume, but while looking only at their own paper. Tone and volume were instrumental aspects of  a provocation, such as a mock-confident declaration; such statements were often taken as a playful challenge. 1204 Mathematical vs social bids While we began with the Gottmans’ framework, as yet we have not categorized bids in our data as toward, away, or against. In part this is because the focal groups in this analysis were almost entirely successful, possibly because no one in this class was overtly dismissive of their peer’s attempts to engage. It is also due to the added construct of pre-bids—what might look like a bid that went unanswered, but did not require social repair, was categorized as a pre-bid rather than a bid followed by a turn aw ay. On the other hand, we have chosen not to code toward, away, or against rather than claiming that all responses were "toward" largely due to the question of content. The original relational framework relies on the assumption that underneath all communication between couples is a tacit questioning “do I matter to you and can I trust you to be there for me?” On this assumption, the content of the bid is effectively neutral—all that matters is whether the partner responds to the underlying bid for human connection. By contrast, in our previous work with these data, we noticed that the social warmth of a teacher’s response does not always track with —indeed can even be ortho gonal to—the underlying treatment of the mathematical contribution (Chapman & Maria Castillo, in press). In other words, asking “do I matter to you as a human being?” and “do I matter to you as a mathematical contributor?” may be noticeably different questions with consequentially separable answers.

We find distinctions at the student level between social bids and mathematical bids, though comparison with other examples from the corpus will help us speculate about the generalizability of this pattern. For example, in this subsample, many more social bids were provocations rather than questions, and social provocations appeared to have more emotional salience than mathematical ones. It may also be that social bids are a necessary precursor to concerted cooperative sensemaking in mathematics. In this cla ss, at a table that split generally into two separate groups (boys vs girls), several of the cross-pair bids that eventually led to mathematics were initiated by social provocations, such as a teasing statement about a non-participant. Even so, whether thi s is due to their content or form is not clear.

Bigger picture This short report presents the early stages of grounded theory development from student-level data in secondary mathematics classrooms. We sampled from well-supported classes with experienced teachers who were dedicated to groupwork, but had varying degrees of success with collaborative mathematical sensemaking. Beginning with the sensitizing concept of bids for connection (Gottman, 2011), we are building theory about the moment-tomoment negotiations that create a foundation for robust groupwork. As we move from these cooperative groups to some that were less successful, we are curious whether we will find turns away or against, as Gottman sees in couples, or instead a different concentration of bids and pre-bids. Beyond the descriptions developed through open coding and axial coding, next steps include comparing our emergent theory to existing literature (Charmaz, 2006). In addition to the literature on groupwork in mathematics classrooms (e.g. Langer-Osuna, 2016), we expect to turn to literature from sociology and gesture studies mentioned previously, and concepts of disciplinary belonging or rightful presence (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2019), as well as sociomathematical norms (Yackel & Cobb, 1996). Further, while t his preliminary study is focused on microanalysis of student groups, we acknowledge that successful cooperative groupwork is not based purely on student contributions, but is more ecological (Bronfenbrenner, 1994). As such, outcomes of the process reported here will ultimately be put in conversation with our teacher-level data (Chapman & Maria Castillo, in press). We contend that teacher habits around responding to noncanonical contributions may provide important models for student bids for making meaningful contributions in groupwork.
