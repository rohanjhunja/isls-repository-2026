# How CS Educators Can Mobilize Knowledge from PD Experiences: Building Bridges Between Research and Practice

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how PreK–12 teacher leaders facilitate knowledge mobilization (KMb) in computer science (CS), acting as brokers between research and classroom practice. Based on interviews with 20 U.S. teachers, it highlights how professional developme nt (PD) supports teachers in applying research, integrating emerging technologies, and navigating systemic barriers. Findings emphasize the importance of positioning teachers as active agents who use, share, and co-construct knowledge, and recommend participatory, iterative PD models that foster collaboration and shape the broader direction of CS education.

Bridging the gap between research and classroom practice requires rethinking how knowledge is created, shared, and enacted across educational communities. Aligned with the 2026 ICLS theme, “Partnering with Purpose,” which emphasizes cross-sector collaboration, this paper examines the practitioner’s role in translating research into practice. Rapid technological change in computer science (CS), including generative AI, heightens the need for PreK–12 educators to engage with research that informs teaching, drives innovation, and prepares students for the future (Code.org et al., 2024; Ottenbreit-Leftwich et al., 2022), yet challenges persist due to differing perspectives among policymakers, educators, and researchers (Sentance, 2021). Drawing on Levin (2013), we frame knowledge mobilization (KMb) as a social process that spreads research through networks and emphasizes multi-directional, co-created knowledge, contrasting with efforts that rely on a “push” model, where researchers control what knowledge is shared, sidelining teachers’ expertise rather than adopting a “pull” approach centering classroom needs (Révai, 2022; Rycroft-Smith, 2022; Torres, 2022). Ecosystem-based models and Research –Practice Partnerships (RPPs) support knowledge exchange by linking schools, universities, and industry to co-design research-informed practices (Falkner et al., 2018; Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Friend et al., 2022). In CS education, where formal degree pathways are limited for K-12 practitioners, professional development (PD) is critical for preparation and access to innovations. Yet PD quality and accessibility vary: many programs prioritize content over pedagogy, lack theoretical grounding, or serve external agendas, while systemic structures and epistemic differences complicate translation (Darling-Hammond & McLaughlin, 2011; Falkner et al., 2018; Cole et al., 2023). Effective KMb requires intentional, collaborative efforts that make research relevant, actionable, and co-constructed with teachers, to integrate evidence with practitioner experience in meaningful PD contexts (Dragni ć-Cindrić & Fusco, 2025; Levin, 2013; Opstoel et al., 2024).

We believe that, considering the contrast between the dynamic landscape of CS education and the systemic structures of traditional schools, the lens of KMb can help us better understand how teachers develop content and pedagogical knowledge outside of a school environment (e.g., in interaction with members of the research community) and bring it to practice. Therefore, our research question explores: What can we learn from teachers' experiences in CS education PD programs about how research knowledge can be mobilized into practice?

This study used phenomenological methodology to explore the lived experiences of educators and capture the essence of their professional learning in CS education (Creswell, 2016; Moustakas, 1994). Semi-structured interviews were conducted via Zoom with 20 PreK –12 teachers in March and April 2024. Participants were recruited through professional networks and included PreK-12 and Career Technical Education (CTE) teachers in the U.S. Throughout recruitment, we monitored saturation to confirm that data collection had reached sufficient conceptual depth and to reduce the likelihood of overlooking important themes (Saunders et al., 2018). Twenty educators (n=13 females and 7 males), from 16 states who taught across different grade levels and content areas participated. Using a thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006), interview transcripts were initially 2752 coded using inductive coding to identify themes, examine commonalities and differences across participants’ experiences in various PD programs, and iteratively refine emerging patterns across accounts and themes in relation to KMb (Levin, 2013) to gain deeper insights and reconsider the framework’s relevance to the data. Teachers’ pathways into CS education are shaped by intersecting policy, structural, and institutional forces that often constrain their preparation and practice. Policy changes, such as new CS graduation requirements, have accelerated demand for qualified teachers, “thrusting” several educators from other disciplines into CS roles with minimal formal training. As a result, many discussed pursuing additional certification and PD on their own— investing significant time and resources—yet still report feeling o nly slightly ahead of their students. Participants described persistent tensions around curriculum, noting that externally developed materials often fail to align with classroom realities or keep pace with the rapidly evolving nature of CS. Teachers emphas ized the need for more flexible, relevant, and inclusive resources, particularly for younger learners, multilingual students, and those with diverse needs. At the same time, they expressed a strong interest in integrating CS across subject areas, but pointed to structural barriers such as rigid schedules and accountability pressures that limit collaboration and interdisciplinary planning. While partnerships with universities and researchers offer opportunities for innovation, many teachers found the resulting curricula difficult to implement without significant adaptation. They emphasized the importance of being involved earlier and more meaningfully in the design process, underscoring the need for more collaborative, practice-informed approaches to developing CS education materials and supports.

Our findings highlight the complex, evolving nature of professional learning in CS education amid rapid technological change. PD serves as a key site for mobilizing and transforming knowledge, yet teachers often navigate it without clarity on what is research-informed. Early adopters of technology are frequently pushed into CS teaching and leadership roles, acting as knowledge brokers in their schools and districts. Participants drew on professional associations, RPPs, and industry collaborations  to inform their practice, but noted that many PD initiatives fail to explicitly connect research to classroom implementation, leaving translation largely to teachers themselves. Some participants expressed uncertainty about what counts as research and how  it should guide instruction, highlighting ongoing epistemic divides between researchers and practitioners (Opstoel et al., 2024; Rycroft-Smith, 2022). Teachers often piece together knowledge from universities, industry, and personal exploration to design curricula suited to their students, emphasizing collaboration, mentorship, and cross-institutional networks as essential supports. Effective PD, the study suggests, moves beyond treating teachers as passive recipients, instead fostering experiential learning, reflection, and co-development of resources, reaffirming PD as both the bridge and the workspace where teachers actively co-create research-informed practices in CS education.

## Limitations

Drawing on U.S. teachers’ perspectives, this study offers detailed insights into their experiences with CS-focused PD. While the U.S. context highlights state policy and certification variability, it limits generalizability internationally. The sample provides depth but is not fully representative across grade levels or demographics, and few participants had experience with research-focused programs (e.g., RETs or RPPs). As qualitative research, the findings are illustrative of systemic challenges, providing nuanced insights to guide future studies. Evidence underscores the value of positioning teacher leaders as active contributors rather than passive recipients of PD. Levin’s (2013) KMb framework illustrates how teachers act as knowledge brokers —adapting and coconstructing research-informed practices through collaboration with researchers, industry, and professional communities.
