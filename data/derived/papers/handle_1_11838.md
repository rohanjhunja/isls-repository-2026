# Becoming an Algorithm: Girls Develop Expansive Computing Practices Through Collective-Embodied Computational Thinking

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Stereotypes in computing often deter underrepresented groups, particularly girls, from entering or staying in the field. This study explores how collective-embodied computational thinking (CT) can broaden perceptions of computing. We introduced a four-day curriculum featuring an embodied mixed-reality environment where youth collaboratively play-acted sorting algorithms. Using mixed-methods analysis—including word embedding, a WEAT test, and qualitative analysis of interviews—we examined shifts in students’ perceptions. Findings reveal that girls moved from technical to introspective, creative computing descriptors after the activity, while boys’ views remained largely unchanged. Interviews indicate that girls linked computing to everyday experiences, suggesting that collective embodied CT can highlight cultural assets and disrupt prevailing stereotypes in computing education.

Challenge Computing communities grapple with diversity and in clusion issues (Margolis & Fisher, 2002). The estab lished computing practices (Rodriguez & Stevens, 2023) and stereotypes (Cheryan et al., 20 09) make it difficult for women to develop a computing identity and feel like  they belong, even more so when other racialized id entities are at play. An extensive body of literature examin es methods for promoting equity in computing by imp roving access and demonstrating the value of computing practices for u nderrepresented groups such as girls (Pinkard et al., 2017; Litts et al., 2021; Vakil, 2016). However, focusing solely on access is insufficient. Marginalized students who enter these communities often encounter the not ion that belonging requires them to exhibit traits such as logical thinking, emotional detachment, and a narrow focus-characteristics predominantly associated with males (Ensmenger, 2015). One approach that has emerged as  a transformative force capable of pushing the limi ts of computational practices is computational thinking (CT). CT is a human abili ty applicable to various disciplines and emphasizes diverse problem-solving skills, offering a more inclusive approach to computing (Grover & Pea, 2013; Kafai & Proctor, 2022). Research connects CT to students' whole selves, valuing students' bodies (Sung et al., 2017), creativity (Kafai et al., 2014), everyday experiences (Eisenberg, 2003), and personal relevance (Rankin et al., 2020). However, just broadening computing to CT is not enough. We must expand practice and culture by incorporating a more comprehensive range of modalities into the instructional process.

## Significance

CT, which stresses a range of problem-solving skills that go beyond coding, can be a more inclusive path into the world of computing. (Grover & Pea, 2013; Kafai & Proctor, 2022). Collaborative embodied activities may further support access and retention by addressing social a nd cultural barriers to the field, building on stud ents’ cultural assets and bodily knowledge while facilitating collective understanding of abstract concepts (Danish et al., 2015; Enyedy et al., 2012; Tu et al., 2023), which are fundament al to CS. We suggest that by expanding computing practices and elevating Funds of Knowledge (FoK) and multiple ways of knowing within computing experiences, we can make sense of and expand learners' perceptio n of computer science (CS) and foster a sense of belonging in underrepresented communities, particularly girls. Our goal is not to impose specific perceptions but for girls to feel their identity overlaps with computational ide ntity. This study and the following research questi on (RQ) examine how collective-embodied CT can support an expansive perception of CS and its practices. RQ:   How does engaging in collective-embodied CT impact girls' perceptions of CS practices?

## Theoretical framework

Perception related to culture and practice As initially observed by Margolis & Fisher (2002) and reaffirmed  in more recent studies (Cheryan et al., 2017), computing education continues to grapple with issues of access and equity, as students’ perceptions of computing fields heavily influence their participation in rel ated educational opportunities. Notably, women encounter significant social barriers in computing fields, hindering their ability to pursue computer science pathways freely. Proponents argue that inaccessible teaching methods, male-dominated cultures, and discriminatory practices contribute to the high attrition rates observed in computing fields (Lagesen, 2007). Underrepresentation of women perpetuates further underrepresentation (Boucher & Murphy, 2017), with stereotypical views trickling down and influencing middle and high school students on thei r beliefs about what it means to be a computer scientist (Cheryan et al., 2009). This “fundamental image problem” prevents girls and other underrepresented groups from exploring CS (Martin, 2004). Scholars have shown that youth hold  strong perceptions of computing fields, often originated from media, including that computer scientists are geeky and technology-obsessed (Cheryan et al., 2009; Margolis & Fisher, 2002), the content is difficult or boring (Yardi & Bruckman, 2007), and the practices are solitary or isolated (Guzdial, 2008) and dissociated from communal goals like helping so ciety (Diekman et al., 2011). Computing initiatives addressing these social and cultural barriers help break this underrepresentation cycle (Anton & Wilensky, 2019; Eisenberg, 2003; Kafai et al., 2014). Funds of knowledge and multiple ways of knowing Students’ lived realities shape their perceptions of computing and, in turn, develop their ways of approaching and utilizing CT practices. Accordingly, there is ample literature that underscores the rich lived experiences that FoK learners bring to sensemaking (González et al., 200 5, 2011). With computer science specifically, Guzdial (2008) claims that novices or non-CS students might encounter difficulties when learn ing to program because it doesn’t build on their natural way of thinking and talking. Therefore, we ought to allow students to develop their thinking skills and programming skills while connecting them  to their FoK and culture. Contextualizing and situ ating learning experiences within their FoK can meaningfu lly broaden participation and extend engagement bey ond classroom settings (Kafai et al., 2014). Early exposure to computer science, role models, and familial connections (Margolis & Fisher, 2002; Rankin et al., 2020) further expand these formative experiences to be integrated as new FoK and ways of knowing. Acknowledging diverse ways  of knowing is essential for engaging learners' FoK. Contemporary computing scholars critique the field for its limited work designing fo r underrepresented communities to share their FoK and ways of knowing and highlight the positive impact when computing initiatives, tools, and communities are created for  and with underrepresented learners (Lee & Soep, 20 16; Shaw et al., 2023; Vakil, 2016). Although these asset-based frameworks aim to disrupt the dominant and de ficit narratives girls face in computing education and ST EM broadly (Wofford & Gutzwa, 2022), they overlook the significance of the body and other FoK in shaping g irls' sensemaking and perceptions of disciplinary l earning (Litts et al., 2021; Solomon et al., 2022).

Embodied-collective CT Recent research on CT shows promising outcomes for body movement's role in enhancing abstract computin g concepts (Brady et al., 2016; Danish & Enyedy, 2020; Sung et al., 2017). By play-acting such ideas (Fadjo et al.,

2009) and building on bodily resources (Sung et al., 2017), learners are better able to ground abstract ideas  and

delve into advanced CS concepts like algorithmic thinking without narrowing engagement to coding only (Ayalon et al., 2023; Danish & Enyedy, 2020). Early studies highlight the value of imagining ones elf as an agent in programming environments (Colella et al., 1998; Pap ert, 1980). More contemporary research  highlights the benefits of embodiment for supporting social and co llaborative approaches to computational practice (Ayalon et al., 2023), in which students can intuitively conne ct with virtual agents while leveraging personal fu nds (Danish & Enyedy, 2020)  to enable a deeper understanding of computational c oncepts as a group (Ayalon et al., 2023; Mørch & Kafai, 2022). Combining collective work tha t positions movement as a tool for CT invites learn ers to bring in their social and cultural resources (Solom on et al., 2022)  and investigate their perception of computing practices together.

In this project, we explore how collective embodied  CT impacts learners’ perceptions of the computing field, intending to help students envision themselv es as members of computing communities. Our approac h incorporates students' real-world experiences and embodied and cultural backgrounds into complex CT concepts vital to the field. Our unique contribution to the embodied CT literature incorporates a collective as pect that acknowledges diverse epistemologies. This paper aim s to enrich computing spaces by in troducing multiple perspectives and ways of knowing, including the body and gesture as resources. Methodological approach Curricular design This study’s curriculum was embedded within a ninth-grade biology unit on adaptation. Designed through a constructionist lens (Harel & Papert, 1991), the cu rriculum emphasized open-ended problem-solving, collaboration, and student-driven learning (figure 1 a). Our goal was to support expansive views of CT by creating space for students’ lived experiences, bodily knowl edge, and cultural assets to shape how they made se nse of complex CS ideas. To explore this, we designed a four-day sequence focused on sorting algorithms contextualized through the migration of finches with varying beak sizes. On the first day, students reflected on everyday uses of CT, surfacing how CT and algorithmic thinking are everyday practi ces they constantly use for problem-solving. This served as a foundation for legitimizing FoK within computing. On day two  and four, students engaged with GEM-STEP (Generalized Embodied Modeling – Science Through Technology Enhanced Play) (Danish et al., 2 022), a mixed-reality environment that tracks movement and supports embodied modeling. Students used their  bodies to play-act and c ollaboratively debug sorting algorithms (figure 1 b). The design positioned learners as scientists usin g embodied reasoning to solve an open-ended problem, inviting them to draw on intuitive, physical, and social strategies rather than abstrac t code. This activity foregrounded the body and social interaction as legitimate tools for computational sensemaking. On day three, students worked in small groups to co-create sorting strategies through unplugged, low-tech activities. They wrote pseudocode, peer-reviewed each other’s strategies, and refined their  thinking through discussion and play-acting. Throughout, we avoided presenting a singular “corre ct” computational approach. Instead, the curriculum emphasized exploration, int erpretation, and iteration —values more aligned with expressive, relational, and culturally situated way s of knowing. In this way, we designed for students — particularly girls—to see themselves in computing not by assimilating into dominant norms but by expanding the boundaries of what computing can look like.

Data sources and collection This study was part of a larger design-based research project exploring embodied approache s to computational thinking. We conducted four 2-hour sessions with small groups of ninth-grade students over four consecutive weeks in a university-based STEM enrichment program in the southeastern U nited States. The program serves a diverse population, though its STEM focus may limit generalizability. Twenty-three students (12 girls, 11 boys; 8 White, 6 Afric an American, 4 Asian, 5 Other) participated using self-selected pseudonyms. Data sources included 17 hours  of video recordings, pre-and post-tests, group artifacts, homework assignments, post-interviews with seven students, and system log data  from GEM-STEP. This paper focuses on students’ pre-and post-test responses and interviews to investigate changes in perception. Pre-and post-tests Students completed surveys before and after the cur riculum that assessed their perceptions of CT and C S, prior computing experience, and evaluated foundational kn owledge through a sorting task. The post-test included the same measures with added reflective questions. Our analysis centers on responses to the questions, “Di d your perspective on computational thinking change during  this unit? If so, how?”, “Describe a computer scie ntist in three words,” “Describe yourself in three words,” a nd “Describe the ideal student in a computing course in three words.” These items helped track shifts in students ’ conceptual and personal associations with computi ng. We hypothesized that the short-term intervention would most impact perceptions of computer scientists, while perceptions of self and ideal student might require longer-term engagement to shift. Interviews After the implementation, we conducted seven semi-structured interviews (5 female and 2 male) based o n students' self-selection. We acknowledge that students who were mo re enthusiastic about the activity were more likely to volunteer. Despite this bias, we suggest that these enthusiastic volunteers offer important insight into the impact of the experience.  Interviews, lasting 17-31 minutes, explored students' experiences with embodied CT and its impact on their perceptions of CT. Sample q uestions included: “What was your experience using your body in the model to explore strategies for sorting  objects?” “What was it like to work with other stu dents while exploring strategies for sorting objects?” “Did thi s experience change your perception of how you can use or explore computational thinking? How so?” Interviews  were transcribed using AI software, with manual corrections for accuracy.

## Data analysis procedure

We employed a mixed-methods approach to capture broad patterns in stude nts' perceptions and to elevate individual voices and experiences in our implementa tion (Guest et al., 2013). We conducted open qualit ative coding of student responses to “Describe a computer scientist in three words.” Observed differences between preand post-activity responses led us to triangulate findings through a quantitative analysis using a Word Embedding Association Test (WEAT) (Caliskan et al., 2017). The WEAT test is usually used to measure a dataset’s bias; however, in this case, we used it to locate if a nu anced shift in perception occurred. We adhered to L incoln and Guba’s (1985) criteria to ensure credibility, inclu ding prolonged engagement, triangulation, peer debr iefing, and member checking.

Two researchers independently coded and evaluated each student's coding experience across four levels, reaching consensus for every student: (1) no prior experience; (2) some experience, defined as 1-2 short/informal coding activities or general knowledge of coding; (3) moderate experience, defined as 2-4 short/informal coding activities, one formal STEM+C coding experience, or specific knowledge of coding languages; and (4) extensive experience, defined as 5+ short/informal coding activities, two or more formal STEM+C experiences, or AP-level proficiency and extensive coding knowledge. These l evels were then grouped into “low” and “high” categ ories. Finally, we coded open-ended responses to “Did your perspective on computa tional thinking change…?” to identify categories of responses: changes in perceptions or no changes in perceptions. A third category emer ged during coding, emphasis on precision, to capture responses in which students expressed either a small or no change but that the unit emphasized the importance of precision in computing. This category is reported with no changes in perceptions.

## Quantitative analysis

We used the RoBERTa model to generate word embeddin gs, assigning a unique vector to each word such  that semantically similar words were positioned close to gether in the vector space. Words were reduced to t heir base forms (e.g., “creativ” for both “creativity” and “c reative”), and contextualized using two surrounding  words to account for meaning shifts  (e.g., “quick” in “quick thinker” vs. “quick run”).  This allowed us to analyze each descriptor within the broader semantic frame of a s tudent’s response. We then conducted a Word Embeddi ng Association Test (Caliskan et al., 2017) to measure  cosine similar ity between students’ words and two conceptually derived categories  — “ Techie” and “Fuzzy” traits (Table 1). These categor ies emerged from our qualitative analysis and were expanded using ChatGP T to generate statistically common, semantically re lated terms. All authors refined the category lists through iterative discussion to reduce bias. The null hypothesis assumed no difference between pre-and post-activity words in terms of similarity to “Techie” and “Fuzzy” attributes. (table 1) (Hartley, 2018). We conducted the test across the e ntire group and separately by gender. Due to the small dataset and diverse races, we could not group by students’ race. ## Qualitative analysis

Quantitative findings guided the qualitative analysis. Three authors conducted multiple rounds of inductive coding of interview transcripts using thematic analysis (Miles, 2014). Initial rounds involved high-level open coding of all interviews, looking for emergent themes in stud ents’ changing perceptions of computing, particular ly around CT and traditional stereotypes. In subsequent round s, we refined codes to capture nuances in  perceptions, constructing narratives and case studies from shift s observed in interviews and pre-post responses. We engaged in peer debriefing after each round of coding.

We identified a subset of students with substantial perception shifts, selecting two for case studies based on how their changes in perception connected to broader aspects of their lives. Triangulating data developed these cases from interviews, pre-post-test, and classroom observations.

## Findings

This section will first report perception changes i dentified in our quantitative analysis. Then, we wi ll showcase two qualitative cases highlighting two different ways girls’ perceptions shifted throughout the implementation.

## Qualitative findings

Our qualitative analysis of interviews further supp orted the quantitative findings, showing shifts in girls' perceptions of CT. Initially, girls associated CT w ith traditional computer science practices, particu larly coding: “You hear computational, and it  sounds like [a] computer. And, so, I kind of thought it was going to be very coding-based, I guess…my brain just doesn’t like computers ” (Sage, post-interview). Often, these initial perceptions were accompanied by negative sentiments, such as “I don't like coding. Like, I really don' t” (Sarah, post-interview). Despite initial reservations, girls' pe rceptions of computational practices evolved across  three main areas.

First, they created meaningful personal connections to computing in their everyday lives: “I used to think sports and science were completely different ideas,  but computational science actually brings them tog ether” (McQuinn, post-test). Second, girls constructed holistic definitions of CT, seeing it as relevant in various contexts: “It made me realize most if not everything requires  computational thinking” (Chabela, post-test). One student explained CT’s application in math: “Even when it c omes to math, I could use it. Because sometimes my brain goes all crazy with math. And it would be good to s ay like, this is the first step. This is the second step. If  this occurs, this happens” (Mac, post-interview). Finally, girls expressed interest in fu rther exploring computing practices: “CT is used in sports, and I believe tha t it can be used in investigations/de tective work. It made me more interested in the planning/CT aspects of inves tigations” (Sunflower, post-test). We present two cases that illustrate these shifts.

Computational thinking as a fund for professional growth. Sarah is a female African American student (15 year s old) with no prior experience in computing “becau se it’s not a subject [she’s] interested in,” as expressed in her pre-test. Sarah showed positive shifts in how she perceives the practices and members of computing communities and how she sees computing as related to her person al goals and identity. She chose technical terms to de scribe computer scientists (coding, math, and co mputer), representing her disconnect with computing fields. After the implementation, we found that she express ed more positive, field-neutral descriptors for computer scientists (creative, problem-solving, and innovative), suggesting shifts in her perception of computing.

Sarah also developed an interest and professional utility for CT, which was concretized during the postinterview. When asked if she was interested in exploring more CT, she first replied no, “I think it's an interesting topic, but I'm more of a medicine girl” After a pause, she changed her thinking, “Although no, I take that back… I did just say computational thinking is kind of in  our lives and in our faces. So maybe I could use m y interests into computational thinking.” She described two sce narios of CT impacting her future career in medicine. Sarah connected her imagined future career with computational practices explored in the implementation. She expressed a desire to further her computing experiences, spec ifically in exploring the ways that modeling o r surgery simulations can be leveraged to help “predict the outcome of [surgeries]”. Computational thinking as a fund for personal growth.

Ahana is a female Asian American student (14 years old) with computing experience from two middle scho ol STEM programs in which she explored robotics and pr ogramming. She expressed positive shifts in her computational perception, specifically in identifying CT as a lens that she could apply to her everyday life. Ahana emphasized intelligence and orientation towards practice to describe a computer scientist (knowing, hardworking, and genius). After the implementation, her descript ion of a computer scientist changed to reflect traits aligned with problem-solving (patient, resourceful, and creative). Ahana elucidated this shift in her perception in he r interview, expressing that she started to apply C T in her everyday life. She explained that she was frust rated with her practice as an archer, and used comp utational practices in class to improve her archery practice,  “I've actually been thinking about this, and like,  ‘what am I changing in my process?’ Like, ‘What am I thinking differently?’ And it actually has helped…It actuall y made me a better archer.” She went on to describe thinking about her archery practice as a computational process, which helped her to focus on and correct the minute diffe rences in her form to get a bullseye. She expressed  pride and excitement that the new lens enabled her to succeed: “I'm glo ating about myself because I'm so proud of it…I haven't done this in like a year…I've gotten like three in the past week.” She went on to describe a new relationship with CT, “It just really opened my eyes, if that makes sense, because now, I actually have it every day in my life. And how when you see something a certain way, sudde nly you see it everywhere.” The implementation transformed Ahana’s thinking and provided her with a new lens for the world.

## Discussion

This paper explores how collective-embodied CT activities that encourage students to i ncorporate FoK and express multiple ways of knowing can affect learner s' perceptions of computing practices, mainly CS, a s a discipline. Our findings reveal significant shifts in girls' perceptions of computer science, moving from technical and pragmatic descriptors to more introspective and  creative ones. This change was not observed in boys’ perceptions. While we suggest this difference may be impacted by the computational FoK that boys brought into the unit, data collected cannot confirm this hypothesis. Further analysis and a larger sample size might shed light on the cause.

The transformation in girls' perceptions is particu larly noteworthy given that our participants were p art of a STEM-oriented program and had prior exposure to computin g ideas through coding, robotics, and engineering. Despite these experiences, they initially expressed normative perceptions of computer science, some with strong preferences not to participate in computing fields. This underscores the persistence of stereotypes and the need for innovative approaches to challenge them.

Our qualitative analysis supports and enriches these findings, revealing how girls began to see CT as an integral part of their everyday lives and future professional endeavors. The cases of Sarah and Ahana demonstrate how CT became a new fund of knowledge  that they could access and apply in diverse persona l and professional contexts. This expansion of CT beyond the curriculum is a crucial outcome, as it suggests a more profound, more sustainable change in perception and engagement with computing concepts. However, this study is limited in that its integrat ed design —combining embodiment, collaboration, narrative, and scripting—makes it difficult to isolate which elements most i nfluenced the shift in girls’ perceptions. Further research is needed to unpack t he specific contributions of collective-embodied CT. Additionally, the study examines short-term shifts in percept ion rather than long-term identity development. As such, we cannot claim sustained changes in computin g identity. While girls' perspectives shifted meani ngfully, boys’ views remained largely unchanged; future work  should explore why certain learners respond differently to these designs. These limitations suggest the need f or more targeted comparisons and longitudinal resea rch to better understand mechanisms of change.

## Implications

Our findings point to curriculum design elements —such as self-directed exploration, multiple pathways, and the integration of embodied, collaborative, and culturally relevant activities—as key factors in reshaping perceptions of computing. These constructionist principles high light the importance of learner s’ agency in computing education. Although perception change is gradual and not tied to a single moment, each design element likely contributed to this shift, helping students transition from viewing computing as purely technical to understanding it as a holistic approach to problem-solving across various domains. Additional analysis of video data and student interviews can begin to unpack how these design decisions impacted these changing, and unchanging, perceptions expressed by students.

This approach bridges the gap between abstract comp utational concepts and tangible, personal experiences, fostering a sense of belonging that ha s long eluded many girls in traditional computing s paces. As we continue to grapple with gender disparities in t ech, this work offers a promising direction: by valuing diverse perspectives and ways of knowing, we can cultivate a computing culture where all students, regardless of gender, can thrive and innovate. The future of computing lies not just in lines of code but in the rich tapestry of experiences and knowledge, each learner brings.
