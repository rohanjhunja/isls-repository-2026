# Scaffolding Students’ use of GenAI: A Pilot Examining Higher Education Instructors’ Attitudes and Reported Practice

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While higher education institutions establish policies for Generative AI (GenAI) use, less attention is given to instructional scaffold s for students’ use of it. To address this gap, we used two surveys (n₁=242, n₂=46; pre-registered before analysis) to evaluate instructors' perceptions toward student GenAI use (RQ1) and SRL-aligned scaffolding practices in teaching (RQ2). We further evaluate alignment between reported practices and SRL theory (RQ3). Results reveal a paradox: instructors recognize GenAI's benefits for reducing workload and improving understanding, yet worry about impacts on critical thinking and independent learning. Mapping scaffolding practices onto the SRL framework revealed instructors emphasize during-task support while neglecting planning and reflection. Surprisingly, scaffolding practices formed a single construct rather thandistinct SRL phases, suggesting lack of differentiated strategies for supporting GenAI-enhanced learning. Findings suggest a possible gap between SRL theory and practice in the GenAI era, pointing to urgent needs for evidence-based professional development moving toward pedagogically-grounded support.

Generative AI (GenAI) has rapidly entered students' study routines in higher education, with evidence of mediumto-large gains in academic performance and higher-order thinking, alongside reduced mental effort (Deng et al., 2025). Benefits include improved revision quality via AI feedback  (Lo et al., 2025) and a more iterative drafting process with AI support (Nguyen et al., 2024). Yet risks persist as students often fail to critically evaluate GenAI outputs, and neglect source-checking and cross-referencing in ways that candegrade academic work  (Abbas et al., 2024; Giannakos et al., 2024). This benefit-risk duality foregrounds a central pedagogical challenge-supporting students’ ability to engage in Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) while using GenAI. SRL is central to students’ academic success (Pintrich, 2004; Winne & Hadwin, 2021)  and can be effectively scaffolded by instructors (Azevedo & Cromley, 2004; Theobald, 2021). While GenAI is an emerging technology, research on SRL in the context of educational technologies is well established  (Winne & Hadwin, 2021; Zimmerman, 2013; Zimmerman & Schunk, 2011). However, less is known about the role instructors can play in supporting students in doing so. Here we focus on this underexplored dimension of students’ GenAI use-instructional scaffolds for students’ SRL as they work with GenAI for academic purposes. We examine the degree to which such support is evident in the student-instructor educational interaction using two surveys distributed to the teaching community at a research-intensive university in Israel. GenAI and SRL GenAI has the potential to reshape study workflows by enabling students to delegate work and receive personalized feedback quickly, but it simultaneously demands critical evaluation of outputs against pre-set criteria and presents an iterative workflow: key features of SRL. SRL is modeled as an iterative, circular process that includes goal setting, planning, enactment of study tactics and strategies, and a meta-cognitive, reflective stage in which learning outputs are evaluated against pre-set criteria and adjustments are made based on this evaluation (Pintrich, 2004; Winne & Hadwin, 2021; Zimmerman & Schunk, 2011). Prasad and  Sane (2024) posit that generative tools can both hamper and help novices’ self-regulation in programming, so interventions should be designed explicitly around SRL phases. Further, Chiu (2024) offers a practical classification tool that maps ChatGPT-supported activities to SRL processes, making forethought, monitoring, and reflection legible to instructors. Giannakos et al. (2024) argue that productive GenAI use depends on verification and regulation rather than access alone. Moreover, Shi et al. (2025) showed that SRL and AI literacy are linked to outcomes in GenAIsupported writing. Together, these contributions position SRL as key for successfully working with GenAI in the context of learning and higher education. However, open questions still remain, especially in asking what can instructors do to promote learners in engaging with SRL productively when using GenAI for academic purposes. The research gap: Policy first, pedagogy later 2071 In response to  the growing use of GenAI in higher education (Erez et al., 2025), higher education institutions work towards establishing guidelines and best practices (An et al., 2025; Luo, 2024). However, while universities moved quickly to establish what counts as permissible use, integrity, and risk management, operationalizing of course-level pedagogy for learning with GenAI is lagging behind. This policy-first, pedagogy-later pattern leaves a gap: how can instructors structure educational supports that help students  work effectively with GenAI ? Our study aims to address this research gap through the following questions:

• RQ1) What are instructors’ perceptions about students’ use of GenAI tools for learning?

• RQ2) What practices do instructors apply to scaffold students’ use of GenAI in their courses?

• RQ3) What is the alignment between these scaffolding practices and SRL theory?

We hypothesize that, similarly to recent work, instructors will hold a mixed perspective regarding students’ use of GenAI, as both  “risky” but beneficial (H1). We further hypothesize to find low reported engagement of scaffolding of SRL (H2). Finally, we hypothesize that the use of scaffolds will align closely with SRL models (Pintrich, 2004; Winne & Hadwin, 2021; Zimmerman & Schunk, 2011).

## Design, participants, academic context, and data analysis

To address our RQs, we used two surveys as described below in a medium-sized research intensive technological university. The institution has approximately 10K students enrolled at the undergraduate level (40% women) and about 2000 teaching staff members in diverse ranks. This study was IRB approved and pre-registered following registration). See survey 2 here (OSF link, data analyzed after the pre-registration). Survey 1, addressing RQ1, evaluated the perceptions of higher STEM education instructors regarding students’ use of GenAI for learning. It captured perceived value on a 5-point Likert scale and Concerns regarding students’ use of GenAI as a check-up item. The survey was emailed to the teaching staff at the Technion in February 2025 to all teaching staff members, yielding 242 responses (n varies by item ~5%-12% response rate). Survey 2, addressing RQ2 and RQ3, collected data on three different professional development seminars for instructors at the same institution (n=46). The survey included items mapped a-priori to SRL-aligned subscales (5-point Likert scale): Task definition, Goal setting/planning, Study enactment/strategies, and Reflection. To answer RQ1, we report descriptive statistics. To answer RQ2, we report d escriptive statistics and a benchmark analysis per subscale compared to a 3.5 benchmark (the midpoint between “ Neutral” and “Agree”) using one-sample t-tests. To answer RQ3, we conducted an Exploratory Factor Analysis evaluat ing the dimensionality emerging from survey responses and their fit to multi dimensionality SRL model s. RQ1: Instructors’ perceptions of GenAI for teaching and learning The perceived contribution of GenAI tools to students’ learning is seen in two main areas: (1) logistically, by reducing students’ workload (M = 3.34, SD = 1.18, n = 113)  and (2) allowing flexibility and availability  (M = 3.21, SD = 1.20, n = 110). Two additional areas that were marked as potential contributions, but to a lesser extent were (1) helping students better understand the material  (M = 2.89, SD = 1.26, n = 115), and  (2) allowing a positive learning experience (M = 2.96, SD = 1.16, n = 108). On the other hand, the use of GenAI is not perceived as beneficial for encouraging continuous learning (M = 2.06, SD = 1.07, n = 89) and focusing learning in the course (M = 2.37, SD =  1.14, n = 108). Notably, none of the items reached  an average “Agree” score (=4), pointing to a rather low perceived benefits for learning. The top concerns instructors  (n=242) hold for student learning and skill  development in the context of their use of GenAI were: (1) students’ ability to apply effort in their learning (40%),  (2) critical thinking (35%), and (3) the ability to work independently (39%). Additionally, instructors are concerned about biases resulting from using GenAI in learning (35%), and issues relating to academic dishonesty such as plagiarism (31%). Issues such as information security and data privacy were not perceived as concerning. This study examined an underexplored dimension of students' GenAI use: the instructional scaffolds instructors provide and their alignment with SRL theory. We found that instructors hold paradoxical views as they recognize GenAI's potential to reduce workload and provide personalized feedback, yet worry these affordances hinder critical thinking, independent learning, and sustained effort.  Second, instructors' scaffolding practices are concentrated on during-task support, systematically underserving the before-and after-task phases of the SRL cycle. Third, these scaffolding practices did not organize into the theoretically predicted distinctive phases structure but instead created a single "more versus less scaffolding" scale. This finding may challenge assumptions about how SRL phases work  in AI-mediated learning environments and points to the urgent need for more research in this area.

Survey 1 is consistent with other emerging work but reveals a deeper tension that may explain instructors' scaffolding choices. We found that instructors recognize potential benefits to students' GenAI use, yet their top worries center on learning quality, creating a pedagogical double-bind. T he very features that make GenAI attractive are precisely what instructors fear will hurt  essential academic skills, supporting H1 and aligned with other work (An et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2024; Luo, 2024) Further, this suggests that universities are right to establish institutional guidelines and practices as these are concerning to teaching staff members. However, our results also suggest a "use it responsibly" messag e will be insufficient as there may be a knowledge gap in how to scaffold students’ work with this emerging technology.

Survey 2 and benchmark analysis (Table 2) revealed that instructors' scaffolding significantly underperforms a 3.5 benchmark for three of the four phases. This  indicates that instructional support is concentrated around active tool use, with less of a focus on the before and after. With recent literature highlighting the important role SRL can play in students' successful use of GenAI for studying (Giannakos et al., 2024; Prasad & Sane, 2024; Shi et al., 2025), these results indicate that there is still work to be done in terms of professional development to encourage  teaching staff members to emphasize the entire SRL cycle. Item-level detail reinforces this: instructors emphasize guidelines, proper use, and quality assurance but put less attention into aligning GenAI with learning goals, setting boundaries before use, or planning how to use GenAI. Finally, we found that the self-reported instructional support fits a "less vs. more" structure rather thandistinct phases, which does not support H3. The EFA revealed that a single dominant factor solution accounts for 56% of the variance. While a three factor solution add ed about 10% to explained variance, examining the items did not support a solution of more than one factor, as would be expected given phase-based SRL theory (Pintrich, 2004; Winne & Hadwin, 2021; Zimmerman & Schunk, 2011). These results raise questions about the assumption that different SRL phases will appear as distinct instructional steps in GenAI contexts. We offer four non-mutually exclusive explanations for this unexpected unidimensionality, also pointing to potential limitations of our work. First, with the fast responses of GenAI, i t is possible that GenAI collapses the regulatory timeline so dramatically so that theoretically distinct phases blur into rapid micro-cycles. Further, drawing on the TPCK framework (Mishra et al., 2023), effective technology-mediated instruction requires intersecting knowledge of the domain, pedagog y, and technolog y. The policy-first, pedagogy-later pattern at universities may leave even experienced instructors lacking specific, phase-differentiated strategies for how to provide it. Moreover, SRL models were built for learners regulating their own cognition. GenAI introduces an external agent that can perform cognitive work on the learner's behalf, shifting the regulatory task from "managing my thinking" to "managing my interaction with an AI that thinks." Phase-based models may not apply straightforwardly in this new context.  Finally, it is possible that the instrument itself could generate a bias and that actual teaching practices are more aligned with distinct SRL phases than reported here. Future work using larger datasets could help address this potential limitation. To conclude, the empirical finding of a single scaffolding construct, regardless of its explanation, warrants caution about assuming traditional SRL frameworks transfer seamlessly to GenAI-enhanced learning environments, highlighting the need for more research in this area.
