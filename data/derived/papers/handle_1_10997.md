**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, we explored data reasoning in a data science learning program that bridged data visualization and art creation. After deductive qualitative analysis of the transcripts of the interviews with the program participants, we found data visualization played a pivotal role in data reasoning.

In the past two decades, data science education shifted from decontextualized mathematical problems to more contextualized data reasoning (Petocz et al., 2018; Rubin, 2020). Although there is no unified definition of data reasoning, inspired by Rubin  (2020), we define data reasoning as the ability to examine the relationships of variables in a contextualized dataset, visualize those relationships, and eventually make inferences about the given dataset. To encourage data reasoning, educators might support learners in creating and explaining their own data visualizations because authoring a data visualization requires  learners to handle quantities, relate variables, and justify inferences (Rubel et al., 2021). In this study, we adapted Rubin's (2020) work to explore five aspects of data reasoning: context, variability, aggregate, visualization, and inference. In this data reasoning framework, context requires learners to understand that data are contextualized– they are numbers with a context (Cobb & Moore, 1997). Within a context, data also show variability, and learners are supposed to know the distribution of a dataset: some values are more common, whereas others are rarer. Knowing the distribution of data leads to the next skill –aggregate; an aggregate view of data allows learners  to consider how to summarize a distribution of values and what this summary does and does not reflect about the distribution (Konold et al., 2015). Aside from comprehending the dataset, data visualization requires learners to think actively about what data to present, what representational forms to use, and how to map their data values to the representational forms. Data visualization can improve data literacy by preparing learners  to communicate effectively and share data (Dykes, 2019). Last but not least, data inference lets learners draw conclusions based on the data they have and predict beyond their dataset. Inspired by Rubin's (2020) data reasoning framework, we will explore how youth engage in data reasoning in a data visualization project.

## Participants and data collection

Six youths were recruited for  this study. Four of them  were in 7th and 8th grade, and two were in 11th and 12th grade. All participants participated  in a 12-week informal education program about data visualization in a medium-sized city in eastern Tennessee. The program included weekly after-school data visualization learning sessions about  data science knowledge and data visualization skills. The program offered opportunities for participants to choose the topic of their interests and collect  relevant data to create their own data visualization artistically.

After the program ended in mid-May 2023, we conducted three group semi-structured interviews. Each interview lasted one hour. We asked eight questions to explore their data visualization and data reasoning: 1) Could you tell me about the data in as much detail as you can? 2) How important is the data to you? 3) Could you tell me about your experience of working with data in as much detail as possible? 4) What stood out to you about the data? 5) Which aspects did you want to make sure that you visualized?  6) Could you tell me about the data visualization you created? 7) What ideas does your data visualization communicate? How does it communicate?

8) What other stories can your data visualization tell?

## Data analysis

After transcribing the interviews, we segmented youth’s  responses during the interview by their response. Inspired by Rubin's (2020) work, the first author applied five deductive codes about data reasoning to youth’s responses to interview questions. Below are the codes used.

• Contextualize data: Using the context where their data was collected to explain the data.

• Data variability: Realizing some values are more common and others are rarer.

The most significant finding is the key role of data visualization in youth’s data reasoning. When asked to talk about their data, all the interviewees  referred to the data visualization they created. Their self-authored data visualization provided them with a structure to talk about data contextualization. One interviewee mentioned that getting insights from the raw survey data was quite hard, but when she was trying to visualize those data, she was able to have a deeper understanding of the data. In addition, talking about what data to visualize, youth were able to rationalize their choice s and explain the approach they used to map the values and their representations. For instance, one student, Nina,  wanted to show the strictness of the parental relationship, so she used the distance between the two icons to represent the closeness between the parent and child. Hence, visualizing data can both improve the contextualization of the data  and enhance the mapping between the data and their representations. Another finding is about youth making sense of the  variability in the dataset. Youth identified common values in their dataset to grip the “mainstream” in their dataset. For example, Suki’s (one of the interviewees) work was about the music people listened  to, and she said, “After analyzing the data, I noticed that the most common answers for both tended to be artists within the rap genre. Usually male rappers, I guess, there were a couple of female rappers.” In this case, she claimed that most people she interviewed liked rap music, focusing on a commonality within the data instead of focusing on its variability. There were also examples of youth finding it challenging to reason about the variability in their data. For instance, the student who investigated the parent-kid relationship said, “but it was really 50/50 against who did get along, and whether they have strict parents or not, like I said, like, I couldn't really come up with the conclusion of like, oh, this  means that it was really split even.” The evenly distributed data impeded her from drawing a strong conclusion. Future research In this study, our preliminary analysis only demonstrated two aspects: visualization and variability. There are also some limitations in this study. First, our analysis was based on post-program interviews. Students needed to recall their experiences and might have lost some thoughts they had when creating their data visualization. Second, the data visualization program had 24 participants, but only six participated in the interviews. With more participants, we can find more precise data reasoning patterns.

## Discussion & Implications

In conclusion, this preliminary work explores students’ data reasoning in a data visualization program. Reflecting on Rubin's (2020)  five aspects, we discovered data visualization could develop students deeper understanding of data and enhance their data-representation mapping and data variability was the key for students to find insights from their data.
