# Belongingness from the Classroom to the Field: Buffers, Boosters, and the Role of Identity Centrality for Minority Students in Computer Science

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Belongingness (i.e., a sense of belonging) in an academic field affects many key outcomes and is especially impactful for underrepresented students. Our study examines why belongingness in the classroom sometimes, but not always, affects belongingness in the field, and whether there are differences between minority and non-minority students in the importance of different factors known to contribute to belongingness in computer science. We found that participating in extracurricular computer science communities and seeing representation in the field was more important for minority students in both buffering negative classroom experiences and boosting belongingness in the field. Critically, we only found these differences when using perceived (i.e., self-identified) versus actual (i.e., numeric) minority status, suggesting that identity centrality may offer a theoretical explanation —and practical implications—for addressing challenges of belongingness for minority students.

Belongingness (i.e., a sense of belonging) in the field is critical for computer science students, leading to higher retention, academic performance, and student engagement (e.g., Benbow & Vivyan, 2016). Belongingness is especially important for minority s tudents, where the positive outcomes of belongingness are amplified (e.g., Hansen et al., 2024; Shortlidge et al., 2024). At the same time, researchers have found that class group projects have many benefits related to belongingness: they create opportunities for social bonding, promote development of collaborative skills, and normalize interactions with peers of diverse backgrounds (Benbow & Vivyan, 2016; Zhan et al., 2015). While there appears to be minimal differences between minority and non-minority students for learning outcomes in group work (Zhan et al., 2015), prior research has found that some minority students report lower belongingness and more negative experiences in group projects (e.g., Dost & Mazzoli Smith, 2023; Strassburger et al., 2024). Furthermore, minority computer science students who experience lower belongingness in class group projects do not necessarily transfer this low sense of belonging to the field more broadly (Strassburger et al., 2024).

Buffers of belongingness We expect that there are factors that buffer low sense of belonging in class group projects, and the classroom more generally, that help minority students maintain a sense of belonging in the field. For example, Aeby and colleagues

(2019) found that women engineering students are often subject to mi cro-discrimination in group projects, and

minority students who experience these micro-discriminations may develop lower expectations of group projects over time compared to their majority counterparts; thus, they do not generalize low belongingness and poor experiences in group projects to the field. Alternatively, minority students may find their sense of belonging in computer science outside of the classroom, and thus poor classroom experiences (e.g., poor group project experiences) have little effect on  belongingness in the field. Specifically, extracurricular computer science communities provide opportunities for minority students to build relationships with other students of similar 2025).

Boosters of belongingness Prior research has also identified numerous factors that can boost  (i.e., increase) belongingness in the field for computer science students. These factors include collaborative learning through study groups and peer support, positive interactions with faculty, extracurricular involvement through clubs and competition groups, hands-on experience through internships and research projects, strong support from family, and representation in the field (e.g., Dost & Mazzoli Smith, 2023; Stephenson et al., 2018). While these factors have all been shown to boost belongingness, it is possible that some of these factors are more impactful than others and have relatively greater impact on belongingness for minority (versus non-minority) students. 1352 The role of identity Identity centrality (i.e., the degree to which a person feels that race, gender, and/or sexual orientation is important to their overall sense of self) influences students’ beliefs about their minority status and the embodiment of stereotypes (Macaulay et al., 2024). It is l ikely that identity centrality affects belongingness in both class group projects and the field for minority computer science students. For example, Mooney and Becker (2020) reported that undergraduate women who self-identified as part o f a minority group, including but not limited to racial/gender identity, had lower math belongingness, but women who did not identify as a minority had comparable levels of belongingness to men. Differences between perceived minority (i.e., whether a stude nt considers themselves to be a minority in the field) versus actual minority status (i.e., whether a student is a numeric minority in the field) may offer a theoretical explanation—and practical implications —for addressing challenges of belongingness for minority students.

Present study and contributions Our present study has three aims. First, we examine the nuanced relationship between sense of belonging in class group projects and in the field; in particular, we investigate why low sense of belonging in class projects does not necessarily lead to low sense of belonging in the field for minority computer science students. Identifying such buffers is critical as it may help educators mitigate the negative effects of group projects —and classroom experiences more broadly—on belongingness in the field for minority students. Based on our review of the literature, we propose two types of buffers: 1) minority students expect poor class group project experiences and therefore do not extrapolate belongingness in the group project to the field more broadly, and 2) minority students find their sense of belonging in the field through extracurricular computer science-related communities rather than in the classroom.

Second, we explore whether there are differences between minority and non-minority students in the importance they place on different factors known to contribute to belongingness in the field of computer science. While prior research has shown that these factors do increase belongingness for minority students, there has been little empirical research examining whether they are more (or less) important for minority students compared to non-minority students. Understanding these boosters of belongingness for minority students will help educators with designing targeted interventions and programs.

Third, we investigate the role of identity as a potential buffer or booster of belongingness. In particular, we are interested in whether there are differences in results when using perceived versus actual minority status. If there are differences when using perceived versus actual minority status, this would provide insight into the role that one’s relationship with their own identity (i.e., identity centrality) plays in belongingness. While perceived minority identity has been studied in research on belongingness in STEM fields (Mooney & Becker, 2020), the comparison of perceived versus actual minority identity has yet to be examined.

## Method

Sample We invited current students and new graduates (< 1 year since graduating) from the Computer Science and Software Engineering (CSSE) department at a small STEM-focused, undergraduate-only college to participate in our study via email. The email invited those who had recently completed a computer science course with a group project of three or more students to participate and included a link to the informed consent form and survey. Students were informed that there was no incentive for participation. Our anal ytic sample (n = 96) consisted of 71 (73.96%) men, 19 women (19.79%), 4 (4.17%) nonbinary, 2 (2.08%) undisclosed participants; 50 (47.2%) participants were White, 50 (47.2%) Asian, 3 (2.8%) Black, 3 (2.8%) Hispanic. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the institution, with all procedures conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

## Procedure

Our survey had four sections containing Likert-style questions: Project Expectations, Extracurricular Communities, Belonging Factors, and Demographics. The Project Expectations section evaluated how participants typically approach group projects (e.g., “In general, I expect a positive experience going into a CSSE group project.”). The Extracurricular Communities section evaluated whether participants were part of an extracurricular community that increased their sense of belonging in the field of computer s cience (e.g., “I am part of a community that makes me feel like I belong in CSSE.”). 1353 The Belonging Factors section asked participants to identify specific factors that contribute to their belongingness in computer science. Participants assessed to what extent, on a scale of 1 (Not Important) to 5 (Extremely Important), Collaborative Learn ing (study groups, peer support), Positive Interactions with Faculty, Extracurricular Involvement (clubs, competition groups), Hands-On Experience (internships, research projects), Support from Family, and Representation in the Field were factors toward their belonging in computer science. Finally, we asked participants about their belongingness in group projects (e.g. “I feel like I belong in my CSSE class group projects”) and belongingness in the field (“I feel like I belong in the field of CSSE”) in general, as well as their perceived min ority status (“Do you consider yourself to be a minority in CSSE?”) and demographics.

Using Python, we conducted independent sample t-tests to calculate group differences, and we used Pearson’s correlations and multiple linear regression to examine variable relationships. Two types of minority status were analyzed: perceived minority and actual (i.e., numeric) minority. Perceived versus actual minority status The perceived minority group included participants who considered themselves as a minority in the field of computer science (n = 26). The actual minority group included participants who are numerically underrepresented in computer science (women, Black, Latinx, and/or LGBTQ+ individuals; n = 28). We were faced with a difficult decision of whether to include Asians as a numeric minority; ultimately, although Asians are a numerical minority in the United States, they are not underrepresented in computer science or STEM more broadly (Stephenson et al., 2018). They also align with race-based STEM stereotypes of cis-gender White/Asian male persons, so we chose not to have straight, cis-gender Asian men as minorities. 9 participants from the actual minority group did not identify as a perceived minority: 3 women, 1 Black, 3 Latinx, and 2 LGBTQ+ individuals. 9 participants that would numerically be considered non-minority individuals perceived themselves as a minority: 3 White cis-gender men and 6 Asian cis-gender men. Buffers of belongingness Project expectations We found that minority status did not predict project expectations; results did not differ when using perceived versus actual minority status. However, students with lower project expectations in general did experience a significantly lower sense of belonging in group projects (r = 0.73; p <.01). Extracurricular communities We found that minority students were not more likely to participate in an extracurricular computer science-related community compared to non-minority students; results did not differ when using perceived versus actual minority status. However, students who were part of an extracurricular community did experience a significantly higher sense of belonging in the field (r = 0.53, p  <.01). Furthermore, minority status (both perceived and actual) significantly (p <.05) moderated the relationship between being part of an extracurricular community and sense of belonging in the field, such that the relationship was stronger for minority students (b  = 3.14, SE = 0.285; b = 3.12, SE = 0.277) for perceived and actual minority status, respectively). Boosters of belongingness Our results showed several significant (p <.05) differences between minority and non-minority students in factors that contribute to their belongingness in the field of computer science: Extracurricular Involvement (3.15 versus 2.42, t = 2.55, p <.05, d = 0.61 using perceived minority status) and Representation in the Field (3.56 versus 2.63, t = 3.00, p  <.01, d = 0.71 using perceived minority status). Interestingly, these differences only existed when comparing groups using perceived minority status, not when using actual minority status. The importance of Collaborative Learning, Hands-On Experience, Positive Interactions with Faculty, and Support from Family did not differ significantly between minority and non-minority students. General 1354 Our study tested two theoretically grounded buffers for why low sense of belonging in computer science group projects does not necessarily lead to low sense of belonging in the field more broadly for minority students. We found that extracurricular compute r science-related communities outside of the classroom is a more likely explanation than poor expectations of group project experiences, as the impact of extracurricular communities on belongingness was significantly stronger for minority students. The importance of extracurricular involvement was further cemented in our analysis of factors that boost belongingness in the field; extracurricular involvement was one of only two factors that were significantly more important to minority students compared to their majority counterparts. These findings suggest that field-related extracurricular communities are indeed critical for minority students and may buffer the effects of negative experiences in the classroom (e.g., poor group project experiences) and boost belongingness in the field more generally.

The other factor that significantly impacted belongingness in the field more so for minority students was representation in the field. The fact that this was only significant for perceived minority status (versus actual minority status) prompts further exploration. We argue that identity centrality offers an explanation; students who report feeling like a minority may have high identity salience, making them more aware of their dissimilarities with others in the field, leading to lower belongingness. On the  contrary, students with lower identity salience may be less affected by low representation in the field and dissimilarities to those around them. Although prior research found that collaborative learning, positive interactions with faculty, hands-on experience, and support from family were critical factors for minority students to experience a sense of belonging in the field (Dost & Mazzoli Smith, 2 023; Stephenson et al., 2018), we did not find that these factors were more important to minority students than to non-minority students. Instead, we found that hands-on experience was the most important factor for belongingness in the field for all  students, while extracurricular involvement and representation in the field were particularly important factors for minority students. Developing extracurricular communities may be particularly effective for educational interventions and programs targeted toward increasing a sense of belonging in minority students.

## Limitations

Our study was limited in several ways. First, our sample was from a small STEM-focused, undergraduate-only college where many courses have a group project. The high exposure to group projects may impact project expectations and experiences as well as the importance of hands-on learning to belongingness. Second, our sample consisted largely of White and Asian students (> 80%); this is reflective of the demographics of the institute as well as the field of computer science (Fry et al., 2021), but nevertheless  resulted in a small sample for Black and Hispanic students and unequal groups for analysis. Finally, students were not forced to rank the factors of belonging in the field, resulting in ties of importance within each student. While this was an intentional  decision made by the researchers, as multiple factors may be equally important for a given student, this made it difficult to establish comparison between factors, which warrants further research. Future directions Future research could explore differences between actual and perceived minority status and their effect on other key outcomes in education research. We believe that our study provides the groundwork for future research comparing perceived versus actual minority status in education and how identity centrality can play a role. Identity centrality may clarify why some minority students thrive in group projects, classrooms, and other environments where they are underrepresented—while others remain alienated. Similarly, future work could also explore the role of students’ private and public regard. Private regard measures how positively students feel about their identity group, whereas public regard measures their perceptions of how others view their group (Sellers et al., 2006). Prior research has found that students with low public regard may find discriminatory experiences to be less mentally taxing and feel less pressure to conform to positive stereotypes (MacNear & Hunter, 2025). Research has also found that students with high private regard are more likely to engage with classmates, even if they are underrepresented (Kyere et al., 2022). Levels of private and public regard could provide further explanation for differences in belongingness in the classroom and field among minority students.

Finally, future work could incorporate socioeconomic status (SES) to identify challenges faced by low SES students in group projects and the classroom more generally. Students from low SES backgrounds may face additional demands, such as part-time work, which reduce opportunities to build relationships in group projects and the classroom—and may also reduce opportunities to foster connections in extracurricular communities. 1355 Aeby, P., Fong, R., Vukmirovic, M., Isaac, S., & Tormey, R.
