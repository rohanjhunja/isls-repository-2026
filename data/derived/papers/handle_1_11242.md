# Critical Algorithmic Literacy for LLM-assisted Decision-making

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In today’s information landscape, algorithm-driven technologies mediate nearly all aspects of information and communication, profoundly shaping our decisions and societal participation. This paper explores a rapidly evolving example of algorithmic influence: the role of LLMs in humandecision-making. As LLMs become increasingly central to these processes, their influence extends beyond simple assistance, potentially transforming how individuals evaluate options and act on recommendations. We examine this through the lens of critical algorithmic literacy, which encompasses the ability to interpret and critically assess algorithmic systems that influence access to information and decision-making. Based on a scoping review of 45 papers, we highlight the need for autonomy-supportive LLM-assisted decision-making approaches that empower learners to critically engage with the entire process and confidently take control of their choices as transformative agents.

## Introduction

In today’s digital landscape, algorithm-driven technologies shape almost every aspect of in formation access and communication. Every day, individuals face diverse situations requiring decision-making that are significantly influenced by these technologies.  The recent surge in Large Language Models (LLMs) li ke ChatGPT has introduced remarkable capabilities in supporting de cision-making processes. These models can handle an impressive array of tasks, from efficiently processing and summarizing extensive text data to aid decision-makers in swiftly comprehending insights, to generating cr eative ideas, alternative scenarios, and solutions (Eigner & Händler, 2024). However, the enhanced capabilities of LLMs also carry significant risks —including generating incorrect or misleading information (hallucinations), reinforcing a user’s perspective without critica l evaluation (sycophancy), and providing justifications that do not accurat ely reflect the model’s true reasoning (unfaithful reasoning) (Turpin et al., 2024). A recurrent concern is the p otential loss of human control because LLMs might pursue objectives misaligned with an individual’s i ntentions, best interests, or values, an especially  troubling prospect for vulnerable populations. Yet a lgorithmic processing and decision-making are not concepts newly introduced by LLMs. Long before their ascension, the learning sciences community has focused on understanding how algorithms shape our worldly perceptions and interactions. Hamilton et al. (2014) first called for a framework to expose algorithms to learners, urging researcher s to investigate their effects in meaningful ways. Since then, the field has increasingly responded, and over the past decade, a rich body of work has emerged to equip learners with tools to understand, question, and critique th ese powerful systems, a line of inquiry known as cr itical algorithmic literacy (Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2023). This literacy involves the  ability to  interpret and critically evaluate algorithmic systems that shape our access to information and influence our decisions, and foster awareness of how algorithms work, the biases and values embedded within them, and their societal consequences.

In this paper, we explore how LLM assistance shapes humandecision-making through the lens of critical algorithmic literacy. Drawing on a scoping review o f 45 papers, we identify both reference points and gaps in existing frameworks of critical algorithmic literacy as they apply to LLM-assisted decision-making. Our findings highlight future directions for developing more autonomy-supportive LLM-assisted decision-making approaches. We emphasize the importance of empowering learners to critically engage with the entire decision-making process and take control of their choices, positioning themselves as transformative agents or future world-makers.

## Background and theoretical foundations of LLM-assisted decision-making

In this section, we examine key theoretical models of humandecision-making and explore how LLMs might assist or influence this process. Broadly, theories on jud gment, decision-making, and choice fall into two categories: normative and descriptive theories of cognition. Normative theories focus on "how we should or ought to reason" and are primarily concerned with formal logic and p robability theory (Fishburn, 1981). Here  we concentrate on descriptive theories that aim to explain how people actually think and make decisions based on empirical evidence (Bell et al., 1988). Among these theories, the information-processing approach to decision-making by Payne and Bettman (2004) stands out as a foundational model. This approach traces back to Simon's (1957) concept of bounded rationality, which asserts that humandecision-making is constrained by both environmental factors (such as information costs) and cognitive limitations (su ch as memory  capacity). According to the information-processing approach to decision research, humandec ision-making can be broken down into a multistep model that typically includes the following stages: (1) s timulus identification (receiving information), (2)  attention and encoding (processing the information), (3) informat ion integration (evaluating options), (4) decision selection (choosing an option), and (5) response execution (a cting on the decision). LLM-assisted decision-making, however, follows a different pathway compared to hu mandecision-making. Unlike humans, who rely on a mix of intuition, biases, and rational analysis, LLMs a re characterized by what are known as "emergent abi lities." (Schaeffer et al., 2024). Emergent abilities refer to unexpected and often surprising competencies ari sing when LLMs are scaled to a certain size and trained on va st data. These abilities are not explicitly program med but emerge as a result of the model’s architecture and exposure to data. Examples include the capacity to perform complex reasoning, generate coherent narratives across  multiple topics, and even solve tasks it was not d irectly trained on. As a result, while LLMs can assist at e ach stage of the humandecision-making process (see Figure 1 for an illustration), their inhere nt design also poses significant risks of subtly nu dging humandecisions. For instance, LLMs may prioritize certain details or pe rspectives, emphasize specific information that can  steer learners’ interpretations, and reinforce initial selections by channeling actions toward those choices. It is therefore important to guide the ways in which LLMs assist and nudge de cision-making to better support learners while preserving their autonomy. We argue that critical a lgorithmic literacy can serve as a valuable tool for achieving this vital balance. By equipping learners with critical algorithmic literacy, we enable them to recognize, interpret, question, and resolve the influence of LLMs in their decision-making processes. ## Methods

We conducted an exploratory review of interdiscipli nary literature on "critical algorithmic literacy" to identify how existing research on critical algorithmic liter acy addresses, or overlooks, the unique challenges of LLMassisted decision-making. Given the exploratory nature of this review, we opted for a scoping approach rather than a traditional systematic literature review. Sc oping studies do not impose "strict limitations on search terms, identification of relevant studies, or study selection at the outset"; instead, the process is iterative and emphasizes identifying research gaps and areas for future expl oration (Pham et al., 2014), which aligns with our goal. Our review was guided by the research question: What aspects of LLM-assisted decision-making are addressed, and what gaps remain, in existing critical algorithmic literacy literature? We began our search with the Education Resources Information Center (ERIC) database and th e ISLS repository to gather results with a focus on education. We then extended it to HCI conferences l ike CHI and IDC. Search terms were refined iterativ ely and included "critical algorithmic literacy," "algorith mic literacy," "data literacy," "ML literacy." This  search identified an initial set of 126 papers, whose refe rence lists we further reviewed to locate additiona l relevant literature. We then read the abstracts and skimmed the contents of papers to exclude those lacking rel evance— specifically, studies that did not include a defini tion of/a critical approach to algorithm ic literacy; or did not indicate the necessary competencies for algorithmic  literacy; or did not provide insights on fostering  learners’ algorithmic literacy. This search yielded 45 papers  for our final scoping review.  We then conducted a thorough reading of these selected papers, documenting key i deas from each. To distill key topics, we cross-referenced these ideas with the key stages of decision-making outlined in Figure 1. Through an iterative process, we identified preliminary themes and grouped the ideas based on conceptual similarity. To ensure consistency and resolve any uncertainties or disagreements, both authors discussed and refined these groupings collaboratively.

## Findings and discussion

This concept of critical algorithmic literacy is re latively new and has been categorized as part of ar tificial intelligence literacy (Ridley & Pawlick-Potts, 2021) and media literacy (Cohen, 2018). It has also been described as overlapping with media liter acy, digital literacy, new media literacy, and priv acy literacy (Dogruel, 2021). However, none of the references specifically focus on LLMs or decision-making; instead, they address broader aspects of algorithmic processing. From our review, we identified three key insights: Where existing theorizations on CAL inform on steps in LLM-assisted decisionmaking Based on our review, existing theorization on critical algorithmic literacy (CAL) offers valuable insights into the “receiving information,” “processing information,” and “evaluating options” stages of LLM-assisted decisionmaking. Several key competencies  emerge as particularly important and directly appli cable: 1) Awareness of Algorithms: This competency involves understanding the “black box” nature of al gorithms, which refers to their hidden internal workings, which are often inaccessible due to proprietary protections a nd the complexity of machine learning models. This occlusion is especial ly relevant in LLM-assisted decision-making, where outputs are generated through opaque processes based on vast datasets. 2) Critical Awareness of Datafication: This topic entails understanding how data is collected, processed, and used, along with its associated power dynamics. CAL research urges learners to look beyond the “mesmeri zing magic” of personalized content to understand how the systems leverag e their data. In the LLM context, this means learners assess ing the reliability of outputs, understanding that they are shaped by inherent data biases and model design rather than by objective truths, which insights are crucial for making informed decisions within AI-driven environments. 3)  Recognition of Algorithmic Biases: CAL highlights the need to identify and question bi ases in algorithmic systems to enable learners to critically evaluate information. Examples include i ntegrating critical race theory, equipping learners  to navigate, resist, and subvert algorithmic racism in everyday technologies (Tanksley, 2024); as well as uncovering the root causes of bias, which may stem from historical inequities embedded in code or training datasets, incomplete data, or algorithmic objectives prioritiz ing accuracy over fairness for minority groups (Ehret, 2024). 4) Questioning Algorithmic Outputs: The literature highlights the importance of cultivating learners' ability to critically evaluate, analyze, and question algorithmic outputs, while reflecting on their emotional responses to media they experience. Scholars argue that understanding algorithms requires not only technical knowledge but also a critical evaluation of their societal impact, including privacy concern s, digital divides, and the reinforcement of biases  like racism and sexism. Approaches li ke fact-checking and questioning algorithmic-driven narratives are surfaced to foster critical engagement (de Groot et al., 2023). Critic al inquiry approaches, such as affordance analysis and comparative studies of data structures, are also encouraged to enable students to explore the political implications of algorithms and challenge systemic injustices within computing (Morales-Navarro & Kafai, 2022). Some studies also encourage students to work on human–algorithmic co-creation to examine how algorithms shape one’s sense of self and belonging (Marlatt & Sulzer, 2021); and  reflect on their role in amplifying algorithmic ou tputs, such as through sharing or reacting to content, which ca n perpetuate algorithm-driven propaganda (Hobbs, 2020). These insights are particularly relevant in the con text of LLM-assisted decision-making, wher e learners must critically assess generated outputs, recognize pote ntial biases, and understand how their interactions  with these systems shape both individual identity and collective societal dynamics. Where existing theorizations on CAL fall short to inform on steps in LLM-assisted decision-making On the other hand, we identified critical gaps in e xisting CAL theorizations when applied to LLM-assisted decision-making. Specifically, while the literature provides  rich insights into information processing and evaluation, it rarely addresses the "what to do" problem—namely, how to transition from evaluating information to choosing an option (“choosing an option”) and ho w to act on a decision once it has been made (“acti ng on the decision”). These crucial steps, involving the tran sformation of evaluation into actionable choices and implementing those choices, remain largely unexplored. For instance, many studies emphasize the importance of learners developing competencies to control and actively manage their algorithmic environments (Tanksley, 2024; Morales-Navarro et al., 2024), building on their understand ing of algorithmic information processing. The literature often highlights that learners should no t feel powerless but instead be empowered to define  their own decisions. Learners are encouraged to “see themselves as doers and creators of algorithmic con tent, individuals who can engage with and use data for their own purposes and goals.” (Dangol & Dasgupta, 2023). However, these discussions tend to remain at a conceptual level, w ith little emphasis on practical pedagogical strate gies for developing these skills —unlike the well-established pedagogical approaches for earlier stag es of decision-making. Within the learning sciences and CSCL research, learner agency and orchestration (Hoogland et al., 2024) have long been recognized as central to supporting meaningful engagement and productive collaboration.  Yet pedagogical approaches to support the final stages of decision-making—selecting and enacting decisions—remain scarce. This gap raises concerns about how learners can eff ectively move from understanding and evaluating information to exercising agency in real-world decision-making contexts, highlighting a critical need for actionable frameworks and practical pedagogical int erventions for guiding learners through the later stages of decision-making. Without concrete methods to bridge the gap between evaluation and action, learners may struggle to translate their critical insights into meaningful decisions and behaviors. This deficiency is particularly concerning in the context of LLM-assisted decision-making, where the sheer volume and complexity of generated outputs can overwhelm learners, making it difficult  to confidently select and then take  a course of action. Developing pedagogical practices that empower learners to not only critique algorithmic outputs but also to apply their evaluations in real-world contexts is essential. Such practices could include scenario-based learning modules where students must choose among LLM-generated outputs, role-playing exercises that simulate real-world algorithmic decision points (e.g., evaluating college or career suggestions), or interactive simulations that require learners to justify, commit to, and revise decisions based on AI feedback—all aimed at bridging the gap between analysis and action.  In the meantime, this  emphasis also creates opportunities for future designs that empower users to engage more critically in the decision-making aspect of LLM-assisted systems, enabling them to make informed, autonomous choices and carry them out effectively. Understanding LLM-assisted decision-making as a sociotechnical process From our review, we identify several critical points for advancing critical algorithmic literacy (CAL) in the context of LLM-assisted decision-making, reframing such decision-making as a sociotechnical process. Existing CAL work offers valuable insights by encouraging learne rs to evaluate how algorithmic outputs shape their social contexts. This perspective is germane when considering LLM-assisted decision-making, where algorithmic recommendations directly shape users' choices and social environments. Polya’s pro blem-solving model (Pólya,

2014) includes a crucial reflection and consolidation phase, centering the importance of revisiting and analyzing

past actions —what worked, what didn’t, and why. We argue that LLM-assisted decision-making should also incorporate a structured reflective phase, akin to an after-action review. This phase would have users critically assess their decision outcomes, fostering sustained discourse on the ethical and social implications of algorithmic outputs and encouraging the exploration of transfor mative alternatives. Such reflective practices tran sition learners from passive consumers to active co-creators of ethical and socially just AI ecosystems. Approaches can include speculative design, which empowers learners to envision and reflect on alternative sociotechnical futures and design decision-making processes aligned with their values and soci etal needs. Moreover, by framing LLMassisted decision-making as a sociotechnical process, we uncover oppo rtunities to create a more autonomy-supportive experience for learners and users. This emphasis involves designing systems that actively inform, involve, and engage users throughout the decision-making process instead of relegating them to passive recipients of algorithmic suggestions. Possible future directi ons include investigating how users comprehend and interact with LLM-assisted decision-making systems. This  research could study how users make sense of algorithmic outputs, how they balance LLM-generated insights with their own reasoning, and wh at factors enhance their confidence in implementing satisfactory decisions.

## Concluding remarks

As LLMs increasingly mediate critical decision-making, their capacity to streamline information sy nthesis, generate insights, and propose actionable steps is undeniable. Yet this growing influence risks undermining human agency when learners become overly reliant on algorithmic outputs. Our review identifies significant gaps in existing frameworks of critical algorithmic literac y applied to LLM-assisted decision-making, particularly in supporting learners during the later stages,  such as selecting and implementing decisions. Addre ssing these gaps requires research and design interventions transcending critical evaluation to provide practical, actionabl e guidance. Building on these insights, a promising a venue for future work is the development of autonomysupportive systems—tools designed not only to help learners understand and assess LLM-generated content but empower them to confidently take control of their decisions as transformative agents—world-makers of the future.
