# Situating Socio-Scientific Representations in Artistic Transformations

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The current study aims to link artistic practices with STEM learning by documenting a case study of five 6th graders who navigated multiple artistic representations to interpret an article about a social scientific issue. Analysis of interviews, observations, video clips, and students’ artifacts point to a set of factors (personal, social, cultural, material, and embodied) that mediated students’ construction, transformation, and communication of multiple artistic (including embodied) representations.

## Conceptual framework

Research in STEM education has widely focused on the role of student-generated representations for deepening and communicating learning (Cox, 1999; diSessa, 2004; Greeno & Hall, 1997). Little research has built on these findings to expand and include explorations of how students engage in representations for Socio-Scientific Issues (SSI) explorations. SSI are controversial issues of public interest that are related to science, such as topics related to climate change. Considering that SSI interventions address controversial problems that could have multiple solutions, the study of SSI is important for grasping the complex and contested nature of science. (Tytler, 2012). The present study aims to fill this gap, including understanding how students transform representations at the intersection of SSI and arts and how they connect mental and external representations with embodied representations, which engenders new forms of learning and representing (Pande & Chandrasekharan, 2017).

The study follows an activity (1.5h duration) during a STEAM elective course in which 6th graders were framed as artists working in a museum and were asked to construct multiple artistic representations to communicate their interpretation of an SSI article. The article raised the question: “Is a person’s fate a matter of nature or nurture?” with references about the influence of the environment and genes on a person’s traits. Students transformed the article (sentential representation) to drawings (pictorial representations), used their bodies to collaboratively transform the drawings to “static sculptures” (embodied representations), and added sound and short movements to transform the static sculptures to “interactive sculptures” (dynamic embodied representations). The guiding research question of the study was: How do middle school students construct, transform, and communicate various artistic representations to make meaning out of an SSI article?

## Conceptual framework

Representations as distributed and embodied Our theoretical framework is informed by current shifts in cognitive science, namely distributed and embodied cognition theories, that stress the role of the material environment, context, motor actions, perception, embodiment, imagination, analogy, and metaphors for cognition (Barsalou, 1999; Hutchins, 2010; Johnson, 2017). Within the distributed perspective, the competences that individuals display are distributed among the tools, the environment and other people (Hutchins, 2010). Particularly for collaborative activities, participants share their representational skills and use the tools of their local environment (Cole & Engeström, 1993). From this perspective, individuals’ capacities to represent need to be understood in context, that is in relation to the local setting, including the tools in use and social and cultural factors (situated perspective, Lave & Wenger, 1991). Embodied and enactive theories highlight the relation between action, perception, and experience (Barsalou, 1999; Varela et al., 2017). Embodiment is “the premise that the particular bodies we have influence how we think” (Hutchins, 2010, p. 428). Enaction is “the idea that organisms create their own experience through their actions” (Hutchins, 2010, p. 428). Hutchins (2010) applied the enactive perspective into representations by arguing that humans integrate perception, action, and imagination and “make material patterns into representations by enacting their meanings” (p. 434). Moving and experiencing the body affect how people perceive the represented world, a world that depends on the affordances and constrains of the bodily experiences (Hutchins, 2010; Varela et al., 2017).

## Methods

We followed a case study research design (Merriam & Tisdell, 2015), in which we developed a curriculum to foster middle school students’ integrated STEM, arts, and literacy practices. Thirty-nine students participated in the elective course (18 female students and 21 male students), and 18 students consented to take part in the study and allowed for video recordings and photos. The current study focuses on a group of five students. All students of the group identified as Hispanic, and three of the students were female while the rest two were male. In the activity explored in this paper (week 4 of the curriculum; 1.5h duration), learners, in groups of 4-5, took on the role of artists who drew paintings inspired from an article about genetics and engaged in a series of representational transformations.

To ensure credibility and increase the trustworthiness of the study we collected a variety of data (Lincoln & Guba, 1986): Video records of the focus group’s activities, intensive 30-min individual interviews with two of the participants, informal conversations with the group, students’ artifacts (drawings, photos), and ethnographic observations (field notes and memos). We fully transcribed the interviews and the video records. For the analysis of the interviews, we conducted open coding and constant comparison (Corbin & Strauss, 2014). For the video analysis, we attended both to verbal (talk) and non-verbal (gestures, bodily postures, tone of voice, etc.) ways of communication (Goodwin, 2007). We also created matrices to examine each studentgenerated representational transformation (Miles & Huberman, 1994). Each matrix included a summary of observations, researchers’ notes/interpretations, students’ quotes from their discussions and interviews, and description of students’ artifacts.

## Findings

First Transformation: Article to Drawing Students used the affordances of drawing to communicate their interpretations of the article. Their creations reveal a variety of perspectives and strategies (e.g., color, symmetry, pictorial metaphor, narrative metaphor). All students reasoned about their choices by connecting their pictorial representations with references from the text, although sometimes they distorted textual references to accommodate their choices (see Figure 1). Students incorporated cultural norms, their preferences, and previous experiences into their drawings. For instance, Camila transformed a linguistic metaphor (“a cosmic dice rolling into one’s favor”) into pictorial cultural symbolism. She used a cultural inference that the best value shown in dice is six and the worst is one, as commonly agreed in boarding games. She also used the symbolism of color, dark color for representing the poor and bright color for representing the rich.

Figure 1 Photo of the Static Sculpture and Students’ Drawing Sofia created a pictorial metaphor to communicate her interpretation of success and failure. She drew a tree with some of its roots “colored all the way to show fulfilled lives” and some of the roots not “colored all the way” to show that some “lives could have been more fulfilled, but they weren’t” (Sofia’s interview). Sofia said in her interview that she always liked drawing trees and now she thought that “maybe, a tree could incorporate in here like a family tree.” Sofia merged her preference in drawing trees with a cultural norm according to which trees symbolize “family trees.” With her choice to “fill” some of the roots and not others, Sofia also created her own symbolism: the level of extension of the roots’ color (not the color itself) symbolized a person’s fulfillment in life. Sofia mistakenly thought that “somewhere in the article it said that some people have fulfilled lives” (Sofia’s interview). Second transformation: Drawing into static sculptures The case of Sofia: From the “tree of life” to “DNA dancing” Initially, Sofia agreed to represent a tree for the collaborative representation. When the members of the group positioned themselves in space, she tried to move like a tree and after 15 seconds, she said to her peers that she did not want to depict a tree (video records). Carlos suggested that she should represent DNA instead, and Sofia tried this option by “cross[ing] [her] arms and legs to make it look like [she] was coiled as a DNA,” as she had seen in drawings of DNA (Sofia’s interview). Sofia reported her “DNA dance” (our term) as fun to do during her interview, and she attributed her movements to her experience as a ballet dancer: “it's just after you start doing this sort certain dance for like months and months, then you start picking up your hands and your positions to be a bit more inclined to that position on how you are.” The dynamic form of the DNA, as compared to the more static tree, aligned with how she preferred to move as a dancer. Sofia also reasoned about representing DNA, saying that “DNA is important” and that the group would not be able to relay any message to the audience without it (Sofia’s interview). For Sofia, the change of the representational medium also led to different representational tools at her disposition.

Third transformation: Static sculptures into dynamic sculptures In the initial frozen posture, Isabella put both her hands on her neck, as if she touched an imaginary necklace. Her face was frozen in an expression that showed excitement. Camila sat on Isabella’s left, and her hands were positioned as if she counted money. Carlos stood next to Camila. He held his palms projecting his hands, as if he threatened someone with an imaginary gun. Next to him sat Luis. Luis sat in a curved position, holding a pencil case, pretending this was a case for people to put money in. Sofia stood in the middle, crossing her hands, as if these were the strands of DNA. When the “sculptures” became interactive, Isabella took her hands off her neck and took a red sharpener, pretending it was a mobile phone that she used to text her friends about her new necklace. Camila used her pencil case as an imaginary phone. “Hi,” she said, and she froze in an expression of excitement. Carlos put his hands behind his back as if he got arrested, and he turned his face on the wall. Luis revealed a paper that wrote “needs money” from his jacket and he spoke in a weak voice: “Money, money.” Sofia moved her hands and froze in a position with her right hand up and her left hand down. The case of Sofia: Hand down for downfall During the group’s rehearsal, Sofia moved her whole body in something that seemed like a DNA dance, and ended in a posture in which she had “one hand down on the side that Carlos and Luis were and the other hand up on the side that Camila and Isabella were” (Sofia’s interview). She explained that she wanted to show “that Carlos and Luis had a bit more of a downfall than Isabella and Camila.” The rehearsal video shows that Sofia “discovered” the gesture through experimenting with her body first, rather than first thinking of this idea and then applying it to her body. Sofia was using an embodied metaphor. According to Lakoff and Johnson (1980), humans understand abstract concepts using their bodily experiences and this is mirrored in the metaphors they use when they talk. Lakoff and Johnson suggest, for example, that our familiarity with the upright position produces the “up/down” conceptual metaphors; “up” is unconsciously perceived as “having control” while “down” is considered as “being subject to control” (e.g., upper social class, she has the upper hand, I am feeling down today, etc.). Interestingly, at this point Sofia’s gesturing of some people’s “downfall” and other’s good fortune indexed her first metaphor (the tree with some roots colored all the way and others non-colored). Just as Sofia had transformed the article’s word “successful lives” to “fulfilled lives” in alignment with her choice to fill the roots, she now transformed the article’s word “failure” into “downfall,” in alignment with her choice to gesture downwards.

## Discussion

Overall, participants devised and enacted creative strategies for generating, transforming, and communicating representations that supplemented the verbal-representations which dominate in traditional schooling. They created and used different kinds of metaphors to communicate their intended message, infused their previous experiences and personal preferences to their representations, considered the audience, reasoned about their decisions, and adapted their strategies to the constraints and affordances of the different ICLS2022 Proceedings 1103 © ISLS representational media. Particularly embodied collaborative representations and use of the surrounding objects in imaginative ways supported non-verbal communication and creative collaboration among the members of the group and enforced the use of embodiment that is rarely encouraged in conventional science classrooms. Therefore, students were able to construct and share meaning among themselves and their audience by using multiple rather than only propositional forms of representations, challenging a dominant schooling assumption that meaning making relies mostly on words.

The present study confirms that learners already possess rich resources for representing (Azevedo, 2000; Danish & Enyedy, 2007). Students used color to create symbolisms in the representational medium of drawing, created symmetry and contrast in space with their bodies, and communicated meaning with non-verbal ways, such as gestures. Embodied representations both facilitated students’ collaborative interactions (verbal and non-verbal) and provided different possibilities of constructing meaning. This aligns with embodied cognition theories that suggest that humans express abstract concepts through movement, without the need for verbal representations (Lakoff & Johnson, 2008). Students’ experiences and movements affected not only their transformations during the representations, but also their word choices when they reasoned for their decisions (Sofia’s case when talking about “fulfilled” lives and “some peoples’ downfall” being the most obvious example).
