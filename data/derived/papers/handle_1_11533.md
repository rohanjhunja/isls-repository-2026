# Exploring Hidden Affordances of Modeling Infrastructures for Epistemic Agency

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In science curricula, there are tensions between providing access to disciplinary practices and empowering students’ epistemic agency. To address this tension, we present a case of a student’s work with a computational model. The student’s creative and intuitive work deviated from the trajectory intended by the curriculum. By illustrating how the student’s resistance to classroom activities produced meaningful learning, our analysis will challenge what researchers and educators might consider “on task” or “productive” engagement.

Objectives Science education has increasingly attended to equi table learning: providing students with access to rich and generative disciplinary practices, like modeling, while also cultivating epistemologically diverse forms of inquiry (Schwarz et al., 2022). One approach to supporting equitable learning involves fosterin g epistemic agency: students’ power to shape the knowledge and practices in their classrooms (Stroupe, 2014). While many curricula are designed to empower students to be “epistemic agents,” Miller et al. (2018) note that they can instead position students as “pseudo-agents” who reproduce disciplinary knowledge and pr actices in the pursuit of a “shared knowledge product” (Berland et al., 2016, p. 8). We  argue that equitable modeling demands heterogenei ty (emphasizing multiple perspectives of phenomena acr oss representations; Pierson et al., 2023). We see heterogeneity not only as a stepping-stone toward disciplinary learning, but also as a valued endpoint. We present a case  in which a student enacted ep istemic agency: a 6th grade student interacting wit h a n agent-based computational model (ABM). ABMs have been used in scientific inquiry to simulate complex systems dynamics (e.g., of ecosystems), but also to produce compelli ng visual effects that highlight emergence, pattern, and structure, either in service of enhancing simulatio ns or for aestheti c reasons. We highlight a particular form of epistemic agency in which the student’s exploration resonated with these histories while al so deviating from the trajectory intended by the curriculum. We call such em ergent connections “alliances,” and our analysis ai ms to understand how student behaviors that could be view ed as “resistance” to classroom activities were in fact developing multidimensional, personally meaningful and generative alliances with these data infrastructures.

## Theoretical framework

Miller et al. (2018) described “epistemic agency” a s drawing on constructivist roots to position stude nts as knowledge-builders (e.g., Scardamalia & Bereiter, 1991) and o n sociocultural theories to describe how students shape resources for their goals (e.g., Varelas et a l., 2015). Supporting students’ agency requires researchers and educators to center students’ voices by empowering them to shape investigations with their own languag es, perspectives, understandings, and practices that mi ght not conform to normative disciplinary expectati ons (Kayumova & Sengupta, 2022). This approach aligns w ith calls for cultivating epistemic pluralism by va luing multiple ways of knowing (Warren et al., 2020).  Yet, students who resist prescribed classroom activities are often seen as “off-task” rather than as contributing to epistemic hete rogeneity. Langer-Osuna et al. (2020) described how apparently off-task behavior in mathematics classrooms can in fact serve socio-mathematical ends, regulating the dynamics of collaboration and the accessibility  of opportunities to exert disciplinary agency. The ir work challenges any division between the social and the disciplinar y—even interactions that may seem like evident diversions can contribute to epistemic progress. Ou r analyses in this paper consider how apparently of f-task interactions between students and modeling infrastructures can also be evidence of alliances that support students’ epistemic agency.

## Data sources and methods

We analyzed data from a student who resisted classroom activities and instead explored modeling infrastructures in multidimensional, meaningful ways. The study was conducted in a publi c middle school in a small suburbandistrict in the southeastern US in collaboration wi th a 6th grade STEM teacher, Ms. S. The unit was designed to center modeling and ecology standards, with ABMs as a central data infrastructure. Data were collected from a variety of sources to triangulate perspectives of students’ interactions with modeling infrastructures. We primarily relied on video and audio recordings of students and their computer screens, using whole-class video and student artifacts as secondary data sources. We analyzed data by transcribing video recordings of focal students interacting with their models. Because students were working on tasks that involved several streams of visual and quantitative output from the models, discourse was interactive a nd multimodal (Norris & Jones, 2005). Thus, we anal yzed discourse as units of mediated action. To represent  simultaneous classroom interactions in the transcr ipts, we created interactive stanzas, drawing from Gee (2014) and Norris and Jones (2005). To consider how focal students enacted agency to explore their models and resist classroom modeling practices simultaneously, we analyzed their actions in terms of interactive footing, describing how students positioned themselves and  others in the flow of activity (Goodwin, 2007).

## Findings and discussion

We follow a 6th grade student, Hunter, demonstrating a gency and resistance in his interaction with a computational ABM over the course of 9 class sessions. Hunter participated in a curriculum that aimed to leverage an ABM to explore agent actions, as well as to stud y system-level phenomena as emergent from these agent behaviors (e.g., Dickes et al., 2016; Wilensky & St roup, 1999). Specifically, Hunter’s class  was instructed to program and manipulate models to explore population  dynamics within ecosystems fr om the perspective of guppies. Yet, beyond being used to represent comple x systems in science education, ABMs have also been  used to attend to aesthetic relationships (e.g., space, structure, symmetries; Reas & Fry, 2007). Hunter in tuitively discovered these aesthetic affordances, and his creative exploration was generative for science learning and for aesthetic inquiry, despite deviating from classroom objectives and activities. After ABMs were introduced in his class, Hunter rep eatedly disengaged from whole-class and small-group activities (apparently “off-task” behavior), choosing instead to interact with his model motivated by the visual display. For instance, after the rest of  the class had worked on programming one guppy to sw im “realistically,” Ms. S led a discussion about how different model s could help students understand guppies’ survival. Rather than participating, Hunter explore d the aesthetic affordances of his single-guppy model, adding multiple instances of new agent types distinguished  by shape and color to create “circles” and “patter ns.” As he worked, he demonstrated surprise and joy with facial expres sions and by attempting to capture the attention of the student next to him by turning his screen and g esturing. In this case, Hunter’s exploration was a form of alliance with the modeling infrastructure that was meaningful because it enabled him to (a) di scover affordances of ABMs as tools for aesthetic exploration, (b) ask  questions about the mathematical attributes of his  programs and the “patterns” he created, and (c) experience a nd follow up on his curiosity. While this behavior resisted the intended learning activity, it was treated as valuable by the researcher and teacher (the researcher followed up on Hunter’s question about “why [the guppies] always s tart in a circle” in an extended discussion; and th e teacher approached Hunter to ask, “I heard you did something really beautiful, can I see it?”). Throughout the unit, Hunter’s exploration of his mo del continued to deviate from the classroom’s path. For instance, after the episode described above, students were instructed to follow directions in a pa cket to add features to their models (e.g., add alg ae for guppies to eat, add energy trackers to model  an ecosystem). Hunter rushed to complete these steps, which he described as “boring,” in order to return to his exploration of the modeling infrastructure. His objective was to see w hat would happen under  different conditions (e.g., adding thousands of guppies), and he became interested in the capacity of the 3D render of the modeling platf orm (e.g., the relationship between the number of guppies disp layed and frames per second), raising questions abo ut aesthetics and computer science. In part to control t he number of guppies represented, he added a predat or. His objectives deviated from the teacher and researcher ’s prompts to make the model “more realistic;” in f act, when another student suggested that Hunter add baby guppies to make the system “more realistic,” Hunter rejected the suggestion because he felt it was not “interesting. ” He instead explored other programming possibiliti es (e.g., how to make guppies “bounce off” obstacles like rocks; how to make a “game” of the predator eating the guppies). These activities were generative for Hunter’s learning ab out aesthetics, science, and computer science as a connected network of ideas, yet also led to tensions  when the curriculum, researcher, teacher, and ev en other students pushed back against Hunter’s deviation from the classroom’s activity. In this case, Hunter appeared to exhibit “off task”  behavior that “resisted” the flow of classroom investigation. However, this appearance masked deep engagement with a modeling infrastructure, which was not only motivated by personally meaningful lines of  inquiry, but also connected to rich practices histo rically associated with this infrastructure. By “building alliances” with these modeling tools, Hunter developed forms of inquiry that were both transdisciplinary and personally meaningful. Yet, we argue that alliance-building might be encouraged even when it does not yield such immediately evident fruit. In particular, classroom designs that prioritize heterogeneity in service of equitable le arning might invite such alliance-building as a way for students to articulate their perspectives with models.
