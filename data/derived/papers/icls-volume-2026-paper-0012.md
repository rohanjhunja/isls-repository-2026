# Elementary Students’ Developing Understanding of Fractions: Insights from Students’ Gestures

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite being a foundational topic in elementary school mathematics, fractions persistently challenge students. The purpose of this study was to examine how elementary school students’ gestures convey the varied ways they conceive of fractions, beyond what could be interpreted from their speech alone. Interviews with 21 3 rd-to 5th-grade students indicated that most students described fractions as the division of a whole object into equal parts (i.e., the area model), in both their speech and gestures. But, in many cases, students’ gestures provided additional, necessary context from which to interpret their spoken explanations —providing insight into the grounding metaphors that framed their abstract thinking about fr actions. Also, although students expressed understanding of fraction notation, this was done either in isolation or in relation to the area model. Findings point to enriching instruction that helps students bridge fraction notation to different and multiple models.

Early fraction skills are a key predictor of later overall mathematical success (Siegler et al., 2016), yet we know that students —and even adults —can have difficulties understanding fractions. In this study, we focus on elementary-school students’ gestures with the goal  of examining what students’ gestures can tell us about their emerging understanding of fractions beyond what we can learn from their spoken explanations. We also consider the instructional context given its influence on students’ fraction knowledge. By analyzing how students express fraction knowledge multimodally, this work can inform instruction that takes account of students’ understanding, even when they cannot express this understanding in words. Challenges in learning fractions Although fraction knowledge is a key predictor for overall mathematical success, even when holding constant other mathematical knowledge and non-cognitive factors (Siegler et al., 2012), understanding fractions remains a challenge for students across ages (Liu & Jacobson, 2022; Siegler, 2016) and countries (e.g., Hariyani et al., 2022; Siegler et al., 2012; Torbeyns et al., 2015). Contributing to this difficulty is that fractions are composed of natural numbers in the numerator and the denominator, which in turn can cause processing interference (Kallai & Tzelgov, 2012), leading learners to activate whole number representations. This results in the tendency to attribute natural number properties to fractions (i.e., whole number bias, Ni & Zhou, 2005). Researchers (e.g., Siegler et al., 2013) have suggested that the fraction properties themselves might not be the root cause of students’ difficulty with fractions because students have shown an early and intuitive grasp of non-symbolic conceptual and procedural fraction knowledge. Instead, such difficulty has been argued to come from interpreting  symbolic representations of fractions (e.g., Mix et al., 1999). Thus, understanding how students map mathematical ideas to representations offers a promising path to address this challenge.

Theoretical grounding: Origins of fraction knowledge in sensorimotor cognition Researchers and theorists have long found that students’ earliest knowledge, in infancy, is formed by action in the world and action on objects (e.g., Piaget, 1950). From this early knowledge, Lakoff and Núñez (2000), in particular, proposed that numerical knowledge also is possible through body-based metaphors that underlie  the conceptual system (i.e., grounding metaphors). These metaphors allow us to build on our basic and innate mathematical knowledge gradually to form more sophisticated ideas  and enable mapping the source domain— action and perceptual experiences—to the target domain —mathematical concepts. The first grounding metaphor, Arithmetic as Object Collection, maps experiences with object collections to numbers, thereby grounding basic arithmetic understanding of whole numbers and preserving mathematical properties. The Arithmetic as Object Construction metaphor continues to map numbers to our experiences with objects but the objects consist of unit-sized parts, which can support fraction knowledge. The Measuring Stick metaphor maps numbers to physical segments consisting of unit length, which extends to understanding continuous numbers. Arithmetic as Motion Along a Path is a sophisticated metaphor that maps numbers to point32 locations, which affords understanding of negative numbers and interprets zero as a point-location, as opposed to the “absence of.” By layering these grounding metaphors gradually, meaning is given to mathematical abstractions (Lakoff & Núñez, 2000). Similarly, Nathan (2008) posits that students must enter a grounding process in which they map abstract mathematical ideas and symbols to sensorimotor experiences. Expressing mathematical knowledge through gestures One rich, yet underused, channel that can uncover students’ fraction concepts is gestures. Gestures are spatial and imagistic manifestations that embody our ideas by partially re-enacting perceptual and sensorimotor states (Hostetter & Alibali, 2008; 2019). As a result, gesture is a powerful data source that can reveal details of students’ emerging knowledge, compared to words or symbolic notations, especially when students may not yet be able to express their ideas in speech (Perry et al., 1988; Singer, 2017; Soto-Johnson & Troup, 2014). Previous studies examining the spontaneous gestures students produce have shown that students rely less on objects and more on gestures as their mathematical knowledge advances (Cho & So, 2018). In fact, simply encouraging students to gesture increases their likelihood to add new correct strategies —often expressed in gesture alone—in  solving mathematical problems, enabling them to gain more from subsequent instruction (Broaders et al., 2007). This positive outcome likely stems from gesture’s ability to activate and reveal emerging, implicit knowledge (Goldin-Meadow & Wagner, 2005). Moreover, gestures often reveal knowledge not yet conveyed in speech (e.g., Goldin-Meadow, 2000), which makes it crucial to uncover the information students express in gesture, but not speech.

To our knowledge, only two studies have scrutinized gestures to explore fraction  concepts. First, Edwards (2009) interviewed pre-service elementary school teachers and found that they primarily explained— and gestured—about fractions in three ways: (1) as a part of a whole; (2) as a physical object; and (3) as a symbolic notation or written inscription. Second, Swart et al. (2014) interviewed 3 rd-and 4th-grade students (ages 8-10) and identified roughly 30 different gestures including shading, slicing, swiping, and tracing, among others, replicating and expanding on Edwards’ (2009) findings. With this study, we seek to contribute to this literature by usi ng general, open-ended prompts that do not include pictorial representations or symbolic notations, whereas prior studies did. This is an important innovation, given that the types of fractions and tasks used to frame a lesson can influence the type of  gestures and verbal explanations employed by students (Alibali & Kita, 2010; Obersteiner et al., 2013). We acknowledge that students will have come into our study with prior knowledge from classroom instruction, so in the next section, we briefly review common instructional practices used in U.S. classrooms (the context for the current investigation) during fraction lessons. Classroom opportunities for grounding and their influence in students’ fraction knowledge In U.S. classrooms, teachers often use manipulatives to provide opportunities for grounding fractions. These manipulatives are used to represent the area model, set model, measurement model, and number line. In the area model, a whole shape is partitioned into equal segments or parts (Common Core State Standards Initiative, 2015). The set model uses sets or collections of objects, each representing fractions of the whole (Neagoy, 2017). The measurement model often involves bars or paper strips where, instead of partitioning, a segment from the origin of the whole is measured (Simon et al., 2018). Finally, the number line model, which is highly recommended yet largely underused (Liu & Jacobson, 2022), converges with the measurement model but involves placing fractions on the number line. Importantly, the area and set models support a discrete-magnitude representation, while the measurement model and number line represent continuous quantities (Neagoy, 2017). Exposing students to a variety of concrete and visual models supports flexible fraction understandings (Cramer et al., 2002; Newton et al., 2020), including different interpretations of fraction symbols (e.g., part-whole, ratio, division, or measurement). However, expanding students’ representational repertoires can sometimes come at the cost of students’ abilities to employ each representation accurately (Liu & Jacobson, 2022). This may speak to the need to situate these models within the broader context of grounding metaphors that highlight different properties of fractions.

Current investigation The current study examines students’ enacted fraction concepts. We conducted interviews by prompting students to describe their understanding of fractions without providing any fraction tasks, symbols, objects, or perceptually available representation s that could favor the types of f raction models students might activate. By analyzing students’ gestures and speech, both when the se match and when the se mismatch, we show that students enact grounded metaphors  thereby revealing underlying fraction cognition. Findings from this study extend the 33 documented utility of gesture as both a mode of meaning-making and a mode that carries distinct conceptual content to a new topic, fractions.

## Method

Sample and procedures Our sample consisted of 21 3rd-to 5th-grade students (7.6 to 10.1 years, x̄  = 8.8, s =.9 years) from three classrooms at one public elementary school in the Midwestern U nited States: 7 were learning 3 rd-grade mathematics, 6 4 thgrade mathematics, and 8 5 th-grade mathematics. Additionally, 76% were female; 29% were White, 62% Asian, <10% Black, and <10% Multiracial. All students had been formally introduced to fraction notation. We conducted open-ended interviews to observe the range of students’ fraction concepts by asking the following questions: (1) What does the word fraction mean to you? (2) If you were to explain what a fraction is to a friend, how would you do it? Interviews were conducted without pictures, diagrams, or manipulatives, so as not to prime student responses, and instead to probe what students were thinking in the absence of external supports or materials. Additionally, both to promote students’ spontaneous gesture production and to ensure students’ thinking was as fully conveyed as possible, the interviewer provided follow-up prompts such as, “Can you show me what you mean?” (Lindgren et al., 2016). The interviewer also took care to refrain from gesturing during questioning. All these design choices were implemented to minimize task constraints and interviewer effects. Interviews were conducted outside of regular class time during separate research sessions, and on average, interviews lasted 2 min 12 sec, ranging from 1 min 20 sec to 3 min 35 sec (s = 31.6 sec). Data coding and analysis First, we examined students’ gesture and speech separately (e.g., Church & Goldin-Meadow, 1986; Perry et al.,

1988) because gesture and speech can express different affordances in students’ understanding of fractions

(Edwards & Robutti, 2014). Then, we analyzed students’ gesture and speech together. The goal here was to attend to what each of these modalities contributed as part of each student’s multimodal system (Arzarello et al., 2009; Radford, 2009). When identifying gestures, we followed McNeill’s (1992) method. Gesture phrases began with hands raising and ended with hands resting; a change in motion or handshape indicated a new clause within the phrase. Speech phrases were identified as all of the talk that followed the interviewer’s query, with pauses indicating a new clause within the phrase. Gestured and spoken phrases were defined and organized into thematic categories (see below), which represent the range of concepts observed during the interviews across the modalities of gesture and speech.

After the initial coding schemes for gesture and  for speech  were created, the authors and another researcher with expertise in mathematics education each worked with a subset of the data (30% of the muted video-recordings and 35% of the speech-only transcripts) to double code the interviews and refine the codebook. We obtained strong agreement (McHugh, 2012) for both the gesture-and speech-only analyses [Cohen’s (1960) κ =.93 for gesture and κ =.81 for speech]. One of the authors coded the remaining data. Disagreements found in the double-coded data as well as ambiguous cases were reconciled through discussions. When we had ambiguous cases in the subset of data set aside for reliability, we replaced these. We first describe how students expressed fraction concepts, analyzed separately, for gesture (Table 1) and for speech (Table 2). Second, we analyzed gesture and speech together, to document how they might represent the same conceptual approach, followed by when they mismatch and present distinct approaches.  We do this to consider the interplay and affordances of each communicative modality and how, together, they express grounding metaphors. Throughout this section, we draw from interviews with students, for whom pseudonyms are used, as illustrative examples.

Gestural constraints and variations in depicting fraction concepts A fundamental definition of a fraction refers to dividing a whole object into equal parts. Consistent with previous studies that examined gestures of young students (Swart et al., 2014) and preservice teachers (Edwards, 2009) during their fractions talk, s tudents in our study produced gestures that reflected the literal action of dividing a whole object into equal parts, predominantly with slicing or cutting gestures. These iconic-physical gestures seem to emerge when students talked  about fractions, regardless of whether the prompts involved problem-solving (Edwards, 2009; Swart et al., 2014), drawings (Swart et al., 2014), or only verbal prompts, as in our study. Cumulatively, the evidence indicates that when people talk about fractions, they heavily draw from this area representation across contexts, ages, and levels of expertise. Our data also included variations within this iconic-physical gesture depicting the area model: Students’ gestures referenced the action of breaking apart a whole by first indicating a whole (e.g., circle), then sweeping their hands out. Another variation depicted a whole, a subsequent enactment of breaking down the whole by elevating one hand up and the other one down (resembling fraction notation), followed with a repeated slicing and sweeping motion on the downward hand. This was an unusual and elaborated depiction in contrast to the 38 cutting motion. These variations suggest that even within the category of Whole  Object and Part s, there is a potential flexibility in representing how the whole is divided. This study included the identification of one category, Composition/Decomposition of Numbers, which emerged from our analysis of speech, but not gestures, yielding mismatches across modalities.  Previous studies have found that gestures often convey non-redundant information that complements or conflicts with speech. For instance, students expressed an addition equivalence strategy uniquely in gestures (Broaders et al., 2007) or expressed different strategies in gesture than in speech during a Piagetian conservation task (Ping & GoldinMeadow, 2010). In the present investigation we often documented complementarity between speech and gesture: Students’ accompanying gestures enacted grounding metaphors suggestive of their fraction concepts, rather than the procedural approaches or strategies for problem solving  that others have found when examining students’ understanding of mathematical concepts (e.g., Perry et al., 1988). In this study, as in previous work (e.g., Broaders & Goldin-Meadow, 2010), we found that gesture may convey information that may be difficult to communicate through speech alone.

Considering students’ curriculum and students’ gestural manifestations Following the extant literature’s suggestion of exposing students to various concrete and visual models to support flexible fraction understanding (Cramer et al., 2002; Newton et al., 2020), the curriculum used in the school where we recruited the students for this study, Everyday Mathematics, features four of our five categories: Whole Object, Parts, Fraction Notation, and Number Line (UCSMP, 2016). In our study, we observed that the majority of students only described the area model in their explanations, documenting their reliance on this model across grade levels, despite different grounding opportunities provided by the curriculum. And although the curriculum asks students to consider fractions that are greater than 1 from as early as 3 rd grade, only one student mentioned that fractions can be larger than 1. In fact, most students (mis)conceived of fractions as being bounded by 0 and

1. Scholars suggest that the dual identity of a fraction (i.e., a fraction as an object and a rational number) can be a

source of confusion (Edwards, 2009). Perhaps some metaphors, such as Measuring Stick or Motion Along a Path, are better suited to ground this fraction duality. Confirming this, researchers have found that the number line model was correlated with higher mathematical achievement and transfer than the area model (Hamdan & Gunderson, 2017).

## Implications for mathematics instruction

We offer several classroom implications. First, giving students the opportunity to use their bodies to explain fractions offers teachers an  opportunity to “see fractions” as the student does. Previous studies have confirmed that paying attention to students’ speech alone could leave teachers with incomplete information. I nstead, attending to gestures can provide teachers a richer account of students’ understanding (e.g., Goldin-Meadow & Singer, 2003), especially when students may not have yet developed the language to articulate their ideas (Özçalişkan & Goldi n-Meadow, 2005; Roth, 2001; Singer, 2017). Our results support this line of research, as students’ gestures often revealed more detail about their fraction concepts when compared to their speech. Second, these results point to the need to study  how instruction can support students’ consideration of multiple fraction models. New studies might scrutinize how to give greater emphasis to multiple models, how to connect multiple models to each other and to their related grounding metaphors, and perhaps even explicitly helping students see how some of these models afford certain fraction attributes.

## Context

Formative assessment and principles of learning sciences 42 Formative assessment is a process by which teachers gather and use evidence from student work to inform instructional decisions, better address students’ needs, and support ongoing learning (Klute et al., 2017; Lembke & Stecker, 2007; Martin et al., 2022; Black & Wiliam, 2009; Cizek et al., 2019). Formative assessment is not about the task itself, but about how the teacher uses the results to adapt instruction. It can range from informal techniques, like asking probing questions during a lesson, to more structured approaches like reviewing student quiz data or analyzing trends in homework performance (Irons & Elkington, 2021; Klute et al., 2017; Black & Wiliam, 2009; Cizek et al., 2019). Recent studies have highlighted the positive impact of formative assess ment on student achievement in mathematics, emphasizing its role in identifying misconceptions and adapting instruction in real time (Misfeldt et al., 2024; Andersson & Palm, 2017). Formative assessment is closely tied to fundamental principles of the learning sciences. Research in learning sciences emphasizes that learning is an active and iterative process, strengthened when students receive opportunities to make their thinking visible, obtain timely feedback, and revise their understanding (Bransford, Brown, & Cocking, 2000; NCSEM, 2018). This perspective highlights the centrality of formative assessment practices, which are designed to generate evidence of student understanding during instruction and to provide feedback that guides subsequent learning.

One key connection lies in the role of feedback loops. Learning sciences research shows that timely and targeted feedback enhances both motivation and achievement by helping students close the gap between current and desired performance (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). Formative assessment provides the structure for such feedback loops, allowing teachers to adjust instruction in real time and enabling students to act on feedback through revision and practice (Black & Wiliam, 2009). Thus, formative assessment translates theoretical insights about feedback from learning sciences into actionable classroom practices. Formative assessment also operationalizes the learning sciences principle of surfacing and addressing misconceptions. Decades of research demonstrate that students often hold robust but incorrect ideas that can impede learning if not explicitly confronted (Chi, 2008). Through formative assessment techniques such as questioning, exit tickets, or analysis of student work, teachers can uncover these misconceptions and design instructional responses to support conceptual change. In this way, formative assessment becomes a bridge between research on conceptual development and the practical work of teaching. Finally, formative assessment fosters metacognition and self-regulated learning, both of which are strongly emphasized in learning sciences research. Students learn more effectively when they monitor their own progress, reflect on strategies, and take ownership of their learning process (Zimmerman, 2002). Formative assessment encourages such metacognition by making learning goals explicit and providing information that students can use to evaluate and improve their own work. In doing so, it not only informs  instruction but also empowers students as active participants in their learning. From learning sciences to classroom practice: Technology’s role in teaching Research in the learning sciences has long examined how advances in technology reshape teaching practices and instructional design. Recent work shows that educational technologies, including artificial intelligence, are reshaping instructional design and redefining teachers’ roles in supporting student learning (Reiser et al., 2024) while also changing how teachers organize instruction and provide feedback to students (Holmes, Bialik, & Fadel, 2019). However, many of these innovations were examined in short  curricular units or with a small number of teachers since installing and maintaining the necessary technology at scale posed significant challenges. In recent years, the broader adoption of technology has reduced many of these barriers, enabling teachers to integrate advanced instructional supports across an entire school year. Modern platforms can collect student responses in real time, generate reports, and highlight patterns in learning that might otherwise go unnoticed (Abramovich et al., 2025; Ganimian et al., 2020; Hillmayr et al., 2020; Reinhold et al., 2020; Serin, 2023). By presenting timely and accessible information, these systems help teachers diagnose misunderstandings, identify gaps, and adjust instruction accordingly (Reinhardt et al., 2022). They also facilitate differentiation by enabling student grouping, targeted feedback, and individualized practice (Klute et al., 2017; Mendicino et al., 2009) — supports that are especially important in classrooms with high student–teacher ratios or limited planning time. Technology, however, is not a panacea. Digital tools do not guarantee improved outcomes on their own; their effectiveness depends on how well they are embedded into teachers’ instructional routines and aligned with curricular goals. Teachers may face difficulties interpreting data reports, acting on insights, or balancing the added demands of new systems. Without sufficient professional learning and sustained support, technology can even increase workload. Thus, while digital platforms offer significant opportunities to strengthen formative assessment, their impact ultimately depends on how teachers adopt them in practice and on the broader instructional systems that sustain their use.

43 A technology-based formative assessment program: ASSISTments This paper focuses on the vPLC-augmented ASSISTments program, an intervention that integrates two essential components: (1) the ASSISTments platform, which provides student-facing practice and teacher-facing tools, and

(2) a vPLC designed to strengthen instructional practice.

The ASSISTments platform The ASSISTments platform (Heffernan & Heffernan, 2014) is built on formative assessment principles, enabling students to complete mathematics problems digitally while providing teachers with immediate insights into student proficiency (Heritage & Popham, 2013). Teachers can assign problems drawn from Open Educational Resources (OER) such as Illustrative Mathematics and EngageNY, either from existing item banks or by creating their own problems. Teachers can choose to assign students regular assignments, whi ch are standards-aligned problem sets from OER curricula, or Skill Builders, which are problem sets focusing on a single skill that students complete until they reach a teacher-defined mastery threshold (e.g., three correct answers in a row). Students typically work on problems independently on paper first and then input answers into ASSISTments, where they receive immediate feedback (a green check mark for a correct answer). Students can also request support including hints, explanations, and step-by-step scaffolds, and they can attempt problems multiple times until they answer correctly.

Teachers receive detailed reports summarizing class and student performance, percent correct by problem, common wrong answers, and alignment with standards, as well as each student’s individual responses to each problem. These data allow teachers to track the knowledge base of their classes, adjust pacing, differentiate instruction, form small groups, and plan remediation. Teacher can also project reports in an anonymized form that masks student names to facilitate whole-class discussions of common errors a nd drive data-informed problem review.

The ASSISTments “four steps” of formative assessment To promote consistent implementation, ASSISTments is organized as a four-step routine of formative assessment:

1. Assign: Teachers assign students standards-aligned problem sets (regular assignments or Skill

Builders).

2. Practice with Feedback: Students complete problems independently, receiving hints, scaffolds, and

immediate feedback on correctness.

3. Assess: Teachers analyze reports to identify patterns, common errors, misconceptions, and student

needs to inform instructional decision-making.

4. Review: Teachers project anonymized reports and use data from the reports to inform which problems

should be reviewed to support class-wide discussions.

Prior evidence of efficacy ASSISTments was an attractive intervention to study because it had strong prior evidence, it demonstrated high fidelity of implementation and had the capacity to change teacher practices. Two large-scale studies provide the strongest evidence. In one U.S. state, Maine, a year-long randomized study across 43 schools showed that 12-13year-olds (U.S. 7th graders) using ASSISTments outperformed peers on standardized math assessments, with gains equivalent to moving from the 50th to the 58th percentile (Roschelle et al., 2016). In another U.S. state, North Carolina, a replication study across 66 schools found that students exposed to ASSISTments in 7th grade continued to perform better on state math assessments in 8th grade (age 13-14 years) compared to students  who received business-as-usual instruction (Feng et al., 2025). These effects were especially notable for lower-performing students (Roschelle et al., 2016) and those in schools serving higher percentages of economically disadvantaged learners (Feng et al., 2023), and they were accompanied by shifts in teacher practice toward more targeted and data-informed instruction (Fairman et al., 2015, 2025). A new component In earlier ASSISTments implementations, teachers received in-person training and individualized coaching via classroom visits, which was effective but costly and difficult to scale. These models also provided limited opportunities for collaboration among teachers. The current intervention introduced a virtual professional learning community (vPLC) as a scalable and community-centered alternative. The  vPLC consisted of eight 75-minute sessions across the school year, supplemented by asynchronous activities. Sessions followed a facilitation guide and included a welcome activity, teacher stories, group discussions, demonstrations of ASSISTments features or math pedagogy resources, and opportunities for asking questions and reflection. Key themes included 44 transitioning from paper-based practice to ASSISTments, using reports and common wrong answers to inform instruction, emphasizing mistakes as a valuable part of learning, and supporting student motivation and engagement. The vPLC aimed to sustain and enhance the instructional impact of ASSISTments while fostering teacher collaboration, reflection, and professional growth—particularly in rural and under-resourced settings. The national impact study A national study was conducted during the 2021-22 (Cohort 1) and 2022-23 (Cohort 2) school years to investigate the impact of the vPLC-augmented ASSISTments intervention. The main hypothesis was that middle school students in classrooms that use ASSISTments for independent practice and formative assessment would have higher math achievement at the end of the year than those in a business-as-usual comparison group. Setting and sample: Teachers and students across the U.S. This study involved 59 middle school teachers (U.S. grades 6-8) and 2,855 students (aged 11-14 years) from 36 schools across 24 states. The sample reflected diverse demographic and geographic contexts: 18 schools were in rural areas, and 23 qualified for Title I funding, a federal program that provides supplemental resources to schools serving high concentrations of students from low-income families. More than half of the students (55%) were eligible for free or reduced-price lunch, an indicator for low socioeconomic status. Many schools had recently experienced declines in mathematics performance, and participating teachers expressed strong interest in leveraging technology for learning recovery.

A key focus of this work was to extend the potential benefits of ASSISTments to rural teachers and students. Roughly one third of U.S. students attend rural schools, and rural districts comprise 53% of all school districts nationally (Johnson et al., 2014). Nationwide, 15.4% of school-aged children in rural areas live in poverty (Showalter et al., 2019), and students in these contexts often face unique challenges in accessing high-quality mathematics instruction. Traditional in-person professional learning models for teachers are difficult to implement in rural areas due to geographic distance, limited availability of substitutes, and fewer training options. Delivering in-person coaching would also require significant time and travel resources. The vPLC offered a more practical and scalable solution. During the study, ASSISTments successfully reached rural communities: half of the participating schools (18) were rural, representing 50% of the student sample.

The study also expanded the intervention to a national scale. Participating schools were distributed across the U.S, making the study truly national in scope (Figure 1). Unlike prior studies conducted in single states — Maine (Roschelle et al., 2016) and North Carolina (Feng et al., 2025)—this broader sample reflects a wider cross-section of American students. As a result, the findings are more generalizable and provide greater confidence that similar benefits could extend to low-performing students in other educational contexts across the country. ## Research design

This study employed a quasi-experimental design (QED) with a matched comparison group to ensure both rigor and meaningful participation. All participating teachers implemented the vPLC-augmented ASSISTments intervention. Each teacher completed a new-user training, joined the vPLC, and was asked to assign pre-built content through the ASSISTments platform as classwork or homework. To support data-driven instruction, teachers were expected to review student performance reports and use the data to inform instructional adjustments, including sharing results with students.

For the comparison condition, a virtual comparison group (VCG) was created by NWEA, a nonprofit educational organization that develops research-based assessments and provides data services for K –12 schools in the U.S. The VCG was formed using students’ bas eline scores on the NWEA MAP Growth mathematics 45 assessment (NWEA, 2019), which was administered to treatment students in the fall. Each treatment student was matched with up to 51 students drawn from NWEA’s national database who had taken the same assessment during the same school year. Matching criteri a included school locale, the percentage of students eligible for free or reduced-price lunch at the school level, test administration dates, grade level, and pre-test scores. The average performance of these matched students was then used as the counterfactual outcome for the corresponding treatment student. The use of a VCG allowed us to construct a large, demographically and academically comparable control group while avoiding the practical challenges of recruiting and managing control schools across mul tiple states. This approach provided a rigorous counterfactual, despite the absence of random assignment, and has been used in previous studies (e.g., Mohammed & Fauteux, 2018; Pane et al., 2017).

## Measures

The study collected a rich set of measures capturing both student outcomes and implementation data. Student Outcomes. Mathematics achievement and prior performance were assessed in the fall and spring of each school year using the online MAP Growth Mathematics adaptive assessment provided by NWEA, which has demonstrated strong psychometric properties (NWEA, 2019). Each administration required approximately 45 to 60 minutes. After testing and matching procedures, NWEA provided the research team with a dataset containing scores for both treatment students and the VCG. The primary outcome measure was the derived Rasch Unit (RIT) scale score, based on the Rasch item response theory (IRT) model, which served as the indicator of students’ mathematics learning.

Implementation Measures.  Multiple sources captured how teachers and students engaged with ASSISTments and the vPLC, including teacher pre-and post-surveys, instructional logs (four rounds during the school year with each covering five days of instruction), teacher and principal  interviews, and observations of selected classrooms. Additionally, for teachers, the platform tracked assignments created and reports accessed, including timing and assignment details. For students, the system recorded problem-solving actions with timestamps, assignment completion, and time spent in ASSISTments. These complementary measures enabled the research team to triangulate student achievement outcomes with detailed evidence of how the intervention was implemented in classrooms.

## Impact findings

We discuss the main impact findings briefly, for context. The full presentation of findings will be the subject of a project report and a forthcoming journal submission and is not reported in detail here. In this paper, we focus on whether variations were plausibly related to student achievement outcomes. In contrast to our initial hypothesis, the analysis did not show a statistically significant difference in mathematics achievement between students in the treatment group and those in the VCG. Across the full sample, treatment students scored slightly high er than comparison students on the end-of-year MAP Growth assessment after controlling for baseline achievement and student-and school-level covariates, but this difference was not significant. When examining subgroups, however, important effects emerged. Among low-performing students—defined as those scoring below the 50th percentile on the baseline assessment—those who received the ASSISTments intervention significantly outperformed their VCG counterparts on end-of-year MAP Growth (p = 0.02, effect size = 0.10 standard deviation). This finding suggests that the intervention was particularly beneficial for students who entered the school year with lower levels of mathematics achievement. A similar pattern of positive effects was also observed for students attending rural schools. Taken together, these results indicate that while the intervention did not produce broad gains across the full sample, it provided meaningful support to students who are often most in need, underscoring the potential of technology-enhanced formative assessment to advance educational equity. A deep dive into implementation of the four steps As described earlier, the ASSISTments intervention structures formative assessment into a four-step instructional cycle: Assign, Practice with Feedback, Assess, and Review. Teachers were introduced to the four steps through the vPLC and were expected to use ASSISTments to integrate these practices into their instruction. Together, the steps are designed to create a feedback loop in which students receive timely support while teachers gain actionable insights to guide instruction. Examining how teachers enacted each step in their classrooms provides a window into the mechanisms through which the intervention shaped teachers’ practices and contributed to promising learning gains observed for low-performing students. In this section, we draw on multiple data sources to examine the extent to which teachers implemented each step consistently, the variation in their enactment, and the ways in which the intervention supported instruction. 46 Step 1: Assign independent practice Teachers’ approaches to assigning independent practice evolved through their use of ASSISTments and participation in the vPLCs where teachers learned best practices for assigning independent practice, shared strategies in breakout discussions, and relied heavily on pre-built content within the platform. Interviews revealed that both the platform and the vPLC influenced how teachers structured math practice for their students. Across the year, teachers consistently implemented Step 1. Log data showed that students engaged in independent practice on 85.7% of reported days, with 68.8% of those days including work in ASSISTments. Postsurveys indicated that 42.7% of independent pra ctice was completed in ASSISTments, compared with 34.2% in other computer-based programs and 35.6% on paper. System data confirmed frequent use: on average, teachers made ASSISTments assignments in 24.5 weeks of the school year, totaling 69.5 assignments. These assignments not only provided students opportunities for practice and feedback but also generated data that informed subsequent steps of the formative assessment cycle.

Most assignments were given as classwork rather than homework, giving teachers real-time insight into students’ knowledgebase during class. Teachers logged classwork on 82.4% of days, while only 36.2% included homework. Post-surveys reflected the same pattern: 54.9% of practice was completed as classwork, versus 34.6% as homework. System data further showed that 70% of assignments were regular classwork sets rather than Skill Builders. Teachers often used these assignments at the end of class as exit tickets to check student understanding (59.0%), and less commonly as in-class practice (27.9%) or warm-ups (8.2%). Some teachers described assigning fewer but more targeted problems to individualize practice. As one teacher explained: “I think my view really changed with ASSISTments… three questions, if they’ve mastered it, they’re done. That was a big eye-opener—it’s fast and gives me the formative piece I need to drive my instruction forward.” The preference for classwork was influenced by unequal home internet and device access, especially in rural areas. On 39.6% of logged days, teachers reported that students’ connectivity affected whether they assigned homework, and nearly half of teachers (47.4%) confirmed this on the post-survey. Assigning classwork in ASSISTments ensured equitable access, allowing all students —particularly those in rural contexts —to practice math with built-in supports. On days when ASSISTments was used, 84.3% of assignments came from pre-built materials, compared with 43.4% from other online sources and 23.3% from teacher-created worksheets. Teachers reported feeling highly comfortable using ASSISTments to assign independent practice (mean = 4.69 on a 1 –5 scale) and found it easy to locate needed curriculum content (93.5%). Step 2: Student practice with feedback Teachers reported that the intervention changed how students engaged with independent practice. In interviews, many noted that students completed more work, showed greater motivation and confidence, worked more independently, and engaged in more deliberate practice. These changes were largely attributed to ASSISTments’ immediate feedback.

System data confirmed relatively high completion rates: in 64% of classes, most students (at least 75% of the class) completed two-thirds or more of their assigned problems. Over the school year, students averaged 350.8 minutes of use, completing  36 assignments  with a total of  342 problems. On average, they used ASSISTments 1.9 days per week, spending about 15 minutes per week on practice. As one teacher reflected: “The biggest difference is that immediate feedback. You [students] didn’t go on for days thinking you were right when you weren’t. That has been a big game changer for me.” During the interviews, many teachers also reported that their students became more self-directed in math. Students increasingly worked independently and took on more responsibility and ownership of their work. For example, one teacher explained: “[initially] they'd be calling me over immediately and like, ‘I need help.’ So, I think as they got more comfortable with ASSISTments and the features that it has, they were able to do the multiple attempts on their own… And that's why I find ASSISTments so helpful is because it's taking it off of me and it's making it their responsibility and their learning.” These shifts were particularly important for low-performing students. By completing more targeted problems with immediate feedback, students identified their misconceptions more quickly, experienced success, and built confidence —outcomes that would have be en less likely with traditional paper-based practice or platforms that assign problem sets without comparable supports. Together, these patterns set the stage for Step 3 47 (Assess), as teachers drew on the data generated from students’ practice to identify learning gaps and guide instructional adjustments.

Step 3: Assess class performance Teachers frequently reviewed ASSISTments reports to guide instruction and make data-informed decisions, a practice that they reported had changed substantially because of the intervention. These practices were especially valuable for low-performing students, as they enabled teachers to pinpoint knowledge gaps and provide targeted support. Survey and system data confirmed high levels of engagement with reports. Nearly all t eachers (93.0%) found ASSISTments data extremely or moderately helpful for addressing student challenges, and 92% reviewed at least half of their class’s assignment reports. Across the year, teachers examined reports on more than half of the days when ASSISTments or other reporting platforms were used (57.3%). Teachers used reports to gauge class understanding, identify misconceptions, and flag struggling students. They often adjusted their instruction in response: on 46.2% of logged days, teachers reported modifying their instructional plans after reviewing ASSISTments data. The most common changes were reteaching certain topics and filling learning gaps (65.8% of changes made; Figure 2), though teachers also used the data to provide targeted support for individual students (32.4%) or small groups (20.0%).

Teachers reflected on the process of using ASSISTments data to drive instruction: “...I would look at the Skill Builder results [...] using that really helped me kind of focus my instruction. I geared it more toward the students that needed extra help while I let the kids that had shown their mastery move on. And that changed, that was a big difference from the first year that I was teaching.” “I feel like it helps me to identify specifically what I need to do, whereas before I wasn't able to directly pinpoint where the problem was. These assessment practices also laid the groundwork for Step 4 (Review), where teachers used the data to structure whole-class discussions and collective problem solving. Step 4: Review Most teachers reported in teacher interviews that their approach to problem review changed as a result of the intervention. Nearly all teachers (94.8%, post-survey) reviewed math independent practice problems with their classes during the year, and on 47.3% of logged days with homework or classwork due, teachers engaged in whole-class review. A few teachers who had never reviewed independent practice problems that they had assigned begandoing so for the first time after starting to use ASSISTments. The nature of problem review also shifted. Following the vPLCs, teachers described their review process as more targeted, focusing on mistakes, missed problems, and misconceptions, and facilitating deeper class responses showed that 74.1% of teachers reported using ASSISTments reports to decide which problems to 48 review, and 89.7% projected reports for their classes during the school year. Teachers projected anonymized ASSISTments reports on 32.6% of logged days when reports were reviewed, enabling teachers to highlight common patterns in students’ error without singling out individuals. Teachers noted that this practice fostered inclusivity and a culture of collective learning, shifting the focus from “who got it wrong” to “what we can learn as a group.” Together, these patterns suggest that the intervention made problem review more frequent, targeted, and data driven. Such practices were particularly valuable and motivating for low-performing students: problem review focused on problems they most often struggled with, and seeing shared errors in anonymized reports reinforced that mistakes are a natural and useful part of learning. Taken together, the four steps of formative assessment —Assign, Practice with Feedback, Assess, and Review — formed a coherent cycle that reshaped teachers’ instructional practices. Teachers regularly assigned independent practice through ASSISTments, students engaged with targeted problems supported by immediate feedback, teachers assessed learning through detailed reports, and classes collectively reviewed common errors in datadriven discussions. This cycle streamlined formative assessment routines and provided benefits for low-performing students, who gained confidence from scaffolded practice, received targeted support from teachers, and saw mistakes reframed as opportunities for growth. These findings help explain why the intervention showed promising impacts for students who were most at risk of falling behind. This rigorous national QED study of the ASSISTments intervention offers several important insights into its impact on U.S. middle school students’ mathematics learning. While no significant overall differences in achievement were detected between the treat ment and the VCG, the intervention produced meaningful gains for low-performing students. These results highlight the intervention’s potential to help narrow persistent achievement gaps and to provide targeted support for students most at risk of falling behind. From a learning sciences perspective, the study demonstrates how technology-enabled routines, coupled with sustained professional learning supports, can reshape teaching practice. Implementation data provided a rich picture of classroom practices, showing that teachers most often used ASSISTments for in-class independent practice at the end of lessons to check student understanding. Teachers relied heavily on ASSISTments reports to guide instructional decisions, reteach concepts, and offer differentiate support, while students benefitted from immediate feedback, which encouraged persistence, confidence, and independence. Teachers also observed that the intervention promoted self-regulated learning, helping students learn from mistakes and persist through challenges—practices long emphasized in the learning sciences as central to deep learning. The vPLC emerged as a vital support for teachers, particularly in rural contexts where professional isolation is common. By fostering a professional community, emphasiz ing data-driven decision-making, and sharing best practices, the vPLC enhanced teachers’ ability to effectively integrate formative assessment into their instructional routines. Together, these findings underscore the promise of combining technology platforms with structured professional learning to support teacher practice at scale. The intervention exemplifies how design-based innovations can operationalize formative assessment in ways that are learner -, assessment -, and community-centered, aligning closely with core principles of the learning sciences. While the overall impacts were modest, the benefits for low-performing and rural students show how such interventions can advance instructional effectiveness. Future research should continue to investigate how scalable professional learning infrastructures like the vPLC can help teachers transform technological affordances into practices that improve learning outcomes across diverse contexts.
