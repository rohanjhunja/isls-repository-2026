# Visualizing Data about Student Well-Being

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Schools regularly collect and display student data, but they often only focus on data about achievement. Intended to motivate academic performance, these data practices can instigate negative emotions in students. This poster presents our ongoing work aimed at designing a component of a secondary teacher preparation course to engage pre-service teachers (PSTs) in considering how they can mitigate harmful accountability data practices in school by collecting and visualizing data that attend to student well-being.

## Introduction

Current pre-service teachers (PSTs) are entering teaching amid the climate of data-driven school accountability and reform, where they will be asked to regularly collect  and interpret student data to inform instruction. But overwhelmingly, accountability data initiatives only focus on student academic performances,  and “ignore the unjust context that shapes student outcomes ” (Bertrand & Marsh, 2021, p. 36). Most visibly, these policies manifest themselves in daily classroom lives as data walls where individual students are publicly ranked on some axes of achievement measures. Data walls are intend ed to promote motivation for growth and achievement, but they can also give students the impression that their worth is tied to their academic achievements, eliciting a range of emotions ranging from stress, anxiety, disappoin tment, and anger (Harris et al., 2020). These practices perpetuate school-related traumas for many students.

Teachers are often encouraged to gather non-academic data about students, such as interests and  past experiences (Powell & Seed, 2010), to enhance classroom instruction. This data not only improves instruction but also fosters a sense of care from teachers, leading to higher self-esteem, well-being, and school engagement among students (Lavy & Naama-Ghanayim, 2020). However, unlike mandated achievement data, this caring-related information is seldom shared directly with students.

In this poster, we present our current effort to design a course component to engage pre-service teachers (PSTs) to considering actions they can take to mitigate harmful data practices in their classrooms.  Collaborating in cross-disciplinary teams, PSTs consider what other data about students they can collect and display in addition to the mandated achievement data in order to learn about their students as well as to communicate to students that their teachers care about them beyond academic outcomes.

## Background of the course

This ongoing work is part of  a secondary teacher preparation course  on c lassroom ecology. The course has undergone a reconceptualization to shift PSTs’ focus from traditional classroom management, which often overly emphasizes controlling student behaviors, to viewin g the classroom as an ecology with interacting comp onents influencing student behaviors. Due to the challenges posed by t he Covid-19 pandemic, the course has been recentered to prioritize equity-centered, trauma-informed education (Vernet, 2021). This framework h elps PSTs understand the broader contexts of students’ lives, how their behaviors may be responses to traumas experienced both in and out of school, and crucially, how they can create a more caring classroom that promote student wellbeing. A primary goal of the course is to help PSTs construct their classroom ecology plan. A classroom ecology plan consists of many components including a teaching framework and a set of policies and procedures that work toward sustaining a functioning classroom. PSTs are also asked to plan what they will do during the first week of the school year to begin to create a sense of class room community, position students as skilled and ca pable, welcome families and communities, situate the discipline as worthy of engagement, and establish an ethos of care in the classroom.

In early drafts, many PSTs included in their classroom ecology plans an activity aimed at communicating to their students that they care about their students as whole persons, most commonly by asking individual students to share information about their interests and hobb ies. In the next section, we describe how we redesign a component of the course to help PSTs further consid er what data about student well-being they can collect and how displaying such data can work towards establishing a caring classroom against the backdrop of data-driven accountability policies.

Exploring data visualizations about student well-being In the last few weeks of the course, PSTs met in their journal clubs and read an article that int roduced making data visualizations as an anchoring activity to pro mote learning through social interactions (Tapee et al., 2019). We supplemented the reading with hand-on activities. In subsequent journal clubs, PSTs considered afford ances of data visualizations about student well-being as a means to communicate to students that they are cared for and to help students see themselves as part of caring classroom community. ## Discussion and next steps

As we continue this work, we remind ourselves that schools already amass a considerable amount of data on students, often with students not  having a say in how their  data are collected and displayed.  In this context, collecting and displaying even more data about students can impede the teachers’ efforts to get to know students, for example, students may perceive data requests as  coercive or misrepresentative (Chen, forthcoming). Moving forward, we urge PSTs as well as  ourselves to scrutinize the implication of collecting and visualizing data on student well-being. It will be essential to consider what this activity makes more or less plausible in our efforts to create caring classrooms.
