# Differences in Novice and Expert Evaluations of Generative AI-Produced Definitions

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Widely available generative AI products such as ChatGPT are transforming the ways in which students and professionals work and learn, but little is known about how novices and experts perceive, evaluate, and judge generative AI-produced materials. Established research from cognitive science suggests that novices and experts will attend differently to surface and deep structures of content-specific materials. This preliminary study focuses on examining the accuracy and confidence judgements and annotations from experts and novices in the field of Information Science, comparing their evaluations across three different conditions: a ChatGPT produced definition of ‘data’; a human crowd-sourced definition of ‘data’; and a ChatGPT produced definition of a nonexistent word, ‘techno-data.’ The paper concludes with next steps in this research study, as well as potential implications for learning, education, and expertise.

## Introduction

Widely available generative AI products such as Cha tGPT are transforming the ways in which students and professionals work and learn, but little is known a bout how novices and experts perceive, evaluate, and judge generative AI produced materials. Whereas previously, production was the key consideration for students and employees, now evaluation is coming to the fore – that is, how students and employees evaluate AI-generated products for accuracy is suddenly crucial. In the field of education in particular, systems like ChatGPT are a new major topic of concern, as students can now easily and freely produce homework content and submit it a s their own, and instructors cannot reliably distinguish betwe en student-produced and generative-AI produced (e.g., Scarfe et al., 2024). However, no-one has yet examined how students-as novices in their content area – evaluate ChatGPT-produced content for accuracy, or compared their no vice evaluations with the evaluation practices of content experts.

Given that generative AI products use previously pr oduced work as their data corpus, and consequently tend to reproduce common surface structures aligned with that content area, novices may expect that those surface structures indicate accurate information. In other words, when the representation aligns with expectations, novices are predicted to assume that the content of those representations are accurate. Experts, on the other hand, are predicted to evaluate the actual content – the deep structures – regardless of the surface structures, due to their expertise. This paper reports on the results of the first phase of a research project investigating evaluation practices of three definitions related to the field of inform ation science. The first definition,  hereafter called AI, was produced by ChatGPT answering the question ‘What is data?’ (OpenAI, 2024a), which included accurate surface structures with some inaccuracy in deep structures.  The second, hereafter called the Data definition, was crowdsourced by experts on Wikipedia’s ‘Data’ page (Wikipedia, 2024), and was intended to present both accurate surface and deep structures in a familiar form (given Wikipedia’s common usage for online information seeking). Finally, the Fake definition was produced by ChatGPT answering the qu estion ‘What is techno-data?’ (OpenAI, 2024b). This latter word was invented by the author following Dunning (2011), and was intended to use familiar surface structures that appeared to repres ent a reason able but nonexistent definition. Participants were asked to rank the accuracy of the definitions and their confidence in the accuracy judgment, both on 5-point Likert scales, and told they could annotate the definitions if they wished. Experts were also interviewed one-on-one, and asked if they would make any revisions to the definitions to increase accuracy. This preliminary paper focuses on the data from the first part (accuracy, confidence, and annotations), as the expert video data is still under analysis. The purpose of this study is to examine and compare  experts and novices as they evaluate ChatGPT-produced content-specific definitions, in order to identify how varying levels of expertise interact with generative AI products. Specifically, the research question is: How do novices and experts evaluate the accuracy of each definition similarly or differently? Answering this question is the first step in unders tanding how expertise and generative AI intersect, and this preliminary resea rch is part of that ste p, supplemented by ongoing qualitative analysis. Below, I present a brief literature review on exper tise and ignorance research, and generative AI with  a particular focus on ChatGPT. Then, I describe the d ata collection methodology, present the data analys is, and discuss the findings. I conclude by sharing limitat ions and suggesting future research directions, as well as methodological implications for future expert-novice and generative AI studies.

## Literature review

Expertise and ignorance The learning sciences community is likely well info rmed on the history of and  established methodologies for examining how experts and novices remember, learn, and see content differently, so I only mention two of the foundational pieces here. Expert-novice studies have shown, f or example, that expert chess players can easily remember a chessboard layout they have only glimpse d before, while novices cannot (Chase & Simon, 1973). This advantage, however, disappears when the chessb oard layout is not one that would occur during typi cal gameplay-then, expert chess players struggle to remember the  layout as much as the novices do. Similarly, research on how physics novices and experts perceiv e and group different physics problems-novices tend to group the problems by surface features, whereas exp erts group by the underlying physical laws (Chi, Feltovich, & Glaser, 1981).

For this study, surface and deep structures have be en operationalized as representation and content, respectively. Representation and content are often conflated: external representations (surface structures) such as written text, images, sounds, symbols, and so on ar e used to communicate about content (deep structure s). However, representations are not actually the content itself, even though this distinction is often lost as individuals gain expertise (Nathan & Petrosino, 20 03), because representations sh ift from arbitrary symbols to truly representing the underlying content through repeate d use  by an individual. In other words, humans use representations as a way to convey concepts, as the  concepts themselves do not inherently have represe ntations. For example, a chess board and its pieces are a rep resentation of the game ‘chess,’ so that players ha ve a shared visual field of the game, and do not have to mentally simulate each move and state. But a chess set is not inherently chess-children who have not yet learned the rules of the game may in fact play with the pieces very differently. So the chess set represents the game of chess, with all its agreed upon rules, but is not in fact the inherent content of chess.

This distinction is key, and applies broadly across content areas; for example, Knuth (2002) found that mathematics teachers sometimes value the representation of a two-column proof too much to identify the faulty proof logic underneath that common representation. In fact, novices and experts alike often miscategorize their own levels of expertise: Kruger and Dunning (1999) demonstrated that poor pe rformers generally do not realize how poorly they performed, and numerous scholars ha ve determined that we are  rather poor at perceiving our own expertise, with a tendency to overestimate our own expertise and knowledge (e.g., Kruger & Dunning, 1999; Atir, Rosenzwig, & Dunning, 2015; Handel & Fritzsch e, 2015; Dunlosky, Serra, Matvey, & Rawson, 2005).  To account for this, Dunning (2011) describes two key types of ignorance: known unknowns and unknown unknowns. Known unknowns are when someone knows tha t they don’t know something – a form of powerful metacognition that can help that someone know what questions to ask to learn what they don’t know. Unk nown unknowns, on the other hand, are when someone doesn ’t know they don’t know something – which has been established as a common problem in human metacognit ion (see Dunning, 2011). When asked about an unknown unknown, people often “take cues from the social situation they are in and their general world knowledge to cobble together enough apparent information to form an imp ression” (Dunning, 2011, p. 258). For example, if g iven words that don’t exist, people come up with reasona ble definitions based upon the context and their pa st experience (Atir, Rosenzweig, & Dunning, 2015; Kruger & Dunning, 1999). A content expert who is being asked about something near their expertise may know what they don’t know, and therefore know to ask for more information, or they may just cobble together enough information to  fake it; whereas a content novice may use their superficial past experiences and rely upon surface features to confidently produce a response. ChatGPT The very process of generative AI systems like Chat GPT make expertise a particularly timely area of st udy, because produced materials are simply copying common representational patterns from their data corpus, with no attendance to accuracy or conten t. In particular, g enerative AI systems that use large language models, such as ChatGPT, use previously produced work as their data  corpus, and generate content by producing the next  token in a chain based on what tokens most frequently follow the previous token in their data corpus (e.g., Azaria, 2022; Kocoń et al., 2023). In lay terms, if ChatGPT (1) has a word like ‘data,’ the next most frequently used word after ‘data’ in its corpus is the word it adds to the sentence, such as ‘data is…” Then, ChatGPT looks for what word is most likely to come after ‘data is…’ and adds that word to the phrase, and then keeps repeating the pr ocess of looking and adding. As a result, the content that C hatGPT produces will tend to mimic the surface stru ctures of the content it is  drawing from. One consequence of this mimicking is that what ChatGPT produces will appear aligned with what novices expect, due simply to frequency. For example, Azaria found that efforts to get ChatGPT to randomly sample numbers were unsuccessful, because it followed the human bias of tending “to sample digits most favorable to humans” (2022, p. 6). As another example, if ChatGPT produced a two-column mathematical proof, this indicates that most mathematical proofs in ChatGPT’s data corpus use the two-column approach, which simultaneously indicates that such formats are more frequently pro duced and encountered by humans. In other words, the surface features of work produced by Cha tGPT are also the primary surface features people h ave encountered in the world – that is, they are likely to be  very familiar to novices and experts alike. However, commonality of surface patterns indicates little ab out accuracy – much like the mathematics teachers described above, ChatGPT uses the familiarity of representati ons from each specific co ntent area to guide its next step or judgment. In other words, ChatGPT simply reproduces  common surface patterns from its human-produced data corpus, even though common patterns can be less accurate than uncommon patterns. In other words, representations commonly known to b e aligned with a particular content area may lead to superficial judgments by those who recognize the  representation and its aligned content, but do not  yet have the expertise to evaluate the deep structures those  surface features actually represent. Adding to the  complexity, domain-specific misbeliefs (e.g., ‘good mathematical proofs use the two-column format’) may lead novices to feel more expert than they are, because they recognize the representations as related to particular content, and that feeling of familiarity can lead them to overconfidence and misjudging their ow n level of expertise. Novices may see representations commonly used in domain-specific content in generative AI produced materials, and-missing the underlying content-related issues-be particularly comfortable with uncritically using those materials. Systems like ChatGPT therefore open up new avenues of expert-novice research with both novices and experts, by reliably providing common surface structures with unreliable deep structures (since ChatGPT pays no attention to content accuracy).

## Methodology and data collection

For novices, I used  undergraduates enrolled in an introductory Fundamen tals of Information Science; and for experts, I used doctoral students and faculty in a College of Information. Research with experts and novices used the same materials: three different Information Sci ence content definitions, followed by judgements of  accuracy and confidence, with the ability to annotate the de finitions. Neither experts nor novices were aware t hat my research involved generative AI. Instead, I explained that I was interested in how information science experts and students evaluated content differently, saying that I previously focused on how people learn mathematics but was now interested in understanding how people learn in formation science. This deception was approved  by my Institutional Review Board, as explaining the study in full during the consent process would have invalidated the results.

Definitions The first definition, AI, was the ChatGPT response to my question “What is data?” (OpenAI, 2024a). The produced definition was 232 words, and two experts in Inform ation Science reviewed the definition for (in)accur acy, identifying some small issues in content and catego rization. I left the definition as is, with no edits to incre ase or decrease accuracy. The full transcript of this exchange is linked in References. The second definition, Data, was a crowdsourced expert definition of the term ‘data,’ retrieved from Wikipedia (2024). I took the first two paragraphs o f content, with two small omissions to better align  this definition with the one produced by ChatGPT, resulting in 235 words. I relied upon Wikipedia for this definition because it is an established resource for novices w ho are seeking quick and accurate information about  a topic. Therefore, I considered the format and language (th e representations) of a Wik ipedia entry to be one that is familiar to both novices and experts, and the content to be sufficiently accurate. To assure this, I ran the definition by one of my experts in Information Science, who verified its accuracy. The third definition, Fake, was also produced by ChatGPT (OpenAI, 2024b). Following Atir, Rosenzwig, and Dunning (2015), who asked respondents to rate their knowledge of topics that did not actually exist, I developed a term that did not appear to exist, techno-data, and asked ChatGPT for a definition. ChatGPT balked, and after some prompting,  finally produced a 197-word definition of techno-data. The full transcript of this exchange is linked in References.

Accuracy and confidence scales Each definition was paired with an accuracy assessm ent and a confidence assessment, both 5-point Likert scales with each position labeled (Not at all, Slightly, Somewhat, Mostly, Completely). The accuracy assessment prompt was “How accurate is the above definition of ‘(techno)data’?” for both studies; while the confidence scale prompt varied slightly between studies. In the expert stud y, the prompt was “How confident are you in your ac curacy judgment above?” In the novice study, I modified th e prompt to better support any inexperienced respon dents who might struggle to disentangle their accuracy and confidence assessments, so that it read “How conf ident are you in your accuracy judgment above? In other words, you judged the accuracy of the above definition – how sure are you that your judgment is correct?” Annotations Annotations were analyzed using the ICAP (interacti ve, constructive, active, and passive) framework (e.g., Chi, 2009), following Nelms and Segura-Totten’s (2019) adaptation for examining how expert s and novices engage with content-specific written work. Th ey define passive as simply consumption of the work (reading); active as engaging in mechanical actions while reading the text, such as underlining the text or jotting notes verbatim from the text; and constructive as the reader producing new connections with the te xt from prior knowledge, non-verbatim summaries, and explicitly evaluating the c ontent. Nelms and Segura-Totten did not include interactive in their work, as the original definition involved two or more people synchronously discussing the content, which did not occur in their study (or in this one). Howe ver, a key difference between our studies is that t heir contentspecific written work was known to have been published in a peer reviewed journal, whereas the texts in this study were – from the pa rticipant perspective – of doubtful provenance by unknown authors. Furtherm ore, since my definitions were produced without the scientific pe er review process validating accuracy, and indeed, even included a fake word, it seemed particularly import ant to account for any emergent types of annotation  in ways not necessarily as important in previous work. I therefore adapted interactive here to include when the participant’s annotations indicate debating with the imagined author about their written work.

## Data collection

Novices I collected data in two different lecture sections of INST201 during the same week late in the semeste r; no incentives were offered. Over a hundred students we re officially enrolled in each section, but lecture  attendance was optional, so the number of students present is unknown but certainly smaller than the enrollment. Each student present was randomly assigned to one condition and received one of the three definitions with the appr opriate accuracy and confidence assessments, bundled togeth er with a blue  pen to make any annotations more visible. They were read an informed consent script that made it clear that their participation was voluntary and anonymous, and would not impact their grades in the course. If they did not wish to participate, they could keep the bundle or return it blank. I received 85 completed responses (29 Data; 30 AI; 26 Fake), none of which had missin g data. I entered each response into a spreadsheet with a uni que ID number, entered accuracy and confidence scor es, and categorized the annotations according to the adapted ICAP framework. Experts I collected data from 18 participants (7 doctoral students and 11 faculty members), whose experience varied from having completed their first doctoral year to havin g faculty tenure; no incentives were offered. I con ducted oneon-one interviews with each expert, ranging from 10 – 30 minutes long. They read, evaluated, and revised each of the three definitions sequentially, with the defini tions in counterbalanced order. To prompt their rev ising, I asked if they would make any revisions to increase the accuracy of the response, and encouraged them t o talk aloud by both asking them to and making encouraging confirmatory sounds, regardless of what they were saying. Because I was recruiting from a tight-knit community and wanted to ensure that my decepti on was not revealed to participants in advance, I then asked if they kn ew anything about the study prior to coming and ask ed them to not talk about the content of the study until after  I finished collecting data. None of the participan ts reported knowing about the content of the study prior to data collection. For the definitions and attendant Likert scales, I received 54 completed responses (18 Data; 18 AI; 18 Fake), none of which had missing data. I entered ea ch response into an excel spreadsheet with a unique pseudonym, and entered accuracy and confidence sco res. I then analyzed their annotations in more dept h, using the adapted ICAP framework. I have also transcribed  the interviews, and am currently engaging in emerg ent coding to better understand what experts notice about the surface and deep structures of the three definitions.

## Results

First, how do novices and experts evaluate the accuracy of each definition similarly or differently? To answer this question, I investigated accuracy across conditions, within each population of expert and novice. The null hypothesis for both populations was that there is n o difference in accuracy between the three categori es of Data, AI, and Fake. The alternative hypothesis was that there is one or more differences between categories. (Please note that due to space limitations, I omit reportin g on descriptive statistics below, and focus on the  inferential statistics instead. Future publications with fewer page limitations will include the full analysis pro cess information.) I planned pairwise comparisons within each populati on, using the Wilcoxon rank sum test with Bonferroni correction to account for multiple compa risons. To do so, I needed to establish that the no vice groupings had homogeneity of variance and an overall effect of group, and verify that the counterbalancing design for experts had worked and there was not an order e ffect. For the novice groupings, I used the R base package to conduct the Levene test for homogeneity of variance, using the more robust version where the center is the median. The null hypothesis that the groups were the same was not rejected (p-value =.8819), establishing equal variation per group. Then, I used the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test, and found that there was an o verall effect of group (Kruskal-Wallis chi-squared = 15.725, df = 2, p-value = 0.0003848). For the experts and counterbalancing, I used the R base package to examine if there was an overa ll effect of order by accuracy. The null hypothesis  – that accuracy was rated the same regardless of order – failed to be rejected, using the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test (accuracy: Kruskal-Wallis chi-squared = 9.378, df = 6, p-value = 0.15; confidence: Kruskal-Wallis chi-squared = 2.2083, df = 5, p-value = 0.82)(2). Therefore, using the Wilcoxon rank sum test with Bo nferroni correction was deemed appropriate for evaluating accuracy across categories, within populations. Conducting the rank sum test with novice data revealed that Data was not significantly different from AI (p-value = 0.13347) or Fake (p-value = 0.08719), but AI and Fake were found to be significantly different (p-value 0.00036). The null hypothesis was thus reject ed for the novice population. Conducting the rank s um test with expert data revealed Data and AI were not statistically significant (p-value = 0.1775), but Fake was found to be statistically significant from Data (p-value = 0.0328) and AI (p-value = 0.0024). The null hypothesis was thus rejected for the expert population.

I had tentatively expected that experts would most highly rate the Data definition, with AI landing somewhere between Data and Fake in accuracy. I had also tentatively expected that novices would rank Data and AI similarly in accuracy, but that Fake would have considerably lower ratings. However, my expectations were not borne out-instead, experts treated Data and AI as similar in accuracy, and novices only distinguished between AI and Fake definitions, with Data not different from either. Consequently, I dug deep er into the data, to better understand how experts and novices were engaging with the definitions that led to those unexpected results. First, I examined confidence scores for the different definitions, again using the Wilcoxon rank sum test with Bonferroni correction. Novices had confidence results similar to their accuracy results: Data confidence scores were not significantly different from AI (p-value = 1.00) or Fake (p-value = 0.104), but AI and Fake were found to be significantly different (p-value = 0.032). Experts, on the other hand, were si milarly confident across definitions, with no statistically significant diff erences between p airs: Data and AI (p-value = 1.00); Data and Fake (p-value = 0.085); and AI and Fake (p-value = 0.054). In other words, novices’ confidence  wavered in tandem with their sense of each definition’s accura cy, whereas the experts were confident in their jud gments regardless of their accuracy ratings. I considered this to further verify the accuracy judgments, as novices struggle with the metacognition needed for confidence judgements (e.g., Dunlosky et al., 2005). Next, I analyzed annotations using the adapted ICAP framework described above. I found low annotation rates for novices: 78% were categorized as Passive engagement (merely reading, no annotations); 12% as Active; 9% as Constructive; and only one response d emonstrated Interactive engagement. Expert annotati on rates were considerably higher, with only 57% categorized as P assive; 20% as Active; 11% as Constructive; and 11%  as Interactive. Interestingly, experts annotated each definition at similar rates, with 7 (AI) and 8 (Data, Fake) per category, while the novices most frequently annotated Data (10) and Fake (6), leaving AI virtually unscathed (3). These results were less insightful than I could hav e hoped: first, since experts were asked if they’d make any revisions to increase accuracy, it is logical that the expert group would annotate more than the novices regardless of expertise level, simply because the novices were not asked about similar revisions. On the other hand, novices could have been expected to  annotate more because they had lesser expertise, and could therefore need more support in annotation to critique the definitions. However, the results were not conclusively supporti ve of any particular conclusion-although suggestions for future research to remedy this are suggested in the Discussion. Finally, I examined experts more closely using additional quantitative data: the amount of time ex perts spent reading each definition, versus reviewing. Specifically, I wondered: if the experts consider ed the AI definition to be as accurate as the Data definition, would they spend the same amount of time suggesting revisions to increase accuracy? In particular, since Wikipedi a entries can contain conflicting language (e.g., Yasseri et al., 2013), and a hallmark of science – including debates on the nature of ‘data’  - is the slow and often reluctant changing of paradigms (Kuhn, 1970), it seemed reasonable that the crowdsourced Data definition could be quite complex. Certainly, the AI definition used bullet points and had other indicators of simplicity, whereas Data was large chunks of text. Indeed, a code emerging from my ongoing qualitative analysis of expert interview s is simplicity-namely, experts are remarking on the simplicity or complexity of the definitions. Thus far, the AI definition is being categorized by experts as “a first approximation,” as one participant said, or as another stated: “data for beginners.” The Data definition, however, is being categorized as more c omplex in both structure and content; one participant noted: “there were indenta tions [in AI], there was a list of five things. Um. ‘Oh, data i s this’ boom boom boom. Where this [Data], it’s like okay, hold on, I'm reading. [laughter].” So I examined whether the complexity of definitions might contribute to d ifferent amounts of time spent reading and revising-perhaps AI, as a simple definition intended for beginners, would take less time to read and revise, whereas Data, as a more advanced definition, would at least take longer to read. ## Discussion

First, the null hypothesis was rejected for both the novice and expert participants: novices considered AI and Fake to be different, as represented in both their Accur acy and Confidence ratings; and experts considered Fake to be different from Data and AI in Accuracy, while no difference was found in their Confidence ratings. I had initially posited that the ChatGPT definition of data would h ave familiar surface structures, due to generative AI relying upon the human-produced definitions and their surface structures, which are also the likely most familiar surface structures to both novices and experts. I consider these results to lend weight to that position, as both experts and novices comfortably accepted the AI definition. I h ad also considered the resource of Wikipedia to lik ely be a familiar format, given its prominent role in inform ation seeking behaviors, but the novices did not fi nd that definition to be as accurate as the ChatGPT definit ion, so I consider the surface structure of the Wik ipedia definition to be less familiar – or at least less persuasive – to the novices. However, the question remained: why didn’t novices consider the Wikipedia definition to be significantly different in Accuracy or Confidence from the Fake definition? Given the fact that techno-data as a word does not exist (3), it would be fair to anticipate that the Fake defini tion would be marked egregiously low in terms of Accuracy. Below, I describe three potential reasons  for this lack of significant difference: 1) the tr ansmission method of education leading students to con sider reading materials in their classroom to be in tended for consumption, not critique; 2) the familiar surface structure of ChatGPT; and 3) the differences in complexity and effort required to understand the definitions.

First, the transmission method of education has led  students to expect that the reading materials they  are presented with are accurate (or at least the versio n of truth that the professor will grade highly). A s a result, students reading and ev aluating materials given within the classroom conte xt may not read as critically as they would in a different context. This remains an under studied area: if a student asks ChatGPT to produce course content for them (e.g., a written paper), do they evaluate that production with the expectation of the accuracy they expect from written work in class (such as my surveys), or do they bring a more critical eye to the content? Given that students have been found to feel disappointed and betrayed when they discover  that ChatGPT is providing inaccurate information (Tlili et al., 2023), they may be attributing the same expectation of a ccuracy to ChatGPT that they attribute to content presented to them in  classrooms. Regardless, it is possible that the no vices assumed that a definition of a nonexistent word would never be presented to them within the classroom context, and inflated their accuracy ratings accordingly.

Another potential reason is the familiar surface structure produced by ChatGPT – even if the platform is producing the definition of a nonexistent word, tha t production comes from pre-existing written work that uses the terms techno and data. So while the deep structure  for Fake was reasonable but nonexistent, so to speak, the surface structure alone may have sufficiently convi nced the novices. The experts, on the other hand, r eadily identified that definition as problematic, confidently identifying the accuracy as low. So the reasonable-ness and familiarity of the deep structure did not convince the experts  – as Dunning and colleagues might have predicted, they recognized a known unknown, and leveraged their expertise to foster skepticis m instead of creating a contextually appropriate but inaccurate definition (Atir, Rosenzweig,  & Dunning, 2015; Kruger & Dunning, 1999).

Finally, I theorized that the complexity of each de finition and the subsequent effort needed to unders tand the definition varied. This becomes particularly cr ucial when we consider that the content itself is t he most important and the most diff icult to learn, whereas the representation of conte nt may be easiest to learn (or to believe you’ve learned).  Understanding the details  of a mathematical proof, for example, is a more co mplex endeavor than merely recognizing what the proof should look like. Perhaps, then, when the surface structures are familiar and if the deep structures they are representing seem reasonable, critiquing is a more effortful experience or at least takes longer, as Data did compared to the simpler AI definition. Although I do not have the data to examine this for the novices, I am digging  deeper into the differences by investigating what experts attended to during their interviews, in order to  offer future  insights. However, determining which of these three potential reasons is most influential – or whether an un-anticipated fourth reason is playing a main role – is a necessary next step for this line of research.

## Limitations

First, future research with both experts and novices in larger quantity should be conducted, to insure that the lack of significant differences between Data and Fake (novices) and Confidence (experts) are not simply due to a lack of power. In particular, novice interviews should b e conducted, to better parallel the data collected from experts in this study. Second, it is possible that the Chat GPT definition of data produced for this study was an atypical example, as entering the same question into ChatGPT  at different times can result in multiple and quite different responses (e.g., Tlili et al., 2023).  Future research should investigate how to calcul ate typicality of ChatGPT products, to lessen such limitations in this type of research. Next, participants may have believed some or all of the de finitions to be AI-produced, but they weren’t contextually encouraged to provide that information. Some experts mentioned ChatGPT as a potential aut hor – across all three conditions, interestingly – in their interviews, and completing the qualitative  analysis will better identify any emergent patterns in their data. However, since the novices were only completing surveys, the results could have been quite different if they were  encouraged but not cued to recognize  generative AI products. Additionally, they were not explicitly encouraged t o admit when they did not know something – like the word ‘techno-data.’ Future experimentation with accuracy scales could include options for “I do not know this word” and/or “this was probably produced by AI” to get a more nuanced picture from the survey data. At the very least, future research in this line could at least ask par ticipants to share where they think the definition came from, as a way to allow them – but not cue them – to include generative AI as a source. Finally, the experts were very diverse in content a reas and expertise, and I had participants ranging from having completed one year of the doctoral program to being tenured faculty. These differences likely affected the results from the expert interviews, which really on ly revealed patterns of expertise that were either directly from the domain or transferrable to the domain (from the  participants who did not directly study or researc h ‘data’) (Nelms & Segura-Totten, 2019). Additional research that more clearly defines who  is considered an expert in ‘data’ would be a useful addition here, as would be gathering information from both expert and novice participants about their expertise with generative AI, in order to better account for that line of expertise.

## Conclusion

Experts and novices evaluate accuracy differently in content-specific definitions, and ongoing qualitative research will further explore what experts considered when evaluating those definitions. Novices were less impressed with Wikipedia’s definition of data than a definition produced by ChatGPT, and both novices and experts were dubious about the term ‘techno-data,’ although experts were confident in their neg ative accuracy evaluation of the word. Put another way, experts knew that the unknown word  was unknown because it did not exist – as the qualitative analysis is suggesting – while novices’ low confidence scores hints at a discomfort with the unknown term. I would be most remiss if I did not mention an emer gent issue with this study’s methodology, which followed most expert-novice studies in their examination of a  single dimension of expertise: the content area. However, with the increased role of generative AI i n our society, there may be two dimensions of expertise in research such as this: the content area (e.g., info rmation science), and familiarity with generative A I and its imperfections. Someone who is a content area expert but not an exp ert in how generative AI works, may simply accept what ChatGPT says as truth as long as it ali gns sufficiently with their knowledge (as recent st ories about lawyers misunderstanding and consequently misusing generati ve AI have illustrated; see Heverly, 2024 for a summary). Consequently, content area experts who ask ChatG PT to produce content still need to read, evaluate, and revise the result using their own expertise. So meone who is an expert in generative AI but a novic e in the content area can easily produce content that they are still in the process of learning, and are likely aware that they must then evaluate that content for accuracy, relevance, and so on. But users of ChatGPT who are novices in both the content area and generative AI are doubly at ri sk for accepting the results without question, as they may be overestimating their own expertise in both areas.

As a result, one challenge facing education and the  workplace is that there are two different expertis e development trajectories that must be progressed upon for someone to successfully use generative AI in a contentspecific and appropriate fashion. In any context, those who are learning the content and generative AI for the first time, will require particular support in both areas. However, this also leads directly to a huge and c urrently unanswered question: must content area expertise truly be painfully and lengthily learned? The process of learning a content area has long been considered a linear process, where one must learn A before learning B before learning C, and so on, where C builds directly on the knowle dge acquired in B, and B on A. In other words, each step has been considered a prerequisite to the one that came  before. However, with generative AI widely availab le to learners, perhaps it can play the role of multiple foundational steps, so the content area learner no longer needs to begin with A, but can instead rely upon generative AI for step s A-G, and begin learning with H instead. This potential is exciting-perhaps it will fundamentally upend both how we def ine expertise, and how many people have access to expertise. If access to quality generative AI replaces 10,000 hours or 10 years as the baseline for achieving expertise, then achieving content area expertise may be exceedingly less onerous and increasingly more accessible.
