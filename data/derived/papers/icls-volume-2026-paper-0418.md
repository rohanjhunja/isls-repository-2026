# Mapping Learning Ecologies in a Human-Centered Graduate Performance Support Course

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study conducted learning experience network analysis on end-of-course reflections from a graduate performance support systems course to examine what worked, what struggles were encountered, and the links to principles from learning theories. Thematic coding resulted in a network map with clustering that revealed distinct learning ecologies. The clusters point to personally meaningful paths toward human flourishing and inform targeted design moves that reduce barriers and support a more humane, project-based learning environment.

The clusters point to personally meaningful paths toward human flourishing and inform targeted design moves that reduce barriers and support a more humane, project-based learning environment. The graduate online course in performance support systems investigated in this study shifted the focus from organizational efficiency (Gery, 1991) to human flourishing (Nasir et al., 2020). Students in this course analyzed complex performance problems and designed interventions that support people’s well-being and growth in authentic contexts (Donaldson et al., 2025). They worked through a complete design cycle, from conducting a needs assessment that surfaces real aspirations and challenges to implementati on and evaluation that look for meaningful growth, not just output. The scaffolded, project-based format can be empowering, yet the shift from an efficiency mindset is difficult. Students grappled with ambiguity in human-centered work, balanced creative and ethical principles with constraints, and managed a semester-long performance support design project. Paying attention to these experiences helped shape a learning environment that supported a human-centered approach to design (Norman, 2013). The course was grounded in principles from learning theories. Social constructivist learning theory principles (Vygotsky, 1978) guided the design of aspects such as the weekly online discussions. Cognitive constructivism (Piaget, 1952) and constructionism (Kafai & Resnick, 1996) helped us emphasize personal meaning built through iterative projects. Transformative learning theory (Mezirow, 2000) was leveraged in reflection assignments to spark perspective shifts. Self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2017) provided the base for fostering autonomy, competence, and relatedness, strengthened by student choice of project context. The study analyzed reflections to guide future design that nurtures diverse paths to flourishing. This study examined student experiences in a graduate course using end-of-course reflections as the data source. We analyzed 27 reflections in which students described what worked, what they struggled with, and how the course shaped their professional growth. Using learning experience network analysis (Donaldson et al., 2024), which interprets qualitative coding through network analysis, we looked for patterns across accounts. The final codebook included 92 codes applied to 465 segments. Each reflection was  coded along three dimensions: what worked, challenges, and principles from learning theories. We then calculated statistically significant code correlations and used the Girvan– Newman algorithm to detect clusters (Girvan & Newman, 2002). The analysis revealed distinct clusters that reflected different combinations of successes, struggles, and theoretical alignments. These clusters informed targeted design adjustments for future iterations of the course. Our analysis of correlations at p <.001 resulted in a network map with four clusters that had a robust modularity of Q =.632. The Purple Triangle cluster represents experiences of students focused on the bigger context and outcomes of their learning. They found success through collaborative learning and community, valuing the relevance of their work to future careers and decision-making. Effective communication with the instructor and the creative agency was also successful. However, these students struggled with ambiguity, unclear instructions, uncertainty, and prototyping. Their experiences align with constructionist, metacognition, and self-determination theories. To support them, the course could add structured peer-prototyping sessions. This would leverage their strengths in teamwork and communication to address their struggles, building on the existing community and trust to help them navigate uncertainty while maintaining ownership of their projects. They valued meaningful reflection, clear structure, and the development of a human-centered design mindset, finding the  experience enjoyable and valuable. However, they struggled with project management and rushed timing. These experiences connect to design science, transformative learning, and cognitive constructivist principles. To support them, the instructor could introduce two required project management checkpoints linked to existing reflection assignments, requiring teams to outline timelines and reflect on progress. This design builds on their appreciation for structure and strength in reflection, turning time management into an intentional learning focus and helping consolidate complex skills through guided self-assessment.

Student experiences in the Light Blue Diamond cluster concentrated on the early stages of design, working to understand problems and define their scope. What worked well was becoming aware of multiple perspectives and learning to see issues from different angles. They struggled, however, with problem framing, empathy-building activities, and working without sufficient background knowledge. To help during these challenging phases, a design change could be a preliminary assignment where students analyze and discuss welldefined and poorly-defined problem statements from past projects. Framing this as a class discussion would build on their strength in comparing perspectives, providing a scaffold and background knowledge to reduce the difficulty of problem framing in their own work.

The Pink Circle cluster included experiences centered on personal growth and building their own understanding. A key area that worked for them was convergent thinking and selecting a solution, and they saw positive growth in self-awareness and reflection. However, they struggled to connect the course to their future careers or a larger purpose. These experiences align with cognitive constructivist ideas, especially individual knowledge construction. To help these students, a design change could enhance the reflection assignments. Prompts could be revised to ask students how their approach to selecting a solution connects to their future career interests, building on their strengths to encourage them to construct their own meaning. Student learning in this course was not a single path to one idea of success, but a set of distinct journeys toward human flourishing. These findings challenge performance support models centered on efficiency (Gery, 1991). The clusters show students engaging with the project in personally meaningful ways, from the collaborative, purpose-driven growth of the purple cluster to the more introspective, skill-focused growth of the pink cluster. When growth comes before productivity, students experience learning ecologies that support their development, not just task completion. The design goal, then, shifts from maximizing efficiency to building environments that enable flourishing (Nasir et al., 2020). By mapping the challenges tied to each cluster, we candevelop design moves that clear barriers. For example, structured peer-prototyping is more than an idea engine because it can potentially build psychological safety and creative confidence for the purple cluster, offering a concrete way to shape project-based learning around the learner’s developmental journey rather than narrow performance metrics. Learning experience network analysis (Donaldson et al., 2024) helped here by making visible interdependencies between what worked, what was hard, and enactment of p rinciples. The resulting moves were intentional steps toward a more humane, developmental course.
