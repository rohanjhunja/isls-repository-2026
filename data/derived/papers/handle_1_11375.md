# “How Can We Lift Them Up and Make Them Feel That Their Voices are Valuable?”: A Case Study of One Teacher’s Pedagogical Responsibilities

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Science teachers seeking to engage students in collective sensemaking must attend to not only students’ epistemic development, but also to their relationships with each other. This paper examines the intertwined epistemic and relational responsibilities of science teaching through a case study of Ms. Shaw, a fifth-grade teacher. The analysis reveals the responsibilities that Ms. Shaw felt in creating an equitable learning environment where all students felt valued, as well as emerging tensions between her epistemic and relational goals for classroom activity. Ms. Shaw’s reflections highlight the complexity of navigating these dual responsibilities as well as the difficulties in disrupting inequitable patterns of classroom participation.

## Introduction

Science education r esearchers and practitioners have recently sought to understand how to support students to take up and meaningfully engage with science and engineering practices in classrooms (e.g., Berland et al., 2016; Schwarz et al., 2017). Similarly, researchers have emphasized the importan ce of involving students in sensemaking, a  process of engaging in epistemic (knowledge-building) activities to construct explanations that resolve gaps or inconsistencies (Odden & Russ, 2019).  This work highlights that sensemaking is a social process because it relies on members of a community working together to propose, evaluate, and justify ideas.  This social component of sensemaking requires a shift in classr oom activity from individual to collective learning processes (Alzen et al., 2023; Feinstein & Waddington, 2020).  Prior work exploring the  social nature of sense-making, however, has not  always attuned to  or offered suggestions for the relational work of classrooms —that is, the moral and ethical underpinnings of teachers’ and students’ collective activity  (Krist, 2024). This paper uses the experiences of one fifth-grade teacher, “Ms. Shaw,” to illuminate the comple xity of teachers’ work to support students to collectively build science knowledge in ways that foster a supportive classroom culture. Specifically, I focus on the epistemic and relational responsibilities that Ms. Shaw felt towards her students and that influenced her pedagogical judgments (Horn, 2020; Horn & Garner, 2022). This work addresses the following research question: What pedagogical responsibilities does Ms. Shaw have for her student s, and how do these show the challenges of simultaneously addressing the epistemic and relational aspects of science activity?

## Literature review

Learning is a social endeavor that occurs within co mmunities of practice that continuously negotiate and refine meanings (Lave & Wegner, 1991). This social nature of learning creates complex learning environments that require teachers to simultaneously attend to multip le aspects of classroom activity. For example, when children engage in sensemaking, a variety of decision points can come up in which teachers need to notice and respond to the disciplinary substance  and epistemic nature of children’s ideas (Krist et al., 2023; Watkins & Manz, 2022). Notably, Schwarz et al. (2021) found that these mom ents can be places where teachers expand, maintain,  or shut down sensemaking. These studies suggest that there is a motivation for continued exploration of how te achers determine what epistemic aspects of classroom activity attend to, how they make sense of these aspects, and why they respond in particular ways.

An emerging area of research explores teachers’ relational goals for their students and how these shape teachers’ actions. By “relational”, I refer to the moral and ethical c ommitments that shape interactions between members of a classroom community (Krist, 2024). Amp le work has argued that the work of teaching is fundamentally relational, and this relational work is influenced by larger power structures (e.g., Freire, 1970; Ladson-Billings, 1995).  Prior work suggests that supporting children’s relational activity during co llective sensemaking can be  challenging for teachers, particularly when considering how to account for the ways that larger systems of power influence classroom interac tions by creating an unequal distribution of status  among students (Alzen et al., 2023; Engle et al., 2014). And, recent literature has sought to foreground that relationships (both amongst students and between the teacher and students) deeply affect the ways that disciplinary knowledge is built (Krist, 2024; Krist & Suárez, 2018). However, what is less known is how teachers’ epistemic and relational pedagogical responsibilities are intertwined, what tensions this creates  for teachers, and how they respond accordingly. While others have argued that epistemi c and relational work is deeply intertwined in clas sroom activity, few studies have shown exactly what tensions can come up in this work and how they affect teachers’ decision-making.

## Conceptual framework

I take up a situated perspective on teachers’ work, by which I refer to the idea that teachers’ learning and decision making are deeply intertwined with the context that they are working within (Greeno, 1998; Horn & Garner, 2022; Lave & Wegner, 1991). What this means for my analysis of Ms. Shaw’s actio ns is that I consider her not just as an individual decision-maker but as a person operating within larger systems of social interaction (Greeno, 1998). The actions that she took in her classroom and the results of these actions were not just due to her own knowledge or values but were also shaped by the environment in which she taught  as well as larger inequitable power structures (Louie, 2020).

Using a situated perspective on teaching, I take up the construct of pedagogical responsibility (Horn, 2020; Horn & Garner, 2022) to explore the rationale  for the decisions that Ms. Shaw made in her classroom. Teachers’ actions are shaped by these pedagogical responsibilities, which include the goals teachers have for their students, ideas that they value in their classrooms, and commitments that they feel beholden to (such as adhering to a curriculum or supporting school-wide policies). Some examples of pedagogical responsibilities teachers could feel include providing students opportunities to exercise their epistemic agency (Miller et al., 2018), supporting multilingual learners or students with disabilities, or preparing students for a standardized test.  Sometimes these responsibilities are clearly expressed by a teacher, and other times they are more implicitly taken up  in teachers’ talk and actions, potentially even without their full awareness of these responsibilit ies. In this paper, I use a case study of one fifth-grade teacher to identify her pedagogical responsibilities that are explicitly and implicitly shown through her discourse, and to explore the intertwined epistemic and relational aspects of these responsibilities.

## Study context and data collection

This study is situated within a larger research project exploring the role of productive uncertainty in ele mentary science investigations, using a co-design model to work alongside elementary teachers in an urban public school district to redesign, analyze, and refine science investigations in their classrooms. The work reported here focuses on the experiences of “Ms. Shaw”, a white, female-identifying fifth-grade teacher who had 8 years of teaching experience at the time of the study. The school district Ms. Shaw works in has undergone significant gentrification over the last 15 years, and so Ms. Shaw’s class of 25 students had a wide range of backgrounds and identi ties. The co-design team, including myself and Ms. Shaw, met bef ore the school year began to  develop goals for students’ engagement in investigation and modeling,  consider when, how, and for whom these goals could  be realized, and discuss anticipated challenges. During the school year, I visited Ms. Shaw’s classroom over 50 times, participating as an observer, thought partner, and occasional co-teacher. We frequently met to discuss how to support children to make connections between their ideas, how to design in ways that involved all stud ents, and how to provide enough support for students to be ab le to access activities but not so much that they r educed opportunities to engage in science practice.   After the school year ended, our co-design team met again over to reflect on the previous school year. I analyzed transcripts from six of these meetings (three in summer 2021, two during the 2021-2022 school year, and one in summer 2022). I corroborated my findings by analyzing field notes from an additional seven meetings that were not audio-recorded.

## Analytic methods

The purpose of analysis was to identify Ms. Shaw’s pedagogical responsibilities by examining her talk during design meetings before, during, and after the school year. I chose to begin my analysis with this data set so that I could understand not only the responsibilities that Ms. Shaw explicitly stated, but also those that more implicitly guided her teaching. I began by reviewing each tran script from our design meetings to identify convers ations in which Ms. Shaw talked about her pedagogical responsibilities that she felt for her students. For example, I looked for conversations in which Ms. Shaw and other team members discussed indicators of productive science learning, design decisions during lessons, challenges teachers and students experience when engaging with uncertainty, and problems we wanted to orient our co-design work around. After identifying these conversations, I examined each in more detail to consider how Ms. Shaw talked abou t responsibilities for her classroom. I used multip le rounds of reflexive thematic coding, iteratively focusing and refining my codes as I looked across the transcripts and my notes to create categories of ideas  (Braun & Clarke, 2021). I then referred to field notes from additional meetings that were not audio-recorded to corroborate and refine  my initial findings and create a list of Ms.  Shaw’s pedagogical responsibilities. After analyzing the transcripts and field notes from design meetings, I  developed a semi-structured interview protocol to member-check my findings with Ms. Shaw, working with her to confirm and refine my findings into a list of ideas that sh e agreed captured the pedagogical responsibilities she felt with this group of students. I then reviewed the prior analysis of transcripts and confirm that her responses in the interview matched what she had said during design m eetings, and consider what differences arose between what she stated in the interview and what she had previously said in design meetings.

## Findings: Ms. Shaw’s pedagogical responsibilities

Developing a collaborative and kind classroom The primary pedagogical responsibility that Ms. Shaw felt during  the 2021-2022 school year was to develop a classroom in which students valued each other’s ideas. When I interviewed Ms. Shaw, she identified this theme as the most important responsibility that she felt for her class, explaining that all her other respon sibilities were in service of supporting children to do this. This aligns with a statement she made in our first co-design meeting, where she said, I do think students valuing each other’s ideas is really important to me, especially when you’re thinking about academic power. Oftentimes, students with less academic power, their ideas are devalued by their classmates. And that’s something I’ve always really cared deeply about. The responsibility of supporting children to value each other’s ideas came up for Ms. Shaw across the school year and was often interwoven with other responsibilitie s. For example, she felt that for students to be able to value each other’s ideas, they needed to treat each other with kindness. She frequently referred to times when she felt that her students used an unkind tone of voice, exp ressing in meetings that she was dissatisfied with class discussions in which children spoke in ways that “p ut each other down”, saying “you can just tell when it’s not coming from a kind place”. To address this, she said that she was working to create a class commu nity where students worked together and developed ideas as a community. She also expressed that students needed to be able to not only share ideas during discussions, but also express disagreement with each other in service of collective knowledge development.

Addressing an inequitable distribution of participation and status Ms. Shaw felt that her classroom was characterized by some students having higher  “status” or “authority” than other children. Ms. Shaw felt that whole-group discussions were important for students’ epistemic development, but for these to be effective for all children she needed to find ways to equitably distribute status and participation. During a meeting discussing goals and hopes for the upcoming year, she said, I always am going back to this. There’s like a few key players that are having the discussion, and I’m wondering what the other students are proce ssing or understanding. This is something I continually reflect on in my classroom, like what are the other students doing or processing or understanding from the dialogue that are not talking?

Ms. Shaw felt a particular responsibility to explicitly support students with identities historically marginalized in education, noting that the students who typically had the highest status in the class were “white and affluent”. She worried that students who were multilingual were “c arrying a heavy load, in terms of vocabulary and la nguage” and wondered how she could support the se students to feel more comfortable verbally participating. She also frequently brought up her students with disabilitie s, wondering how we could “lift them up and make them feel that their voices are also valuable”?  In disruptin g these inequitable power structures, she hoped to help these students feel their ideas w ere important for developing explanations, and that  this would and allow class participation to be distributed more equitably. She explained that she used the strategy of cold callin g (randomly calling on students rather than only inviting stude nts who raise their hands to join the conversation)  to more equitably distribute participation, and that she di d this to address the inequitable distribution of s tatus that she sensed amongst her students.

Engaging children in meaningful science activity Ms. Shaw felt a responsibility for supporting students to feel that their science work was meaningful. In meetings throughout the year, she frequently expressed wanting lessons to feel “meaningful” or “purposeful”. When I asked what she meant by these terms, she explained that this meant she wanted students to be able to connect what they learned in science class to their lives outside of school. She explained  at the end of the school year, “When we did more things that students could connect to in t heir own life experience, it was much more successf ul for all students”. Importantly, she said that this wasn’t j ust about students having fun – they alsoneeded “to feel that they’re experiencing excitement in service of something”. She felt that if she prioritized students feeling that their work was meaningful, then it would feel more enjoya ble to them, saying, “I feel like if it's purposefu l and meaningful to you, therefore it's more interesting, more exciting”. Emerging tensions between Ms. Shaw’s pedagogical responsibilities In Ms. Shaw’s talk in design meetings across the school year, several tensions between her responsibilities began to emerge. The responsibility of supporting children to disagree with each other seemed particularly challe nging for Ms. Shaw, and perhaps in tension with her respo nsibility of creating an environment where children treated each other with kindness. In some meetings, Ms. Shaw identified disagreement as a key component of a productive learning community, but during a mid-year meeting she said that she was “shifting away from saying ‘agree or disagree’ to saying ‘my ideas are similar or different’, and I think that’s been a good move”. She expressed making this shift because she felt that when students disagreed with each other it felt “too combative”. While she expressed satisfaction with this pedagogical shift (by calling it a “good move”), it did not seem in service of the responsibility she felt for supporting children to feel comfortable disagreeing with each other. Another tension emerged between her responsibilitie s of supporting meaningful science activity and disrupting inequitable participation patterns. Throughout the year, Ms. Shaw began to increasingly focus on using activity structures that increased the num ber of students verbally participating during whole-group discussions. When she talked about using these strategies, though, she focused only on the number of “voices heard”—not on the scientific merit of these ideas or how to posit ion them as tools for sensemaking. Ms. Shaw felt that using the strategy of cold calling was one way to address her  responsibility of creating a classroom that incorp orated the voices and ideas of all children, not just the ones who were typically positioned as authors of knowledge.

## Discussion

This study explores a fifth-grade science teacher’s pedagogical responsibilities (Horn, 2020;  Horn & Garner,

2022) as a way to understand the intertwined epistemic and relational nature of teaching. The findings add to the

literature by using a case study to explore  specific examples of how teachers feel responsibilities for creating learning environments that are supportive for all s tudents to engage in science sensemaking, and  Ms. Shaw’s discussion of her responsibilities suggested tensions between epistemic and relational aspects. For examp le, Ms. Shaw felt a responsibility to support students to v alue each other’s ideas, and her rationale focused on relational aspects – she expressed discomfort with times in which she felt that students put down each other’s ideas and felt that the tone of these interactions prevented other children from feeling comfortable. However, there is an epistemic component to this responsibility as well:  Students need to feel that their ideas have scienti fic merit to be taken up and used by their peers. When Ms. Shaw focused solely on the relational aspects of student interaction, she may have inadvertently de-emphasized the importance of student ideas to be taken up as epistemic resources for sensemaking, and thereby limited her ability to make scientific activity feel meaningful for her students. These tensions may not always be explicitly realized by teachers like Ms. Shaw, who ultimately felt dissatisfied with her attempts to create an equitable and kind classroom culture. These findings support those of others who have fou nd that teachers are often aware of issues of statu s in the classroom but can be unsure how to navigate or disrupt them (Baldinger, 2017; Braaten & Sheth, 2017; Louie, 2020). Ms. Shaw began the school year with a sense of excitement and urgency to support her students to work collaboratively, feeling that this was importa nt for children to engage in sensemaking  together. Her commitment to creating a classroom where all ideas were valued was a common thread across all design meetings before, during, and after the school year.  However, she frequently expressed feeling dissatisf ied with her ability to achieve this in her classroom, such as when she wondered  during the interview, “Why was it so hard for me, why did it feel like nothing was successful?”. Ms. Shaw’s experiences demonstrate a need for continued exploration of teachers’ pedagogical responsibilities, the tensions that can arise from these, and how  they inform pedagogical decision-making in the classroom.
