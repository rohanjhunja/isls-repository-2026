# Exploring Different Facilitator Roles in Maker-Based Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The purpose of this study is to investigate how different facilitator roles (educator, adult caregiver, peer and self-facilitation) influence children’s learning engagement--specifically, the learning practices of seeking and sharing resources (SSR)--in a museum’s makerspace. We address two research questions: 1). In what ways do learners' engagement vary when facilitated by different facilitator roles? 2). In what ways do different types of facilitators influence SSR practices across age ranges? The results show that facilitation from caregivers and self-directed facilitation is associated with significantly more SSR practices. Additionally, we found that the influence of different types of facilitators on learning engagement varies across ages. Self-directed learning is associated with an increase of SSR as children grow, while facilitation from caregivers is associated with a decline of SSR as children age. Based on these findings, we discuss implications for facilitation in museum makerspace.

## Introduction and background

Facilitated museum makerspaces can function as a ne xus for creative learning experiences. However, learning does not come inherently with a museum makerspace. Facilitation is a key element of supporting children’s l earning and development  that can boost satisfaction and create a gateway for equitable access through personalization of a learning experience to learners’ needs and interests (Gutwill et al., 2015; White et al, 2021). In successful facilitated making, the educator helps a child progress in the complexi ty of learning, connects the child to activities at  a reasonable challenge level, and encourages interest and engagement (Gutwill et al., 2015). The focus of prior research on facilitation in muse ums, more generally, often centers on educators; mo re research is needed to investigate the influence of different types of facilitators in a museum makersp ace (Krange et al., 2019). In this paper, we explore how the facilitator role influences children’s learning engagement -- specifically the learning practice of seeking and sharing resources (SSR). In this study, we address two research questions: 1) In what ways do learners' SSR vary when facilitated by educators, adult caregivers, peers, or working independently? 2) In what ways do different types of facilitators influence the SSR practices across age ranges? Through this study, we gain insight about which facilitator role tends to support learners to seek and share resources and how this varies by age. The more we understand how to differentially s upport learners across developmental stages, the mo re we can train educators and offer suggestions to caregivers  to be meaningful partners in learning. Second, thi s work builds upon previous research to understand how to concept ualize and study learning through making (e.g. Ward rip & Brahms 2015). In particular,  this work can more ex plicitly articulate learners' engagement in seeking  and sharing resources through the lens of the Learning Practice s of Making framework and how facilitators are part ners in the learning process.

Theoretical and methodological approaches The perspective of overt learning behaviors guides this work. Overt behaviors that learners display in a learning environment can help researchers to understand and analyze learning engagement (Chi, 2009). The Learning Practices of Making framework articulates forms of overt lear ning behaviors in a museum makerspace. One such beh avior is seeking and sharing resources (Wardrip & Brahms, 20 15). The practice of seeking applies to tools, info rmation, or other supports. Sharing refers to an individual offering to others material objects, help or support, suggestions, or ideas. We used the SSR Tool (White et al, 2022) to measure  learners’ seeking and sharing practices in the makerspace. This study included 51, 3-minute video clips including one focal learner (ages 0-18) engaging in a maker activity. The video included seeking and sharing re sources prompted by 4 facilitator roles including e ducators, caregivers, peers, and children themselves. Two res earchers coded the video clips with the SSR tool and yielded a high interrater reliability for SSR practices (alpha=0.94).

## Initial findings and implications

The result indicated tha t facilitation from caregivers and self-directed facilitation is associated with significantly higher SSR practices than facilitation from peers or educators. Compared to educators, when facilitation is prompted by caregivers (p<0.01), and when learners self prompted (p<0.05), they yield a higher amount of SSR practices. This highlights the important role that caregivers and self-directed facilitation can play in a makerspace.

As children age, we expect to see fewer instances of SSR when facilitated by caregivers and more instances of SSR when engaged in self-directed facilitation. A graph of predicted linear regressions for each type of facilitator illustrates the interaction effect of the caregiver group and self-directed group (Figure 1). In the self-directed facilitation group, SSR practices increase as children grow older. On the contrary, children’s SSR practices decline when the facilitat ion is led by their caregivers as they age. Additio nally, we see a slight downward trend in the educators' facilitation as children’s age increases. Facilitation from peers also showed a positive correlation with SSR as age increases. Multivariate regression analysis indicated that the influence of different types of facilitators on learning engagement varies across ages.(RQ2) In our analysis, we see an incline in self-directed facilitation in the makerspace as learners get olde r, with older learners tending to seek and share re sources independently. We also observed that peers play an increasingly important role in makerspaces for chil dren in the early adolescent stage.

These findings opened up additional potential approaches to further support and promote engagement in informal learning environments. First, it would also be beneficial to provide support and guidance for  caregivers so that they can better facilitate childrens’ learning  other than just supporting educators. Our study su ggested that museums also encourage self-directed learning of young learners. Future design decisions, including the exhibit and facilitation design, should also consider how to further enhance the self-directed learning of young learners.
