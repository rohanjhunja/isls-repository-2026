# Viviendo Aquí: Designing for Transformative Agency Through Museum Exhibit Curation With Latinx Youth

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper showcases preliminary research on the process of curating a museum exhibit with Latinx youth. During the 2020-2021 school year, a group of Latinx teenagers developed an exhibit with a local museum. They reported feeling excited to see themselves and their stories represented in their mostly white community. This paper explores preliminary analysis of their engagement with the museum project and the potential community curation holds for disrupting whiteness perpetuated in public spaces.

## Introduction

Community curation has emerged as a common practice for museums working to engage community members in the interpretation and storying of artifacts. Howev er, the boundaries of museum curation and the commu nities that curation is meant to reflect raises questions about the relationships between communities, artifa cts, and curators, particularly when the stories being curated center issues of justice (Black, 2010). This paper showcases preliminary research on the collaborative curation of a museum exhibit to showcase the lives and stories of Latinx youth. Viviendo Aquí which took place over the 2020-2021 school year, included a series of 14 workshops where youth designed and curated a museum exhibit feature d at a U.S. mountain-west museum. Youth participant s engaged in conceptualizing, planning, and designing the Viviendo Aquí museum exhibit. Our research included: How do activities engaging youth in exhibit curatio n afford opportunities for transformative agency? H ow do youth use material, relational and ideational resou rces? What hybrid practices were developed and what opportunities did those provide for reconfiguring power relations within the museum and the community?

## Background

For this study, we drew on notions from sociocultural, cultural-historical, and practice theory to begin interpreting boundary work of community curators. We understand youth participants as experts in distinct communities of practice with boundaries that contrast those of his torically white institutions. Youth became agentic actors who drove expansive learning (Engeström, 2001). To make sense of the trajectories of identification and participation (Polman, 2012) of those involved in curation boundary work, we adapted Nasir and Cooks’ (2009) framework of material, relational, and ideational resources. We examined how resources act as mediational means, influencing identifications and participation of youth and educators in this context (Polman, 2006; Wertsch, 1998).

## Methods

Drawing from participatory, community-based research, the Viviend Aquí exhibit took place in three phases over the 2020-2021 school year during the COVID-19 pandemic. Viviendo Aquí was designed as a series of 12 twohour workshops that were held in a hyflex environment that took place both person and online depending on the constraints presented by the pandemic. A core design group of three teenage co-designers collaborated with one undergraduate research assistant, two university researchers and one museum educator to design the exh ibit. 41 middle and high school students participated in the  final phases of the project. All youth participant s and the undergraduate researcher were bilingual and identified as Latinx, Chicano, Mexican, and Mexican American. The final exhibit was displayed from April to June of 2021 and drew more than 1,500 visitors. Researchers collected audio recordings, artifacts, and interviews and used ethnographic approaches to establish themes across the data.

## Findings

Initially, the researchers, museum educator, and te en designers met to understand the museum context a nd codesign workshops that would engage additional youth participants. Throughout workshop sessions the designers developed ideas to support participants in telling their stories. The design team questioned the histories of museum practices and defined the questions the exhibit would address. Participants and their families were invited to the exhibit on its opening night (see Figure 1). Below, we describe one boundary practice youth added to the exhibit design to leverage their own material, relational, and ideational resources within the museum context. ICLS2022 Proceedings 2018 © ISLS Figure 1 Museum Exhibit and Shadow Box In the first weeks of their engagement with the exhibit, the design team decided to focus on supporting youth participants to tell the “stories of their lives and cultures” in the mountain west. As they engaged in storytelling, the youth voted to focus on “stories of the senses—stories that focused on taste, touch, smell, sights, and sounds”—that they felt defined and represented their experiences across the localities where they had lived, learned, and engaged with family and friends. While these sensory stories led them to design potential exhibits around food, clothing, music, and family gatherings, the design team eventually recognized that they wanted to engage more deeply with the materiality of museum exhibits. Due to the pandemic, the museum’s archives and makerspaces were closed to visitors, which meant that participants had fewer opportunities to engage in traditional museum design practices. However, these barriers meant that designers were offered more opportunities to bring in their own ideas for engaging in building their exhibit. The design team engaged in conversations about the colonizing practices of museums. To disrupt these practices, Teyo, a teen designer, suggested using shadow boxes to allow participants to “tell their stories.” The shadow boxes, which had not before been used in exhibits at the museum, featured materials that participants collected or made and offered youth opportunities to represent their ideational resources—their cultural identities, stories, and relationships—and take on roles as designers. For example, Figure 1 illustrates the ways that Leigha, a high school participant, chose to share the story of her family’s immigration. She explained, “I was born in Durango, Mexico; my parents, my sister and I came to the U.S when I was 4 years old. The scorpion represents where I come from…Durango is known as the ‘land of the scorpions.’” The shadow box acted as her own miniature museum in which she leveraged material resources to depict cultural and familial identities. She went on to explain that she chose the paper cutouts to represent her family members because it was a way to “show how we’re so close.” Connecting these figures acted as a means of representing her family’s bond, one that was forged both through the hardships they faced when immigrating to the U.S. and through their love of their culture and the mountains that characterized home in both Mexico and in the U.S.

## Significance

Expansive learning is characterized by transformation (e.g., Engeström, 2001). By learning about museum curatorial practices and then disrupting those practices to build new boundary practices defined by their own material, relational and ideational means, the youth who participated in Viviendo Aquí were able to reconfigure what it meant to be a museum curator. As Ana, one of the youth designers explained, “I felt like a designer … we can show other people that we, the Latinx community, doesn’t struggle all the time. We also have our culture that’s very important to us.” As Nasir and Cooks (2009) suggest, when youth have opportunities to engage the multitude of resources embedded in their cultural identities, they can leverage their identities toward more expansive and agentic forms of learning and representation.
