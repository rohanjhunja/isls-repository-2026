# How Do Libraries, Archives, and Museums Portray Learning? A Discourse Analysis of Mission and Vision Statements

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Approaches to learning in library, archive, and museum (LAM) spaces vary and institutional barriers make it challenging to design connected learning experiences involving more than one type of LAM. To support the design of high-quality educational experiences, this discourse analysis investigates learning in LAMs as portrayed by their mission and vision statements. The analysis reveals that libraries, archives, and museums use similar linguistic strategies to construct rhetorical communities along geographic, disciplinary, and interest-driven lines. Libraries and archives differed from museums in that they positioned themselves as conduits to learning resources rather than as direct providers of learning experiences. Shared elements of organizational discourses around learning present promising starting points for conversation and collaboration.

## Introduction

Even though they are all cultural institutions, app roaches to learning in library, archive, and museum  (LAM) spaces vary. For example, public and school librari es may have makerspaces that lend themselves well t o openended design and making (Halverson & Sheridan, 2014) whereas instruction in archives may more closely resemble formal, instructor-led educational approaches (Lyons, 2002). Similarly, some museums offer interactive exhibit spaces where youth can explore their own in terests in a hands-on way; others may rely on staff or family members to facilitate a young learner’s experience (Degotardi et al., 2019).  Regardless of differences, how LAM professionals design for and support learning in their institution is inextricably linked with their beliefs, theories, and attitudes toward learning. Similarly, strategic  goals and missions at the organizational level inf luence the kinds of support given to various education-related activities and the extent to which they are/not rooted in various learning theories. To better understand the possibilities for connected learning across LAMs, the present study begins by investigating ideological assumptions, values, and attitudes towa rds learning  in each sphere  as articulated in organizational mission and vision st atements. Specifically, I seek to answer the question, “How is learning constructed in library, archival, and museum discourses?”

## Methods

The present study  analyzes a sub-set of 45 mission and vision statements embedded within annual reports and strategic plans ranging from 10-175 words each and published by 22 libraries, 12 archives, and 10 museums. ## Data analysis

Following Gee’s approach to discourse analysis (1999), I employed close reading, linguistic analysis, and thematic analysis to examine how these documents, collective ly and individually, “shape, produce, and reproduce ” the worlds of education and learning in libraries, arch ives, and museums. Following an inductive coding process, I first coded the mission and vision statements with emergent themes informed by the discursive features of the text and the literature. The analysis process involved reading across and ac ross each subcode for convergences and divergences, especially when it comes to how libraries, a rchives, and museums are discursively constructed as learning spaces.

## Findings

Connectedness to communities was a major theme across library, archival, and museum discourses. LAMS use similar linguistic strategies to construct rhetoric al communities along geographic, disciplinary, and need-based lines. All three positioned their missions and visi ons as fundamentally in service of learners’ indivi dual and collective interests. Using first-person plural pronouns (“we,” “our,” and “ours”), possessive nouns, as well as sentence structure, organizations discursively constructed community along geographic, disciplinary, and cultural lines. Additionally, LAMs shared discursive representations of processes for community engagement. Many positioned themselves as acting in tandem with their target audiences, i.e. “work[ing] with artists and communities to build a better world.”  LAMs’ discursive construction of  community engagement can be interpreted as an implicit endorsement of learning as a process that,  in line with activity theory  (Kaptelinin & Nardi,  2009) empowers learners to act in their own interests. Many LAMs explicitly called out the well-being of their patrons as a core goal, exemplified by the vision that all residents of a s tate “are engaged, informed, enriched, and thriving.” In this regard, LAMs constructed themsel ves as catalysts, convenors, and supporters of lear ning such that community members are empowered to achieve their “aspirations and dreams.” As such, the corpus seems to reflect the gradual shift in information and museum studies toward viewing “the [institution] in the life of a user” (Wiegand, 2003; Crawford Barniskis, 2017) rather than the other way around. One of the major differences in how learning was co nstructed by libraries, archives, and museums in their mission and vision statements was that museum s repeatedly referenced learners’ “experience” wher eas libraries and archives tended to focus on “access”  to resources, services, and programs. Libraries more often described their resources, i.e. “free and easy access to a vast array of knowledge, ideas, and information” whereas museums and archives described activities, such as “[connecting] topics, people and communities throug h generative learning experiences.” In the corpus, museums overwhelmingly described their mission and vision as offering concrete experiences to visitors. By situating learning as an activity that happens in situ, this discursive approach aligns with learning theories such as situated lear ning (Lave & Wenger, 1991), situative approaches (Greeno, 1998), and connected learning (Connected L earning Alliance, n.d.). Libraries, by contrast, discursively positioned themselves as portals to ex periences. Within the corpus, libraries and archives  situated their relationships to learners as those of gatekeepers a nd connectors who provide access to cultural, materials, and human resources such as special collections, books, programs, expert assistance and other services. Linguistically, this theme is illustrated by libraries’ and archives’ repeated use of abstract nouns, namely things like “knowledge” and “information” that cannot be perceived by the senses. Librarians and a rchivists are positioned to serve as scaffolds for  learning there as the “more knowledgeable other” in Vygotsky ’s zone of proximal development (Wood et al., 1976).

## Conclusion

In this corpus, LAMs shared a discursive approach to “learning” as an encounter between an institution (whether library, archive, or museum) and what it can offer (by way of resources or experiences) its audience (be it placebased, disciplinary, or interest-driven). Libraries and archives differed from museums in that  they narratively positioned themselves as conduits to learning resources rather than as direct providers of learning experiences. The shared participatory and community-driven discourses of learning in LAMs point toward a promising shared foundation for coordination and co llaboration.  All three institutions centered the active participation of their users, positioned themselves  in relation to other sociocultural structures, and  focused on providing access to authentic tools, experiences, and contexts. However, in this corpus,  libraries, archives, and museums operate independently of one another. In no mission  or vision statement did an organization mention another library, archive, or museum as a potential partner. Given the overlapping audiences served by LAMs and their ambitious missions, LAMs would be well-served to consider how partnerships with other cultural memory institutions might help them accomplish their goals. This study reveals that, in addition to signaling institutional goals publicly, mission and vision statements might provide a much-needed blueprint to do exactly that.
