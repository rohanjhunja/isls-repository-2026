# Using Epistemic Network Analysis and Sequential Pattern Mining to Explore the Impacts of Human Facilitation on Collaborative Mathematical Problem Solving

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning algebra concepts, particularly those involving functions, is crucial yet challenging in mathematics education. Research suggests that collaborative learning, where students work in small teams to solve math problems, can support conceptual learning. However, collaborative problem solving (CPS) is complex and requires students’ social and cognitive skills for success. This study investigates the impacts of guided facilitation on supporting CPS in mathematics tasks during small-team collaboration. Using epistemic network analysis and sequential pattern mining, we analyzed chat logs from student teams and human facilitators and compared the interactions in facilitated and unfacilitated teams. Results indicated that near-peer facilitation effectively promoted constructive behaviors like multiple turns of social negotiation, while reducing inappropriate communications. Different facilitation strategies, such as encouraging students to respond to each other or clarify their statements, elicited targeted CPS behaviors, possibly leading to improved team performance. Findings have practical implications for facilitating CPS in mathematics classrooms.

## Introduction

The concept of function is central to the study of algebra but also challenging for students to learn (e.g. Vinner & Dreyfus, 1989). For example, previous research using a learning progression and associated tasks to assess student understanding of the concept of function showed that while extending patterns is a straightforward activ ity for students, capturing relationships in abstract form is not (Graf et al., 2021). Both individual and collaborative work are important to mathematics instruction,  as elaborated in the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics (NCTM) guiding principle for school mathematics: “ An excellent mathematics program requires effective teaching that engages students in meaningful learni ng through individual and collaborative experiences  that promote their ability to make sense of mathematical  ideas and reason mathematically” (NCTM, 2024, p.5). Research suggests that collaborative  problem solving can support conceptual mathematics learning (Mullins et al., 2011). As a result, interventions combining small-team collaboration and classroom-based discussion with individual work have been developed to support learning of complex algebra concepts (Moses & Cobb, 2001). Collaborative problem solving Collaborative problem solving (CPS)  involves people working together while sharing info rmation and pooling their individual knowledge to obtain a solution to a problem (OECD, 2013). CPS provide s opportunities for students to actively engage in knowledge  building, which can enhance learning outcomes compared to passive, lecture-based instruction or large group discussions that limit individual  participation (Andrews-Todd et al., 2023). Furthermore, engaging in collaborative activities support s other outcomes such as social, emotional, and psychological well-being, retention of content, self-esteem, peer relationships, motivation, and attitud es toward subjects (Nichols, 1996). In addition to supporting educational outcomes, CPS is also critical for success in today’s workforce and often indicated by employers as a skill they expect from new hires  (Andrews-Todd & Forsyth, 2020). As a  result, technology-based CPS environments have been developed for  real-time assessment and scaffolding of collaboration (Flor & Andrews-Todd, 2022). Facilitation of collaborative activities Despite its importance, CPS is complex and challenging and requires students’ social and cognitive skills for success (Andrews-Todd & Forsyth, 2020). One of this study’s objectives was to explore whether and how human facilitation, particularly near-peer facilitation—where mentors with slightly more expertise guide student discourse (Freeman et al., 2023)—can support productive mathematical conversation during collaboration. While previous research has explored the role of teachers in guiding students’ mathemati cal discourse (e.g., Nathan & Knuth, 2003), less is known about the mechanisms by  which near-peer mentors guide small-team mathematical discussions in online contexts. Near-peer mentors, while not professional educators or subject-matter experts, are slightly more advanced learners who may provide scaffolding and facilitation that is more aligned with students’ needs due to their shared experiences, identities, and social status  (Freeman et al., 2023). To address this gap, in our study, near-peer mentors (e.g., college students) were trained in the use of talk moves (Michaels & O’Connor,

2012) to guide and support productive mathematical conversation  as high school students collaboratively solve

math problems, by eliciting student reas oning and encourag ing students to respond to each other, rather than providing answers. To understand the nature of facilitation, we applied the Low-Inference Discourse Observation (LIDO) framework (Al-Adeimi & O'Connor, 2021; LaRusso et al., 2023) to categorize facilitator discourse moves (i.e., chat turns by facilitators). This approach allows us to examine how facilitation  strategies shape team discourse and collaborative processes.

Educational data mining and learning analytics With rapid advancements in technology, collaboration increasingly occurs virtually through computer-based platforms (Hao et al., 2017). Online collaborative platforms provide valuable oppo rtunities for real-time interaction among learners and enable us to capture rich process data, such as event logs and discourse data (e.g., chats). Process data provide insights about learners’ CPS competence and skills, revealing fine-grained details of their interactions not evident from teams’ final responses or scores. In recent st udies, researchers have applied educational data mining (EDM) and learning analytic s (LA) methods on process data to study CPS in grea ter depth. For example, chat logs during collaboration are often coded based on established frameworks, and the frequencies of different behavioral codes are then computed for teams  to quanti fy the collaboration. Such aggregated frequencies allow researchers to examine the relationships between CPS behaviors and variables such as performance or team composition (Andrews-Todd et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023 b), or to create profiles o f collaborative teams through clustering methods (And rews-Todd et al., 2018). However, these aggregated frequency measures fall short of capturing the dynamic and temporal characteristics of complex CPS processes. Advanced EDM/LA methods such as epistemic network a nalysis and sequential pattern mining have been shown to be powerful in revealing complex, dynamic processes such as self-regulated learning and problem solving (Jiang et al., 2023a; Paquette et al., 2021). These methods are now being explored in CPS studies to shed light on the CPS processes but are still in their e arly stages (Ouyang et al., 2023; Saqr et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). These studies, however, primarily focus on the dynamics of CPS behaviors alone and their relationship to performance. To our knowledge, the current study is the first that applies these advanced methods to investigate the interaction between CPS behaviors and facilitator discourse. Present study Leveraging both process data (e.g., student and facilitator chat logs) and response data, the present study aims  to explore high school students’ collaborative problem solving (CPS) processes as they work in small teams to solve math problems via chats. By comparing facilitated and unfacilitated teams, we investigate the impacts of human facilitation of small-team collaboration on CPS behaviors and learning outcomes. The research questions (RQs) we seek to answer are: RQ1: To what extent does human facilitation affect students’ CPS processes and team performance as they complete math tasks in small teams?

RQ2: How is human facilitation implemented within student discourse during small-team collaboration? How do facilitated teams respond to facilitation moves?

## Participants

Data used in the study w ere collected from 134 Algebra I students in 10  classes across two high schools in the U.S. Data from one school was collected in June 2023 while data from the other school was collected in February

2024. While demographic information was not gathered for the sample, publicly available data from NCES

(nces.ed.gov) indicates that more than 95% of stude nts at the schools identify as either Black or Hispanic and more than 75% qualify for free or reduced-price lunch. The students completed three pairs of parallel math tasks in 42 teams  in an online platform as part of the study. These teams included six dyads, 22 triads, and 14 four-person teams. Using a block randomization approach (Suresh, 2011), teams were randomly assigned to either the treatment condition where a mentor facilitated the discussions and provided guidance to enhance the collaborative experience, or the control condition where no facilitation was provided. Most facilitators in the treatment condition were near-peer mentors. In total, 24 teams received facilitation, and 18 teams completed the tasks without facilitation.

Instruments Three pairs of parallel tasks targeting the concept of function  (Graf et al., 2021; 2024) were developed and used in this study. Each task comprises 5-6 questions with a variety of  response types and was designed based on a Learning Progression (LP) for the concept of function. The task pairs include  Backpacks and Book Orders, Jogging Paths and Trail Map, and Restaurant Tables and Shape Patterns, with each pair of tasks assessing identical concepts and skills. These tasks were embedded in an online collaborative platform where s tudents can work in teams and engage in discussion to solve problems via a text-based chat box (see Figure 1). The question responses were scored using rubrics which provided context-specific interpretations of the LP. In the rubrics, each possible response is mapped to a level of the LP, which include s five levels. The rubrics were developed iteratively and refined across successive data collections (Graf & van Rijn, 2016). ## Procedure

The study took place in a traditional classroom setting across four 40-minute class periods on consecutive days. Students within e ach class were assigned to complete a pair of tasks  using individual computers. On Day 1, students completed a task individually. On Day 2, they revisited the task and worked in teams to solve it via online chats. Teams were randomly assigned to complete the task with or without facilitation. On Day 3, teachers led a classroom discussion on the same task to deepen students’ understanding. On Day 4, students completed a near-transfer task (the parallel task) individually. The order of tasks was counterbalanced at the class level to minimize order effects. In this study, we analyzed the process data and response data collected during the team collaboration phase on Day 2.

## Chat data analysis

A total of 2,658 chat turns produced by facilitators (658 turns) and students (2,000 turns) were coded and analyzed. Coding CPS skills Each student chat turn was coded using the CPS Ontology (Andrews-Todd & Forsyth, 2020; Andrews-Todd et al., 2023), which provides a competency model  for CPS  that lays out the  concepts aligned with CPS, their relationships, and behaviors that show evidence of each skill. The ontology includes three dimensions and CPS behaviors (codes) within each dimension. The first dimension  (Communicative Participation)  includes general participation in communication  with behaviors maintaining communication (SMC), sharing informatio n (SSI), and establishing shared understanding (SESU). The second dimension (Social Regulation) involves behaviors for addressing differences in perspectives and monitori ng the organization of the team and evaluating prog ress, and includes negotiating (SN) and monitoring (CM)  behaviors. The final dimension (Task Regulation and Activity) includes behaviors used to manage, coordinate, and enact tas k activities with the CPS behaviors  exploring and understanding (CEU), representing and formulating (CRF), planning (CP), and executing (CE). Two raters trained on the CPS ontology coded all student chats for the presence of CPS behavior codes (Cohen’s Kappa =.71). Coding facilitation moves As noted in Introduction, we used the LIDO (Al-Adeimi & O'Connor, 2021; LaRusso et al., 2023) teacher codes to categorize chat turns by facilitators. Each facilitator chat turn received a LIDO code. The code categories in the teacher LIDO are defined as follows:  T1—Encourage student(s) to respond to each other; T2 —Encourage students to explain, clarify, provide reasoning; T3—Encourage students to continue; T4—Pose open, contestable question; T5—Pose partially open question; and T6—Pose closed response question. A turn that did not fall into any of these categories was coded as “No Claim.”  For the June 2023 data, 70% of the facilitator chat turns were double-coded by two pairs of raters using the Teacher LIDO, achieving an inter-rater reliability of Cohen’s kappa =.64. Once substantial agreement was achieved, the raters  coded the remaining data independently. For the February 2024 data, a single experienced rater coded the facilitator chat turns.

## Data analysis

To address the research questions, we analyzed the chat data from both students and facilitators as the teams completed math tasks, as well as response data on these tasks. EDM and LA techniques such as feature engineering, epistemic network analysis (ENA), and sequen tial pattern  mining w ere applied to explore how students collaborate in small teams and how facilitation is implemented. Specifically, we generated features from the chat turns to quantify CPS skills. Key features include the number of chat turns produ ced by teams per question and the average frequency of chats that belong to each CPS skill per question. These features, along with  team performance on the tasks (average Learning Progression level per question), were compared between facilitated and unfacilitated teams to investigate how human facilitation supports CPS behaviors and team perfor mance (RQ1). Two-tailed Mann-Whitney U tests were conducted on the process and response variables for comparisons to account for non-parametric distributions. Given the relatively small sample size, we reported Cohen’s d (1988) as a measure of effect size, with d = 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 indicating small, medium, and large effects, respectively. To address RQ2, we focused on the facilitated teams and analyzed the distribution of LIDO codes to characterize the nature of facilitation. In addition, ENA and sequential pattern mining were  conducted to explore interaction dynamics and answer RQs.

Epistemic network analysis Epistemic network analysis (ENA) was applied to the chat data using the ENA webtool (Marquart et al., 2018). Networks were generated using a moving window  of th ree chat turns, following Paquette et al. (2021). This moving window assumes connections between a chat turn and the two preceding turns, accounting for cases where a student responds to an earlier chat  rather than the immediately preceding one. We also tested different  grain sizes like a window of 4 and obtained similar results.

We generated ENA networks of CPS and LIDO codes for the facilitated teams to examine how facilitators support the collaboration and how stud ents respond to the facilitation moves (RQ 2). In the resulting networks, the CPS and LIDO codes as described above are represented as nodes, with node size reflecting code frequency and edge thickness indicating the strength of the co-occurrences. Thicker edges suggest more frequent co-occurrences and connections between nodes. These networks enable us to study the connections  between human facilitation moves and CPS behaviors.

In addition, we compared the networks of the CPS sk ills between the facilitated and unfacilitated teams to identify potential impacts of facilitation on CP S behaviors and processes  (RQ1). Subtracted networks were examined to identify differences in the connections among CPS behaviors. By subtracting edge weights between networks, the subtracted networks allow us to identify connections that are strengthened by facilitation. Sequential pattern mining To capture temporal patterns in chat data, we conducted sequential pattern mining to identify seq uential patterns representative of students’ and facilitators’ inter actions. These sequential patterns provided us with  unique insights into the transitions within and between CP S behaviors and facilitation moves (e.g., multi-turn patterns), beyond the aggregated team-level features and epistemic networks indicative of the overall interaction processes. Data preprocessing was first conducted to clean and  transform the raw chat logs into sequences that re flect transitions between CPS and facilitation behaviors  (e.g., T2→SSI→T1→SN). To capture nuances in speaker transitions, we labeled utterances from the same student as the previous chat with “_same” to distingu ish them from chats where there is a student change between chats. For example, “MC → SSI_same → SN” indicates that a single student first maintained communication, and then shared information, after which another team member negotiated. Events without the “_same” label indicate a student change between turns. We then applied sequential pattern mining on the sequences to identify meaningful and interesting interaction patterns. A set of most frequent sequential patterns that meet the thresholds for frequency metrics (20%) were identified by employing the cSPADE sequential patte rn mining algorithm, which has been applied in numerous educational studies (Jiang et al., 202 5; Zhang & Paquette, 2023). Consistent with Jiang et al. (2025) and Zhang and Paquette (2023), we computed support and instance values as frequency measures. The support value is defined as the proportion of sequences in which the  pattern (e.g., A → B) occurs. The instance value corresponds to the number of times a given pattern occurs within a student’s sequence.  Frequent sequential patterns of consecutive moves were selected with the cut-off thresholds of support  = 0.2 (i.e., at least 20% of teams in one group showed the pattern). We further conducted differential pattern mining to  identify patterns of CPS behaviors that are differentially prevalent between facilitated and unfacilitated teams. The instance v alue of each pre-identified pattern was calculated for each team in each group, after which Mann Whitney U  tests comparing the instance values between the group s were conducted to select patterns that were diffe rentially frequent.

## Results and discussion

Interaction between facilitation and CPS behaviors In facilitated conversations, more than half of the facilitation moves were coded as "no claim" (53.6%), followed by T1 (encourag e students to respond to each other) at 15.7%, T2 (encourag e students to explain, clarify, and provide reasoning) at 14.0%, T5 (pose partially ope n question) at 11.2%, T6 (pose closed response ques tion) at 4.6%, and T3 (encourage students to continue) at 0.9%. No facilitation moves were coded as T4  (pose open, contestable question). In general, dialogic scaffolds, such as T1 and T2, were more frequently observed than content-specific questions (T4-T6).

Figure 2 presents the network of facilitation moves  (coded by the LIDO framework) and student moves (coded based on CPS skills) within facilitated teams. The most prevalent CPS behaviors were sharing information and negotiating, followed by establishing shared understanding and maintaining communication. The network revealed that T1 facilitation – encouraging students to respond to each other – was more likely to co-occur with negotiation (SN)  behaviors, where students  express agreement, disagreement, or try to resolve conflicts. In contrast, encouraging students to explain, clarify, and provide reasoning (T2), as well as other facilitation moves, were more likely to co-occur with sharing information (SSI), where student s share their own information, task/resource information, or understanding. The network analysis indicates that T1 and T2 moves are instrumental in fostering communicative participati on and collaborative negotiation among students. These associations align with the intended purposes of the discourse moves and suggest the effectiveness of facilitation in prompting students to engage in meaningful exchanges. ## Conclusions and implications

To explore high school students’ CPS processes, we applied EDM and LA methods such as epistemic n etwork analysis and sequential pattern mining on the ir chat data as they completed math tasks in small teams. By comparing facilitated teams and unfacilitated teams, we investigated the impacts of guided facilitatio n on CPS dynamics and team performance. We also examined the  network structures and sequential patterns among teams whose conversations were facilitated to understand the interaction between facilitation moves and CPS behaviors.

Our findings reveal that near-peer facilitators frequently guided the collaborati ve processes by encouraging students to respond to each other, expl ain, clarify, and provide reasoning, and posing par tially open questions to promote deeper engagement with t he task.  This facilitation effectively triggered different CPS interactions. For instance, prompting students to r espond to each other frequently was effective in fo stering negotiation, as students took turns to express agre ement or disagreement with other  team members. In contrast, encouraging students to explain led to more instanc es of information sharing, where team members provi ded knowledge, resources, or perspectives. This differe ntiation illustrates that targeted facilitation can  help scaffold CPS in ways that match the specific social and cognitive demands of the task at hand, ultimately supporting more meaningful and constructive interactions.

Our results show the benefits of the interactive, stude nt-centered facilitation in fostering constructive collaborative behaviors (e.g., engaging in negotiat ion) that are beneficial for understanding math con cepts. Furthermore, facilitation led to fewer inapp ropriate conversations. The facilitation moves prom pted students to take ownership of their learning by actively engaging with their peers and constructing knowledge collaboratively, and ensured team members stayed on task. These interactions likely led to  better performance on the math task for facilitated teams, as students engaged in deeper processing when responding to prompts and negotiating with peers. Unfacilitated teams, on the other hand, got fewer opportunities to process and acknowledge others’ thoughts given the need to manage their conversations by themselves without external coordination. This work contributes to understanding how trained human facilitators support collaborative behaviors in small-group teams and promote math learning. Findings also have practical implications for educators seeking to leverage facilitation strategies to enhance student engagement and support learning in collaborative classroom settings. They also inform the design of computer-based collaborative math tasks, in which near-peer facilitation could be embedded. While these findings provide ins ights into the effectiveness of facilitation in fostering  CPS, the relatively small sample size limits the generalizability of the results. Future research would benefit from larger samples and a broader range of tasks to test the robustness of the findings. While this study identified differences in team performance on math tasks between conditions, it will be interesting to explore the relationships between the CPS behaviors and performance outcomes, including performance on the near-transfer task. Establishing direct connections between CPS processes and perfor mance would allow for a better understanding of facilitation’s effects on learning outcomes. Furthermore, examining temporal shifts in interaction patterns across different problem-solving stages  (e.g., early versus later phases) would provide deeper insights into how facilitation influences student engagement and strategy use over time. Investigating these trends would shed light on whether facilitation effects are sustained throughout the task and whether students develop long-term CPS skills as a result. Previous research indicates that team composition s uch as knowledge level and group diversity has significant impacts on team dynamics and outcomes (Jiang et al., 2023b). Future research should also consider team composition variables to explore how different group structures might interact with facilitation strategies to optimize collaborative learning.
