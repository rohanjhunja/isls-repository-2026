# “He probably did something really, really badder than anything”: Leveraging Black Children’s Sociopolitical Sensemaking as Resources in Co-Learning with Educators

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigated how educators interpreted and responded to a case of a Black child’s sociopolitical sensemaking. As part of a larger social design experiment and interviews conducted with 12 justice-oriented teachers, I presented participants with an extended example of a child (“Chase”) describing circumstances surrounding the highly-publicized murder of Freddie Gray. The purpose of this paper is to (1) report on select findings related to educators’ analysis of the Chase example and (2) discuss the relationship between their sensemaking and subsequent pedagogical design thinking. Findings show how many defaulted to developmental lenses that emphasized naivete, empathizing while overlooking the complex reasoning embedded in Chase’s ideas. This shaped the pedagogical possibilities they imagined in response. This work highlights the need for conceptual and methodological tools that attune educators to the sociopolitical and intellectual lives of Black children and, that position children as epistemic contributors and partners in justice-oriented educational design.

The political savvy that Black children regularly embody to interpret, navigate and/or resist unjust social systems is often overlooked as a valuable index and mover of learning (Davis et al, 2020; Lee, 2017). This exploratory study is oriented toward the development of attuned methodological and conceptual tools that help educators learn from and design for Black children. More precisely, I consider when and how close analyses of Black children’s sociopolitical ideas carry potential in shaping teachers’ pedagogical philosophies and imagination (Domínguez, 2019). Data for this preliminary study is taken from a larger project focused on critical educators’ conceptualizations of childhood, children’s meaning making, and the interpretive repertoires that teachers draw from to design and enact justice-oriented pedagogy. As part of that study, 12 teachers were asked to engage with an excerpt from a 4th grade child (Chase) taken as he discussed a highly-publicized instance of police homicide.

In this paper, I report findings related to educators’ analyses of Chase’s sensemaking alongside their emer gent conceptions of what it might look like to design curricula in support of Chase’s/his peers’ learning. Where current discourses in critical pedagogy and sociopolitical learning tend to assume adolescence/young adulthood as the developmental starting place, I consider in this work how a recentering and reimagining of Black childhood could facilitate generati ve forms of teacher co-learning. The  side-by-side (Erickson, 2012), intergenerational study focus is squarely aligned with the 2026 ICLS theme of centering partnerships and connected learning. In this intense moment of political instability, where matters of identity, educational justice and civic participation are entangled with theories of learning (Philip & Sengupta, 2021), there is an urgent need to ensure that teachers have the capacity to support students’ sociopolitical sensemaking. Part of this work, I argue, hinges on direct support and engagement with elementary teachers with the goal of bridging the gap(s) between critical theory, design and children’s learning. While there has been growing attention to critical civic pedagogies and adolescents’ sociopolitical development (e.g. Melendez et al, 2023), children’s (and in particular, Black children’s) sociopolitical meaning making is seldom the empirical focus. In both the learning sciences and education research more broadly, the relative inattentiveness to Black children as social and political actors (Dumas & Nelson, 2016; Garlen; 2019) has also limited opportunities to identify the types of pedagogical and relational practices that support their critical analyses and learning. Conceptions of what it looks like for teachers to navigate the complexities of civic pedagogies in elementary classrooms and learning environments are principally based on theories developed with adult or adolescents’ positioning and meaning making in mind. The emphasis on adolescents, while undoubtedly important, has obscured our ability to see how configurations of place, lived experience, age, race, and disciplinary knowledge may be relevant to theory, design and practice (Nasir et al., 2006). The dearth of research in this area also contributes to the status quo of reluctance to engage younger children in explicit conversations about race/racism, structural inequities, and human rights issues, even when it 1237 is evident that they are already actively thinking (and living) with these concepts (Davis, 2026; Davis & Schaeffer; 2019; Woodson & Love, 2019; Nasir, 2018; Davis  & Philip, 2026). Such conversations and forms of knowledge are often a core facet of home and community life for children of color but remain largely unattended to in spaces of formal learning. So,  while the field has advanced valuable research related to children’s identity and disciplinary meaning making (e.g. Varelas et al, 2013), research leaves unanswered questions about educators’ in-the-moment interpretations of and engagement with Black chil dren’s sociopolitical ideas. Relatedly, the practical examples of critical pedagogical design that are readily available to teachers do not generally offer close accounts of the nuances and tensions in advancing this curriculum with and for Black children. Prior research leaves unanswered questions of if/how critical, justice-oriented frameworks align with the needs of Black children who might already be engaging in emergent sociopolitical sensemaking and would further benefit from the humanizing experience of having their thinking taken seriously (Espinoza et al, 2020). This context suggests a need to attend to Black children’s sensemaking alongside efforts to develop critical, justice-oriented models. Attunement to Black children’s ideas while engaging in justice-oriented design-work also coincides with the broader call for research that conveys who Black children are, “what they think, and what they desire in their lives now,” without presupposing a static condition (Dumas & Nelson, 2016). This study was designed to leverage Black children’s sociopolitical sensemaking as resources in elementary teacher learning. For a larger project, I conducted a social design experiment (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016) with 12 justice-oriented educators. Social design experiments are defined as educational interventions focused on “pressing problems of practice and the realization of a learning and social change agenda” (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016, p. 570). In preparation for the co-design of an educational artifact,  educators were guided through a series of individual and group explorations as tied to their professional practice. In a focus group and semi-structured interviews, educators were also asked to directly engage with Black children’s sociopolitical ideas. The anonymized examples of children’s sensemaking were  drawn from prior research with 9-12 years olds and broached timely topics of the presidential election, anti-Black police brutality and local environmental crises. Given the historical, cyclical nature of these ideas and relevance to the present, my goal was to first understand how teachers were generally conceptualizing and navigating these recurrent dynamics in their work. Toward the end of teachers’ initial interviews, I presented participants with an  extended example of a child (“Chase”) describing the circumstances surrounding the Baltimore murder of Freddie Carlos Gray. The purpose of this short paper is to (1) report on findings related to educators’ interpretation of the Chase example and (2) disc uss the relationship between their sensemaking and subsequent pedagogical design thinking. More precisely, the analysis offered here was guided by the following research questions: When presented with an excerpt from a child (Chase), how did educators interpret the content and significance of his sociopolitical sensemaking? What pedagogical insights into stance and pedagogy surfaced in response to this case? Reflected in these questions are my beliefs in the need to honor Black children’s actual, rather than imagined sociopolitical understandings. In positioning children’s ideas as ideational and pedagogical resources, this project aims to contribute to the development of critical design frameworks that recognize the brilliance and needs of Black children in context. Data sources, case materials and analysis While not an exhaustive account, below I provide additional methodological context and then hone into the aspect of the project that is to be the focus of this paper. This qualitative study of teacher sensemaking used artifacts from a prior research project. Teachers (n=12) enrolled in (or alumni of) a graduate program in creative and critical education took part in 65-minute semi-structured interviews. All teachers (2 men, 10 women) in the study identified as Black and worked primarily with Black youth in a major southern city. At the time of data collection, I was already well acquainted  with each participant because all were affiliated with M.A. program where I was employed. Through this program, educators engaged in explicit training focused on expanding conceptions of learning, understanding children in society, and innovative  design work. The interview protocol first captured participants’ backgrounds and pedagogical philosophies, with explicit examples from their practice. Next, they were asked questions about their own childhood, (Black) childhoods  and children in society intended to surface working conceptions of childhood relative to broader sociohistorical contexts for living. Toward the end of the initial interviews, I shared an example taken from the prior ethnographic study referenced above. Below I show, via an excerpt from the protocol, how Chase’s commentary was introduced to participating teachers: I want to share a couple of exchanges between a 4th grader and me.  He was discussing the death of Freddie Gray (offer background if needed, a handout with the following then shared): 1238 Chase (child): That remind me of what the cops did to that man – [the police] put a man in handcuffs because he did something bad or something...he was not strapped in, and they was driving real fast, hitting corners, hitting the brakes real hard... and they speed around this one corner and the guy hit his neck on the seat or something and broke it.

Natalie (researcher): So when you mentioned the problem about the guy not being strapped in, do you think that that had anything to do with that man's skin color? Chase: No but what I was thinking he probably did something really, really badder than anything that they didn't want to strap him in, and they was just trying to hurt him but not kill him...

Teachers were shown and (with time to process) asked to make meaning of the details of this exchange. I then prompted them to think about how they might respond to a similar sentiment if it arose in teaching, to include potential design interventions. Above, Chase articulated a working theory of the practical and ethical circumstances surrounding Gray’s killing. His everyday sensemaking of police as rational authority seemingly disencumbered them of their responsibility to uphold statutes, to include citizens’ rights to due process. If provided with an opportunity to engage with Chase’s around his ideas, teachers in his school may have thought differently about how to approach the complex topics of policing and rights. For instance, teachers might consider incorporating into civics unit activities that underscore why checks and balances exist, speaking to implicit/explicit biases and the histories of discriminatory practices within our legal system.

Using a comprehensive coding scheme, I engaged in an iterative process of data reduction and interpretation aimed at understanding justice-oriented elementary teachers’ noticing (Van Es & Sherin, 2008) and layered meaning making with regard to Chase’s sociopolitical understandings. Transcripts of interviews with individual teachers were read and excerpts coded to discern patterns of similarity and dissimilarity (Maxwell & Miller, 2008). I traced emergent patterns in how critical educators articulated their role(s) when presented with an example of children’s talk that spanned the intellectual/civic, developmental, cultural, moral and sociopolitical domains. These articulated and embodied ideas around roles are in line with what Goodwin (2007) refers to as stance(s). Goodwin’s (2007) concept of stance offers analytical guidance in attuning to the layers of meaning making and thinking that surfaced for teachers --this is key given the nature and goals of critical pedagogy. More specifically, data was coded to d etermine if/how teachers’ analyses of the Chase example during the interview corresponded with the following dimensions: (1) Disciplinary  Specific Knowledge (e.g. civics, anatomy & physiology, physics) (2) Affective & Ethical, (3) Sociopolitical and Sociohistorical (e.g. histories of racism), (4) Cooperative Civic Behaviors. Additionally, I consider in my analysis patterns in how teachers  identified areas of knowing/unknowing (e.g. naivete) for Chase and children more broadly. I also analyzed teachers’ ideas about how their pedagogy could become responsive to these forms of sensemaking. Analysis is ongoing. I turn now to report preliminary findings related to teacher talk and with consideration of layers of meaning making.

## Preliminary findings

Educators’ analyses of the Chase example most frequently privileged the (1) affective dimensions and potential traumas he was experiencing and (2) perceived areas of unknowing and innocence. For example, teacher Ciara said, “I can kind of see that as traum atic on his end, like even if right now, at this age, he didn't feel like it was about color…”. Ciara’s focus here was on perceived trauma while also suggesting that due to Chase’s age, it’s expected that he’d be less aware of racial biases. Ciara framed C hase’s response as developmental but did not elaborate on the buds of development (Vygotsky, 1978) to be nurtured by adults beyond awareness of racialized discrimination. Similarly, others expressed interpretations like “I mean, that to me just shows the pure – like what I mentioned before, like the child has no baggage, and so they’re assuming...There's no way that this man would get this punishment having done nothing wrong. So he’s just like so pure and sweet” (Zoey). Again, the participants' focus seemed to be on innocence, purity and what Chase did not-yet-fully understand about the harsh realities of the world. Though they offered compassionate replies, a limited spectrum of layers and nuances in Chase’s response were taken up directly in my initial conversations with teachers. Participating teachers were clearly committed to justice-oriented practice and concerned about children’s sociopolitical learning. Still, normative conceptions of childhood (e.g. as apolitical) seemed to be pervasive and influential. I highlight these co-design opportunities.

To demonstrate this, I provide an illustrative example from Amani, a dynamic educator who had a particular interest in child development. Like others, Amani understood Chase as emergent in his knowledge of anti-Black police brutality: …he has some understanding, but you can tell he clearly didn't really understand like you know or it was naive to like the underlying issues as to why 'cause even when you asked, ‘do you think it had anything to do with the man's skin color?’ and he said “no” I thought then…he probably doesn't really understand like that. This is probably what the whole thing is about. Though she offered a thoughtful take on police violence as a sociohistorical and racially-charged issue, Amani’s ideas seemed to be mediated through an understanding of children as inherently “naive” to these dynamics. Her use of “probably” conveys tentative thinking, and there seemed to be a confluence of her personal analyses and expectations for how a savvy child would respond. When I asked Amani how she  might respond in practice to one of her students if they said something similar to Chase, interestingly, she defaulted to common topics and practices at her school. These ideas are evident in the following reply: [I’d] kind of relate it back to like Martin Luther King or something like that, 'cause that's something we do learn about and talk about in school so I would say, you know….what Dr. King was fighting for, like people get mistreated for their skin color like that does happen– if they were to ask me about that I would definitely tell them that, yeah. What I think would be overstepping boundaries would be like “oh yeah, the police killed him because he was a black man…that's a conversation you have with your c hild…that's your opinion, you know? Notice that this concept for a lesson responds to a perceived deficit or misunderstanding and does not necessarily account for areas of depth and complexity in Chase’s reasoning. Amani also raised an important tension in justiceoriented practice–concerns about overstepping or indoctrinating students based on teachers’ own ideologies. She was reluctant (perhaps rightly so) to advance what she saw as her individual truth. Reluctance was also a theme across other responses. For example, when I asked Ciara how she might respond she expressed, “That's something I struggle with like, yeah, you told me the story, but what do I say now?” and after some thinking replied, “[I’d say] oh no, that's terrible what happened...My question would have been based off—his emo tions. I don't know if I would have been able to bring it to that question of getting him to think about [race and justice]”. Ciara’s concept of pedagogical intervention stemmed from her interpretation of Chase’s words as a window into his innocence and the threat of trauma. Based on teachers’ responses, the next phase of the study involved a follow-up workshop that explored strategies for navigating and responding to Black children’s complex sociopolitical understandings. A subset of teachers also initiated “dreaming” projects that were designed with the children’s holistic needs and sensemaking in mind.

Through a close analysis of teachers’  engagement with Chase’s ideas, this study offers preliminary insight into how a focus on (Black) childhood can necessarily deepen and shift theories of sociopolitical/civic learning, thus intervening on the adult and adolescent-centered discourses and design models that have long characterized the field. To date, research on sociopolitical learning and critical pedagogy have not adequately addressed how children’s intersectional identities, meaning making and context are germane to the “what” and “how” of justiceoriented teaching in elementary schools (Philip et al, 2018). The field has therefore missed opportunities to leverage the everyday, sociopolitical sensemaking of Black children as resources in pedagogical  design and attunement. This paper reports findings with an eye toward sharpening methodological and conceptual tools necessary for "purposeful partnership” between elementary teachers, Black children and researchers. Supporting teachers to better understand children  and build from their sociopolit ical ideas is in line with what Philip (2019) specifies as the relational, “principled improvisational” work necessary for justice-oriented teaching. Structured opportunities to engage in critical praxis are likely to directly support the work of elementary teachers, especially in this era where they may feel particularly taxed in contending with complex sociohistorical issues and when most schools offer limited or no resources to this end (Lisle-Johnson & Kohli, 2020).
