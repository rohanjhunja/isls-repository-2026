# Pedagogical Issues in Virtual Reality Mathematics Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We discuss pedagogical challenges that arise when head mounted displays (HMDs), are used for mathematics tutoring. 16 middle school students used HMDs to participate in seven VR mathematics tutoring sessions. Sessions were video recorded and analyzed using Social Infrastructural Framework. We identified three pedagogical issues: managing student discipline; enacting social identities; and managing virtual bodies. We describe each issue, illustrate its occurrence, and discuss possible remedies.

## Introduction

In this paper, we discuss  pedagogical issues that ar ose when  head-mounted displays (HMDs)  were used for tutoring middle school students in mathematics. HMDs are wearable virtual reality (VR) devices (Makranksky & Petersen, 2021; Milgram & Kishino, 1994)  which are immersive and dynamic (Onyesoly & Eve, 2011). That is, HMDs surround a person in a computer-generated world (immersive) where they can interact with elements of the virtual environment  (dynamic). While m any HM D devices are designed for individual use, our HMD technology supports collaborative and remotely accessed activity. That is, multiple people in physically separate locations (remotely accessed) can use this HMD technology to work together (collaborative). The immersive and dynamic nature of HMDs allow people to view and interact with difficult-toconceptualize mathematical concepts  (Hohenwater & Fuchs, 2004). T he collaborative and remotely accessed affordances of our specific HMDs allow people to engage in shared mathematical activity without h aving to be in the same physical location  (Marky et al., 2019; Philippe et al., 2020; Zheng et  al., 2018). Despite these affordances, there is little research which examines how people learn mathematic s using HMDs (Pellas et al., 2020). Furthermore, even if they prove useful for math teaching, technocentric school reforms can often reinscribe injustices in K-12 education, rather than ameliorate them (Philip, 2017).

To address these challenges, we conducted a mathematic s tutoring study with middle school students using immersive, dynamic, collaborative, and remotely accessed HMDs. We set out to identify pedagogical issues unique to HMDs for mathematics tutoring. The research question guiding this study was: What pedagogical issues that are novel to HMDs were salient to participants in a middle school math tutoring environment?

## Theoretical framework

In this study, we drew on Social Infrastructural Framework (SIF; Bielaczyc, 2006). SIF outlines four dimensions for critiquing how  technology interacts in classroom spaces: First, the cultural beliefs dimension describes the “mindset that shapes the way of life of the classro om” (Bielaczyc, 2006, p. 303). These are the belief s about teaching and learning that emerge after a new technology has been integrated. Our research used HMDs to engage middle grade students in math tutoring. Tutoring typically involves small groups, low stakes learning, and flexible pacing. We did not anticipate many changes to cultural beliefs surrounding tutoring. However, our use of HMDs for tutoring was intended to expand what counts as mathematical competence. In this study, the body and gesture became rich sites through which mathematical reasoning could unfold. Second, the practice dimension describes the “ways in which teachers and students engage in both online and offline learning activities relatin g to the technology-based tool” (Bielaczyc, 2006, p. 303). In other wor ds, the new patterns of human behavior made possible by a technology. We anticipated many chang es related to this dimension. HMDs allow learners to view and manipulate ideal shapes  that are difficult to visualize in the real world. HMDs also allow learners to collaborate in ways that are immaterial and reversible. Third, the socio-techno-spatial relations dimension describes “the organization of physical space and cyberspace as they relate to the teacher and student interactions with technology-based tools.” (Bielaczyc, 2006, p. 304). Technologies can bridge vast spatial and temporal extents and create social connections that were previously difficult or impossible. We expected this to be the  most significantly impacted dimension. The learners in our study  could meet and work with adult tutors or other students from far-away areas and easily move between virtual classrooms. Fourth, the ‘outside world’ dimension describes how technology enhances or changes the ab ility of teachers and students in one physical loca tion to work with people in different physical locations. In our case, we find this similar to the socio-techno-spatial relations dimension. HMDs change the socio-techno-spatial relations such that people and places from the ‘outside’ world can be brought in close (virtual) proximity to the classroom.

## Background on pedagogical issues

We defined pedagogical issues as shifts in the social infrastructure which caused power imbalances among tutors and learners. In this paper, we discuss three pedagogical issues: (1) classroom management through surveillance and isolation; (2) the avatar system and social identity; and (3) management of mobility and gesture. Before we describe how these issues emerged in our data, we provide some background on each issue: First, surveillance and isolation  in U. S. classrooms have long been a hotly debated topic (Warnick, 2007). While many have considered the consequences of surv eillance and isolation, Brady (2012) made four recommendations for ethical surveillance: (1) surveillance should be open or always up for reconsideration; (2) surveillance should empower those who are being surveilled; (3) the process and  goals of surveillance should be transparent; and (4) video records should be erased as soon as they are able to be.  We extend Brady’s recommendations to our own case where tutors used surveillance to encourage positive student behavior. Second, avatar s are authorable artificial identities for represen ting people in virtual environments (Allmendinger et al., 2009). Avatars were designed to allow learners to express themselves, identify othe rs, and achieve social presence in a virtual environment (Peterson, 2006). However, less is known about the relationship between people’s own social identities and the proc ess of representing these identities in virtual spa ce. We take up this discussion in our findings and recommendations. And third, the way in which students’ movement and gesture is directed or controlled may become an important issue in VR environments. Vickery (2023) presents study of children with moderate-to-severe disabilities impacting communication engaging with an immersive mixed-reality learning environment. She found learners grappled with issues related to overstimulation, wanting to control and have choice about their degree of movement, and having preferences for acting independently versus collaborating. Both ensuring accessibility and allowing for bodily autonomy are critical considera tions for virtual reality learning activities. Indeed, research suggests that doing physical movement activities can cause some learners embarrassment (Zohar, 2022), and that students’ willingness to participate in activities where they move their bodies in physical and visible ways should not be assumed (Mathayas et al., 2022). However, other perspectives highlight how typical school mat hematics experiences seek to control and regulate the bodies  of racialized and minoritized students, and that movementbased activities can also be a source of liberation (Liu & Takeuchi, 2023). Such activities can “open up more spaces for making sense of mathematics and modelling, rooted in students heterogeneous lived experiences” (Liu & Takeuchi, 2023, p. 282). We take up further discussion of these three issues in our concluding remarks.

## Context and participants

The tutoring study took place in three physical contexts: a private university and two public middle schools. Each site w as located in a different neighborhood  within a large city in the U. S. Southwest. All three sites were connected by the virtual tutoring environment acces sed through HMDs. This virtual environment allowed participants from the university and middle schools  to engage in synchronous and collaborative mathema tical activity. The larger tutoring study took place after school for seven consecutive weeks, in the Spring of 2023.

The study involved four tutors and 16 middle grade students. The tutors were recruited from the university. Three of the tutors were undergraduate students studying education and had prior experience teaching middle school mathematics. They chose the following pseudonyms: Jandy, Norbert, and Yuxi. Jandy (she/her) identifies as a Hispanic/Mexican woman, Norbert (he/him) identifies as a White man, and Yuxi (she/her) identifies as Chinese woman. One of the tutors, Kevin (he/him, also a pseudonym) was a staff member of the university’s technology design laboratory and was extensively familiar with the virtual tutoring environment. Kevin identifies as an Asian man. Prior to the study, the tutors received training related to the virtual reality tutoring environment and relevant geometry concepts. During the study, the tutors participated in weekly check-in meetings to discuss pedagogical issues related to VR or geometry concepts.

The middle-grade students were recruited from two local middle school after-school programs. The afterschool programs were designed by the school sites t o provide additional instruction and enrichment to students. Our research team recruited from within the existing after-school programs. Nine students were in seventh grade, and six were in eighth grade. Five students identif ied as girls and 10 students identified as boys. Te n students identified as Hispanic, five students identified as African American, and one student identified as White. Because of the physical separation, tutors and students never had a chance to meet one another in person. Virtual reality tutoring environment The VR tutoring environment was co-designed by members of the research team and developers from GeoGebra. The environment was deployed using Oculus Quest goggles (a brand of HMD). The environment was rendered to resemble an open room with a tiled floor, a door, a room number, multiple windows, and a pastoral environment just beyond the windows. For brevity’s sake we will explain three facets of the environment. Selecting a pseudonym and avatar When tutors or students put on the goggles and opened the tutoring environment, they were prom pted to enter a name. Students were instructed to enter a pseudonym in place of their real name for the duration of the study. This pseudonym would appear over the avatar’s head during the tutoring sessions. After students entered a name, they were prompted to select an avatar. Students were instructed to choose any avatar and could switch at any point in the study. Unlike other VR environments, where users can ful ly customize their avatar, this environment had a menu of 12 avatar options to select from (Figure 1). The avata rs in this menu represented a range of skin tones, body types, hair types, hair colors, and body accessories. However, as noted by students in the study, the diversity of avatar characteristics was considerably lacking.

Data sources and analysis In this study, we focused exclusively on pedagogica l issues which were unique to our specific VR tutor ing environment. The primary data source we examined we re video recordings of the tutoring lessons. A tota l of 45 tutoring sessions were recorded and collected across the study. The recordings ranged from 10-50 minutes. During the study, a member of the research team met with t he tutors weekly to discuss issues related to teach ing and learning using HMDs. Two issues emerged from these discussions: (1) issues related to representation o r performance of social identities in VR; and (2) issues related to student discipline in  VR. After meeting with the tutors, members of the research team met, divided u p the corpus of video recordings, viewed the corpus of video recordings, and took notes on other issues they saw  as relevant to discuss. This process yielded a thi rd issue: (3) management of mobility, proxemics, and gesture in VR. With these three issues in mind, we returned to the video data to identify when these issues unfolded in virt ual interaction. Two members of the resea rch team independently viewed and coded each video recording for instances  when issues related to social identity, discipline, or body management occurred. Once coding was complete, members of the research team viewed each of these instances and decided whether it warranted  closer analysis. We transcribed each instance and paid close attention to turns-at-talk, gesture, gaze, and proxemics. To complete our analysis, we wrote analytical notes guided by the four dimensions of SIF. These analytical not es documented which of the four dimensions  were changing in such a way to generate the pedagogical issue. In  our findings, we present each pedagogical issue al ong with candidate examples and analysis.

Three pedagogical issues Issue 1: Classroom management through surveillance and isolation The first issue involved the two practices that tut ors used to manage student behavior in VR: surveill ance and isolation. We describe each practice, illustrate its occurrence with data, and problematize the practice. Surveillance Surveillance refers to moments when tutors reminded  students that their actions in the VR tutoring env ironment were being audio and video recorded. This reminder was meant to entice students to behave in a manner that was believed by them to be conducive for classroom learning. All four of the tutors, at multiple points in the beginning of the seven-week tutoring study, leveraged this practice to manage student behavior. Typically, tut ors reminded students of the video recording at the  beginning of the session before the tutoring  activities commenced. For example, on the second we ek of the tutoring study, Norbert began class by saying: “You are being recorded. Whatever you say here, pretend that your mom is going to see this. So don’t do anything crazy. Treat this  like a normal cl assroom.”. Although the tutors brought up the possibility of recordings being used to discipline students, there was no mechanism through which this this threat could have been realized.

The practice of surveillance reveals a shift in the  cultural beliefs dimension. During weekly meetings, tutors explained that they believed surveillance in public  schools was a questionable ethical practice. Howev er, because video recording a native feature of the HMD s (and part of the research design), the tutors shifted their beliefs to be more favorable towards using surveillance for classroom management. We consider surveillance a pedagogical practice which should be discouraged and prohibited before VR technologies are researched further with students o r adopted in K-12 school settings. The research team did not collect video recordings to be used for behavioral purposes. Furthermore, in garnering consent and assent for the study, students and parents were assured that these  video recordings would ensure anonymity and be des troyed after the completion of the research project. We illuminate this pedagogical issue because we are concerned about the rise of surveillance practices in schools more broadly (Brady, 2012; Warnick, 2007); and believe V R technologies could easily be co-opted for these purposes. Isolation Isolation refers to moments when tutors used the ca pability of ‘moving students’ to manage student beh avior. Tutors were able to select a student’s name and mov e their avatar into a separate virtual classroom wh ere they would be alone. Similar to surveillance, the threat of this practice was employed to ensure students would behave in accordance with the tutor’s perceptions of the c lassroom norms. If students behaved in a manner inc onsistent with these norms, they would be disciplined by being virtually isolated. Three of the four tutors leveraged threats of isolation as a practice to manage student behavior. Typically, this occurred in the middle of a tutoring session when students were losing interest in the lesson and beginning to find other ways of engaging in  the VR environment. Take for example Nobert’s virtu al classroom during the fourth week of the study. Norbert and two students (Que and TBBA) were working through seventh grade geometry simulations related to triangular prisms. In this lesson, Norbert leve raged isolation as a tactic to discipline TBBA three times. First, at 21-minute mark in the lesson, Norbert interpreted TBBA ’s response as an interruption and responded by saying “Hey TBBA we a re going to stay focused on the lesson or I’m gonna  send you to another room, okay? That sound good?”. Second, at the 25-minute mark in the lesson, Norbert interpreted TBBA’s behavior as off task and replied “ Hey TBBA, this is going to be your final warning if you  don't want to participate. Don't wanna contribute to the lesson. I'm gonna move you to the room. Do you understand? TBBA?”. Finally, at the 47-minute mark in the lesson, Norbert decided to take action: “Alright. TBBA I’m going to move you into the waiting room. It has been fun but unfortunately we need to have you listening okay? I’m going to put you into the room so we can continue with the lesson and I am going to come by and talk to yo u. [Norbert attempts to put TBBA in a waiting room but it doesn’t work] Okay. Well we will just keep going then.” Norbert attempted to move TBBA, however, the simula tion wasn’t working as planned. Norbert then decide d with the little time remaining in the session to allow TBBA to stay. We interpret the practice of isolation as a shift i n the cultural beliefs dimension because of a shift in socio-techno-spatial relations dimensions. The HMDs afford a way  for tutors to place students in a virtual classroom by themselves (shift in STS relations dimension). The tutors agreed that placing a student in a classroom by themselves (during in-person tutoring or teaching) was not ethical teaching practice. However, since the HMDs allowed students to be isolated ‘safely’, the practice of isolation seemed ethical for tutors to engage with. W e believe the practice of isolation is a pedagogical strategy that should be discouraged amongst people using VR tutoring environments. Fortunately, the th reat of isolation was leveraged  but no actual instances of isolation occurred in our study. Considering the discomfort HMDs can cause some users, researchers and teachers should modify our assumptions about how long users can work within VR environments before they need a break. Issue 2: The avatar system and social identity The second issue involved two problems related to how students engaged with the avatar system: The first problem involved students struggling to identify others based on knowledge about their r eal-world social identities. The second problem involved students feeling that the avatar system did not adequately match their own racial, ethnic, or gender identity. These issues arose from technical and practical con ditions of our study. Technically, the VR tutoring environment was designed by the research team to have 12 pre-created avatars. Practically, the tutors and students were located in physically separate spaces  – and therefore never got to meet each other in perso n. We describe each problem, illustrate its occurrence with data, and discuss causality.

## Discussion and recommendations

In this paper, we identify three pedagogical issues  that arise in Virtual Reality environments – surveillance and isolation, representation of identities with avatars, and management of student gesture and movement. Regarding surveillance and isolation, we believe future VR studies should consider Brady’s (2012) four dimensions of ethical surveillance. Researchers/teachers should guarantee that recordings will only be used for instructional purposes and engage in member-checking with the students who engaged in the study, thus empowering these individuals. Related to the avatar system, we believe avatars should maximize the degree of customization and researchers or teachers should meet students either in person or o ver a video-call prior to conducting the study. While VR technologies afford remotely accessed learning, we believe a face-to-face connection remains crucial. Our research also supports prior work suggesting the im portance of considering bodily autonomy in technolo gyenhanced mixed reality environments (e.g., Vickery, 2023). We provide the following recommendations:

1. Teachers who use VR should be explicitly trained to identify the issues with using surveillance/isolation

and learn about how these approaches may harm stude nts. They should also be trained to use other approaches for behavior management, in the absence of real-world proximity.

2. Designers of VR environments should consider the de gree to which students’ feeling s of being

represented by avatars may impact their learning in VR, particularly as students’ bodies take on increased significance that leverage gesture and body movements related to mathematical principles.

3. Teachers in VR environments who use physical moveme nts where students, for instance, are asked to

move around a large coordinate plane or gesture or manipulate objects in particular ways, in front of an audience, should consider how they can integrate el ements of consent. Activities can be structured in such a way that students continually give their consent to engage in physical movements.
