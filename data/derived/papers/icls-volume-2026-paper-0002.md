# Designing AI to Scaffold Learners’ Cognitive Engagement in Generative Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Recent advances in AI have raised concerns about students’ overreliance on automated systems that may complete learning tasks without requiring meaningful cognitive effort and engagement. This paper explores how AI can be designed to scaffold and sustain cognitive engagement in generative learning activities. Drawing on generative learning theory and scaffolding, we present a conceptual account of how AI can guide learners through generative processes such as selecting, organizing, and integrating information, while gradually fading support as competence develops. We illustrate this through the design of an AI-assisted system that helps children (aged 8–13) create educational science videos --- a task that embodies generative learning but poses cognitive challenges for young learners. Our work sheds light on how to integrate scaffolding to foster but not replace meaningful engagement with AI in generative learning.

One growing concern of using AI in education is what some researchers describe as overreliance or overdependence on AI, where students use AI systems to complete tasks that require personal effort and thinking (Han et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2025a). While AI tools can solve problems and provide answers quickly and often accurately, they may diminish learners’ active participation and engagement in the mental processes involved in these tasks, which are essential for understanding and actual learning. This issue is especially salient in generative learning tasks. Generative learning theory was first proposed by Wittrock (1992), who viewed learning as an active process in which learners generate relationships between new information and their prior knowledge to make sense of experience. Later research has proposed cognitive models to explain the mechanisms of generative learning and the tasks that support it. Notably, Fiorella and Mayer

(2016) describe the SOI model, where learners deepen understanding by selecting  relevant information,

organizing it into coherent structures and integrating it with existing knowledge. They also proposed eight types of tasks that promote such learning, such as teaching others  or self-explaining, as these tasks would strength and support the SOI process involved (Fiorella & Mayer, 2013, 2016). However, AI systems can now automate many of these generative tasks, which means students may complete them without engaging in the underlying cognitive work. For example, instead of explaining a concept in their own words, a student might ask AI to generate the explanation, resulting in a finished product but limited cognitive engagement (Cai et al, 2025b). This challenge, however, is not unsolvable. The solution can be framed as how to design AI systems that support these mental processes rather than allowing students to avoid them. M any researchers have attempted to do this. In the field of Human–Computer Interaction (HCI), which focuses on the design of systems, many studies have used approaches based on behaviorism, such as adding friction or nudges to influence user behavior and many showed positive results (Buçinca et al, 2021; Bo et al, 2025). For example, some systems intentionally add small difficulties or interruptions to have users wait or slow down, prompting them to think more carefully. Some systems embed nudges in the interface that reinforce positive behaviors (Caraban et al, 2019). As mentioned in some of the studies, one problem with such approach is reduced user satisfaction and productivity, as they are obtrusive than supportive (Buçinca et al, 2021; Bo et al, 2025). A different approach, which is also what we propose in this paper, can be drawn from Vygotsky’s Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) theory (1978), where AI functions not as an automation but as a form of guidance to guide them through the cognitive tasks they would not be able to accomplish alone. The AI decomposes the overall task into subtasks  that fall within the student’s ZPD and acts as a temporary scaffold that supports their progression in key mental processes such as organizing, reflecting, and integrating information (Vande Pol et al, 2010). Over time, the AI gradually reduces its support as the learner gains competence and confidence, allowing the student to internalize these processes and perform them independently (Wood et al, 1976).

In this paper, we contribute a conceptual understanding of how AI can be designed to scaffold and foster generative learning, drawing on theoretical perspectives from both generative learning theory and scaffolding.
