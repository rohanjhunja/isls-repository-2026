# Does Shrinking and Growing in VR Induce Awe In Young Students?

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Awe enhances scientific learning, and is thought to be triggered by experiences of vastness or accommodation. We examined self-reported causes of awe among grade 3-6 students in a virtual environment where students can shrink and grow from the scale of an atom to the Sun. We implemented, reworded, and restructured survey items to modify a previously identified potentially leading item. Similar results suggest that the previously leading item did not inflate results. Most students indicated experiencing feelings of awe, with vastness as the primary cause. One response did relate to accommodation. Therefore, the results demonstrate that young students can experience awe in VR, and have the self-awareness to describe the need for accommodation.

## Introduction

Awe, a profound emotion linked to positive educational and psychological outcomes (e.g., Yang et al., 2016; Piff et al., 2015), is thought to be triggered by experi encing vastness or accommodation (e.g., Cruz, 2020;  Keltner & Haidt, 2003). Our NSF-funded project has developed an immersive virtual environment called Scale Worlds (SW) that allows students to shrink or grow over 20 orders of magnitude from an atom to the Sun. In previous work, we investigated whether experiences in SW  induce awe, and if so, what elicited awe (Delgado e t al., 2023). We directly asked grade 3-6 students whether they experienced awe (amazement,  astonishment, or wonder), which may have biased them towards answering in the affir mative. We report on a follow-up study with participants of the same grade levels using restructured survey items designed to be less biasing, guided by the research question: Does shrinking and growing in VR induce awe among young students? If so, why?

## Theoretical framework

Awe Keltner and Haidt (2003) propose that awe is caused  by vastness and accommodation. Vastness involves experiences much larger than oneself. Accommodation  is the process of adjusting mental schemas as a re sult of novel experiences (Piaget & Inhelder, 1969). The literature points to incomprehensibility, viola tion of expectations, novelty, and gap in knowledge as face ts of accommodation, and explanatory power, sublime ness, social importance as aspects of vastness, and we operationalized these in a codebook (Delgado et al., 2023). Awe and virtual rreality Chirico et al.’s (2018) work demonstrated that virtual environments that portray vast landscapes elicit awe among graduate students to a greater degree than other scenes. In our prior study we found that 96% of grade 3-6 students reported that they felt aw e. Around half the responses corresponded to the a priori codes, with 25% mentioning vastness, 8% sublimeness, and 5% referring to gap in knowledge.

## Participants

Participants in this study consisted of 40 elementa ry and middle school students participating in our summer outreach activities. Students were recruited via ou r collaboration with a local institution serving students of historically underrepresented and u nderserved communities. Students in grades 3-4 experienced SW in a room-sized, projection-based CAVE, and the grade 5-6 students experienced SW in CAVE and Quest 2 head-mounted displays. Students rotated through stations as described in Delgado et al., 2023. Data source We used a previously developed electronic survey fo cused on SW use in the CAVE (Delgado et al., 2023) that assessed scale cognition and situational interest, which was administered immediately following the student’s experience in the CAVE. The survey item of interest in this paper was about awe. It was revised by asking students whether they experienced any of the following emoti ons/feelings: awe, excitement, frustration, and fea r. Synonyms were provided for each. Students who indicated that they experienced awe were then presented with a follow-up item asking what caused those feelings.

Analysis We used deductive and inductive qualitative methods. Deductive coding used the codebook with a priori categories. After three rounds of coding, discussion, and clarifications to the codebook, the first and third authors reached 87% agreement, after which t he remaining student responses were coded by the th ird author. We used grounded theory to analyze the remaining responses, with two coders generating candidate themes and discussing to reach consensus.

## Findings

All students reported feeling excitement, 93% awe, 18.4% fear, and 5.2% frustration. Around half of the responses corresponded to the a priori codes: 15 vastness (41 %), 3 violates expectations (8%), 1 gap in knowledg e (3%), 1 accommodation (3%), and 1 incomprehensible (3%). Responses for vastness included the very large, very small, and large differences in scale, as in prior work (Delgado et al., 2023). One response was coded as accommodation: “I didn’t realize how wrong I had originally been”. One emergent theme was the role of the affordances of VR in producing feelings of awe: a sense of presence and the ability to interact with the environment in an embodied manner such as “walking through it.” Another emerge nt theme was the evaluation or opinion about SW, e.g., “it was super fun, and it was good”, which did not directly respond to the prompt about awe.

## Discussion

Almost all students experienced awe, suggesting tha t the earlier version of the question (Delgado et a l., 2023) may not have biased students. In contrast, much sma ller proportions felt frustration or fear, suggesti ng students were able to distinguish among the emotions and willing to state they did not feel some of them. The most common cause of awe among participants was vastness, consi stent with prior literature (Chirico et al., 2018; Cruz, 2020; Keltner & Haidt, 2003). In our earlier study, no students’ identified accommodation as a cause of awe. Our finding of an instance confirms that even young students ca n have the self-awareness and vocabulary to describe a perceived need for accommodation.
