# How Students Talk About Race and Identity: Unpacking Social-Emotional Talk

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This exploratory study aims to conceptualize a model of shared meaning-making and identify social-emotional talks in politically-charged discussions in CSCL. We conceptualized social-emotional talk as the extent to which team members engage in self-disclosure and emotionally support each other. We collected and analyzed politically charged discussions of 13 intergroup teams over four-time points. Our analysis yielded that teams engaged in three categories of social-emotional talk: personal engagement, social connectedness, and constructive engagement. We further identified associated behaviors for each category and discussed how these behaviors are desirable for politically-charged discussions in CSCL.

## Introduction

Empowering learners as socio-political agents that could develop shared meaning across divergences has been posed as the ultimate goal of the CSCL scholarship (Uttamchandani et al., 2020). Yet, we have tended to overlook how sociopolitical factors influence power and socio-emotional dynamics in s hared meaning-making during collaborative learning (Uttamchandani et al., 2020). Recent studies examined the  development of new technological tools to moderate charged discussions or intersubjective meaning-making in historically charged discussions (e.g., Slakmon & Schwarz, 2019). These initiatives, however, are short of a concrete conceptualization to evaluate the quality of shared meaning-making. This exploratory study is part of our ongoing efforts to develop a theoretically-informed and data-driven model of shared meaning-making for  politically charged discussions in a CSCL context (Aldemir et al., 2022) that considers the role of social-e motional processes (Gurin et al., 2013). By identifying the social-emotional behaviors associated with charged topics, w e inform our goal of conceptualizing and operationalizing a shared meaning-making model in CSCL. More  specifically, this study aims to address the following research question: RQ: What behaviors of socia l and emotional talk did online intergroup teams engage in during politically-charged discussions? Shared meaning-making and social-emotional talk We aim to identify intergroup teams' emotional-social communication patterns in politically charged discussions. Positive socio-emotional climate, self-disclosure and risk-taking during collaborative activities are emphasized in CSCL and intergroup dialogue literature (e.g., Gurin et al., 2013). When the discour se topic is closely related to one’s identity, emotions can arise. In intergroup dialogues, trained facilitators can intervene when negative emotions arise. In the absence of a facilitator, teams need to regula te their social-emotional interactions. How team members engage in self-disclosure and risk-taking and regulate emotions  are essential indicators of the productivity of the discussion. So, we conceptualized social-emotional talk as the extent to which team members engage in self-disclosure and risk-taking and socially and emotionally support each other.

## Methods

This study reports a multiple case study conducted in an undergraduate psychology course on how race, culture, and privilege impact psychology. The development of collaborative skills was a core objective. Thirty-five undergraduate Psychology students enrolled (25 females, 71.4%; 9 males, 25.7%; 1 non-binary, 2.9%). Student s were divided into 13 teams of three and two based on self-identified race/ethnicity/gender to create diverse teams. The teams engaged in (1) synchronous discussions in a text-based CSCL environment; (2) individual assessments of their collaborative performance; and (3) collective assessment to identif y team weaknesses and solutions for times throughout the semester. A text-based CSCL environment called CREATE was used. A trained coder scored the discussions. Based on our theoretical conceptualization of social-emotional ta lk, we implemented a focused coding strategy to examine how teams engaged in social-emotional talks (Salda ña, 2016). Then, we conducted another cycle to determine what initial codes made the most analytical sense. Next, we identified each category's primary behaviors by constantly comparing the themes across the teams (Corbin & Strauss, 2014).

## Findings

Analysis of the 13 teams' discussions over four-time points yielded three major categories of social-emotional talk: personal engagement (n=121), social connectedness (n=46), and constructive engagement (n=216). ## Discussion

This study explored the social-emotional talk behaviors of diverse teams as they engaged in shared meaningmaking around politically charged topics in a CSCL environment. Team members  (1) reflected on the shared narratives to disclose their lack of knowledge and biases or (2) sympathized/empathized with others’ experiences. The first set of reactions are desirable personal engagement behaviors because they suggest that members actively listened to others’ sharing. Intentionally listening to others, personal sharing of biases, identifying similarities and differences in perspectives are critical components of intergroup dialogue and require critical reflection (Gurin et al., 2013). Similarly, the second set of behaviors are desirable because the y could foster intergroup relationship building and positive socio-emotional climate (Gurin et al., 2013). Teams also f requently appreciated others' sharing and group performance, which could foster relationship building (Gurin et al., 2013). In short,  this study laid out a foundation for our larger goal of conceptualizing a model of shared meaning making around politically charged topics in CSCL; however, further studies with multiple case compa risons are needed to identify the quality range of these behaviors.
