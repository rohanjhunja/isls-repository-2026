# Autonomy, Agency, and Belonging: Adults using Mobile Augmented Reality for Language Learning in the Real-World

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This qualitative multi–case study examines how six adult English language learners appropriated mobile augmented reality (MAR) through Google Lens to support language learning beyond the classroom. Guided by andragogy, mobile learning, and consequential learning frameworks, the study explores how participants used MAR to exercise choice, respond to real-world language needs, and expand their identities as capable actors in English-dominant spaces. Data from MAR activities, interviews, and artifacts show that learners self-directed vocabulary development, mobilized home languages to support understanding, and engaged in just-in-time learning in everyday contexts such as museums, markets, and public transportation. MAR enabled participants to integrate English practice into daily life, enhancing access to information and opportunities for belonging while strengthening confidence to communicate across settings. Findings highlight the potential of MAR to foster autonomy and consequential learning in adult second language education and call for further research on equitable, technology-supported language learning environments.

For many adult immigrants in the United States, navigating daily life requires communicating in English across domains such as healthcare, employment, and community participation (Aning, 2021; Kamisli, 2023). Although nearly half of immigrant adults report  speaking English less than proficiently  (Rao et al., 2024), an asset-based perspective emphasizes learners’ multilingual repertoires and lived experiences (Bigelow & Vinogradov, 2011; Norton, 2013). From this view, language learning extends beyond communication to include participation, belonging, and identity transformation (Norton & Toohey, 2011; Gutiérrez, 2023). Andragogical theory further suggests that adult learning is most meaningful when it is self-directed, connected to learners’ goals, and immediately relevant to their lives (Illeris, 2018; Knowles et al., 2015; Merriam et al., 2020). These perspectives highlight the importance of learning designs that support autonomy and agency for adult language learners. Mobile-assisted language learning (MALL) has gained attention for enabling contextualized and portable learning opportunities that support such autonomy and agency  (Bachore, 2015). Mobile augmented reality (MAR), in particular, overlays digital informatio n onto physical environments, allowing learners to access language resources at the moment of need while navigating real-world settings such as restaurants, institutions, or cultural sites (Christou et al., 2025; Garzón et al., 2023). Within the learning s ciences, MAR has been shown to support situated and embodied learning by connecting digital supports to real-world environments (Dunleavy & Dede, 2014; Garzón et al., 2023). Such affordances align with theories of consequential learning, where learning becomes meaningful when it transforms what learners cando and who they can become across settings (Gutiérrez, 2023; Greeno & Gresalfi, 2008). Despite growing interest in mobile language learning, relatively little research has examined how adult immigrants appropriate MAR in everyday contexts to support English learning and participation. This gap is significant because MAR may allow learners to access resources in the moment, navigate unfamiliar environments, and position themselves as capable participants in new communities (Norton, 2013). This study addresses that gap by examining how adult immigrants used a mobile augmented reality tool, Google Lens, to access English resources in everyday settings. We explore how these practices shaped learners’ perceptions of choice, capability, and belonging. The research questions guiding this study were: (1) How do adults learning English leverage mobile augmented reality (MAR) to appropriate language resources in real-world contexts?; and (2) How does the appropriation of MAR for language learning influence adult learners’ perceptions of what they cando and who they can become across settings? Adult language learning, consequential learning, and mobile technologies Second language acquisition (SLA) research has long examined differences between child and adult language learning, raising questions about age, cognition, motivation, and context (Slobin, 2012). While children may be more likely to achieve native-like fluency (Birdsong, 1999; Ellis, 2015), adult learners bring distinct strengths, including motivation, prior experience, and goal-oriented learning strategies (Oxford, 2017). Consequently, adult 1182 language learning is often framed through andragogy,  which emphasizes that adults draw on lived experiences and personal goals while directing their own learning (Knowles et al., 2015; Merriam et al., 2020). Research shows that effective adult language learning environments support self-direction, allowing learners to pursue personally meaningful goals and regulate their learning through reflection and practice (Illeris, 2018; Ozfidan et al., 2014). These principles align with consequential learning,  which frames learning as meaningful when it connects to learners’ lived experiences and expands what they cando and who they can become across settings (Gutiérrez, 2023; Illeris, 2018). For adult immigrants, English learning becomes consequential when it enable s participation in workplaces, institutions, and everyday social interactions. Mobile technologies have expanded opportunities for such learning by providing portable and personalized tools that integrate language practice into daily activities (Bachore, 2015). In particular, mobile augmented reality (MAR) overlays digital information onto physical environments, enabling learners to access language resources at the moment of need and engage with authentic contexts (Christou et al., 2025; Garzón et al., 2023). Despite these affordances, most learning science s research on MAR has focused on inquiry-based science learning among younger learners (Kamarainen et al., 2013; Li et al., 2024; Zimmerman et al., 2021). Far less is known about how adult immigrants appro priate MAR to support language learning and participation in everyday contexts.

## Participants, context, and design

This qualitative multi-case study examines how adult immigrants appropriate MAR for English learning outside the classroom, guided by andragogy, consequential learning, and mobile learning. Participants included six (6) beginner English learners (ages 27-7 2) who had lived in the United States fewer than seven years and were enrolled in a church-based ESL program (Table 1; https://tinyurl.com/Figures-and-Tables-Adults-MAR).

The study unfolded in three phases: rapport-building, MAR activities, and reflection (Table 2; https://tinyurl.com/Figures-and-Tables-Adults-MAR). Google Lens was selected as the MAR tool because it is free, widely accessible, and provides real-time text r ecognition and translation for everyday contexts (https://lens.google/). Following five weeks of volunteer engagement in the ESL class to build rapport and obtain consent, participants completed four activities using Google Lens outside of the classroom in everyday activities: reading English texts (e.g., newspapers or books), ordering from a restaurant menu, interpreting museum exhibits, and independently using the tool in a context of their choice. To examine how adult immigrants appropriated the mobile tool, we used a combination of directed and conventional qualitative content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005). Data sources included transcripts from observed activities, participant reflection journa ls, post-interviews, and MAR drawing worksheets that captured participants’ perspectives on using MAR for language learning. First, the research team familiarized themselves with the dataset through repeated readings of all discourse materials (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Audio and video recordings from activities and interviews were professionally transcribed, reviewed for accuracy, and organized in a spreadsheet. Transcripts were supplemented with annotations describing gestures and embodied actions to support richer interpretation during analysis (Mondada, 2019). We began with directed content analysis, drawing on theoretical constructs from adult learning and mobile learning research to develop initial coding categories related to agency, self-efficacy, and MAR-mediated learning practices (Bachore, 2015; Christou et al., 2025; Garzón et al., 2023; Knowles et al., 2015; Merriam et al., 2020). Coding was conducted line by line, with each participant turn of talk treated as a unit of analysis. At the same time, we remained open to inductively emerging themes through conventional content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005). Two researchers independently coded the dataset and met regularly to compare interpretations and refine the coding scheme through constant comparison (Miles & Huberman, 1994). This process resulted in additional categories capturing participants’ evolving perceptions of value, cultural understanding, and meaning-making. Through iterative comparison and consolidation of overlapping codes, the research team refined the co ding scheme and continued analysis until theoretical saturation was reached (Hennink et al., 2017). In the final analytic stage, patterns were interpreted through the lenses of andragogy, mobile learning, and consequential learning (Miles et al., 2014), resulting in two overarching themes: (1) Learners’ appropriation of MAR based on individual needs, choices, and situational contexts, and (2) Expanded capability, agency, and belonging through MAR use. 1183 Content analysis of interviews, activity transcripts, journals, and MAR drawings revealed two themes: (1) learnerdriven appropriation of MAR based on individual needs and contexts, and (2) expanded capability, agency, and belonging through MAR use.

Learner-driven appropriation of MAR according to learners’ needs and contexts All six participants described using Google Lens (GL) to pursue language learning aligned with personal goals and everyday needs outside the classroom. Ellie, a 41-year-old participant from Iran, contrasted MAR-based learning with traditional ESL instruction: “In class, we talk about what they want to talk about…from the book. But now I can go anywhere… I order without worry.” She also used GL to communicate with her daughter’s teacher: “I wrote in Farsi and then used Google Lens to show her what I was trying to say,” demonstrating how she combined home language and MAR to accomplish real-world tasks. Bella, a 62-year-old participant from Mexico, similarly emphasized control over her learning: “I learn more than I do in class because I learn the English I want when I want.” Autumn, a 35-year-old participant from Colombia, described comparable autonomy during the museum activity: “It’s more fun than in class… I learn on my own.” She also used GL to communicate with her children’s teachers, noting that it helped others “understand me better and I understand her better too.” Marco, a 27-year-old participant from Colombia,  described MAR as enabling flexible, self-directed learning, using it “when I need to expand my vocabulary” and “wherever or whenever we want to learn.” Similarly, Ahmed, a 44-year-old participant from Turkey,  highlighted its role in meeting everyday language needs: “You select things that you want to know and it tells you… I can learn the words I need,” supporting tasks such as reading mail and navigating travel. Finally, Antonio, a 72-year-old participant from Mexico, also used MAR to complete everyday tasks such as reading internet setup instructions, interpreting legal paperwork, and understanding labels in stores. His drawing of a MAR tool emphasized its value for communicating in medical settings. Together, these examples illustrate how participants appropriated MAR to meet immediate communication needs while directing their own language learning across everyday contexts.

Expanded capability, agency, and belonging through MAR use Four of the adult immigrants in this study, Marco, Ellie, Ahmed, and Antonio, described feeling more capable of learning and using English across different settings when using mobile augmented reality (MAR). For example, Marco reflected on reading a magazine with Google Lens: “This was the first time I read most of a magazine in no time… I understood it because I saw it in Spanish.” He explained that seeing English and Spanish together helped him remember vocabulary and practice pronunciation. In his interview he noted that the tool allowed him to “tell someone, or talk to someone… and say different things,” highlighting how MAR expanded his ability to communicate. Ellie similarly described how MAR supported shared communication among learners with different languages during the museum activity: “We have different languages. But after using this, we can all talk in English and feel good about what we are saying.” Her comm ent reflects how MAR enabled participants to draw on their home languages while engaging more confidently in English interactions. Ahmed expressed a similar sense of expanded capability while reading a magazine: “Look… I can read this here. I’m good at English reading but now I am better.” As he continued reading, he described being able to “understand,” “ask questions,” and share opinions with others. He connected this experience to his professional identity, explaining that understanding English texts allowed him to feel “more normal here… like a citizen who can talk about the politics in the world.” Antonio also described a growing sense of belonging during the museum activity. After using Google Lens to understand exhibit text, he explained: “I do not feel strange standing here… I can understand like everyone else.” He later described wanting to return with his granddaughter so he could discuss the exhibits with her in both Spanish and English. Through MAR, the museum became a space where he could participate, learn, and connect with family across languages. Across participants’ accounts, MAR enabled learners to mobilize their existing linguistic resources while engaging more fully in English-speaking environments. These experiences illustrate how technology-mediated language learning can support consequential  learning, where learners expand not only what they cando but also how they see themselves participating in new social and cultural contexts. Findings show that participants used MAR to exercise autonomy, expand capabilities, and negoti ate identities as capable actors in English-speaking environments. In 1184 this way, MAR functioned as a catalyst for consequential learning, enabling learners to transform what they cando and who they can become across settings (Gutiérrez, 2023). Autonomy and consequential learning in everyday contexts Consistent with principles of andragogy (Knowles et al., 2015), participants used MAR to direct their learning based on immediate needs and interests, deciding when, where, and how to engage with English beyond the classroom. Ellie, for example, contrasted  instructor-led ESL lessons with the freedom MAR provided to “go anywhere” and “learn what these words mean and not use the book,” allowing her to “order without worry.” Similarly, Marco explained that he used the tool “when I need to expand my vocabulary, ” highlighting learning on demand. These practices illustrate how mobile technologies support just-in-time learning aligned with adults’ daily routines (Bachore, 2015). Participants used MAR across everyday contexts, transforming interactions into opportunities for language learning and participation, consistent with consequential learning (Gutiérrez, 2023). Expanding linguistic capabilities and participation Participants also described how MAR expanded their linguistic capabilities and supported more confident participation in English-speaking environments. Ahmed noted that using Google Lens helped him feel “better” at reading English, reflecting growing self-efficacy as he navigated unfamiliar texts. Similarly, Antonio described using the tool to understand museum exhibits and later share that knowledge with his granddaughter. In these moments, MAR acted as a mediating tool that enabled learners to mobilize their full linguistic repertoires, access new forms of cultural knowledge, and participate more fully in everyday activities. These experiences illustrate how technology-supported language learning can extend beyond vocabulary acquisition to broaden the range of practices learners can engage in (Gutiérrez, 2023; Greeno & Gresalfi, 2008). By enabling comprehension, communication, and exploration in real-world contexts, MAR supported the expansion of learners’ capabilities in ways consistent with consequential learning (Gutiérrez, 2023; Greeno & Gresalfi, 2008). Identity, belonging, and participation across contexts Finally, participants’ experiences suggest that MAR supported evolving identities and a growing sense of belonging in English-speaking environments. Google Lens created hybrid linguistic spaces where learners could draw on home languages while participating in English interactions. Ellie reflected that although participants spoke different languages, using the tool allowed them to “talk in English and feel good about what we are saying.” Such experiences illustrate how MAR enabled learners to navigate multi lingual environments while building confidence in English use. Antonio’s reflections further demonstrate this shift. After using MAR to interpret museum exhibits, he described feeling that he could “understand like everyone else” and planned to return with his granddaughter to share the experience in both Spanish and English. In this way, MAR supported participation not only in linguistic practices but also in social and cultural activities, reinforcing learners’ identities as capable participants within new communities. These findings align with sociocultural perspectives that view learning as the process through which individuals appropriate tools to participate meaningfully across contexts (Greeno & Gresalfi, 2008).

This study contributes to research on adult second language learning by showing how mobile augmented reality (MAR) can support autonomy, consequential learning, and identity development in everyday contexts. Participants used MAR not only for translation or vocabulary support but to navigate real-world interactions, positioning themselves as capable participants in community life. Although limited in scope, these findings highlight adult learners as agentive and resourceful technology users and point to MAR’s potential to support more equitable and inclusive language learning environments. Future research should examine how sustained use of MAR influences longer-term language development and how such tools can be integrated within and beyond ESL programs to support multilingual learning and participation.
