# Conceptions of Learning With (and Without) Technology in Undergraduate Mathematics Courses

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Employing the Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge framework, t his paper investigates undergraduate students’ per ceptions of digital technologies in mathematics education. While technologies are often promoted as catalysts for transforming mathematical learning, little is known about how students themselves conceptualize their value. Based on the qualitative strand of an ongoing mixed-methods study in the UK, we present an emergent analysis of three in-depth student interviews. Thematic analysis yielded three categories: learning without technology, technology as access to learning, and learning through engaging technology. Students frequently regarded technologies as barrier s when they replaced core mathematical activity but valued them as efficient tools for accessing resources or as environments for independent exploration. These findings suggest that students adopt a bounded and strategic stance: resisting substitution, embracing access, and leveraging exploration. Implications are discussed for designing digital environments that support curiosity, experimentation, and differentiated pathways in undergraduate mathematics.

Digital technologies have long been regarded as a powerful force for transforming mathematics education. As early as the late 1990s it was argued that the use of computer algebra systems and graphing calculators could shift instructional emphases away from routine calculation toward conceptual understanding (Heid, 1997). Subsequent work has shown that digital environments cando more than ease task execution: they can generate new forms of mathematical expression and reasoning  (Konold & Lehrer, 2007; Tatar et al., 2014). Yet contemporary research highlights an ambivalent impact of technology. Reviews indicate that digital tools can serve multiple functions

— ranging from instruments for carrying out calculations and practicing skills to media that support conceptual

development — yet their success hinges on task design, educational context, and accompanying pedagogical support (Drijvers, 2013; Hofer & Reinhold, 2025; St Omer et al., 2025; Laru et al., 2015). Empirical studies at the university level show that undergraduates predominantly rely on widely available digital resources such as recorded lectures, videos, and online notes, while specialized mathematical systems that could alter mathematical learning praxes are adopted far less frequently (Ní Shé et al., 2023). D espite the substantial literature base focused on technology and mathematics education, little is known about how undergraduate students perceive and characterize the role of technology in their mathematics learning. Further research in this area could address the  acknowledged underutilization of technologies in mathematics education for pedagogical change.  The present study addresses this gap by adopting the Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK) framework to examine which functions of technology — from digitization of course materials and automated feedback to computational support and modelling for inquiry — students identify as salient to their learning experiences within university mathematics. According to Mishra and Koehler (2006), TPACK conceptualizes the interrelations among technology, teaching, and learning through multiple knowledge domains: technological knowledge (TK), pedagogical knowledge (PK), and subject-specific content knowledge (CK). Their intersections yield technological pedagogical knowledge (TPK), technological content knowledge (TCK), and pedagogical content knowledge (PCK), while full TPACK represents an integrated understanding of all three. Although this frame work has been repeatedly used in mathematics  education and learning technology research (see Bergner & Wang, 2023; Bretscher, 2022; Niess et al., 2009; Tan & Chen, 2022), systematic reviews note that much of the TPACK literature remains oriented toward instructional practice while students’ perspectives are comparatively under-examined (Kholid, 2023; Schmid et al., 2024). The recent proliferation of generative artificial intelligence illustrates  the importance of this student-centered perspective. Tools such as ChatGPT extend the traditional triad of “technology—ped agogy—content,” foregrounding social, ethical and institutional questions (Mishra et al., 2023). In turn, technology ceases to be merely a means of access or calculation and increasingly acts as an intellectual intermediary that reshapes the structure of learning activity. To investigate student perceptions of learning mathematics through technological engagement, we are currently conducting a mixed-methods study combining a survey of undergraduate mathematics students at UK universities with a series of in-depth interviews. Preliminary  findings reveal a complex picture: while many students adopt a pragmatic stance, viewing technologies primarily as a convenient means of accessing materials 1153 and explanations, they also express concern about over-reliance on technological shortcuts  and emphasize the value of tools that extend opportunities for exploration—such as modelling, visualization, and programming. These findings thus highlight the value of TPACK as a framework for exploring learning, as it emphasizes how students conceptualize the intersections of technology, content knowledge, and pedagogical praxes in their own development. By foregrounding students’ own voices, this study contributes to ongoing debates in mathematics education about the contested role of technology and highlights the need to design digital environments that support both access and deeper conceptual engagement.

This article presents emergent findings from  the qualitative strand of our ongoing mixed-methods study investigating the use of digital technolog y in undergraduate mathematics education in the UK.  The study began with an open recruitment of students in undergraduate mathematics courses across the country. Once each student completed the survey, we invited them to take part in a follow up interview. While the larger study combines our survey and interview data, quantitative survey findings are still under active analysis and are not included here. Our semi-structured interviews were conducted online via Microsoft Teams with undergraduate students enrolled in mathematics programs.  Recordings were transcribed verbatim and anonymized through pseudonyms. At the time of writing, we had conducted 45-minute interviews with three students at universities in Northern England: Brian (third-year), Orion (first-year), and Sam (second-year). The semi-structured interview protocol was designed to elicit students’ perspectives on their use of digital technologies in mathematics learning. Broadly, the interviews covered: (a) everyday practices with digital tools and the contexts in which they are  used (e.g., lectures, self-study, exam preparation), (b) comparisons between learning mathematics with and without digital technologies, (c) motivations for adopting specific tools and perceived affordances or limitations, (d) experiences with AI-powered resources such as ChatGPT or Wolfram Alpha, including both perceived benefits and ethical concerns, and (e) expectations about the future role of digital tools in mathematics education. Interview data were analyzed using an open and iterative approach to thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2012). In the first round of analysis, we applied descriptive coding (Saldaña,

2015) to the transcripts to capture salient ideas, practices, and references to digital tools. In the second round, we

used pattern coding (Saldaña, 2015) to group initial codes into broader categories that reflected recurring themes across participants. All codes and code applications were compared and refined by both authors, allowing central aspects of students’ perceptions and practices with digital technologies to emerge. This preliminary qualitative analysis does not aim for thematic saturation or statistical generalizability, but for analytic insight into how undergraduate mathematics students conceptualize learning with and without digital technology. We treat the three interviews as theoretically informative cases, enabling in-depth examination of students’ assumptions and rationales regarding technological engagement. Although the small sample limits claims about prevalence, the consistency of themes across cases suppor ts their analytic coherence. These themes do not represent an exhaustive typology, but rather highlight recurrent patterns that emerged across the cases examined. Taken 1154 together, these themes paint a comprehensive picture of how these students conceptualize TPACK  within their own mathematical learning processes with technology.

While our interviews were ostensibly about using technology, participants routinely emphasized the value of learning in the absence of digital tools entirely. Brian illustrated this theme in the following way: “I want to be focused on what I'm doing and not have to deal with this other thing. I prefer working with pen and paper… and I will use technology when it improves my process. ” Sam extended this thinking when discussing the use of AI in his maths course: “it cando the algebra for you, but that's specifically  what it does. You still need to know how to solve the problem a nd you still need to learn how to do the algebra, so it's best you do it yourself.” Both quotes frame learning as happening best when students “do it themselves” without technological support.  This theme reflects CK, as students emphasized mastery of mathematical processes, and PK, as they articulated beliefs about how mathematics should be learned — through direct problem solving rather than technological mediation. This positioning also underscores students’ implicit boundary-drawing, where technology is seen less as a facilitator of understanding and more as a potential distraction from authentic mathematical practice. When participants did position technologies as valuable for learning, that value more often than not came from technology’s ability to access non-technological resources that could support pedagogical processes. Sam described this framing of technology succinctly: “ I tend to use the computer as an infinite book of lecture notes and information.” Brian mirrored this claim when he said, “It speeds up the process of finding information and studying. Rather than having to dive through a textbook, you can just immediately get the information you need.” Learning thus does not necessarily happen when engaging with the technology, instead providing efficient access to resources that can help with subsequent learning. Orion provided a similar example: “I will use a calculator if I'm not disallowed because I'm bad at arithmetic. [But] it isn't there to help with understanding. It is there to get stuff done, so that you can get moving. ” While the resource being accessed here differs, the reason for engaging with technology remains the same: providing  access to other materials that allow for further learning. L earning happens as they  “get moving” on solving the problem, not as they engage with  technology to provide a simple calculation. This category illustrates TK, as students used tools instrumentally to reach information or perform basic tasks, and aspects of TPK, since technology supported the organization of learning without directly shaping mathematical content. Such uses highlight a pragmatic orientation toward technology, where digital tools function mainly as mediators of access rather than as environments for conceptual engagement. Despite the questions that participants raised about the pedagogical value of working with  technology, they conceptualize some learning processes in their engagement with digital tools. This learning often  occurred through independent exploration, rather than following teacher-directed tasks. As Orion discussed, “sometimes I will do a question and go, ‘That's a funny looking function. What does it look like in D esmos?’... [That] play is good. M essing around, just seeing what happens is good.” In this exampl e, the tool provides opportunities to follow one’s curiosity,  experiment with abstract concepts, and develop a deeper understanding beyond the intended outcomes of the immediate learning activity. Sam provided a similarly independent moment of learning: “I created a script to generate polynomial approximations of functions … by three different methods and graph [them] so I can choose which one looked best. I [wrote] my own approximations because the built-in standard library ones were too slow.” These examples highlight how students strategically leverage technology for self-directed inquiry, extending their mathematical understanding in ways that were not prescribed by instructors. This exemplifies TCK and, at moments, full TPACK, illustrating that student curiosity candrive integration of content, pedagogy, and technology. Importantly, these findings demonstrate a student-centered use of technology, showing that learners can create opportunities for conceptual exploration independently without formal guidance. The findings of this study highlight the ambivalent role of technology in undergraduate students’ conceptualizations of mathematics learning. W hen technologies were perceived as performing mathematical procedures in place of the learner, students position ed digital tools as adjacent to or disconnected from learning processes. This finding echoes concerns raised in mathematics education research that reliance on technological tools may lead to an erosion of students’ reasoning and symbolic skills if not carefully balanced with opportunities for conceptual engagement (Heid, 1997; Drijvers, 2013). When participants did acknowledge the value of digital resources, their benefits were most often articulated in terms of access rather than conceptual development. Technologies were positioned as efficient means of retrieving lecture notes, supplementary explanations, or quick calculations that enabled students to proceed with more substantive, “off line”  tasks. In these cases, learning occurred not through the technological engagement itself, but through the subsequent activity that access facilitated. Through this orientation, participants aligned their own learning with the TPK domain of the TPACK framework by relying on an adept understanding of technology’s shortcomings and affordances when choosing how to use certain digital tools for learning (Mishra & Koehler, 2006; Kholid et al., 2023). 1155 At the same time, students occasionally described moments where technolog y extended their mathematical capabilities in ways that enabled genuine conceptual exploration. Instances of playful experimentation with dynamic graphing software or the creation of scripts to approximate functions exemplified TCK (Bretscher, 2022; St Omer et al., 2025), where tools directly mediated engagement with mathematical processes otherwise beyond the learner’s immediate reach.  In these cases, elements of full TPACK became especially salient, as students did not merely use technology instrumentally but actively integrated technological tools, mathematical content, and self-directed pedagogical strategies in the pursuit of understanding. This illustrates how student-led exploration can move beyond isolated uses of technology and evolve into genuinely integrated TPACK practices  (Niess et al., 2009).  Our findings show that undergraduates can strategically and independently use digital tools to deepen their mathematical understanding. This third theme — learning through technology — adds a student-centered perspective to TPACK, highlighting learners’ ability to create opportunities for conceptual exploration without formal guidance. From the student perspective, TPACK components are not experienced as a stable or integrated whole; instead, students selectively activate technological, pedagogical, and content-related resources in response to the demands of specific tasks. In this sense, TPACK functions less as an integrated knowledge domain and more as a strategic repertoire shaped by learners’ goals and boundaries. Because the present analysis relies solely on three interviews, we cannot make claims about the stability or distribution of the identified themes across the wider undergraduate mathematics population  at present. However, these findings do provide an analytic structure to consider as we complete our broader mixed-methods study, one supported by survey results from across the UK and additional interviews. For now, our analysis suggests that our participants’ perspectives on technology in mathematics educatio n are both bounded and strategic. They resist technolog y that supplant mathematical practice  and embrace tools that open opportunities for exploration beyond their current abilities. If our findings hold in the larger study, t he implication for educational design will be clear: technologies should be positioned as on-demand resources that support curiosity, experimentation, and differentiated pathways—particularly for students motivated to explore mathematical ideas independently. More concretely, this suggests three design priorities. First, digital tools should be developed to complement rather than substitute core mathematical activity, ensuring that problem solving and conceptual engagement remain central (CK and PK)  (Konold & Lehrer, 2007; Tatar et al., 2014).  Second, digital environments — whether platforms or applications — should provide streamlined explanations, examples, and organizational scaffolds that support students in navigating and sustaining their learning processes, while ensuring that such support complements rather than bypasses underlying conceptual work (TPK) (Hofer & Reinhold, 2025; Ní Shé et al., 2023). Third, environments should actively encourage playful exploration, offering opportunities for visualization, modelling, or programming that extend students’ capabilities and foster inquiry beyond formal tasks (TCK) (Bergner & Wang, 2023; Laru et al., 2015). Together, these considerations emphasize that technology in undergraduate mathematics should not be treated as a uniform solution, but as a flexible set of resources whose effectiveness depends on their alignment with students’ motivations and learning goals.  O ur study therefore contributes to extending the TPACK framework by incorporating students’ perspectives  on learning and highlighting design principles that may inform future university mathematics courses.
