# Understanding and Supporting Competent Outsiders’ Epistemic Performance with the AIR Framework

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
One of the greatest challenges for science education today is preparing students as competent outsiders who can navigate the complex information landscape of the ‘post-truth’ era. A critical but under-examined skill is the competency to evaluate and appreciate the value of large bodies of evidence. To understand students’ capabilities and difficulties in this area, we analyzed their written responses to a pre-assessment involving conflicting newspaper-style articles. Using the AIR framework from an assets-based perspective, our analysis shows students’ epistemic criteria in evaluating different evidence and suggests potential implications for instruction design to support more sophisticated epistemic performance.

In the 'post-truth' era, citizens are facing conflicting information, particularly on scientific topics like climate change and public health, making it a formidable challenge to determine what is accurate and make informed decisions for laypeople (Barzilai & Chinn, 2020). A central goal of science education, therefore,  has shifted toward preparing students as "competent outsiders," who can critically engage with science-related issues to make informed decisions (Feinstein, 2011; Osborne & Pimentel, 2023). A key obstacle for competent outsiders is navigating the strategic presentation of evidence. Sources often mislead by “cherry-picking” evidence, which means selectively presenting a single study that supports a desired claim while intentionally ignoring th e larger body of conflicting evidence (Caulfield & Weinberg, 2023; Mochizuki et al., 2024). Research shows that laypeople struggle to detect such manipulations of evidence. For example, Oura et al. (2022) found that undergraduates often fail to recognize cherry-picked data unless it is explicitly flagged by an opposing source; the students pay little attention on their own to how comprehensive the evidence is. This suggests that learning to appreciate the epistemic value of a large body of evidence, such as systematic reviews that synthesize the evidence, is a critical and difficult step in becoming a competent outsider. Although instruction can help students learn to favor claims supported by systematic reviews over single studies (Oura et al., 2023), instructional efforts would benefit from a clearer picture of how students think about reviews versus individual studies. The present study aims to provide that picture. We ground our analysis in the AIR framework of epistemic thinking (Chinn et al., 2014), which analyzes epistemic thinking into three components: Aims (epistemic goals), Ideals (criteria for evaluation), and Reliable Processes (Strategies used to meet goals and ideals). The AIR framework is advantageous to capture the situated practices learners use when they evaluate specific knowledge claims (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018). This allows us to adopt an assets-based approach by identifying the specific aims, ideals, and processes students are actually using. Thus, we can gain more insight into the strengths and weaknesses of their reasoning and evaluation, which in turn can suggest promising strategies for instruction.

This study, therefore, uses the AIR framework  to analyze ninth-grade students' epistemic performance on a task that embodies this challenge. Given the nature of the task design, this study focuses specifically on students’ epistemic ideals. We argue that in this task, their aims are largely fixed by the prompt (i.e., to determine the safety and efficacy of low-carb diets), and the written format doesn’t allow for a deep observation of the reliable processes students use. However, ideals are more explicitly articulated in students’ justifications. By examining students' written evaluations of three articles on three conflicting articles, we aim to answer the question: How do students’ epistemic ideals shape their evaluation of different evidence types?

## Participants

1682 The participants in this study were 219 ninth-grade students enrolled in science classes taught by six teachers at a private high school in the Northeastern United States during the 2023-2024 school year. The reported study is part of an intervention study in which students experienced a two-week epidemic unit that emphasized inquiry with computer simulations and model construction. As this is part of a larger, ongoing study, the present paper reports on a preliminary analysis of the pre-assessment data. For this initial phase, the written responses of 30 students from one teacher's classroom were coded to allow for an in-depth characterization of their epistemic performance. Task design In this task, students were first presented with three newspaper-style articles that discussed the safety and effectiveness of low-carb diets. The articles were designed to present different types and qualities of evidence. Article 1, "Low-Carb Diets are S afe and Effective," made a strong positive claim based on a single study that included a control measure. Article 2, "Understanding the Effects of Low-Carb Diets," offered a more nuanced perspective, acknowledging both side effects and limited long-term effectiveness, based on a review of 38 experiments. Finally, Article 3, "Low-Carb Diets and Your Health," presented mixed results, inconsistently evaluated evidence from different studies, and included personal anecdotes to encourage readers to try the diet. The assessment itself included two components. In the first, students were asked to draw a conclusion regarding the safety and efficacy of low-carbohydrate diets and to state what advice they would give to someone considering such a diet. They then provided three reasons to explain and elaborate on their conclusion. In the second component, students rated the three articles on a scale from 1 to 7, with 1 indicating the least reliable and 7 the most reliable, and explained the reasoning behind each rating.

## Data Analysis

The unit of analysis was the whole student response for each question. We employed a three-phase iterative coding process: (1) the first author conducted an inductive line-by-line review of 30 responses to generate preliminary codes; (2) two additional coders independently applied these codes to the data, identifying borderline cases and points of controversy; and (3) a n expert (the second author) joined to help resolve coding disagreements and to make high-level conceptual refinements by reviewing the entire coding scheme for theoretical alignment with the AIR framework.

Students’ overall evaluation of the articles Before examining the specific ideals students mentioned, it is helpful to examine their overall reliability ratings for the three articles. On a 7-point scale, students rated Article 1 (the single-study article) the highest on average (Mean = 5.03, SD = 1.19), followed closely by Article 2 (the review study; Mean = 4.87, SD = 1.46). Article 3 (with mixed methods and the author’s anecdote) was rated the lowest (M = 4.37, SD = 1.56). The ratings for Article 3 show students’ varied attitudes toward its mixed evidence and use of personal anecdote. It gives us two experiments and the author tells us about their low-carb diet experience. ” A central finding emerging from this analysis is that students exhibited limited appreciation for the epistemic weight of a large body of evidence. This limited appreciation can be first illustrated by how students 1684 applied certain ideals to Article 2, a review of 38 experiments. Students who rated Article 2 highly often did so because they valued its balanced nature  rather than the strength of its evidence. This was reflected in ideals such as Presenting Both Sides. One student who gave the article a 7/7 explained, “This article explains that the low carb diet can help with weight loss, but at the same time damage the heart, which is lots of understanding I feel like because they have both a pro and a con of the diet.”  Interestingly, while some students interpret the inclusion of conflicting results as Presenting Both Sides, some other students consider the same characteristic as indicating a lack of certainty. For example, a student, who gave a 5/7 to Article 2, wrote, “however, the conclusion does not answer the question ‘is it safe or not’ clearly, so that is why I can only give it a 5.” Several students also found it “confusing” or “asking too many questions”, while others evaluated its persuasiveness, such as one who wrote, “the author was not convincing me that low-carb diets are bad for you.” It is worth noting that a higher rating (ratings of 5-7) is not necessarily associated with an apt understanding of the value of the review study. Only 3 (out of 16) stude nts rated Article 2 five or more while also commenting on the fact that it was based on a large amount of evidence. None of the students recognized that it was a review study and understood systematic review as a scientific method.

Taking together, these alternative ideals help explain what students focused on when it came to the document containing the review study. This is further evidenced by the frequent use of some ideals they employed for conclusions. For instance, a student noted, “My second reason is scientist are never sure. One day they will be telling you it is good the next they will say it is bad. Some scientists are always switching there (sic) opinion and not really sure about if it works…….”, which we coded as the Con sistency of Scientists, an expectation of stability and consistency within scientists and their knowledge products. Also, students often rely on the Majority of Sources heuristic, such as “After reading the three articles majority of the results ended with zeronegative health affects (sic). Two articles said that they lost more weight than on the low-fat diets.” This vote-counting approach reflects a nascent epistemic i deal of valuing the weight of evidence and seeking scientific consensus. Although students recognized that claims supported by multiple sources are more credible, they implemented this ideal through a simplistic, less reliable process of assigning equal weight to each article. Similarly, when evaluating an article, they frequently used Evidence (Unspecified), where they restated a finding without evaluation, showing that they often engaged with the content of the article rather than recognizing the complex nature of evidence. One of the goals of epistemic education is to promote learners’ fully apt epistemic performance, which refers to successful epistemic performance (e.g., reaching the best justified conclusions) achieved through competence (e.g., prioritizing large bodies of evidence over single studies) (Barzilai & Chinn, 2024). Our findings reveal that while the ninth-graders possess significant nascent epistemic assets, they struggle to coordinate these resources to appreciate the value of synthesis of large bodies of evi dence. From an asset-based perspective, we can see the heurisitic of reliance on majority source as a nascent ideal for seeking consensus –a recognition that convergence across sources increases credibility. The instructional challenge is to refine these existing ideals into sophisticated weighting processes.

Notably, our data reveal that high reliability scores assigned to documents do not always signify sophisticated reasoning. Some students who assigned Article 2 a perfect score of 7 still relied on the idea of good explanation rather than the article's evidentiary weight. This confirms that students frequently use communicative quality as a proxy for reliability. Like  Abed & Barzilai (2023), we argue that students' comprehension strategies can influence their source evaluation judgments. Communicative ideals  are functional for the goal of making sense of a text; however, students struggle to coordinate these with advanced evidentiary ideals. We suggest that instruction should support students in coordinating multiple epistemic ideals. Students should experience learning environments that help clarify different types of scientific evidence and the reasons why a synthesis of many studies is generally more reliable than a single one. Educators can leverage students' existing appreciation for balance and the reliance on majority sources while explicitly scaffolding the recognition of evidential premium in systematic reviews. Engaging students in classroom debates to collaboratively develop shared epistemic criteria (Duncan et al., 2018) might also he lp refine these assets into the competencies required for navigating today’s complex information landscape.
