# Understanding the Factors Influencing Persistence: What Can Novices Learn From Experts?

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Experts become experts in part because of their willingness to engage in thousands of hours of deliberate practice. What motivates them to persist in the face of constant failure? This question was addressed in a study of experts and novices in the domains of mathematics and English literature. We compared how experts persisted and spoke about their failures while thinking aloud during problem-solving on difficult tasks, in and outside of their domain of expertise. Experts persisted longer and gave different reasons for their failures than novices did, and their implicit theories of intelligence explained some of these patterns. Our findings document how an expert’s failure attributions and implicit theory of intelligence may help them persist when tackling challenging problems in their domains.

## Introduction

Experts are those who persist past proficiency to a level of competence in knowledge or skill that exc eeds most others (Bransford, 2000). Their extensive knowledge  libraries enable them to quickly understand challe nging problems in their domain, and to select and apply relevant strategies to solve them (Ericsson, 2014). Thus, experts can efficiently solve complex tasks novices cannot.  Beyond the cognitive, motivational behaviors of ex perts contributes to their ability to persist to achieve the elusive goal of expertise. Everyone repeatedly fails on the path to expertise. Chase (2013) showed experts exhibit persistence in the face of constant failures. The current study is the first attempt to replicate her findings in a larger sample, and we extend her work to directly investigate the role of motivation in persisting on challenging tasks and explaining attributions made during failure. Specifically, it views motivation through the lens of implicit theories of intelligence (Dweck, 2000).

## Literature Review

Chase (2013) investigated the role of motivation in  supporting persistence and understanding one’s fai lures on the long path to expertise. She found experts and novices differed in how they attributed failure during problem solving. Attributing failure to external factors ca n support learners’ self-esteem, while alternativel y, attributing failures to internal factors can negatively impact persistence and performance (Weiner, 1979). Specifi cally, experts in a domain attributed their failures externally, such as to the complexity of tasks or limited resources of the environment, which they could accurately judge given their knowledge. Because of this, they persisted longer in tackling these problems. By contrast, novices in  a domain attributed their failures internally, to their lack of intrinsic ability. Thus, they were less likely to persist in tackling these problems. To further bolster the current understanding of the  relationship between failure attributions and persistence, we considered an important construct in the motivation literature, Dweck’s (2000) notion of implicit theories of intelligence. In this theory, individuals who hold an incremental theory view intelligence  as a pliant trait developed through effort, while those who hold a fixed theory believe intelligence is stable within individuals and cannot be changed through effort over time. We predict those with more incremental theories of intelligence about their performance in a domain are more likely to persist in the face of repeated failure, and to  continue to grow in their competence to eventually reach expertise. O’Keefe et al. (2018) established that novice persistence was influenced by their theory of interest, which is parallel to theories of intelligence. Whether the focus is interest or intelligence, fixed mindsets believe the focus is stable and unchanging, while incremental (growth) mindsets see it as malleable and changeable with effort. But interest in a domain may not be enough to propel learners through expertise development. A learner’s identification with a discipline can also impact their persistence, and this may be nurtured through experiences with the domain directly or by teaching it to others (Mercier et al., 2006). Whether learners see the experience of failures during exploration of a topic as a normal part of the process or as a reason to give up can vary (Williams-Pierce, 2019). These various potential responses to failure impact persistence after failure as well (Chase, 2013). As failure is inevitable, whether failure can be a productive source of future learning or not (Kapur, 2014) motivates our focus on experts as a model of how novices can persist following repeated failure. ## Participants

Mathematics and English experts were recruited from a U.S. university to participate in this IRB-approved study. The experts were defined as faculty, postdocs, graduate students, or undergraduates researching or studying in a domain. This included 31 participants, 17 mathematics experts, M = 25.65 years, SD = 3.12 years and 14 English experts, M = 28.36 years, SD = 4.77 years, who completed both sessions of the study.

## Design and Procedure

Participants were instructed to think-aloud during 12 minutes of problem-solving for each task and were allowed to cease problem-solving before time was up, if they desired. In the first session, half the participants in each expert group completed the math task first followed by the English task. The mathematics problem required proving a statement about prime numbers. The English problem required interpreting a poem with oblique language. The other half completed the English task first, followed by the math task. All participants then completed an online demographics questionnaire. They were then requested to return for a second research session. Finally, they were compensated $15 for the first session. After at least 24 hours elapsed, participants returned for the second session. They revisited the tasks in the reverse order in which they originally completed them. They then responded to an online questionnaire including one question regarding whether they spontaneously revisited each task in between the two sessions. The remaining questions were drawn from a version of Dweck’s (2000) theories of intelligence questionnaire, They were duplicated and specialized to query their implicit theories of intelligence for mathematics and for English. Participants were compensated $20 for the second session. Data coding Failure attribution statements from the verbal protocol, such as “I’m really bad at this” and “This problem is unnecessarily difficult”, were categorized as internal or external, respectively, depending on the themes brought up by experts in the think-aloud sessions. These were used to compute a locus score for each task, defined as the number of external attributions minus the number of internal attributions, divided by the total attributions made during the task. Thus, positive scores indicate more external attributions of failure, and negative scores more internal attributions.  Intercoder reliability was strong for failure attribution coding (! =.97), and any discrepancies between coders were resolved via discussion.

## Discussion

The current study replicated Chase (2013) and extended it to investigate implicit theories of intelligence (Dweck, 2000). The observed interaction of expertise and task domain on persistence time shows English experts tend to spend more time working on challenging in-domain problems than challenging out-domain tasks. If experts do indeed persist for longer in their domains of expertise, how are they able to do so in the face of constant failure? Consistent with the results of Chase (2013), English novices make internal attributions for the failures they experience, i.e., they blame their own lack of ability. By contrast, English experts know enough to understand the difficulties they experience as a consequence of external factors such as the difficulty of the problem or constraints on problem solving time. Spontaneous revisitation of either problem in the days between the first and second sessions was not statistically related to the domain of expertise or the domain of the problems. Descriptively, math experts tended to revisit the in-domain task more, while English experts revisited both tasks equally. The lack of significant findings here could simply reflect the small sample size. Although we have run 50% more participants than Chase (2013), both the math expert and English expert groups are small in size, limiting our power to detect significant differences between them. Finally, English experts tend to have increased incremental implicit theories of intelligence in comparison to novices. These results help illustrate the relationship between expertise and domain and how their interaction affects problemsolvers’ beliefs about their intelligence in specific contexts. The path to expertise is long, populated by challenging problems, and fraught with failure. We found that for a given domain, experts tend to have a more incremental implicit theory of intelligence and novices a more fixed implicit theory. Given this finding, and the other results here, how can we design interventions to support novices when they inevitably face failure? Perhaps these methods are relevant to consider in tandem with the expertise literature to supplement the effectiveness of deliberate practice.
