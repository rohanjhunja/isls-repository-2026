# Designing a Dashboard for Insights into Complex Skills

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper describes the design and implementation of a functional prototype of a researcher-facing dashboard for insights into complex skills from multi-source data, including school records, chat/communication data, and item responses. Features include statistical insight panels and customizable layouts, incorporating visual cues (color coding by demographic variables) and flexible panel splitting for comparative analysis. Socio-Semantic Network Analysis (SSNA) maps student interactions and content mentions, revealing discourse patterns. The dashboard empowers researchers to uncover meaningful associations and generate analytic insights. Working with and visualizing multi-source, complex skill data Modern complex skill research generates rich datasets. Unlike dashboards designed for instructional decision-making or student self-monitoring, researcher-facing tools must support exploratory data analysis, hypothesis generation, and validation of constructs across diverse data types. The handling, cleaning, and linking of multi-source data can be an arduous undertaking, and tools to support researchers in easily viewing, manipulating, and testing their data are lacking. This paper describes a functional prototype to support researchers in their visualization and analysis of complex data. A study involving a collaborative social and emotional learning (SEL) curriculum (Hao et al., in press) provides the motivating context for developing and testing a flexible and user-friendly data dashboard, with the heart of a data explorer. The study collects various data, including conversation (chat) text from small groups of students, time on screen, and individual responses to teacher questions and personal skill reflections (using different item types and collected during SEL lessons). Simultaneously, researchers collect student school records, including pre/post academic outcomes, student self-report ratings on their social and emotional health, and teacher observations of student SEL skills. With these diverse types of collected data, it is both a challenge and an opportunity to think through and plan exploratory data analyses and data visualizations that may elicit insight into the targeted complex skill. Existing dashboards often focus on teacher, school, or student use; however, unlike teachers or students, researchers often work with multi-source datasets that require complex wrangling, integration, and statistical modeling, and should be guided by the educational learning theories that are the foundation of the research investigation (Wise & Shaffer, 2015). This project addresses the gap for an analyst-oriented analytics platform for investigating complex skills. The foundation for the proposed researcher-facing data dashboard is customizability of data visualizations, which can alert an attentive analyst to dig more deeply into the dataset(s) in meaningful ways. Based on data handling strategies proposed by Nicolini (2012), Goodyear (2020, p. 1052) advocates for “zooming in” and “zooming out” of data, to allow the broad patterns observable in high level data to trigger deeper analysis, and vice versa. The author argues that this capability is essential for generating actionable insights. Goodyear’s 2020 paper on co-configuration in hybrid learning spaces (such as this case of the SEL classroom), and suggests that analytic tools should support iterative refinements, adjustments, and even personalization of their data analysis environment. This approach is particularly relevant for dashboards designed to work with complex, multi-source datasets. To handle and link multi-source data, Bakharia et al. (2020) advocate for using star schema architecture (a powerful and versatile data structure commonly used in business analytics and data warehouses; see Microsoft Ignite, 2024) to organize and group data, allowing for aggregate calculations on grouped variables for various timeframes. Using this architecture, comparative analyses can target specific variables across the dataset that are expected to interact with other variables in meaningful ways based on relevant learning theories. The authors’ work highlights the need for dashboards that allow researchers to correlate diverse data types (e.g., process data, survey outcomes, academic measures) to surface statistically significant relationships, outliers, and group differences. Rather than conducting blind omnibus tests of significance across the various data components, this approach avoids the risk of false positives and misinterpretation that can arise from testing everything without context, and it aligns with the dashboard’s designed features, such as statistical insight panels and customizable visualizations. In addition to traditional statistical analyses and representations, student chat and communication data can be visualized using Socio-Semantic Network Analysis (SSNA). De Liddo et al. (2011) describes a discourse network structure consisting of a concept network (chat content) and a social network (human nodes), which are assumed to be strongly connected. The aim of De Liddo et al.’s discourse-centric learning analytics is to logically c onnect students to the content of learning, thereby providing meaningful insight into what students learn, at what point, and with whom. As further proof of concept, Zhu and Chen (2023) prototyped a similar SSNA model in their Joint Attention-Interaction-Creation framework and were able to effectively model how ideas are generated in collaborative discourse. By integrating an SSNA framework into the dashboard design, this project aims to move beyond static representations of simple correlations and statistical tests to offer researchers a dynamic, interactive environment for exploring complex relationships in SEL data. Finally, because the dashboard is inherently visual, best practices in visual data communication should be followed. Franconeri et al. (2021) offer many concrete insights on how to communicate with data effectively so that data insights become clear. The authors recommend thinking first about the goal of the displayed data; for example, use connected lines to show a continuum of connected data (e.g., avoid bars for time series) to encourage users to see trends over time. One goal of good data visualization is to reduce cognitive load for the data user, which ranges from including direct labels (or hover text) rather than relying on legends, to controlling comparisons with grouping or splitting options, such as the flexibility to view the data in side-by-side panels or overlays with color coding schema to distinguish groups. The authors strongly encourage respecting common conventions for spatial and visual channels (e.g., up for more and left to right for the passage of time), as well as avoiding using visual channels like length or area to plot purely categorical data. This also implies that nested data, such as students in classrooms or groups, follow hierarchical top-down structures to illustrate nested mappings. Following the best practices and analytic frameworks put forth by these studies has the potential to offer researchers a powerful, customizable environment for exploring and interpreting complex skill data. While the data to be used as a basis for this design is from a SEL hybrid-learning school environment, the design principles and practices are transferable to other complex sets of educational data to support actionable insights through dynamic visualization and data exploration tools.

Functional prototype The functional prototype contains the following design aspects:

• Correlation Panels: Scatterplots with regression lines; points color-coded by demographic variables;

relevant statistical test calculation options.

• Network Analysis Module: Interactive graphs with selectable nodes (students, top lesson content

mentions); relevant density and connectiveness calculation options.

• Visual Grouping Mechanisms: Flexible view options (e.g., side-by-side panels, color distinctions, cluster

arrangement, and data overlays) to compare data; annotated data points with identifying hover text and “zoom-in” capabilities when the data are explorable (such as a data table with the actual chat text).

• Statistical Insight Panel: Automated highlights/flags of outliers and significant group differences; a

tooltips panel is incorporated to provide statistical insight and suggest areas for deeper exploration. Figure 1 shows an example of a correlation panel. In this display, two SEL rating sub-constructs are displayed side by side for all teachers (this can be updated so that data for only one teacher is displayed at a time from the drop-down menu in the upper right corner). Statistical data is noted below each panel, in this case, for the relationship between student and teacher rating scale responses. Data points are color coded by student demographics and green indicating a data point with mixed demographics; the point size is indicative of multiple datapoints being represented. Data outliers are circled in red, and every data point displays the (x, y) information when the user hovers on the point. The data can also be downloaded as a.csv file for additional analysis. Figure 1 Example Side-by-Side Correlation Panel with Drop-Down, Color Grouping Figure 2 is an example of a socio-semantic network, in which student nodes are color-coded by within classroom student-partner/team groupings, and semantic nodes (in this case, the top 10 most frequently used words), are colored in yellow. The filtering criteria are by lesson or unit, and by the teacher, or all lessons and all teachers can be visible. When the filtering criteria are updated, so is the list of semantic nodes available, and these can be deselected (such as in the example, where good, sure, and really are deselected). While not pictured, the sematic nodes can also be pre-entered based on a list of unit or lesson keywords that are of interest; mainly, these w ould be the lesson content words that might indicate whether the lesson content was discussed and to what extent.

Figure 2 Example Socio-Semantic Network Although the motivating use case for this work involves SEL data, the functional prototype is designed to support components are designed to be applicable to any exploratory data analysis involving multi-source data, including log data, digital communications (e.g., chats or transcripts), and ratings or selected-response items. Researchers working with complex data need tools that go beyond basic analysis and visualization. The current system executes statistical analyses, renders dynamic visualizations, and supports exploratory interaction using simulated multi-source data. Significant aspects of the dashboard include:

• Manipulability and versatility – to filter, split, and compare data across demographic, nesting (classroom,

small group), and contextual variables, as well as timeframes and lesson units and content.

• Statistical insight – to provide basic statistical information, visually highlight outliers and significant

differences, thereby drawing attention to important coefficients and data interactions.

• Network analysis functionality-to show clusters of student chat data, including relevant content

mentions as nodes to reveal cross-group patterns.

• Visual Cues: Color coding and panel splitting by gender, ethnicity, school/classroom, or other variables.

This early working prototype is intended to support basic visualization and statistical exploration of multi-source data representing complex skill constructs, which aims to align analytic functionality with researcher needs within established best practices of data dashboard design. Future work will examine scalability as the prototype is extended to real-world datasets.
