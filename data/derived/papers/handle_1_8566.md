# Understanding and Leveraging Contradictions of Instructional Activities for Fostering Students’ Agency

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Instructional activities are situated in a web of interdependent activity systems such as students’ families, educational institutes, research programs, industries and governments. Historically accumulating tensions between these systems are said to impose contradictory demands on students. For example, during learning, students often feel compelled to choose between being a grade maker versus a sense maker. Such deep-rooted problems cannot be addressed by simply finding better ways of implementing existing practices. Hence we revisit key ideas such as instruction, feedback, competence and their interconnections by considering contradictions across activity systems. Then we carve out a space for students’ agency within such interconnections by introducing and investigating a construct called ‘students’ feedback seeking actions’ using cultural historical activity theory (CHAT). Finally, its affordances in further uncovering and leveraging contradictions of instructional activities for fostering students’ agency are explored using a case study from undergraduate chemistry education.

## Introduction

Currently, there are multiple notions of instructio n and feedback varying significantly in terms of wh ether they limit or extend the scope for students’ agency (Ask ew, 2000). Such variation arises from the underlyin g contradictions in how different learning research programs view knowledge, competence, learning and individuals (Engeström, 2018). Some programs view knowledge and competence that is worth learning as stable, well-defined and out there to be acquired and transferred; while others view it as distributed, evolving, or yet to be discovered, constructed and validated. Similarly, learning is a linear process for some and a non-linear process m ediated by multiple cultural factors for others. Then some acc ount for just students’ and teachers’ cognition whi le others consider their emotional, motivational and identity aspects. Acknowledging these contradictions has led to a shift in viewing both instruction and feedback as dialogu e (Ajjawi & Boud, 2018; Skidmore, 2017). However, t heir relationship when both are viewed as dialogue is unclear. Also, studies with instruction as dialogue have focused mostly on different kinds of talk (Kim & Wilkinson, 2019). On the contrary, there is increasing recognition that instructional activities, culture and dialogue all shape each other. To address these and other contra dictions discussed below, we use CHAT to reconceptualize key notions as follows: ● Instructional activity is a teacher's predetermined plan involving the organization of certain tasks, tools, representations, norms, individuals and ways of coo peration (division of labor) estimated to support students' learning towards particular valuable outcomes. ● Feedback is any information concerning the given in structional activity that addresses students’ needs tied to their knowing, doing and being for accompli shing, modifying, or even avoiding the expected outcomes by the teacher. Students canderive feedback either by working with representations and tools or having a dialogue with themselves and multiple others or using a combination of these modes. ● Students’ feedback seeking actions are those where decisions such as purpose, timing, mode, amount of feedback seeking, choice of feedback source and how feedback is used are all made by themselves. ● Competence is a set of students’ interconnected actions tied to their knowing, doing and being judged as valuable in relation to particular activities and culture within which they are (or will be) enacted. The above definitions also account for contradictions across interdependent activity systems. This idea of instruction acknowledges the complexity of any g oal directed activity and does not reduce it to jus t tasks or talk. Further, instruction as an estimate and prede termined plan accounts for classroom realities such as student diversity, teacher workload and large pupil-teacher  ratio. Consequently, we also do not view teachers’  role as orchestrating instruction and feedback on a moment-to-moment basis. Then the idea of feedback acknowledges that it can be any information that meets students’ diverse cognitive, emotional, motivational and identity needs. Especially, viewing feedback’s role as not always a ccomplishing teacher's expected outcomes aligns wit h the notion of instruction as an estimate where it may or may not address students’ diverse needs. This along with the idea of students’ feedback seeking extends the scope for students’ agentic actions significantly. That is, students ICLS2022 Proceedings 1661 © ISLS can choose to seek feedback from multiple sources and through multiple modes for accomplishing, modifying, or even avoiding the aspects of instruction. This is unlike many prevailing practices where teachers initiate and drive feedback events even when peers exchange feedback. Moreover, the focus on students’ feedback seeking aligns with Nicol’s (2013) emphasis on getting students re ady for life beyond university (like occupational s ettings) where feedback seldom comes from others proactively. Instead one has to identify potential sources fro m multicultural communities and elicit and judge feedback from them. Also, feedback thus received can be vague, ambiguous, and even conflicting. Compare this with the formative feedback guidelines in education which require an external agent such as a teacher to continuously  monitor student performance, simultaneously accoun t for various student and task characteristics and accord ingly adapt the content, mode, timing and frequency  of the feedback messages to be provided (Shute, 2008). Las tly, the idea of competence acknowledges that it is  not an isolated set of stable skills but one which can als o become obsolete with the changes in particular activities and culture within which it is situated. Overall, the a bove notions might help inform sustainable alternat ives to prevailing unilateral, expensive and culturally dis connected classroom practices. This in turn may hel p address large scale educational challenges before governmen ts. Mor eover, it makes a key CHAT principle explicit i.e, the object  of an instructional activity like students’ feedba ck seeking actions carries within it the foundational contradictions i.e, use-value and exchange value of interactions between student, instructional activity and culture (Engeström, 2018). More specifically, interactions between the student, instructional activity and culture are predicted to  influence students’ cost-value perceptions underly ing their various feedback seeking decisions. Here students’ cost-value perceptions can be of biological or cultural origins with cognitive, emotional, motivational and identit y dimensions. Consequently, our research question a ims at uncovering such interactions “How do the factors co mprising instructional activity and culture interac t to influence students’ cost-value perceptions underlying their various feedback seeking actions?”

## Methods

To answer the research question, we gave students a  representational problem pertaining to the synthes is of a medicinal drug called warfarin. The choice of the problem aligns with the idea that knowledge worth learning is continuously evolving. While there were other metho ds of synthesis, the particular technique underlyin g the chosen problem is recent, precise, cheap, fast and environment-friendly. Solving the problem required students to employ major forms of representational reasoning li ke analogical reasoning and/or thought experimentat ion in order to predict expected drug’s final structure. Complexity of the problem meant that students would experience numerous challenges nudging them to go beyond just verbal exchange and use multiple modes as part of t heir feedback seeking. Thus the problem helped us generate rich analyzable data where we could examine how aspects of instructional activity, culture and students’ fe edback seeking dialogue were all shaping each other. For data collection, we invited a group of three undergraduate chemistry students. The study was conducted in a lab setting outside their classroom. The given problem was an h our-long activity designed such that students could  either work individually or choose to collaborate as and w hen needed. This was to ensure that there was no ex ternal ICLS2022 Proceedings 1662 © ISLS compulsion to seek or provide feedback. Besides, wo rksheets with task details were minimal in design i.e., we intentionally did not include any prompts for self/peer assessment, hints, or questions for reflection. Data collected included video recording of the students’ interacti ons with each other and the researcher and their ta sk specific responses on worksheets such as sketches, highlight s and comments. It also included post-activity inte rview responses. Researcher’s presence during students’ problem solving was limited and occasional. Data analysis was done using a microgenetic method. It emphasizes tracing emergence of particular student actions to momentary processes by making high density observations (Chin n & Sherin, 2014). Unit of analysis is an episode o f a student's proactive feedback seeking from peers and  the researcher where one episode was distinguished  from another by considering the change in the purpose for which the feedback was sought. During analysis we found emotions as significant markers of students’ cost-value perceptions.

## Findings

Analysis of students’ feedback seeking actions reve aled the complex and multilateral ways in which cul ture, instructional activity, students’ characteristics a nd their actions shape each other. For e.g. in one episode, the purpose behind student’s feedback seeking was determined by the task specific difficulty arising out of one’s selfdirected dialogue while comparing representations. In addition, the timing and choice of feedback sour ce was influenced by the emergence of feedback availability signs with the peer working nearby, one’s own hesitancy in seeking feedback due to perceived embarrassment shaped by the prevailing cultural norms (i.e. shaming ignorance or incompetence) and the norms of given instruction al activity (i.e. freedom to work individually or collaboratively) which further heightened such embarrassment. Then the evaluative nature of feedback s eeking and that the feedback received was subjected to constant verification reflected the student’s perceived credibility of the peer as a feedback source. Also throughout t he episode, both the student and peer were observed  to constantly regulate shifting between having dialogue with each other versus having dialogue at self le vel to aid their mutual sense making. Over and above, the amou nt of feedback seeking was influenced by the extent  of specificity, clarification or elaboration that was required to meet one’s unique feedback needs, cooperation needed by peer to generate feedback, communicative afforda nces of available representations/tools and also pe er’s enthusiasm derived in part due to culture where fee dback provision was perceived as an opportunity for  selfenhancement. Here various emotions of student and peer triggered by the interactions between factors of culture, instruction and student reflected the cost-value perceptions tied to their cognition, motivation and identity aspects. This in turn steered the overall nature of student’s feedback seeking dialogue. Similarly when multiple episodes were analyzed and compared it was observed that in some episodes the purposes for which feedback was sought was infl uenced largely by students’ cost-value perceptions tied to their motivation while in others it was mostly thei r identity or cognitive aspects. Also, in some inst ances the purposes were triggered by the emergence of salient feedback availability signs amongst feedback sources (e.g. an utterance signaling new info) without experienci ng any prior need at the moment. Further, the purpo ses for which students sought feedback were not always aimed at accomplishing the goals of the activity, instead some were specifically directed at modifying or even avo iding the aspects of it. These observations suggest ed that students were probably making changes to the factors of instructional activity in order to adjust it to their actual zone of proximal development as opposed to the inst ructor’s estimate. This inference was further probe d and confirmed using post-activity interview. For instan ce in student group, even though it had members who successfully completed the given tasks, one student who faced difficulties expressed later in the interview that he would have liked to have more members in the group i.e. more feedback sources possibly due to the natu re of emotional, motivational and identity aspects shared  with the participating members. In addition, stude nts were also observed to prefer feedback seeking at interpersonal level i.e., from peers in some cases and tools in others because of their perceived identity costs such as embarrassment. Here the underlying cost-value analysis was also observed to occur in an hierarchical manner highlighting the historical nature of feedback seeking. For example in a particular instance, where the student anticip ated a possible testing event, the opportunity to e xchange feedback at interpersonal level was perceived as valuable. In the subsequent instance, when the testing event was now ruled out, anticipated costs tied to interperso nal exchange, i.e., particular reasons for which as king others would be more embarrassing became a major concern.

Overall, students' sensemaking during the instructi onal activity was observed to occur as a constant comparison with their previous experiences. Here pr evious experiences were not just limited to their p rior instruction but also included those related to family, friends, industry and state. For instance, a student’s feedback seeking aimed at renegotiating norms around usage o f mobile devices occurred as a comparison with the way mobiles are used in the context of family and friends. It is this comparative sense-making process that gives rise to the emergence of heightened cost-value perceptio ns culminating in their feedback seeking actions and determining various aspects of it. Also since the triggers for students’ sensemaking are both multiple internal and ICLS2022 Proceedings 1663 © ISLS external factors, the nature of students' feedback seeking is a complex and multilateral phenomena. Then in line with the CHAT perspective, we also obs erved that the multilateral nature of students’ feedback seeking plays a crucial role in them harne ssing as well as adapting to the distributed compet ence of participating members. For instance, sensemaking in  activities rich with multiple representations requ ires perceiving, analyzing and synthesizing different representational features with the target phenomena. On one such occasion, where a student’s feedback seeking from t he peer emerged as a result of challenges experienc ed at individual level, it was observed that during feedb ack dialogue both became aware of the differences a nd also errors in their individual perception of representational features. In subsequent conversational turns they together abstracted, analyzed and synthesized different feat ures of representations iteratively, thus gaining a  deeper understanding of the target phenomena. Here in essence, feedback dialogue was driven by the distributed nature of their individual attention, memory and thinking. Moreover, the role of feedback seeker and feedback provider was constantly shifting amongst participants as they progressed in the tasks. This again indicates the distributed nature of knowledge and competence amongst participants. Students were also observed to adapt to each other’s representational competence by revising use of different representations to get their message across.

## Discussion & Conclusion

Findings confirm CHAT’s prediction that the nature of students’ agentic feedback seeking would be driv en by their cost-value perceptions resulting from interac tions of instruction, culture and individual charac teristics. Findings also suggest that feedback seeking plays an important role in students harnessing as well adapting to the knowledge and competence distributed across peers. Further students’ feedback seeking actions aimed at modifying instructional activity supports CHAT’s ke y assertion that the nature of relationship between individual’s agency, immediate activity and culture is essentially reciprocal. Together these findings validate the significance of reconceptualization of key notions such as instructional activity, feedback, students’  agentic feedback seeking actions and competence by accounti ng for contradictions across activity systems. Narr ow conceptualization of these interconnected notions naturally limits the scope for students to exercise their agency. Findings also suggest that teachers can further uncover and leverage contradictions of instructional activities by viewing them as open activity systems. That is, viewing instructional activities as open to student modifications will help invite individual student’s culture into the classroom. It could in turn help foster students’ agency along with their self-learning capabilities, eliminate culturally obsolete skills and trigger teacher reflections. However, student modifications can be a source of both problems as well as innovation. Here collective cost-value analysis as per CHAT can be used to help resolve such issues. That is, teachers should try to uncover both the underlying cost and value perceptions that might have guided students’ modifications of instructional activity. Then regarding sustainability, if student groups of different academic years worked in a single classroom on activities of varying complexity and overlapping learning goals, then the ir various actions might help signal and elicit eac h other's feedback seeking on much more diverse purposes. This hypothesis is based on finding that feedback seeking also occurs as result of emergence of salient external feedback availability signs.
