# Developing a Counter-Space for Learning, Belonging, and Becoming in and Through Calculus

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents the design and implementation of a counter-space that integrates disciplinary rigor with identity-empowering practices to support historically marginalized STEM students at the University of California, Berkeley. Situated within a social design experiment, the counter-space fostered a collaborative community where students engaged deeply with core calculus ideas while cultivating positive mathematical identities and a stronger sense of belonging. The paper elaborates on the key design principles of the counter-space as well as its structure and curriculum. Analysis of students’ weekly reflection journals revealed significant gains in students’ mathematical understanding, performance, confidence, sense of belonging, and self-perceptions as capable mathematical thinkers. This study offers a model for disciplinary-rigorous and identity-empowering counter-spaces that institutions can adapt to address systemic barriers and promote sustainable STEM pathways for historically marginalized students.

## Introduction

At the University of California, Berkeley, a highly selective public university in the United States, more than onethird of students (555 out of 1511) dropped or failed Math 1A (Calculus) in Fall 2023. This alarming attrition rate highlights a widespread calculus crisis that dispro portionately affects women, students of color, firs t-generation college students, and those from socioeconomic disadvantaged backgrounds (Seymour & Hunter, 2019). Urgent, proactive measures are essential to address both the academic challenges these students face and to counteract the erosion of their mathematical identities.

Counter-spaces—safe spaces where marginalized students can advance  their learning and build camaraderie (Solórzano et al., 2000)—offer a promising mechanism for tackling these issu es. However, existing counter-spaces discussed in the literature (e.g., Ong  et al., 2018; Solórzano et al., 2000) are often rea ctive and informally structured (Zuñiga-Ruiz, 2022), emerging from the efforts of struggling students or individual mentors without deliberate design. This paper addresses thi s critical gap by presenting the design and impleme ntation of a theoretically grounded and locally responsive cou nter-space for historically marginalized STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics) students at the University of California, Berkeley. As the lead designer, facilitator, and researcher i n this social design experiment (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016; Gutiérrez et al., 2020), I aim to cultivate a  community of mathematical sense-makers engaged in learning, belonging, and becoming through calculus. This counter-space builds on Treisman’s Emerging Scholars Program (Asera, 2001; Treisman, 1992), emphasizing collabor ative sense-making around challenging calculus concepts through problem-based discussions. However, it goes further by expl icitly fosterin g positive identity development—an aspect often overlooked in cooperative learning models in STEM higher education (e.g., Arendale, 1994, 2014, 2020; Wilson & Varma-Nelson, 2016). The identity work in this counter-space involves three interrelated dimensions. First, I center and amplify the voices of historically marginalized students—as critical scholars have long advocated (Collins & Bilge, 2016; Ladson-Billings & Tate, 1995; Mar tin, 2000; Solózano & Yosso, 2002) —through structured activities of reflection and storytelling (detailed in the method s section). This approach positions students as exp erts of their lived experiences, fostering shared understanding, camaraderie, and mutu al support within the counter-space. Second, I engage students in reading, reflecting, a nd discussing how broader power structures (e.g., p atriarchy, racism, ableism; Langer-Osuna, 2011; Larnell, 2016; Martin, 2000; Shah, 201 7; Stinson, 2008) intersect wit h their mathematical learning, providing space and resources to challenge harmful master narratives and affirm their capabilities and aspirations. Third, I guide studen ts in reflecting on moment-to-moment group dynamics, collaboratively developing positioning practices (Harré & van Langenhove, 1999; Herbel-Eisenmann et al., 2015; Langer-Osuna, 2011) that affirm diverse ways of learning a nd being. Integrating these three dimensions into mathematics-intensive learning spaces is innovative, and my wor k explores the new possibilities this integration holds for enhancing student learning and identity development. This paper addresses the following research question: How does participation in the counter-space impact students’ mathematical content learning, sense of b elonging, and mathematical identities from the stud ents’ perspectives? Adopting a narrative approach, I conceptualize mathematical identities as the st ories and beliefs students construct about themselves in relation to mathematics (Huang, 2024; Martin, 2000; Sfard & Pru sak, 2005).

The goals of this paper are twofold: 1) to describe the activities and experiences within the counter-space, and 2) to present a model of a disciplinary-rigorous, identity-empowering counter-space that can be adapted across campuses and STEM courses to fos ter deep conceptual learning, positive disciplinary  identities, and more inclusive pathways to STEM careers for historically marginalized populations.

## Methods

Positionality As a designer-facilitator-researcher, I acknowledge the profound influence of  my identities and experiences on this work. My prior experience teaching calculus at  universities has equipped me with the expertise to  facilitate meaningful mathematical discussions and develop problems that deepen students’ con ceptual understanding. As a woman in mathematics, I have encountered societal  assumptions that link my female peers’ struggles t o their gender, which has often been frustrating and dishea rtening. These exp eriences fuel my commitment to empowering students—particularly those from historically marginalized communities—to recognize their agency and challenge harmful master narratives.

At the same time, I am aware that my Chinese herita ge can pose challenges in connecting with students who face different racial-mathematical stereotypes. For some, my presence may  unintentionally reinforce the “model minority” stereotype in American societ y, which links Asian identities with mathematical s uccess. To address this, I have actively fostered reflective dialogues that unpack these stereotypes while amplifying students’ strengths and aspirations. Throughout this work, I remain attuned to each student’s individual needs, dedicated to creating a learning environment where everyone feel s valued, supported, and empowered to thrive in the ir mathematical and personal journeys.

Social design experiments This research is situated within the framework of social design experiments (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016, Gutiérrez et al., 2020), which integrate design-based research methodologies (Design-Based Research Collective, 2003) with a social justice agenda (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016). Following Gutiérrez et al. (2020), this study embraces six core commitments: ● Historicity: Highlighting historical inequities in STEM and centering students’ lived experiences. ● Prolepsis: Cultivating new possibilities for learning and identity development. ● Diversity: Embracing and integrating students’ varied perspectives and strengths. ● Equity: Ensuring meaningful and inclusive engagement with mathematics for all students. ● Transformability: Empowering students to challenge inequities and imagine new futures. ● Resilience: Building robust support systems to help students navigate obstacles. The intervention is grounded theoretically in scholarship on mathematical learning (e.g., Schoenfeld, 2020, 2022) and mathematics-related identities (e.g., Gresalfi & Hand, 2019; La nger-Osuna & Esmonde, 2017). By weaving together these commitments, the lar ger research project aims to generate local theorie s on how students’ mathematical sense-making and identity development co-evolve within the counter-space.

## Participants and context

The counter-space was implemented alongside Math 1A (Calculus) at the University of California, Berkeley, in Spring 2024 and Fall 2024. Math 1A is a foundational course required for most STEM majors and plays a critical role in shaping students’ academic trajectories.

Three students participated in the Spring 2024 iter ation, and ten students participated in the Fall 20 24 iteration. Among the 13 participants, 11 were first-generation college students, and nine came from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds. The co hort included ten women (including one identifying as bisexual and another as lesbian), two men, and one non-binary individual. Racially, seven participants ide ntified as Latine, two as African American, one as Banglade shi, one as Peruvian, one as White, a nd one as half-Latine, half-White.

Nine participants (69.2%) rated themselves in the bottom 25% of their peers in the Math 1A class, while four (30.8%) placed themselves in the middle 50% and below average. Their majors spanned neuroscience, engineering, astrophysics, psychology, biology, b usiness, and mathematics. This diverse cohort is re presentative of the students disproportionately pushed out of ca lculus at the University of California, Berkeley, p roviding critical insight into how the counter-space supports historically marginalized populations. Design principles Design principle 1:  Promote mathematical proficiency by setting high expectations, designing challenging and generative problem sets, and fostering a culture of inquiry and engagement in mathematical practices At the University of California, Berkeley, the aspi ration for academic excellence is a shared cornerst one of the campus community, and it played a key role in how I  designed the counter-space to be locally responsive. I co-constructed high academic expectations with every m ember of the counter-space, establishing norms that emphasized rigorous reasoning and the collaborative  sense-making of complex calculus problems. These expectations are framed by three core principles in spired by the productive disciplin ary engagement framework (Engle & Conant, 2002): intellectual accountability to the individual (i.e., ensuring genuine sense-making), to one another (i.e., engaging with others’ ideas), and to  disciplinary norms (i.e., meeting the standards of  the broader mathematics community).

The problem sets I designed were crafted to stimula te mathematical inquiry and deep engagement with core calculus ideas. These problems were developed to meet the following criteria: (a) focusing on cen tral concepts that interlink with various mathematica l topics and representations, (b) addressing studen ts’ emerging needs in learning Math 1A materials, (c) offering a ccessible entry points and multiple solution pathwa ys (Cohen & Lotan, 2014), (d) presenting significant cognitiv e challenges (Treisman, 1992), and (e) serving as s eeds for further exploration (Schoenfeld, 2020). Students were encouraged to explore ideas beyond the provided problem sets, embrace multiple approaches, and integrate th e diverse resources each member brings to the conve rsation. Engaging with these designed problems facilitated parti cipation in core mathematical practices, including sensemaking, connecting, problem-solving, conjecturing, justifying, and generalizing. Regular engagement in these practices provided opportunities for students to develop their intellectual authority, ownership over mathematics, and a stronger sense of themselves as capable mathematics learners. Design principle 2: Foster positive identity development by creating spaces dedicated to communitybuilding, storytelling, and critical engagement with broader power structures and local power dynamics A key focus of the counter-space was fostering a sense of community, where trust, belonging, and mutual support served as the foundation for students’ personal and academic growth. This community-building process consisted of three interwoven components: (1) cultivating a sense of connection, (2) ensuring that every member felt valued, and (3) using the counter-space as a launchpad for integration into the broad er STEM community. I nurtured students’ sense of connection through regular check-ins, open discussions about shared concerns, and re gular affirmation of individual and collective contributi ons. To ensure every student feels seen and valued,  I provided varied opportunities for them to exert agency and c ontribute, such as co-designing community norms, guiding mathematical discussions, proposing new topics for collective sense-making, suggesting improvements to the counter-space, and addressing power imbalances in group int eractions. These opportunities created rich engagements in both mathematical and c ollaborative practices that mirrored those of the b roader STEM community.

Storytelling played a crucial role in amplifying students’ voices and fostering mutual understanding within the counter-space. Personal and mathematical check-ins, coupled with weekly reflection journals, provided structured opportunities for students to express themselves. I scaffolded expansive conversations on mathematics and equity by introducing students to critical literature (e.g., Larnell, 2016), facilitating brainstorming sessions to confront problematic master narratives, and support ing them in r e-authoring their identities in ways that affirm their capabilities and aspirations.

Critical engagement with local power dynamics was v ital to the well-being of the counter-space, especially when students with stronger mathematical backgrounds dominated conversations. When that happened, I took two complementary approaches: (a) in the mom ent, I highlighted the strengths of non-dominant students’ contributions and invited them to present their ide as publicly; and (b) over a longer time scale, I ad justed subsequent learning activities by designing reflect ion prompts that encouraged students  to reflect on recent interaction patterns, consider different perspectives on the interactions, and collectively brainstorm  strategies for enhancing collaboration and inclusivity. This two-fold strategy supported the continuous growth of ou r counterspace, centering the emerging needs of every member. Design principle 3: Implement regular and frequent formative assessments on both students’ content learning and identity development Formative assessment, often referred to as assessme nt for learning, involves continuously monitoring students ’ progress and using the insights gained to guide and  refine subsequent instruction (Black et al., 2003;  Burkhardt & Schoenfeld, 2019). I employed two key approaches to content-focused formative assessment. First, I monitored students’ mathematical discourses during group discussions, providing strategic hints and questions to guide their thinking without taking over the conversation. This  allowed students to author their own ideas, collab orate, and engage in productive struggles (Stein & Smith, 1998). Second, I designed weekly reflection prompts to gain insights into students’ perspectives on their learn ing. Prompts such as “What progress do you see in y our math learning—whether in skills, understanding, or confidence?” and “What specific topics would you like to focus on in our next group meeting?” provided valuable insights that inform the design of future problem sets and learning activities.

While formative assessment literature has traditionally centered on content learning, expanding its scope to support students’ identity development is both timely and critical (Huang, in preparation), especially given the growing evidence of identity’s pivotal role in shap ing student learning (Boaler & Greeno, 2000; Huang,  2022, 2024; Huang & DosAlmas, 2024; Larnell, 2016; Schoenfeld et al., 2023). In addition to monitoring how students positioned themselves and one another in real-time during group meetings using positioning theory (Harré & van Langenhove, 1999; Herbel-Eisenmann et al., 2015; Langer-Osuna, 2011), I designed weekly reflection prompts to tap into students’ narratives about their experi ences (Sfard & Prusak, 2005; Shah, 2017), their per ceived roles in the learning environment (Boaler & Greeno, 2000;  Langer-Osuna & Esmonde, 2017), and their beliefs and feelings about themselves in relation to mathematic s and broader power structures (Martin, 2000; Schoe nfeld, 1985). Beyond engaging students in sharing their stories, I used prompts such as “How did you feel after leaving our last group meeting?” and “Did you feel included  and able to be yourself throughout the group meeti ng?” to gain insights into their experiences in the counter-space and identify areas for improvement. Information gathered from my participant-observations and students’ reflection journals info rmed subsequent learning activities, such as exploring problems of personal significance to s tudents, designing reflection prompts respons ive to emerging themes in their conversations, and raising observed issues for collective resolution in structured discussions. Structure and curriculum of the counter-space Weekly three-hour group meetings, held throughout Spring and Fall 2024 terms, serve d as the cornerstone of the counter-space. Each meeting began with personal and mathematical check-ins, followed by structured discussions and collaborative problem-based learning.

During check-ins, each member shared reflections on their week, including personal, social, and mathematical experiences, along with any other thoughts they felt comfortable bringing into the community. This open sharing process strengthened community bonds and created a strong, personal foundation for mutual support and understanding.

Structured discussions were designed to foster community within the group while addressing learning or personal needs that extended beyond collaborative m athematical sense-making. Each week, we explored a planned topic (e.g., desired community norms, effec tive learning strategies, harmful master narratives) or an emergent issue requested by students or identified through my participant-observations (e.g., self-care strategies, time management). This flexible structure ensured t hat the counter-space evolved to meet the changing needs of its members.

Collaborative problem-based learning invited students to work together on  tackling complex problems drawn from the week’s problem set. The mathematics curriculum aligned closely with the Math 1A syllabus at the University of California, Berkeley, with an added focus on connecting concepts and strategies that were often treated separately in textbooks or lectures. The problem sets were intentionally crafted to engage students in core mathematical practices such as justifying, evaluati ng, and connecting ideas. Each set consisted of ten carefully designed problems that meet the criteria outlined i n the previous section. Students collaborated to wo rk through their selected problems, ensuring genuine understanding of the underlying mathematics before progressi ng. An example problem is shown in Figure 1.

## Data collection and analysis

The larger research project utilized multiple data sources, including background surveys, students’ we ekly reflection journals, post-study interviews, video recordings of weekly group meetings (81 hours total), and my field notes. Grounded in sociocultural  theories of learning (Wenger, 1989), the larger stu dy examines students’ mathematical learning through detailed video analysis, focusing on changes in their conceptual understanding and participation within the counter-space. Additionally, it explores the  mechanisms underlying students’ evolving mathematical identities by analyzing how the counte r-space facilitated or constrained opportunities for students to engage in core mathematical practices (Schoenfel d, 2020) and to develop agency, authority, and own ership over mathematics (Langer-Osuna & Esmonde, 2017; Schoenfeld et al., 2023). Due to space constraints, this paper focuses on students’ reflection journals (n=115) as the primary data source to understand their learning experiences and  outcomes from their perspectives. These reflection journals, each ranging from one to three pages, provide rich qualitative insights into students’ mathematical and identityrelated growth over the course of the study. I employed thematic coding to identify recurring pa tterns across students’ reflection journals. The analysis was guided by key themes —mathematical content learning outcomes, sense of be longing, and mathematical identities—selected based on their alignment  with the research question and their prominence in prior literature on mathematics learning (e.g., Gresalfi & Hand, 2019; Huang, 2024; Schoenfeld, 1992, 2020). The coding process began with open coding of all 11 5 reflection journals to identify emergent patterns. Preliminary codes included students’ growth in unde rstanding specific mathematical concepts (e.g., lim its, derivatives), perceptions of safety and inclusion within the counter-space, and shifts in their self-perceptions and beliefs as mathematics learners. These codes were iteratively refined through constant comparison and reflection to ensure consistency throughout the coding process  and alignment with  existing literature (e.g., Huang et al., 2024; Martin, 2000; Sfard & Prusak, 2005).

An abductive approach was used to construct the fin al set of themes and codes, integrating inductive insights from the data with deductive guidance from  the literature. For example, the theme of “mathema tical identities” included subcodes such as “confidence in problem-solving,” “self-perception as capable mathematical thinkers,” “enjoyment of mathematics,” “sense of ow nership in mathematical knowledge,” “sense of agenc y in mathematics learning,” “sense of contribution to th e learning community,” and “connection to personal lives.” These subcodes were informed by students’ reflectio ns and prior research (Huang, 2023, 2024; Langer-Osuna & Esmonde, 2017; Martin, 2000; Schoenfeld et al., 2023). Results from the analysis process were triangulated with post-study interviews and video analysis of group meetings to ensure validity and depth.

## Results

This section highlights students’ mathematical cont ent learning outcomes, sense of belonging, and math ematical identities as revealed through their weekly reflection journals. To represent the breadth of experiences, I selected examples that illustrate recurring themes across participants and showcase dive rse student voices. All names are pseudonyms chosen by the students themselves.

Mathematical content learning outcomes All students reported significant improvements in their mathematical understanding and performance in the Math 1A (Calculus) course. For example, Yashal, a Black woman, first-generation college student from a socioeconomically disadvantaged background majoring in business, shared in her 12th reflection journal: I originally joined [the counter-space] not expecting it to help me a whole lot, bec ause I really thought I was hopeless at math…. The sessions are n on-judgmental and low stress…. I spent all of winter break stressing about Math 1A, but I’ve got just about a 100 in the class. I used to forget a lot of rules and had to relearn them befor e tests, but I feel like that happens much less because we go through them and make sure we underst and why, when, and how to apply them…. It changed my perspective on what I’m able to do. Similarly, Carlos, a Peruvian man, first-generation college student from a socioeconomically  disadvantaged background majoring in mechanical engineering, shared in his ninth reflection journal: I think I’ve made a lot of progress… and am really happy with how far I have reinforced my foundation of mathematics and understanding. Algebr a is the main thing that I struggled with throughout mathematics, and I can confidently say t hat I have come to a very strong comprehension of it. I feel a lot better when doing  math, and it’s truly enjoyable learning new topics and doing complex problems…. I think I could have done better for the second midterm, but overall, I am happy with myself because I serio usly feel an improvement with my mathematical ability and so on and so forth…. I fee l ready to move on to more advanced mathematics classes and feel a great sense of improvement and foundation in my mathematics. Notably, both Yashal and Carlos exceeded their init ial expectations for the Math 1A course, earning an  A-at least one grade higher than the goals they had set for themselves at the start of the semester. Sense of belonging The counter-space fostered a strong sense of belonging among participants by creating a safe, inclusive, and nonjudgmental environment for learning. This is especi ally crucial for women, students of color, and stud ents with disabilities, who often face significant challenges in developing a sense of belonging in introductory mathematics and other STEM classes (e.g., Seymour & Hunter, 2019).

Many participants highlighted the contrast between the sense of connection they experienced within the counter-space and their previous experiences in mathematics  classrooms or the broader Berkeley campus. For example, Lili, a Latine woman and first-generation college student aspiring to attend law s chool, captured this contrast in her second reflection journal: “I really liked our group meeting because I felt sa fe in the environment and felt like it was a non-judgmental space where I don’t feel the same way as in my discussion or lecture. We really enjoyed our little group because everyone makes us feel comfortable, and even me, because I felt like I have less knowledge than everyone else in the room…. even when I didn’t understand the concepts and material, I didn’t feel out of pla ce or judged. Overall, I felt good in the environment.” Mia, a half-Latine, half-White woman, first-year first-generation college student preparing to major in neuroscience, echoed this sentiment in her seventh reflection journal: “I feel very included in the co mmunity…. I didn’t feel that I had to hide things about myself or that I was excluded…. I am not scared to mess up because I know this is the place to learn.” For Ally, a White bisexual woman and first-generation college student with a psychological dis ability, the counter-space provided a meaningful space for healing. In her fourth reflection journal, Ally reflected: I don’t feel I belong in Math 1A or at Berkeley in general. But in this community, I feel safe, valued, and at home, because I see people like me a nd we do a great job establishing and adhering to the norms we want to set.

Mathematical identities The counter-space transformed how students viewed themselves as  mathematical learners, helping them build confidence and develop a stronger sense of agency. For example, when asked whether the counter-space had changed their beliefs about themselves or what they  were capable of achieving in mathematics, Daniel, a Latine non-binary, first-generation college student from a socioeconomically  disadvantaged background majoring in astrophysics, wrote in their 11th reflection journal: Being part of this group has shown me that I’m capa ble of so much more than I used to think. I’ve learned how to really engage with the material  and apply it beyond the math class, even helping others who find these topics challenging. I  never thought I’d say this, but I actually enjoy math now, and that’s something I’m deeply appreciative of. Angie, a Bangladeshi woman, lesbian, and first-generation college student from a socioeconomically disadvantaged background majoring in biology, shared a similar sentiment in her fifth reflection journal: Although I haven’t been in this community for long,  it has built my confidence in math, especially in understanding and applying concepts I  learn, and in believing I cando math…. I felt especially proud when I shared my ideas on the  board, as it made me f eel like I was contributing to the community—something I haven’t had a chance to do in my math classes.

## Discussion and conclusion

This study demonstrates the transformative potentia l of counter-spaces in fostering mathematical learning, sense of belonging, and identity development among histor ically marginalized STEM students. By centering disciplinary rigor alongside identity-empowering practices, the counter-space model addresses critical barriers that these students face in traditional STEM environments. The findings reinforce prior research that emphasizes the importance of equitable, inclusive learning env ironments (e.g., Schoenfeld et al., 2023), while of fering new insights into how such spaces can be tailored for marginalized students in rigorous disciplines like mathematics. The substantial improvements in students’ mathematical understanding and performance underscore the importance of collaborative, non-judgmental learning environments. Unlike traditiona l classroom settings that often emphasize individual achievement and hig h-stakes testing, the counter-space allowed students to engage deeply with mathematics through authentic sense-making, collaborative problem-solving, and reflective practices. Participants such as Yashal and Carlos shared how t heir confidence and understanding in mathematics grew significantly as a result of these practices, highl ighting the need for learning environments that pri oritize both disciplinary rigor and community-based support.

A key outcome of the counter-space was its ability to cultivate a strong sense o f belonging among students who had previously felt alienated in tradi tional STEM settings. Participants consistently des cribed the counter-space as a safe, inclusive environmen t where they could engage authentically with mathem atics without fear of judgment. For students like Lili, Mia, and Ally, who struggled to find a humanizing space wher e they could fully be themselves while learning mathematic s, this sense of safety and acceptance was particul arly significant. The findings highlight that fostering belonging requires intentional community-building efforts, including co-designing community norms, engaging in deep conversations of human experiences in mathematical learning, and continuously affirming students’ diverse ways of thinking, learning, and being. Equally important, the counter-space played a pivotal role in reshaping students’ mathematical identities. By providing a space for students to contribute to collaborative discussions and receive validation from their peers and instructors, the counter-space helped participants like Daniel and Angie recogn ize themselves as capable mathematical thinkers. The opportunity to actively engage in problem-solving and celebrate their intellectual growth significantly enhanced their belief in their  own potential. These findings highlight the import ance of not only offering academic support but also providing opportunities for students to see themselves and be recognized as capable mathematical thinkers, thus promoting positive identity development in STEM fields. This study provides compelling evidence for the int egration of counter-spaces into STEM education, particularly in gateway courses like calculus that often serve as barriers to historically marginalize d STEM students. The counter-space model demonstrates that it is possible to simultaneously uphold disciplinary rigor and promote equity by addressing students’ academic, so cio-emotional, and identity-related needs in tandem. Such models can serve as prototypes for institutions seeking to support historically marginalized students in achieving success and sustainability in STEM pathways.

While the findings are promising, this study is not  without limitations. The data were primarily drawn from a single institution and a relatively small sa mple size, which may limit its generalizability. Fu ture research could expand this work by examining the scalability of counter-space models across diverse institutional contexts and exploring long-term impacts on students’ STEM persistence and care er trajectories. Additionally, while this study primarily focused on students’ experiences, additional research into the role of facilitators and institutional support in sustaining counter-spaces would provide valuable insights into how the se environments can be most effectively implemented and maintained.
