# Epistemic Agency in Teacher Education: A Design-Based Research Study of Students’ Video Reflections on Course Readings

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In higher education, reading research-based literature is a key practice for students and their educators. However, students are newcomers to this practice, and to support them, educators need to find ways to monitor the “silent” processes of student reading. Thus, we employed a sociocultural perspective to design the concept of “video reflections,” allowing insight into students’ reading processes. The analysis revealed that students used video clips to reflect upon their course readings and that, as part of the material, the students oriented toward relations between texts. Such link-making is a possible focus of attention for identifying and supporting epistemic agency among students in future practice.

## Introduction

Higher education is full of opportunities, but it c an be d ifficult for students to interact fruitfully with researchbased knowledge. Higher education involves socializing into a myriad of epistemological practices, which can be considered ways of using, acquiring, and applying k nowledge (Sawyer, 2022). In universit ies, reading researchbased literature is a key practice. Although indepe ndent reading of course literature is a central learning activity, it is largely unobservable to others and can be challenging for teachers to facilitate (Baker et al., 2019). This is problematic because academic discourse has proven d ifficult for newcomers to decode (Heller & Morek, 2 015), and students tend to deprioritize individual readin g and underestimate the effort it takes to fully master a subject area (Gorzycki et al., 2020). Accordingly, new methods for supporting students’ academic reading are needed. Epistemic agency involves the process of becoming a productive participant in knowledge crea tion and knowledge-laden activities (Chan & Van Aalst, 2018; Damşa et al., 2010). In the learning sciences, the notion of epistemic agency was first introduced in relation t o the development of shared cognitive responsibilit y through various forms of reflective activities, such as wri ting summaries of teaching sessions and constructin g structures for sharing and reflecting on knowledge in a learning community (Scardamalia, 2002). Later, Damşa et al. (2010) pursued shared epistemic agency  as a specific form of epistemic agency that emerges  in collaborative activity. Prior studies have shown that epistemic agency is a suitable lens for studying the development of research skills and examining student teachers’ orientations toward knowledge (Heikkilä et al., 2023).

In this paper, we explore epistemic agency in relation to student teachers’ video reflections on their own reading and learning processes. Video has specific affordances that enable students to take more control over their learning processes and has shown to support students’ learning in higher education (Noetel et al., 2021). As part of a design based research study (Barab, 2023), we introduced video-recorded reading reflections in advance of campus activities to support students in adopting a  more active stance to ward their course literature. The video reflections consist ed of 3–5-minute video clips  in which students discussed their individual reading activit ies before sending them to their educators. For the educators, the video reflections provided an opportunity to see the students, hear their individual reflections, and give support. In this paper, we zoom in on the students’ reflections, how they linked different texts and topics from their course litera ture, and how this relates to epistemic agency. We pose the following research questions:

1. What were the main issues the students prioritized to talk about in their video reflections?

2. How do the video reflections on course  literature relate to the identification and support  of epistemic

agency?

Design study Design based research  is characterized by theory-driven innovation projects  in which teaching practice and research interact (Barab, 2022). Here, we aimed to facilitate what can be described as a “best-case scenario” (Roth, 2006, p. 36) by designing and refining video reflections to support students’ academic reading. Based on the study objectives and the theoretical framework outlined above, the following design principles guided the intervention:

• Reflecting on their reading and formulating connections in the course literature can help students achieve

a deeper conceptual understanding.

• Students should be given the opportunity to navigate freely and explore different types of texts.

• Organizing the curriculum and instruction around key concepts and principles provides a framework for

students to understand the subject matter and make connections between different topics.

• Video has specific affordances for “ materializing” ref lection, facial expressions, and the display of

artefacts, allowing people to interact across education’s traditional time and space boundaries. Based on these principles, the students were tasked with uploading video clips prior to each semester meeting to answer the following questions: Tell us about the texts you have read since last time. What did you find interesting, useful, or difficult in the texts? What do you want  from the upcoming lecture? The clips were video-recorded on the students’ own devices and uploaded to the unive rsity’s learning platform, Canvas. The students used their student accounts to log in, and the settings were adjusted  so that only the instructors could view the clips. This decision was made with the students to create an environment where they could comfortably discuss uncertainties without being visible to fellow students.  The use of video reflections  was conducted in two iterations with two different student teacher classes over a two-year period. Some amendments were made between the two iterations. From the start, the educators perceived the video reflections as a means to get to see the students, follow their reading processes, and adjust their teaching to meet the students’ needs. However, it was not evident to students how the videos  influenced the teaching  and campus activities, and they called for more individual feedback. Therefore, in the second iteration, the educators provided individual responses o n the learning management system and explored ways to share video reflections within the class community.

## Participants and data collection

The study was conducted in a teacher education cour se at a Norwegian university among fourth-year students in a five-year master’s program. In the first iteration of the project, 25 student teachers took the course and submitted three video assignments in the fall semester. The next fall, 22 additional teacher education students took the course and submitted three video assignments. Authors Hont vedt and Oddvik were responsible for teaching the c ourse, and author Næss was responsible for the teacher educatio n program. We collected 98 video reflections based on the task presented in the previous section. Of these, 57 were from the first year and 41 were from the second year. These video reflections were thematically coded, and selected excerpts from the films were presented and analyzed in detail based on video analysis (Derry et al., 2018). The number of video assignments is slightly lower than the number of students multiplied by the numbe r of submissions because of some exemptions due to illness and two students not wishing to participate.

## Data analysis

To answer the first research question, we used NViv o 12 to code the 98 video transcriptions into five broad thematic categories. Codes were constructed  inductively through shared coding and discussion among the thre e authors in light of the task and project objectives. These categories were not predefined or considered absolute, and some categories of talk overlapped. The codes were based on easily identifiable types of talk, and the chunking of the data material served as a descriptive overview (Derry et al., 2018). To answer the second research question, we analyzed the students’ enactment of linkages between texts  in detail, which we identified as a rewarding practice. Through the analysis, we aimed to gain a deeper understanding of students’ reading, ways of confronting literature, and seeing different texts in relation to each other. A  better understanding of students’ reflections is important for the further design of video reflections and for supporting students’ reading of academic texts. Analysis The video reflections were intended to provide students with a space to reflect on their reading. Since the task description was open, it was pertinent to analy ze how the students chose to use these minutes of re flection and communication with their educators. Thus, we coded the material to obtain an overview of the video reflections. The categories covered broad types of talk, and the coding was done as a s tarting point for going deeper into the data and detail the students’ reflections. Since all the transcribed material was coded, it was possible to state what percentage of the transcribed talk belonged to each code and to graphically present the students’ emphasis on each category (Figure 1). Talk about how the students perceived their reading  process in terms of time management, level of difficulty, and so on were coded as  talk about reading process (21%). The students’ reflections on connections between the topic and lectures or other formal learning activities at the university were also coded as a separate category (2%). Discussions of texts in relation to practice situations included references to their inpractice teaching (12%). Students’ requests for the upcoming lecture typically addressed topics and concepts they wanted educators to focus on in the coming teaching session (8%). The reflections on text content, “accounts for single text content” (45%) and “reflections and linkages across different text s” (12%), were first part of a larger category but were split into two types of reflection because we found a distinct difference between them. The strategy “reflections and linkages across different texts” was part of the reflections on text content that stood out as more independent and analytic than the accou nts of one text at a time  that tended to be more descriptive. Students tended to employ one or the other throughout their video clips. We subjected the category “Reflections across texts,” which constituted 12% of the total material, to a more detailed analysis, given its particular relevance to our research interests. It enabled students to make connections, identify key concepts, and take a stance more actively. Becoming a competent teacher requires more than acq uiring facts, methods, and routines; epistemic agency involves students’ confronting and negotiati ng their personal ideas with those of others and se tting an ideal for students’ development as professionals. In the video reflections, we found the category “reflections and linkages across texts” to be particularly relevant to students’ development of epistemic agency  because students tended to have an active and critical approach. ## Discussion and concluding remarks

We critically examined the data material to see  how reading scientific literature can be guided and  monitored without conflicting with the goal of supporting students to be productive participants in knowledge work. The first research question was: What were the main issues the students prioritized to talk about in their video reflections? As the task was open, we mapped the video reflections to see how the students used them to thematize different topics and articulate requests for future teaching. T he analysis revealed that only a small portion of the material (2%) consist ed of the students making linkages between course readings and campus teaching. The students emphasized linkages to in-service training to a larger degree (12%). Most students, but far from all, concluded their video reflections by addressing issues that they wanted their educators to pursue in the upcoming teaching session (8%). The main foci of the video reflections were explanations and reflections on text content. When detailing this category, we found that  in parts of the  videos, the students showed a particularly active stance on the course readings. Although all the different categories of talk stood o ut as productive for the students, we found the category “reflections and linkages across different texts” as particularly rewarding and set this as a focus for further analysis.

The second research question was: How do the video reflections on course  literature relate to the identification and support of epistemic agency?  To answer this question, we focused on instances in which the students actively created linkages and discussed theoretical concepts in relation to each other. Through purposeful linkages, the students actively positioned themselves in connection with  the research literature.  We found that video reflections may provide a context in which students recognize course literature not just as sources of content but also as resources for taking a stance and refining their conceptual understanding. The e ducators responded to reflections on the learning management system  and brought students’ reflections into in-class activities. As such, the video reflections related to known challenges regarding  the lack of support from teachers to aid students’ comprehension of academic discourse (Baker et al., 2019; Heller & Morek, 2015). C ompetently navigating research is pivotal to episte mic agency (Heikkilä et al., 2023), and although this  short analysis does not  encompass the full complexity of epistemic agency, it exemplifies how students may enact knowledge and use texts from course readings agentically. We argue that the way video enables reflection to materialize as an object for further analysis has specific affordances of value to students and educators. Stu dents and educators are given opportunities for soc ial interaction in a d ifferent way than in classrooms or auditorium s. Future research could pursue the notion of epistemic agency and how it can be nurtured through the use of video as a means of social interaction.
