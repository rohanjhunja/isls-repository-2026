# “We were learning from each other:” Nuancing Learner-AI Relationships in Assessing Rhetorical Features of Disinformation

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Assessing the reliability of information is one among several potential uses of Large Language Models (LLM). However, little research documents its application for educational purposes. We developed an educational conversational agent (Aide) that aims to help learners identify rhetorical aspects of textual disinformation. In this paper, we document different traits that users attribute to Aide through verbs that they use to describe its actions (such as analyze, teach, and explain). We also describe what attributes users in our sample identify as markers of reliability in Aide’s analysis of text (such as reference to data and technical vocabulary). Finally, we present a concise case study to highlight discrepancies between users’ perception of learning outcomes derived from using Aide (perceived as effective) vis-à-vis their approach to evaluate news stories pre-and post-interaction with Aide (unaltered) We conclude with a reflection on implications for the design of technologies that employ conversational agents for instruction aimed at enhancing learners' ability to assess information.

## Introduction

The emergence of user-friendly applications driven by large language mode ls (LLMs), like ChatGPT's introduction in late 2022, has reinvigorated educat ional debates that date back decades. Sleeman and B rown's seminal work in 1982 on intelligent tutoring systems and the persistent relevance of these discussions, alongside modern concerns such as algorithmic biases (Tlili e t al., 2023) and misuse in educational contexts, hi ghlight the evolving challenges in this field. The application of LLMs for identifying m isinformation in education, despite growing interest, remains underexplored. Key contri butions by Da San Martino et al. (2019) on detectin g propaganda and Jose et al.’s (2023) verification of  its effectiveness underscore the potential yet unt apped in education. Similarly, Chen and Shu’s (2023) review of LL Ms in combating online misinformation points to significant, yet unutilized, educational applicatio ns. This paper extends previous research (Russo & B likstein, 2023a; 2023b; Hong et al., 2023) by exploring  the use of LLMs in education, particularly through the lens of learner perceptions of conversational agents like A ide, developed with OpenAI's API, in teaching about disinformation. Through literature review, discourse analysis, and a case study on Aide, we critically examine its design and educational potential. The findings info rm design considerations and theoretical implicatio ns for leveraging LLMs in educational settings, aiming to foster engaging learning experiences that equip lea rners to critically engage with disinformation.

## Literature review and theoretical framework

In their pivotal work that preceded by decades the popularity of the term chatbot, Salomon et al., (1991) proposed a distinction between the effects of technology on cognition and the effects that workin g with technology can exert on performing tasks. In general terms, and translating to contemporary contexts, having a text automatically corrected by software without attending to the rule s or words corrected would represent work with technology; using artificial intelligence (AI) to learn grammar  rules, the logic behind conjugations, or etymology  of obscure terms would potentially represent the effects of technology on cognition. In non-extreme scenarios, it is possible to imagine benefits from both types of use.

That distinction between with and of (which, for clarity, we henceforth refer to respect ively as cooperative vs determinant effects)  finds a parallel in science and technology studies (STS), especially in the examination of the relationship between human/non-human actors in which we engage while interacting w ith those interfaces. In his discussion of that type of  relationship, Pinch (2010) resorts to the Social Construction of Technology (SCOT) as a lens to understand the impacts of non-humans on groups of humans, especially in terms of the “interpretive flexibility” of those technolo gies. In the case of general-purpose conversational agents, that means their potential uses might include anything f rom well-meaning support in writing to straightfor ward cheating in exams and essays (see Suspicion, Cheating and Bans, n.d., for students’ accounts of using ChatGPT). In the case of material artifacts, that range of us es is signaled through both material and symbolic i ndices; in digital artifacts, the symbolic prevails, and barri ers to avowedly undesired uses become easier to ove rcome. A chatbot’s stated neutrality, f or instance, can be overridden by engineering queri es or changing settings. For example, a chatbot might state that it cannot moral ly assess situations, but one can simply instruct t he system to respond in such a way that it will return moral judgments (see Krügel et al., 2023, for results of an experiment on ChatGPT’s moral advice). In other words, some conversational agents leave a considerable margin of decision-making to the user. Oftentimes, this leeway is “mar keted” as an increased agency, but its effects include a high degree of interpretive flexibility, that is, a user might see a chatbot as a trustworthy source to assess the reliability of information based on output formatting and apparent accuracy (Solyst et al., in press). From another point of view, an actor-network perspective proposes that some technologies become accountable to cover for humans’ lack of ability (or interest) to make moral decisions (Latour, 1992). Users would, according to that view, “delegate morality” to arti facts and the moral criteria embedded in their desi gn. Those features would then be responsible for allowing or forbidding certain courses of action, a decision that is beyond the user’s ability. We understand that it is fair to expect a balanced stance that lies between those two perspectives: designers of technology that is used in educational  settings have a role in anticipating some undesire d uses and embedding blocks to prevent those in the design (a focus on the actor-network perspective). However, it is desired that educational technologies also provide learners  with a certain freedom to experiment beyond the ri gid constraints of purely instructional technologies (t he SCOT perspective). The importance of striking that balance between learning guided by discovery and constraining potential courses of action was already present in Sleeman and Brown’s (1982) collection of articles on intelligent tutors, not the totality of which implied AI. Those considerations about determinant and cooperat ive effects of technology, in optimistic scenarios, are part of the design process of educational techn ologies, but actual usage blurs the borders that se parate the types of effects: as is the case with other artifac ts, when technological systems like conversational agents reach users, people resignify the scripts embedded in the  design, and the users ascribe functions that fulfi ll their needs (Akrich, 1992). Lloyd and Bonventre (2020) argue th at usage blurs the binary division of technical sys tems between the user and technology. This is no different in the case of students using chatbots because learners bring along their repertoires, expectations, and needs, which shapes the conversation in encounters of these factors with those systems, whose efficacy is intensely elevated by narratives put forth by industry. Personification of conversational agents The conversational aspect that was present in old-day tutoring systems described by Sleeman and Brown  (1982) evolved considerably until contemporary educational  chatbots. However, those two groups of technologie s separated by 4 decades, have an identical a ttribute at their core: they are built upon a personification metaphor (Lakoff & Johnson, 1981) in  which we ascribe the values of a person to an inani mate object or concept through discourse. The personification of conversational ag ents starts from their category name: in its conven tional meaning, conversations are held between people; als o, people have agency, and objects do not. The same  is true for tutors: they used to be people responsible for teaching someone else. Anderson et al. (1995) discuss how the “human tutor” analogy “stuck” despite attempts to place less importance on it. Intriguingly, reporting 10 years of their pioneering work with intelligent tutors, Ande rson et al. (1995) found that anthropomorphizing systems, in the cases they describe, led to poorer learning outcomes. Directly or indirectly, the metaphor of personifica tion, if we subscribe to Lakoff and Johnson’s hypothesis,  shapes how we conceive of our relationship with conversational agents.

The attributes we assign to computational systems a re important from an educational perspective, especially regarding instruction that prepares students for epistemically unfriendly environments, i.e., those where discussion is ill-structured, and authority is constantly negotiated (Chinn et al., 2021). Those settings are different from classrooms but are part of the multilayered li ves of learners. In those less controlled environme nts, AI systems may fulfill the role of a trusted peer in e pistemic cognition, a “fundamentally social” activi ty through which individuals form an understanding of how the world works (Chinn & Rinehart, 2016). For example, Tlili et al. (2023) reported that some users felt an emot ional attachment to ChatGPT because of its use as a  “personal assistant.” Outside text-only interactions, assistants like Alexa have been shown to partake in family rituals and forms of family cohesion, such as teasing and shared laughter, although sometimes not working as expected (Beirl et al., 2019). In proposing a “contrastive learning” framework, Ojeda-Ramirez et al. (2023) explicitly distinguish between the learner’s process of thinking and AI’s process of “simply” generating text: the former may  develop metacognitive skills by contrasting their thought p rocess with the technical models that an AI agent f ollows, but the agent’s limitations as a non-human remain observable. In this work, we direct attention to the concepts discussed above: the personification of a conversational agent and how that relates to learners’ perceptions of the role that agent has in their epistemic practices in assessing the reliability of informat ion. More specifically, we seek to understand how learners perceive feature s of a conversational agent designed specifically to act a s a learning support (a determinant effect) as oppo sed to an assistant (a cooperative effect). As such, features of the design emphasized heuristics that learners were expected to assimilate and use in the future. Referring to Pea’s (2004) framework for defining scaffolding, this work seeks to assess (1) our design’s capability to channel and focus on the task of identifying rhetorical features of disinformative texts; (2) its modeling of heuristics that could be generally employed in i nterpreting news stories; and (3) the conversational agent’s fading, such that the learner is capable of executing the task modeled.

## Methods

Aide’s design This paper reports findings from testing Aide, a co nversational agent designed to offer a guided learn ing experience about rhetorical strategies used in textual disinformation. It was developed with Open AI’s API in the Fall of 2022, and several iterations  followed user testing since then. The goal of this conversational agent is to help learners identify instances of rhetorical devi ces employed in malicious discourse, especially in the news. From a cognitive perspective, Aide’s intended outco mes are strongly related to metacognition, that is, learners’ “knowledge about cognitive phenomena” (Flavell, 197 9). In that sense, the objectives are twofold: Firs t, improving learners’ procedural metacognition so the y are aware of effective strategies in assessing in formation; second, conditional metacognition, or assessing the  situations when those strategies are required (Sch raw & Moshman, 1995). Those objectives guide the design o f the learning experience, in which a conversationa l agent and the learner challenge each other to identify examples of disinformation strategies like conspiracy theories, and unwarranted correlation, among other textual rhetorical strategies (figure 1). Figure 1 Example Screenshot Showing Aide’s Evaluation of a False and Misleading News Headline. When evaluating information, the conversational agent emphasizes its “own” heuristics for judging the reliability of a piece of written text. For example, it highlights the lack of evidence to support a claim, or that certain phrases contain politically lo aded terms. Previous research has identified metaco gnitive gains in K-12 students who underwent training that employed a hybrid approach,  meaning that instruction emphasized both cognitive  and metacognitive aspects of reading comprehension (Schuster et al., 2020), an approach that is similar to Aide’s. The use of a conversational agent is further justified by its dialogical, real-time interaction which serves as a proxy for epistemically unfriendly settings (Chinn et al., 2021). Additionally, with the customization and “blackboxing” of prompts, we could program the agent to emphasize aspects of text that are relevant to our broader research goal (instructing learners about linguistic aspects of disinformation).

## Procedures and data analysis

Data for this study came from task-based interviews conducted via Zoom with 7 female students (4 undergraduate, 3 graduate) at schools in the United States. The students, who were between the ages of 19 and 31, were recruited via convenience sampling and were pursuing studies in a variety of disciplines including finance, educ ation, psychology, digital game design, and economics. In the past, task-based clinical interviews have been used by learning scientists to identify how learners contextually employ their knowledge resources (Holbert et al., 2015). We understand that using a task to orient a clinica l interview is appropriate to investigate the dynam ic process that learners go through when assessing the reliability of textual content. Participants were asked to first evaluate two short news articles about US elections that were presented in an otherwise blank text document. The first was false and misleading, using data that showed that the amount of money spent on election campaigns in 2022 was the highest in history, leading to the conclusion t hat money runs the government. The second piece was  real and discussed how Democrats would not have won the presidential election without the support of young voters. After evaluating verbally and with the support of Zoom annotation features, participants interacted with Aide (see Figure 2) for the sequence of interaction; see also Russo & B likstein, 2023, for a thorough description of the design). A week later, they were interviewed again, when t hey were asked to evaluate three other news articles. In both interviews, participants were enc ouraged to use Zoom’s annotation features as they r ead the articles and interact with the prototype. Both interviews were audio and video recorded, and later transcribed. For this article, we focus mainly on the task in the fi rst interview, that is, participants’ interaction w ith Aide, except for the case study (more information below).

Our data analysis was informed by features upon whi ch we elaborate in our literature review above (aspects of the personification metaphor and determ inant vs cooperative effects of technology). We emp loyed Gee’s (2010) discourse analysis tools  to inquire about participants’ interactions with Ai de in the two categories below. That approach to discourse analysis is deeme d appropriate because it considers the social and p olitical aspects of language, which are also germane to the issue of disinformation.

1.  How did participants refer to Aide? More specifical ly, we aimed to go deeper into the personification

metaphor to understand how that played out in participants’ perspectives.

## Metaphors participants used to refer to Aide

“I would probably trust it because it is using a lot of data:” How users describe Aide’s text analysis In referring to the application’s analyzing capacities, users employed vocabulary pointing to a  perception of skillful, rational heuristics for analyzing news st ories. Aide is perceived by users to be using methods gr ounded in reason (represented by sentences like “it is using a lot of data,” “missing evidence,” and “thinking about cause and effect”). In the excerpt below, Emma attributes  credibility and trustworthines s to the app based on its seemingly logical methods: [reads the evaluation of a made-up news story] If this was like a news app, I would  probably trust it because it is using a lot of data [chuckles]. [...] ‘Cause I feel like the platform is trying to say, like, 'I am not that an expert at analyzing the news, but I'm trying to do my best by collecting evidence, and think about incorporating as much as data I can find,’ so the platform sounds credible in analyzing the news. [reads] OK, so that makes it more trustworthy. [emphasis added] In describing her perception of trust in the evalua tion of a news story provided by the app, Emma just ifies her trust in the app on the grounds of it resorting to a rational method (use of data). Then, she offers her interpretation of the app’s text by resorting to a “feeling” (“I feel that…”)—the original user interface text says “I’m not perfect,” which she interprets as “I’m not an expert;” but it  does not mention data at that point. Emma attribut es the app’s credibility to its ability to use data in analyzing the news (“so the platform sounds credible”). She concludes with the verdict that the process makes the app more trustworthy. Other examples in our data also point to similar cases in the form of language employed by the app. One instance is in Charlotte’s explanation that using s pecific terminology (e.g., “unwarranted correlation s,” “emotional triggers”) made Aide’s evaluation more c onvincing. Emma and Charlotte seem to juxtapose the ir previous experiences as undergraduate students with  their experience with Aide to construct a rational e that because the app is using “a lot of data” or specific terminology, its analysis is trustworthy. “We are learning from each other:” Users’ perspective on Aide’s instruction: Together, the actions “explain” and “teach” made up  27% of all explicit references to Aide. We underst and that those references could be grouped to illustrate how  users interpreted their experience in situations w here Aide helped them improve (or had the goal of improving) their understanding. Although there was not one pre valent instructional strategy, users seem to assign a central role to the triad composed of lear ner-interface-bot, that is, were the application a teacher, it would initiate and guide learning. We notice that pattern through the prevalence of phrase constructions in which the triad is seen as the provider of opportunities to learn, enhance learners’ thinking, and even test the learner. Specifically, we note that many times the activity is seen as giving or offering something that will be useful for the user to later  improve their understanding. In the example below,  Samantha describes what she believes she learned from working with Aide: It's a platform that can help me understand and give awareness that I need to be more critical in analyzing information. [...] First [...] the pla tform needs to analyze, then the passage given from the platform for me to analyze. I think it's giving the chance for me to learn with the platform. So I want to give an analysis [...] from the passage, then the platform does the same thing. So I think me and the platform are learning from each other. [emphasis added] In that passage, Samantha first receives something from the app (which helps her and gives her awareness), that is, she initially takes a passive role in the inter action and receives an awareness that the app has and she needs (but does not have, she implies). She signals that she will be using that awareness in the future whil e analyzing information, a point where the agency compass start s by pointing to her: later, she will be the one giving an analysis. In that, she will be followed by the app, which will be doing the same thing and, with that, learning from her. That change in roles is her conclusion in the passage. In our corpus, other examples repeat the same patte rn at different levels and for different purposes. For example, Zenith sees Aide as “an AI that helps me u nderstand more about disinformation.” Here too, the  help from the AI is expected to contribute to the user’s understanding. Later in the interview, she says that the platform is learning “like reCAPTCHA,” that is, using user input for learning that migh t be used in contexts beyond the proposed triadic activity system. That seems to be in contrast with Samantha’s perception, for whom the learning derived from the interaction seems to be task-specific and serves the purpose of analyzing textual information. ## Case study: Samantha’s practices of content evaluation

We opted to report Samantha’s case study due to her  marked optimism toward what she learned during the session. That is, she seemed to hold the perception of a relatively high level of determinant effect of Aide on her ability to evaluate information online. When asked what she learned while interacting with Aide, she m entioned that she gained an ability for “deeper analysis,” a nd that Aide “gave [her] awareness” to “think criti cally and analyze information” and to assess “what makes sens e and what does not make sense.” She said that she gained “understanding, insight, and awareness” on how to approach information. Taken at face value, her words indicate that she believes that, through interaction with Aide, she acquired some metacognitive skills from Aide, especially in monitoring her own performance in facing the task of analyzing  information. We will now proceed to a brief analysis of how she dealt with the task prior to the interview and one week after. Before the interaction with Aide: Samantha states that her strategy for assessing the  news is based on the source and the “grounding theory” that the information is based upon. She summarizes that strategy as being suspicious about “random” news stories (those that do not have sound grounding and that come from unreliable sources). In analyzing the first news story provided by researchers, she notes the absence of sources, especially for a monetary amount presented in the text. She also emphasizes t hat the story reflects the author’s opinions and makes mino r annotations in the text (one related to the suspici ous monetary amount; she does not mention in speech  anything directly related to the other annotations). In the second news story, she underlines a phrase (“must continue”) and points to the sentence containing the term “data” t o justify her claim that that was an opinion piece and that it does not present sources, respectively. In other wo rds, her declarative metacognitive knowledge presen ts sound strategies for dealing with the news.

After the interaction with Aide: For the second interview, she chooses a news piece published by CNN. She reports that she watches the channel frequently  and knows about their criticism of Republicans, es pecially Donald Trump. When asked about the trustworthiness of the text, she says it is “50-50,” because of the channel’s bias “in favor of Democrats and against Republicans.” Again, she emphasizes the opinionated nature of the piece, basing that statement on the lack of sources in the  piece. The researcher then asks about her percepti on of Aide, which she reports is an app that could help her analyze information. She does not annotate the text. Case study discussion: As seen, Samantha’s declarative metacognitive knowledge is mostly the same before and after working with Aide: in both pre-and post-tasks, her declared strategies are similar (judge t he source of the news piece; evaluates the weight of the writer’s opinion; look for references for data mentioned in the text). Those strategies were different from her stated learning at the end of the first interview when she judged t hat she and Aide had learned from each other. In light of the difference between the determinant versus the cooperative effect of technology, she declared a determinant effect, b ut that effect did not transfer at the end of a wee k after the supposedly mutual learning experience. The question  remains whether there was any co operative effect of working with Aide.

## Discussion and conclusion

In this paper, we draw on an STS perspective and on  the conceptual metaphor theory to analyze the use of a conversational agent specifically designed to identify (and instruct about) rhetorical traits of disinformation. Our findings show how learners, in personifying the agent, identified factors associated with their trust in the agent’s evaluation of news pieces (presence of data and technical vocabulary). The data also points to users’ stated belief that the agent was helping them learn. However, further engagement with the data of one participant demonstrated the perception of learning did not necessarily tran slate into changes in her evaluation practices. We draw a few considerations related to those findings.

First, learners’ reliance on data and technical vocabulary, such as “emotional triggers” and “unwarranted correlations,” hints toward the tendency regarding the form of text evaluations, which might overshadow the evaluation of its content. This distinction becomes important as learners tr ansition across epistemically friendly and unfriendly scenarios and have to adapt their ep istemic practices as they move (Chinn et al., 2021). In that sense, we understand that one important design decision for AI-based applications like Aide relates to the presence of a human mediator in the context of use: in a con trolled setting like a classroom (epistemically fri endly), an educator can guide the learning experience and have  the conversational agent highlight form, a possibl y more generalizable feature of the text. The human mediator can then start discussions that put the app’s evaluation into broader perspective, considering less generalizable  ideas —for example, moral judgment. That use would highlight the determinant effects of the app on learners’ metacognitive skills. Conversely, employing an app like Aide in less epistemically friendly environments su ch as social media —that is, seeking a cooperative effect — should be considered with attention to users’ tendency to perceive its evaluation of text as authoritative (as shown by Samantha’s description above).

In other words, despite our initial intention of bu ilding a stand-alone app, we realized that the complex nature of the task, cognitive operations, and interaction made it so that the effect was problematic. This points to the need for careful research on how learners interact with AI conversational agents in general, and agents dealing with dissecting misinformation in particular. In ou r case, it points to the likely need for human medi ation to augment the learning through the app, but also to a void unintended effects such as what was observed i n Samantha—her self-reported perception that there was sense-making, whereas the data indicated otherwise. This could be important for learning scientists trying t o design AI agents for a variety of purposes since the idea of apps that require no human facilitation is seductive. Our research highlights the limitations of “stand-alone” apps and software designed for complex meaning-making and the need for designs of human-AI learning environments that take into consideration, among other factors, the metacognitive effects of those environments on learners.
