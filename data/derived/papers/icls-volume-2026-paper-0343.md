# Investigating, Understanding, and Generating Concreteness in STEM Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
What role concreteness plays in education is a key question, especially in STEM where topics can be considered rather abstract. In this symposium, we wish to highlight several issues and opportunities related to concreteness. First, we offer a critical per spective on the value of concrete representations and the learners who use them. Second, we detail a data-driven taxonomy that aims at fully describing concreteness and abstraction, allowing for a fine-grained understanding of these topics. Third, we illus trate how these lines of research can support practice by presenting an automatic approach to the generation of concrete representations. Finally, we open the conversation to other domains of STEM by attempting to connect discourse in mathematics education to other disciplines. We believe that by aggregating empirical evidence, theoretical contributions, and practical implications, this symposium offers a complete overview of the questions around concreteness in STEM education and supports interdisciplinary and impactful conversations at ISLS. Framing our dialogue Designing and investigating concrete or abstract representations for learning has a long tradition in the learning sciences. Scholars have investigated single representations (Kaminski et al., 2008; Trninic et al., 2020), multiple representations (Rau, 2017), as well as sequences of representations, typically progressing from abstract to concrete (concreteness fading, Fyfe et al., 201 5). However, there remains a set of open questions, going beyond representations themselves. First, for which learner is a certain concrete representation helpful and how is this representation-and this learner’s use of it-valued? Second, what key characteristics make a representation concrete, and how can practitioners design and create concrete representations? Finally, it is not clear how these results translate across STEM domains (Kokkonen & Schalk, 2021). This symposium aims to contribute to this debate by highlighting and elaborating on existing issues as well as offering a taxonomy to situate past and future research on concreteness. Specifically, the first contribution discusses how concreteness and abstraction are valued differently during the learning process. The authors offer an analysis of a student who mostly uses a tangible (and thereby concrete) representation and show how the student’s knowledge is valuable in and of itself. They suggest that this reframing of concrete knowledge as valuable warrants a better understanding of what the learning sciences community means by concrete. The second contribution aims to achieve this goal by offering a taxonomy of meanings currently assigned to concreteness in mathematics education. The authors have found eleven axes between concrete and abstract: five that describe how learners may perceive a certain representation as concrete and six that describe how certain attributes of a concept may be mapped to a certain representation. The third contribution moves from theory to practice, describing how generative AI can help producing scalable concrete representations that are both mathematically accurate and relatable. Indeed, although teachers and practitioners may want to leverage concrete representations in their practice, they might not always have the necessary time and skills, and automated approaches could offer an interesting solution. Finally, as the first three contributions are situated in mathematics education, the fourth contribution opens the discussion up to other STEM domains. Specifically, the authors situate the framework for concreteness 2227 fading in chemistry by Beier and colleagues (2024) in the aforementioned semantic taxonomy of concreteness and abstraction. Overall, this symposium offers various perspectives on concreteness in STEM education: learners, representations, practical implications, and variations across disciplines. We thereby touch on systemic issues (who is considered knowledgeable and what representations are valued), offer a way to structure the discourse about concreteness by being more explicit about its meaning, show how this may be applied in practice by generating concrete representations with AI, and open the curtain for other disciplines to join the conversation. We believe that such interdisciplinary and wide conversations are necessary to fully understand the role of concreteness in education and to learn from each other, and we hope this symposium will foster interesting and impactful conversations at ISLS 2026. Who gets to be “concrete?” problematizing concreteness Rosanne Luu, Sofia Tancredi The way abstraction is conceptualized has considerable implications in what actions and representations are taken up and taught within standard academic instruction. In recent decades, learning scientists have questioned implicit conceptualizations of concreteness and abstraction in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) education. Constructionists have argued for valuing concreteness as a valid mode of thinking alongside abstraction as a move towards embracing epistemological pluralism (Turkle & Papert, 1992). Embodiment scholars have taken up concreteness, conceived of as engaging learners in sensorimotor activity, as the grounding of mathematical concepts (i.e., Abrahamson & Lindgren, 2022; Lakoff & Nuñez, 2000). Educational psychologists have found familia r, physical, and/or sensorial “concrete” representations to be effective tools for scaffolding mathematics instruction (Fyfe et al., 2015). Across these distinct yet related fields of literature, concreteness has been identified as valuable for supporting student learning. Yet, even as several decades of scholarship move to value concreteness, current practices and perspectives position concreteness in ways that often marginalize learners who embrace it. In mathematics education, concrete representations can be viewed as lesser representational forms compared to abstraction, with concreteness conceptualized as a stage to progress away from, rather than as a valuable resource for learning (Luu, 2025). Because of this lesser valuation, students can also be discouraged, in impli cit and explicit ways, from drawing upon concrete, embodied forms of knowledge that would otherwise aid students in their mathematics learning (Takeuchi, 2018). Approaches that value concreteness as critical to development can still treat it as a stage to be overcome. For example, Piaget’s work on the stages of development (Piaget, 1936) has been applied in many domains of research and practice to presume this same kind of progression away from the concrete, where children’s maturation means progression bey ond the kinds of exploration that characterizes the sensorimotor stage. We propose that what is considered concrete or abstract also has implications for who is positioned as knowledgeable (or not) in the classroom. We analyze micro-interactional data to interrogate existing conceptualizations of concreteness within observable evidence of mathematics learning in ecological settings, holding the theory accountable to the practice (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). The data is from a larger project colle cted in the Midwestern United States 2228 examining the use of multiple representations in mathematics. The lesson, titled “Dozens of Eggs,” was intended to help third grade students practice their understanding of equivalent fractions. In the activity, students add together fractions of differing denominators to make a whole, working within denominators that are factors of 12 (e.g., ⅙, ½, ¾). Each student had optional access to a concrete representation, in the form of an egg carton, string, and colored tiles. The string is used to segment the egg carton to change the denominator (initially 12) to represent fractions with denominators of 2, 3, 4, or 6. The teacher instructed students to start with the concrete representation if needed, then to color in their fractions on area diagrams on their worksheets. Figure 1 illustrates the concrete egg carton representation on the left, and an unfilled area diagram worksheet on the right. Our analysis focuses on a three-minute video episode where our focal student, “Ali,” uses the egg carton model as she works through a fraction problem with her partner, “Bea.” The two students are trying to determine whether they could add the fraction 4/6 to 2/12 to equal one whole or less. Ali uses her drawn worksheet to model 2/12, then switches to using her egg carton to find the equivalent of 4/6 in twelfths. Bea has chosen to engage only in the area diagram worksheet. As Ali tries to translate her egg carton model to the drawn worksheet, she argues, “We have to start a new one,” indicating to Bea she doesn’t think adding 4/6 to 2/12 equals less than a whole. Ali gestures to the egg carton model’s four empty spots, saying, “There’s four of them remaining.” Bea and Ali consult Bea’s drawn worksheet, and Bea corrects her that there would be 2/12 as a remainder: “There’s two remaining.” Ali returns to the egg carton model, “adding” in 2/12 with her fingers, and has an “Aha!” moment as she recognizes Bea’s statement as true. Problematizing “concrete” representations First, we problematize the implicit conceptualizations of concreteness and abstraction operating within teaching materials presented to Ali and Bea. The optional precursor egg carton is positioned as “concrete” due to its sensory richness, in contrast to the “abstract” area diagram. The egg carton model does foreground certain qualities compared to the area diagram. The carton offers greater tactile and enactive richness, inviting distinct motor actions with different objects that evoke distinct tactile sensations for the acts of segmenting versus filling in segments. However, the egg carton model does not simplify and is no more context-specific and no more inaccurate than the diagram. The egg carton model is not situated within some highly situated context, such as counting eggs for a cake. We propose the egg carton model is no less symbolic than the area diagram, since the learner coordinates both of these representations with numerical fraction symbols. Thus, when defining abstraction as both the capability to use canonical symbolic represe ntations and the ability to engage with the underlying shared mathematical structure of different problems, these representations are equally abstract. Instead, what positions the egg carton representation as “concrete” in common practice is that it affords the use of certain sensory modalities to engage with the model, modalities that are often deemed invalid and omitted for learning in mathematics education (Tancredi et al., 2022). Given the history of mathematics which privileges visual models as valid and acceptably abstract representational forms (Abrahamson et al., 2019), tactile, haptic, proprioceptive, or vestibular experiences are treated as stepping stones towards later visual abstraction or omitted altogether. In this sense, conceptualizations of abstraction and concreteness reinscribe a modality hierarchy. The ocular is privileged over the sensorimotor, rather than specifying that visual and tactile representations are always encountered through bodily perception–action processes. We argue there is no inherent reason to value the visual model’s affordances over the tactile one for supporting the learning of mathematical concepts. Problematizing “concrete” learners In turn, conceptualizations of concreteness and abstraction in instructional media implicitly position their users differently within classroom practice. We interrogate whether conceptualizing concreteness and abstraction as hierarchical adequately characterizes students’ mathematical strengths. In our illustrative case, the two representations each afforded Ali and Bea the ability to equally engage in mathematical discourse during their fractions problem-solving, regardless of their representational preferences. Ali favored touching and placing strings and physical squares to segment a whole, whereas her peer favored the less stimulating act of coloring in boxes. This does not tell us anything about their relative sophistication in reasoning about fractions. In fact, what it may tell us is simply our learners’ sensory, or personal, preferences. An interpretation of Ali as a more “concrete” learner and therefore less knowledgeable than Bea overlooks Ali’s engagement in mathematical sensemaking during the episode. Ali uses her egg model to conceptualize 4/6 as having a remainder of 4 when the fraction is converted to 12ths, works to connect her carton model with what is represented in her partner’s drawn area diagram, and engages in argumentation with her part ner about the correct answer. The learners in this case do not engage in concreteness as a bridge to abstraction, but rather in a continual dialogue between symbolic and differently-sensorially-situated models. Ali and Bea’s knowledgeability and contributions to their shared 2229 mathematical sensemaking are not limited by binary choices between concreteness and abstraction. Rather, Ali’s use of the “concrete” representation, an action positioned as wholly optional by the lesson, enriches the interaction between her and her partner. Conclusion We problematize the ongoing hierarchization of concreteness and abstraction that positions concreteness as less valuable than or preliminary to abstraction, despite the identified value of concreteness for learning. Conceptualizing concreteness and abstraction as hierarchical fails to adequately characterize the learning-critical affordances of instructional media and the richness of learners’ mathematical activity using these media. We further propose that conceptualizing representations as concrete or abstract can be problematically conflated with how concreteness and abstraction reflect upon the learners participating in the learning activity. Notably, learners with disabilities of ten favor other sensory channels for exploration, such as neurodivergent people’s stimming and blind people’s tactile exploration (Tancredi et al., 2022). When we position sensorimotor action as less valuable, we are rendering learning spaces less accessible to their neurology (Tancredi & Abrahamson, 2024). We must disrupt these notions of sensory periodization (Nolan & McBride, 2015) within individuals’ ontogenetic and microgenetic development if we are to truly liberate concreteness to unfold its full mathematical potential. Disrupting hierarchical conceptualizations of knowing helps challenge deficit discourse s that narrowly define what and who can be successful in mathematics and STEM spaces (Adiredja & Louie, 2020). Conceptualizations of mathematics learning as a sequential endeavor reinforces categories of hierarchy that position students as either ahead or behind, restricting students to narrow pathways of success (Parks, 2010). This manifests in educators’ co-optation of the language of hierarchy to categorize students into “high” and “low” learner profiles, with these labels being leveraged to justify why certain students should be held back in grade level (Myers et al., 2015). As a field, we would benefit from discussions about concreteness and abstraction that have more nuance than current conceptualizations afford, which often position the two concepts as binary and hierarchical. Interrogating concreteness and abstraction will allow us to move as a field towards more expansive ways of knowing and being that, in turn, offer all students richer opportunities to learn. Müller, Keny Chatain The words concrete and abstract are central to mathematics education: both when discussing the nature of the discipline or how to teach mathematics. However, we suggest that these terms are used with various meanings, for example, concrete as relatable, specific, tangible, or useful, and abstract as meaningless, universal, virtual, or irrelevant. Past work argued for a finer-grained and multi-dimensional framework of concreteness and abstraction (Chatain et al., 2023; Trninic et al., 2020), as different aspects of concreteness activate different learning mechanisms (Chatain et al., 2023), and result in different learning outcomes (Kaminski et al., 2008; Trninic et al., 2020). For example, Kaminski and co-workers (2008) showed that concrete (as in real-world) examples impair learners' transfer performance when learning about mathematical groups of third order. However, a follow up study showed that making these examples familiar, another interpretation of concrete, erased this difference (Trninic et al., 2020). Generally, we suggest there is a terminological inconsistency or a verbal dispute in the field of mathematics education, that is a disagreement in the semantics of the terms concrete and abstract (Chalmers, 2011). Addressing verbal disputes is often productive, both as output and process. Generally, offering a more precise and finer-grained way of talking about concreteness can enable a better understanding of the effects of concreteness, but also account for the diversity of experiences and effects tied to concreteness, especially for different learners. In this work, we present a data-driven qualitative analysis of 368 papers to map the semantic landscape of concreteness and abstraction through a proposed taxonomy. Specifically, we do not aim to offer one definition of concreteness. Rather, we are asking what meanings of concrete and abstract are frequently found in mathematics education research articles and how they can be organized to facilitate situating research and designing external representations.

The coders included: two learning scientists with expertise in concreteness and abstracti on in mathematics (R1) and chemistry (R2), one linguist knowledgeable in verbal disputes (R3), and one researcher with a psychology background (R4). The researchers were randomly assigned to the different contexts until all contexts were coded. The coding approach was iteratively discussed and tested until all coders agreed on the task, resulting in the following prompt: Identify the intended meaning of the words concrete and abstract in this text. Accordingly, select in green the words that are more related to concrete and in purple the words that are more related to abstract. If a word is not related to either, leave it blank. Proposed taxonomy Across our corpus, the words concrete, and abstract were either used to describe the perception of or interaction with a representation by a learner (e.g. boring, clear, or tactile), or the mapping between said representation and the underlying concept (e.g. rigorous, formal, or exemplar). Thus, our taxonomy includes five dimensions at the interaction level, and six dimensions at the mapping level (Figure 2). We highlighted a verbal dispute in mathematics education. Using a qualitative data-driven approach over a corpus of 368 articles, we identified eleven axes of concreteness and abstraction, organized at the interaction level between learner and representation (five dimensions) and at the mapping level between representation and concept (six dimensions).

This taxonomy offers a multi-dimensional framework to better situate research on concreteness and abstraction and provides actionable design recommendations for future work. The application of this taxonomy may be two-fold. First, existing research can be situated in this taxonomy to enable comparison between studies investigating abstract-to-concrete trajectories. Second, practitioners as well as educational researchers may use the taxonomy to design new representations intended to elicit specific learning mechanisms. We hope that this taxonomy will further evolve through discussions with educational researchers of differing backgrounds, and we believe that the presentation of the taxonomy at conferences is a crucial part of this iterative process.

How to scale concreteness? Creating concreteness with generative AI Candace Walkington, Jiabao Wen, Robert DiBiano Research suggests that implementing various forms of concreteness, at various places in students’ learning progressions, can enhance students’ understanding of key concepts and ideas (e.g., Fyfe & Nathan, 2019; Hu et al., 2021; Trninic et al., 2020). However, while these important concrete representations are relatively easy to generate for the lab studies showing their effectiveness under various circumstances, in the real world the situation is much more complex. Generating concrete representations to accompany learning tasks is time-and labor-intensive, and this cost disproportionately impacts the development of curriculum materials that arise from low-cost, wide-accessibility models, like Open Educational Resources (OER). OER, which have flexible licensing and may be revised and redistributed (Green, 2017), are a critical avenue for equity and access, as they offer students, schools, and districts a free alternative for curriculum materials. However, research suggests that OER texts tend to have lower quality visuals than their major-publisher counterparts (Howard & Whitmore, 2020). For example, if we examine the widely-used Illustrative Mathematics (2025) OER for 7th grade, not only are many of the visuals low quality or even inaccurate, but there are few concrete visuals offered at all in this curriculum, despite the name being “illustrative.” Looking at the 877 practice problems, only around 160 have visuals (that are not tables). And out of these 160 visuals, only around 20 show an object with any real-world properties or referents that fits the definition of concrete objects being familiar, interesting, practical, or real-life. The remainder are abstract diagrams of plain line segments, symbols, and numbers. Entire units about topics like geometry are taught to students without almost any visual references to the people, places, objects, and events in the real world that students live in and experience everyday. What might the implications of this be for how students come to understand and practice mathematics?

Generative AI (GenAI) has been proposed as a way to improve the quality of OER (Bozkurt, 2023) and also is a scalable way to increase the quantity of visual representations. However, GenAI’s current lack of ability to supply useful visuals to learners has been repeatedly noted as a major limitation of these models. In particular, GenAI lacks the ability to generate visuals in a precise manner, which is important when visuals are both experientially concrete and also communicate numerical or spatial informat ion. For example, Li et al. (2025) present a platform for grades 3-5 called Read Solve Create, that uses DALL-E3 to generate images. However, all images they present show mathematical inaccuracies. An image of 14 snacks accompanies a problem text describing 30 snacks, an image with a pizz a cut into 10 slices accompanies a text scenario about a pizza cut into 8 slices, and a scenario about ¼ of a garden row being planted has an image with only completely-planted rows that already have mature plants. These in accuracies are due to fundamental properties of how diffusion-based image generation models currently work. In the present study, we test a novel method for generating concrete 2232 visuals to accompany learning tasks, using ControlNets, which can instruct an image-generation model to use particular aspects of an inputted model with great precision. Our current approach has three steps. First, we prompt GPT-4o (i.e., Chat-GPT) to use the matplotlib library in Python (a simple programming language) to render our desired math diagram, giving instructions in natural language about how the diagram should look (e.g., “Draw a half of a sphere that has a circumference of 20 cm.”). GPT-4o generates code and automatically feeds it to an online version of Python, outputting the image that Python renders. Second, the image goes into pre-processing, generating a depth map, outline map, segmentation map, etc., depending on the type of ControlNet that is going to be being used. This preprocessing is handled through a few settings and button clicks in the Flux interface (Black Forest Labs, 2025), which builds on the widely used, free Stable Diffusion GenAI engine (Stability AI, 2025). Concerns have been raised about image-generating AI utilizing human artists’ work in their training sets without permission. Stable Diffusion 3 (the current version) gave artists the opportunity to opt-out of being included in its training set and is thus more ethical than many of the alternatives. Third, the pre-processed image is fed into the ControlNet in Flux with a second prompt used to specify what illustrative features to layer on top of the Python diagram (e.g., “Create a mathematics textbook illustration of half an orange. Design features a spherical orange, cut in half, sharp clean cut.”). The final illustrative diagram is then output. Comparing concreteness across disciplines: Is it possible? Charlotte H. Müller, Martina A. Rau The landscape of research on concrete and abstract representations can be confusing. On the one hand, research on the impact of concrete and abstract representations is highly diverse: some studies focus on variations of single representations (Kaminski et  al., 2008), whereas others examine concreteness fading with sequences of representations that range from concrete to abstract (Fyfe & Nathan, 2015). On the other hand, the learning content that is represented is equally diverse, ranging from algebraic gro ups in mathematics (Kaminski et al., 2008) to electromagnetic induction in physics (Kokkonen et al., 2022) and Boyle’s law in chemistry (Beier et al., 2024), to name a few. This raises the question of how well insights from research on concrete and abstract representations translate across disciplines.

Specifically, disciplines may differ in the way in which a concrete representation relates to an abstract representation (Kokkonen & Schalk, 2021). For example, in math, a concrete representation is often highly analogous to the abstract representation, meaning that students may conceptualize the target concept in terms of the concrete representation. A pie that misses one quarter is more concrete than the numerical term ¾. Simultaneously, one whole pie maps directly to 1 and dividing the pie into four equal slices and eating one maps to ¾. By contrast, in the sciences, concrete representations are often important by themselves (Kokkonen & Schalk, 2021). Consider Boyle’s law as an example. Boyle’s law describes how volume and pressure relate to each other in  the context of a gas in a closed space. Specifically, if the volume of this space decreases, but the amount of gas remains the same, the pressure will increase, and vice versa. Representing Boyle’s law with a simulation of particles in a closed space with changing volume is considered more concrete than the corresponding equation. However, the role of the simulation goes beyond being a stepping stone to understanding the equation, but it is part of and is valued within disciplinary discourse itself (for a discussion about the value of concreteness in math education, see Luu & Tancredi, this symposium). Therefore, in contrast to math, the learning goal in the sciences often is not confined to understanding the abstract representation but also involves understanding how concrete representations relate to the abstract representations (Kokkonen & Schalk, 2021, Beier et al., 2024). This symposium contribution explores whether, despite these disciplinary differences, concrete and abstract representations such as the ones of Boyle’s law can be situated within a semantic taxonomy developed for mathematics education research (Chatain et al., 2025, also this symposium). Specifically, we will situate the integrated framework, which Beier and colleagues (2024) recently offered for concreteness fading in chemistry, within this semantic taxonomy. In the following, we will refer to Chatain and colleagues’ (2025) taxonomy as the semantic taxonomy and to Beier and colleagues’ (2024) framework as the integrated framework. The semantic taxonomy conceptualizes concreteness across eleven axes. Five of these axes describe a student-dependent form of concreteness. We will focus on the other six axes that lie on what the taxonomy terms the mapping level, which describes how abstract-concrete mappings are concept-specific, and by that very nature discipline-specific. Specifically, these axes conceptualize concreteness as situatedness, experientiality, specificity, informality, approximation and physicality (see Figure 3).

Using the semantic taxonomy allows us to examine to what extent math-specific findings on concrete-abstract representations may map to a different domain in the sciences and vice versa. Doing so also allows us to highlight gaps in the research on concrete-abstract representations. For practitioners, this offers new insights into 2234 how they candraw on findings related to concreteness irrespective of discipline to inform their teaching. In the following, we will first give a short introduction to the integrated framework for concretizing abstract visual representation in chemistry (Beier et al., 2024). Then, we will explore how the four levels that this framework entails, the symbolic, submicroscopic, macroscopic, and experience level, map to the semantic taxonomy described earlier (Chatain et al., this symposium; Chatain et al., 2025). We thereby open the conversation from math education to other scientific disciplines.

Beier and colleagues’ integrated framework Beier and colleagues (2024) recently published a suggestion for the implementation of concreteness fading in chemistry. They drew on the multilevel framework of chemistry by Johnstone (1991) that categorizes chemical representations into three main levels: the macroscopic level of observable scientific experiments, the submicroscopic level that describes this experiment in terms of atoms and molecules, and the symbolic  level of equations and formulas that allows to make predictions about this experiment. This framework highlights the need in chemistry to understand a phenomenon on different levels and to be able to connect between these levels. Beier and colleagues (2024) added a fourth level to these three: experience. This level highlights the need of students to relate new information to their everyday experiences. In the example of Boyle’s law, the experience may involve feeling suction when holding a syringe onto a finger while reducing the volume. The symbolic  level includes the equations associated with the law. The submicroscopic  level may be a simulation of the particles’ behavior within the syringe. Finally, the macroscopic level may entail a discussion of how the representations on the submicroscopic and symbolic level can explain the function of a human lung (Beier et al., 2024). Figure 3 Semantic Taxonomy The experience level (green, right most), macroscopic level (purple, second right most), submicroscopic level (orange, second left most), and the symbolic level (blue, left most) as conceptualized in the integrated framework by Beier et al. (2024) situated in the semantic taxonomy by Chatain et al. (2025).

As described earlier, the fading component of concreteness fading is not the same in the chemistryspecific integrated framework as in mathematics education, as the goal in chemistry is not to understand an abstract representation, but to explain a phenomenon on multiple levels. Still, while the final learning goal differs, the question remains whether we can situate the representations on the different levels within the semantic taxonomy to be able to compare findings across disciplines. To highlight a path towards answering this question, we situate the four levels described in the integrated framework in the semantic taxonomy (see Figure 3): The experience level is concrete in every sense of the mapping level described in the semantic taxonomy. In the example of Boyle’s law, the syringe is physical and situated in the real world. Students can interact with it, 2235 that is experience it, the experience is playful and informal  as well as highly specific. Finally, the experience is not an exact measurement or analytic procedure, rather, it is approximate. The symbolic level is abstract along most mapping dimensions. The equation P=k/V that describes Boyle’s law is less physical than the syringe in the sense that it is not a physical object, but also not technological as it is still an analogue representation. It is also much less experiential than the syringe and the equations are not situated in the real world, that is they are decontextualized. The level is highly formal  and general. Finally, it is highly analytical as the students can calculate the pressure or the volume exactly. The submicroscopic level as conceptualized in the integrated framework is situated between the experience and the symbolic  level in the sense that it is not concrete along all axes (see Figure 3). While the simulations of Boyle’s law include particles represented as balls and are thereby situated in the real world (although less so than the experience level), they are not experiential and physical. However, as the representation depicts a specific number of particles in a specific space, it is more specific  than the symbolic  counterpart. The simulation is less formal than an equation as one would report the equation and not the simulation in a publication. Yet, it is more formal than the experience, as it is part of disciplinary discourse. Similarly, it is less accurate than an equation that would result in a specific number, but more precise than the vague bodily experience. Beier and colleagues (2024) did not conceptualize the macroscopic  level as a representational level but as a level that applies the symbolic  and the submicroscopic  level to a macroscopic problem. This differs fundamentally from abstract representations in mathematics education and illustrates again the difference in how disciplines conceptualize relations among representations (Beier et al., 2024, Kokkonen & Schalk,  2021). Nevertheless, we can situate this level in the semantic taxonomy (see Figure 3). The level is more abstract generally than the experience level since the macroscopic level does not only entail the macroscopic phenomenon, but also the scientific explanation. For example, a macroscopic problem could require explaining the function of the lung with Boyle’s law. This level is concrete in the sense that it is highly specific (the equations of the symbolic level are applied to a specific macroscopic phenomenon) and situated  in the sense that the symbolic  and submicroscopic representations are applied to a real-world problem. The level is less experiential  than the experience level, but more than the submicroscopic  or symbolic level. It is rather formal  and analytical in the sense that students aim to explain a phenomenon scientifically. The level is a bit physical, since the problem may be analyzed with experiments, and without the use of much technology (although this changes with more and more laboratory equipment including technology).

From this analysis, we conclude that the experience level as well as the symbolic level of the integrated framework are most aligned with what the semantic taxonomy considers as concrete and abstract representations in math education. For the other two levels, it will be worth exploring whether the findings along the axes that are most clearly either abstract or concrete translate across disciplines. For example, the submicroscopic level is abstract on the physicality axis, and the macroscopic  level is concrete on the situatedness axis (see Figure 3). However, this is out of scope for this contribution.

In this contribution, we situated an integrated framework for concreteness fading in chemistry (Beier et al., 2024) within a wider semantic taxonomy of concreteness in mathematics education (Chatain et al., 2025). In doing so, we re-illustrated the complexity of such a transfer between disciplines as well as a possible common ground to design and analyze the effectiveness of representations. Specifically, we pointed towards transfer opportunities, such as the symbolic level in chemistry to abstract represen tations in math education or the macroscopic  level to concrete representations along the situatedness axis.

Among possible future avenues is the designing of sequences of representations in different disciplines that differ in specific dimensions of the semantic taxonomy and exploring whether the same cognitive mechanisms are activated, and similar inferences arise.
