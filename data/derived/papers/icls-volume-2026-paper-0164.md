# Transdisciplinary Teaching and Learning with Physical Computing: Teachers’ Perceptions

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Transdisciplinary experiences where learners traverse different disciplines to solve problems are critical to preparing students for dealing with complex real-world problems in the future. However, transdisciplinary learning remains scarce in schools. Physical computing, which engages students in creating tangible interactive artifacts with the Internet of Things devices, can potentially serve as an anchor for transdisciplinary learning. In this study, we investigated in-service teachers’ perceptions of transdisciplinary learning with physical computing. Our analysis of 19 teachers’ questionnaires, observation notes of a brainstorming session, and focus group interviews revealed that teachers overall hold a positive feeling of transdisciplinary learning, although most of them viewed transdisciplinary learning as interchangeable with multi/interdisciplinary learning. Teachers’ perceived challenges in implementing transdisciplinary learning in classrooms included a tension between allowing students to struggle and preventing them from being overwhelmed with frustration, as well as confusion about the need to support students in realizing disciplinary connections.

Over the past decade, there has been significant interest in supporting transdisciplinary learning experiences for students that span boundaries of different disciplines to take a holistic approach to education (Takeuchi et al., 2020). While there has been significant work done to this end around integrating computing and STEM (Grover et al., 2020) and integrating arts and STEM (McKinley-Hicks et al., 2022), it was still noted in a recent White House report (National Science Technology Council, 2022) that schools remain largely organized by content area, and that most STEM instruction remains heavily discipline focused. This siloed approach to education can result in disconnected and sometimes inconsis tent learning experiences that leave students ill-prepared for realworld job tasks that require traversing and applying knowledge and skills across disciplines in a meaningful way to solve problems (Stevens et al., 2005).

Physical computing emerges as a compelling anchor of transdisciplinary learning because it naturally involves working across multiple STEM disciplines through a hands-on, design-centered approach that attracts and engages students (Finch et al., 2021). Yet, a lot of the existing work on physical computing takes place in informal settings or in computer science (CS) classrooms (Escudero et al., 2013; Juškevičienė et al., 2021). Little is known about how non-CS teachers perceive using physical computing to foster transdisciplinary learning in classrooms or how to best support these teachers in creating transdisciplinary experiences. In this paper, we report a qualitative study that investigates 19 in-service teachers’ perceptions about transdisciplinary learni ng with physical computing. We analyzed teacher questionnaires, observation notes, and focus group interviews to investigate: (1) What are teachers’ thoughts and understandings about transdisciplinary learning?, and (2) What are teachers’ perceived challenges of implementing transdisciplinary experiences in classrooms? Rationale Transdisciplinary learning For decades, researchers have been calling for integrated approaches to teaching STEM in schools, so that students are prepared for solving complex problems in the workplace that demand workers to connect, integrate, and apply knowledge across disciplines (National Academy of Engineering & National Research Council, 2014). The literature has identified a spectrum of disciplinary integration (Drake, 1998; Finch et al., 2021): Multidisciplinary or interdisciplinary experiences focus on combining or merging the learning of content from different disciplines, and transdisciplinary experiences emphasize holistic learning goals where learners are engaged in self-driven reflections on and expansions of disciplinary boundaries to solve problems. For example, a teacher may want students to learn about how computation can control growth conditions for plants by learning about biology, computation, and data science content related to the topic. Students are presented with a real-world problem of designing an automated greenhouse that helps plants grow. They are so engaged in the problem that 1302 they use previous science knowledge to acquire new knowledge from another discipline (e.g., they may have known the importance of temperature for plant growth and are encouraged to learn how to program sensors and microcontrollers to control temperature), traverse back and forth across disciplines (e.g., after learning how to use sensors to collect temperature data, they recognize a need to visualize the data and learn data science topics), and select and interweave disciplinary practices that are beneficial to solve the problem. In this case, the envisioned learning experience is transdisciplinary because the context of the problem drives students’ transdisciplinary inquiry, whereas in multi/interdisciplinary experiences, students learn content from each discipline either at separate times or together and the greenhouse context is used as an example context for better understanding of the disciplines (e.g., students are asked to program the temperature sensor to reinforce their CS knowledge). To enact such transdisciplinary learning experiences, teachers need to allow students more freedom to decide on their learning and the knowledge needed to find solutions to authentic problems (Costantino, 2018; Edelen et al., 2019). Unfortunately, there are few exemplars of transdisciplinary-driven projects that teachers can use as models in the K-12 classrooms (National Science Technology Council, 2022). Physical computing Physical computing covers the design and realization of connecting the digital world to the real world and allows students to develop concrete, tangible artifacts and solutions that require integrated STEM practices that blend technological design, scienti fic investigation, and engineering design to solve a complex real-world problem (Kelley & Knowles, 2016). It is engaging because it involves students in working with real, tangible devices, and could engender deep interest and spark creativity (Sentance et al., 2017). Although the field is new, Hodges et al.

(2020) summarized the major benefits of physical computing, e.g. increasing motivation, providing natural

connections to the real world, and promoting collaboration. We argue that physical computing naturally enables a transdisciplinary project exemplar. By engaging learners in consciously moving  back and forth between the virtual (software) and physical (hardware) worlds, physical computing promotes the formation of new knowledge structures by integrating new ideas gained from the experience with existing conceptualizations. In recent years, the cost of electronics has plummeted, lowering one of the significant barriers of access to physical computing tools and resources. Most empirical studies on physical computing, however, have taken  place in informal settings or CS/technology classes with a strong emphasis on programming and CS concepts (e.g., Theodoropoulos et al., 2018). We are fascinated about the potential of leveraging physical computing to promote transdisciplinary learning and seek to bring it into nonCS/tech classrooms as well, to support deeper disciplinary integrations.

## Physical computing context: Smart greenhouse

The work presented here is an early part of an ongoing longitudinal project where teachers are supported to iteratively design and implement transdisciplinary learning with physical computing. This paper is the first step of the work where we capture and l egitimize the voices of teachers and will inform the design of the subsequent teacher professional development program. To ground our description of the findings, we first outline the physical computing context, i.e., the Smart Greenhouse, upon which teachers’ perceptions of transdisciplinary learning will be built.

The Smart Greenhouse is an automated tabletop greenhouse that allows users to control and experiment with environmental conditions to grow plants. It includes a suite of low-cost plug-and-play electronics to collect data and act based on the data, and multiple laser-cut frames and 3D-printed parts for assembling and attaching electronics to the greenhouse. The electronics include (1) micro:bit, a pocket-sized computer designed for beginners to learn how to program and create interactive projects; (2) Crowtail shield, a  grove extension board that features socket connections; (3) temperature and humidity sensor and light sensor; (4) actuators including an OLED screen to display the real-time data collected by sensors, light bulbs and fans controlled using relays that can b e automatically turned on and off based on the data collected by sensors, and a LED strip that can be programmed to show different colors based on environmental conditions data. Programming with these electronics was conducted through Microsoft’s MakeCode, a web-based programming platform featuring color-coded blocks and a switch to JavaScript/Python so that users can see the text-based code behind the blocks. Figure 1 shows an example greenhouse and part of a sample code to collect data from sensors. The Smart Greenhouse serves as a holistic goal to spike and sustain students’ transdisciplinary inquiry.

## Research design and data analysis

1303 A qualitative research approach was utilized to investigate teachers’ perceptions of transdisciplinary learning experiences with physical computing and their perceived challenges of bringing transdisciplinary experiences into classrooms. In total, this study involved 19 teachers (7 high school science teachers, 10 middle school science teachers, and 2 district STEM Curriculum Supervisors; 4 males and 15 females) who were recruited to participate in the larger longitudinal project. The study participants were experienced classroom teachers, with an average of 17.3 years of teaching experience.

Figure 1 Smart Greenhouse and Sample Code This study used data collected through three sources: (1) an individual teacher questionnaire administered to all teachers before a weeklong institute in Summer 2025 where they learned about how to work with the physical computing devices of the Smart Greenhouse, (2) observation notes of a session on Day 1 of the summer institute where teachers brainstormed and shared ideas about transdisciplinary teaching and learning, and

(3) focus group interviews with teachers (3~4 teachers per focus group) on Day 5 of the institute after teachers

became familiar with physical computing. In total, five focus group interviews were conducted, each lasting approximately 50 minutes. The first two data sources included open-ended questions, “What do you think transdisciplinary learning is?” and “What do you see as the benefits and challenges of using a transdisciplinary approach of teaching STEM in your classroom?”. The third data source (focus group interview) asked the same questions with a specific context of physical computing or the Smart Greenhouse, e.g., “ What do you think transdisciplinary learning with the Smart Greenhouse is?”. The data analysis used grounded theory methods (Charmaz, 2015). We began  with a close examination of teacher questionnaire data and categorized emergent themes related to teachers’ understanding of transdisciplinary learning and the challenges they envision. Afterwards, we used the same method to analyze the focus group transcripts and observation notes. Finally, we consolidated the themes to determine what themes were dominant in the data.

The data analysis revealed three major themes. The first theme was derived mainly based on the analysis of the teacher questionnaire and observation notes. The remaining  two themes were based on the analysis of teacher focus group interviews.

Theme 1. Viewing transdisciplinary learning as interchangeable with multi-or interdisciplinary learning All teachers believed in the benefits of transdisciplinary learning, although most of them viewed it as interchangeable with multi/interdisciplinary learning. For instance, some teachers explicitly explained that transdisciplinary learning is a multidiscip linary approach that  “includes all areas of curriculum including art, music, math, writing, science, computer and social studies and helps students learn best” (Teacher MF, questionnaire). One teacher brainstormed that transdisciplinary learning is “a teacher means of bringing in or emphasizing Math, when it arises in science, or using tech to teach science”  (Day 1 observation notes). Only three teachers provided examples of transdisciplinary learning that suggested the traverse across disciplines. For example, a teacher described how students can work on a transdisciplinary project around ocean pollution. He envisioned that students would “first learn about the pollution in a science class, then learn about the historical context of pollution in their social studies class ”, afterwards the students would need to “interview with the community members on a field day, learn how to design a solution in an engineering design or capstone class, and finally attend a government [meeting] to propose their solution [to] local officials” (Teacher AW, 1304 questionnaire). Overall, this finding shows a positive feeling yet mixed understanding of transdisciplinary learning among teachers.

Theme 2. A tension between allowing students to struggle and normalizing student frustration in transdisciplinary learning with physical computing Teachers expressed concerns about how to balance between allowing students to struggle and providing immediate support when their physical computing projects do not perform as expected. On one hand, they unanimously agreed that using plug-and-play electronics and block-based coding would significantly lower the barrier to student participation, yet the complexity involved in working on physical computing could potentially overwhelm students and lead to frustration and loss of interest, “if they have code that's not working and they try something and it still doesn't work and they try something else, it still doesn't work. And they have no success in 55 minutes, then they'll reach a point of frustration” (Teacher KH, interview). On the other hand, teachers also recognized the importance of allowing students the freedom to explore and fail, because failure can “make them critically think about it instead of you giving them the answers or what they perceive as the answers. So giving them those challenges to really think about what they're doing and redesign or re-devise whatever they're doing," (Teacher SB, interview). They felt that learning how to deal with failure and troubleshoot is indispensable to transdisciplinary learning and advocated developing activities such as prefacing students with teachers’ own failed experiences and providing students with necessary resources to support them, e.g., “ I'll preface it when we're building one, say mine fell off 300 times and I was really frustrated. So just know and let them know that they will fail and it's nothing what you're doing”  (Teacher CA, interview). Theme 3. Confusions about the need of supporting students to recognize disciplinary connections during transdisciplinary teaching and learning Most teachers called for activities such as reflection or creating visuals to support students in realizing what disciplines they traverse during teaching. For instance, a district curriculum coordinator explained that one challenge in her district was that students do not recognize the connections between subjects and the greenhouse can allow students to have "aha moment” where they could witness how the temperature sensor is physically controlling the on and off of the relay/switch. She felt that supporting students to make disciplinary connections is“critically needed in our education system because we grew up making those connections ourselves, and these kids' minds are in some ways compartmentalized" (Teacher DS, interview). However, three high school teachers and the other district STEM curriculum supervisor questioned the necessity of incorporating these activities into transdisciplinary experiences. The other curriculum supervisor reflected on what happens in the real-world workplace to question whether such activities of supporting disciplinary connections are crucial, e.g., “In real life... no one says, use what you learned in this subject or this subject or this subject to solve this problem. Use whatever you've got. Just solve the problem. And you do integrate them.” He further acknowledged that these activities were beneficial, but “in the practice of it, I'm not sure that is critical” (Teacher JS, interview). A chemistry teacher also added her observation that high school students may have developed some preliminary ideas about the connections between disciplines (chemistry and math) without being explicitly taught, “in my class…many different areas of math are used in [things] like investigating data or comparing to find the line of best fit. They already know that math and chemistry are connected” (Teacher GR, interview).

Transdisciplinary experiences are critical to preparing students for dealing with real-world complex problems in the future. However, realizing such approaches is challenging in formal education settings (National Academy of Engineering & National Research Council, 2014). Physical computing provides a potentially exciting anchor for creating transdisciplinary learning environments in classrooms. In this qualitative study, we investigated teachers’ perceptions of transdisciplinary teaching and learning with physical computing. Our findings show that teachers hold an overall positive feeling of transdisciplinary learning, even though they often misunderstood transdisciplinary learning with multi/interdisciplinary learning. After learning about physical computing, teachers envisioned more concrete challenges when implementing transdisciplinary learning, including a tension between allowing students to struggle and providing support to reduce student frustration, and a confusion about whether it is necessary to s upport students to recognize disciplinary connections. Overall, our study reveals valuable insights on teachers’ existing views of transdisciplinary learning with physical computing and sheds light on what kind of teacher support and professional development may be necessary to support teachers toward bringing transdisciplinary experiences into classrooms.
