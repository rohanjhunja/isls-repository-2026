# From Words to Networks: AI-Powered Knowledge Graphs for Understanding Collaborative Learning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents an analytics tool that leverages knowledge graphs to make visible the complex, collaborative discourse in Knowledge Forum. The tool extracts relational triplets (subject, predicate, object) from student posts using a hybrid pipeline combining LLM-based, transformer-based (Triplex), and rule-based (spaCy) methods. Extracted triplets are normalized and visualized as interactive graphs, where nodes represent concepts and edges represent relationships. A case study on the salmon life cycle demonstrates how the visualization surfaces central ideas, thematic clusters, and knowledge gaps, aligning with key Knowledge Building principles. By transforming unstructured discourse into an explorable network, the tool supports students and educators in tracing idea development and identifying opportunities for deeper inquiry.

Knowledge Building, as proposed by Scardamalia and Bereiter (2006), is a pedagogical approach that prioritizes collaborative discourse to create knowledge and advance collective understanding. Knowledge Forum, a digital platform designed to support collaborative knowledge creation, enables students to post, discuss, and refine ideas through text-based contributions (Scardamalia, 2004). However, analyzing the complex, interconnected nature of student discourse to assess knowledge advancement remains a challenge for educators. This paper introduces a novel knowledge graph-based analytics tool that transforms student discourse into structured, visual representations, making the progression of ideas explicit and actionable. The concept of knowledge graphs was popularized by Google’s Knowledge Graph in 2012, which shifted search from keyword matching to entity-based understanding (Uyar & Aliu, 2015). Knowledge graphs are distinct from other data representations because they integrate information into an ontology and apply reasoning to derive new knowledge (Uyar & Aliu, 2015). These knowledge graphs represent information as a network of entities (nodes) connected by relationships (edges), typically in the form of triplets (subje ct, predicate, object), enabling complex queries and reasoning (Ehrlinger & Wöß, 2016). For example, in the sentence “Salmon live in the Humber River,” the triplet (Salmon, lives in, Humber River) captures the relationship (i.e., lives in) between the entities “Salmon” and “Humber River.”  These graphs emphasize semantic relationships, allowing for flexible integration of diverse data sources and supporting advanced analytics. Knowledge graphs have gained prominence in various fields, including artificial intelligence and data integration, due to their ability to model complex, interconnected information (Ehrlinger & Wöß, 2016).

In education, knowledge graphs are increasingly used to represent and analyze structured knowledge, such as in personalized learning, curriculum design, and concept mapping (Abu-Salih & Alotaibi, 2024). Prior work has constructed knowledge graphs through e xpert-curated resources or automated pipelines relying on single-method approaches, including rule-based or machine learning techniques. While these methods are effective in controlled settings, they often struggle with informal, diverse student discourse,  leading to trade-offs between precision, coverage, and adaptability.

Our approach builds on these traditions by integrating complementary paradigms within a unified pipeline. By combining LLM-based, transformer-based, and rule-based methods, the system leverages their respective strengths to produce a more robust and semantically rich representation of discourse than single-method approaches. Beyond methodological integration, this tool extends existing Knowledge Building analytics by transforming discourse into an explicit network of relationships, rather than relying solel y on frequency-based or note-level analyses. This enables the identification of idea connections, gaps, and misconceptions, making the structure and evolution of collective knowledge more visible and actionable. Triplet extraction Knowledge graphs can serve as a tool to visualize the collective knowledge constructed by the community, revealing how students’ ideas are connected and evolve over time. To construct knowledge graphs from student discourse, our tool extracts relational tr iplets (subject, predicate, object) using three complementary methods. These methods are applied to capture different types of linguistic patterns in student writing, improving the completeness and reliability of extracted relationships. ## Method 1: LLM-based triplet extraction

We leverage a transformer-based Large Language Model (LLM) to automatically extract relational triplets— subject, predicate, object —from unstructured student discourse. LLMs, built on parallelized attention mechanisms, excel at capturing long range dependencies and nuanced contextual cues in text (Vaswani et al., 2017). This makes LLMs particularly suited to identify diverse linguistic constructions and implicit semantic links that simpler, rule-based extractors often miss. For our implementation, we employ Zephyr-7B, an open-source GPT-style language model developed by Hugging Face.

Such LLM-based extraction offers substantial flexibility: it readily adapts to varied sentence structures, captures subtle semantic relations (e.g., cause– and–effect, part–whole), and is robust against noisy or colloquial language typical of student discourse (Ateia et al., 2025). However, its performance depends critically on prompt clarity and can incur higher compute costs compared to rule-based methods. To mitigate these challenges, we combine LLM outputs with complementary extraction strategies (see Methods 2 and 3), ensuring both precision and coverage in our knowledge graph construction.

## Method 2: Transformer-based triplet extraction with Triplex

To complement our LLM-based approach, we employ Triplex, a transformer-based model explicitly optimized for knowledge graph construction. Whereas general-purpose LLMs excel at language understanding across diverse tasks, transformers like Triplex are fine tuned to detect structured relations and domain-specific entities, delivering higher precision and efficiency for large-scale discourse analysis (Chen et al., 2023). Triplex, like other transformers, leverages self-attention mechanisms to process all tokens in a text sequence in parallel, modelling long-range dependencies and complex contextual interactions (Vaswani et al., 2017). These properties make transformer architectures a reliable tool for tasks like Named Entity Recognition (NER) and relationship extraction, where precise identification of entity boundaries and their links is critical (Eberts & Ulges, 2019). Through fine-tuning, Triplex learns to:

1. Recognize predefined entity types (e.g., animal, location, life stage) with high recall and precision.

2. Classify relations using a fixed ontology of predicates (e.g., lives in, eats, is part of), ensuring extracted

triplets align with our domain taxonomy.

3. Handle noisy text, such as informal student posts, through robust tokenization and context-aware

inference.

Triplex’s domain specialization affords faster inference and higher extraction accuracy compared to more general LLMs. By limiting its relation vocabulary, it reduces noise and ensures consistency with our knowledge graph schema (Chen et al., 2023). When c ombined with the flexibility of LLM-based extraction and the precision of rule-based patterns (Method 3), Triplex helps us achieve a balanced pipeline that maximizes coverage while controlling false positives.

## Method 3: POS tagging and dependency parsing with spaCy

For this method, we used spaCy, which is a high performance, open source Python library offering non-destructive tokenization, part-of-speech (POS) tagging, and dependency parsing, among other NLP components (Honnibal et al., 2020). POS tagging assigns each token a grammatical category (noun, verb, adjective, etc.), providing the foundation for identifying core sentence elements like subjects and objects (Meftah et al., 2018). We begin by preprocessing each student's note with spaCy’s sentence boundary detection, segmenting the discussion into coherent sentences before normalizing punctuation and expanding contractions to enhance tagging accuracy. To focus on factual statements, the tool then filters questions by analyzing linguistic features, including:

• Wh-words: Words like “what,” “where,” or “how” that often signal questions.

• Auxiliary Verbs: Sentences beginning with verbs like “Do,” “Is,” or “Are.”

• Sentence Structure: Patterns where the main verb is linked with a subject and auxiliary verb, typical in

questions (e.g., “Is salmon black?”).

Each sentence is then run through a lightweight English language model provided by spaCy, to produce token-level part-of-speech tags and a full dependency parse tree that reveals the grammatical structure of the sentence—such as which words are subjects, o bjects, modifiers, and nominal subject (nsubj) of a verb that, in turn, governs either a direct object (dobj) or a prepositional phrase (prep) introducing another noun. For instance, spaCy parses “Salmon swim up rivers” by marking “salmon” as the nsubj of “swim” and linking “rivers” through a prep→pobj chain, yielding the triplet (salmon, swim up, rivers). SpaCy’s rule-based method provides high precision on grammatical constructions, effectively capturing triplets from standard sentences. 440 Integration and visualization The pipeline preprocesses and segments student notes, followed by parallel triplet extraction using LLM-based, Triplex, and spaCy methods. Extracted triplets are then normalized, deduplicated, and merged into a unified dataset through rule-based aggregatio n and conflict resolution. Each method contributes complementary strengths—spaCy provides syntactic precision, Triplex ensures schema consistency, and LLM-based extraction captures implicit and context-dependent relations —resulting in a balanced approach that improves coverage, accuracy, and robustness. In this integrated framework, spaCy anchors the graph in structurally precise relations, while the other methods enrich it with semantic and domain-specific connections, enabling a more comprehensive representation of idea progression in Knowledge Forum.

The resulting dataset is stored in a central CSV file and visualized using Pyvis as an interactive graph, where nodes represent concepts and edges represent relationships. Edge types are color-coded, and users can explore the graph by adjusting layouts, inspecting source sentences, and filtering relations, supporting the identification of idea pathways, central concepts, and under-explored areas in student discourse. This knowledge graph visualizes the semantic relationships derived from student discourse on the salmon lifecycle. The graph consists of nodes representing key concepts (e.g., "Salmon," "Egg," "Fry," "Ocean," "River") and edges indicating relationships (e.g., "lay eggs in," "grow in," "eaten by"). The graph highlights central concepts and reveals a hierarchical and interconnected structure of knowledge. The "Salmon" node serves as a hub, with radiating connections to lifecycle stages, environmental factors ("River," "Ocean," "Waterfall"), and biological interactions ("Eaten by Bear," "Eaten by Eagle"). The central node, "Salmon," also connects to multiple stages of the lifecycle ("Egg," "Fry," "Smolt," "Adult"), reflecting the cyclical nature of the topic and the students' understanding of its progression through stages (labeled as Stage 1 to Stage 4). This centrality underscores “salmon” as the focal point of discourse, while the density of connections around lifecycle stages (e.g., "Egg" to "Fry" to "Smolt") indicates a strong grasp of sequential development. Notably, the graph also captures external influences, such as predators ("Bear," "Eagle") and human-related factors ("Fish Hook," "People"), suggesting that students are integrating ecological and anthropogenic dimensions into their understanding.

The graph also highlights patterns in knowledge construction. For instance, the cluster around "Egg" (connected to "Lay eggs in," "Hatch," "River") shows that students associate early lifecycle stages with specific environmental contexts. Similarly, the "Ocean" node connects to "Smolt" and "Adult," reflecting an understanding of habitat transitions. However, some nodes, such as "Adipose Fin" and "Salmon Jump," are less connected, indicating potential gaps in discourse or areas where students' knowledge is l ess developed. These peripheral concepts may require targeted instructional strategies to better integrate them into the broader conceptual framework.

## Implications and discussion

The knowledge graph provides actionable insights into student discourse by making relationships between ideas visible. Prominent connections (e.g., Salmon → River → Egg) indicate strong foundational understanding, while sparsely connected concepts (e.g., Adipose Fin) highlight opportunities for targeted scaffolding. By surfacing misconceptions (e.g., incorrect relations), the graph supports peer critique and iterative refinement, enabling a more transparent and collective knowledge building process. It also facilitat es structured classroom activities, such as identifying gaps, guiding discussion, and supporting data-informed instructional decisions. By representing discourse as a network, the tool allows educators and students to identify central ideas, emerging themes, and underdeveloped concepts, providing a holistic view of learning. The integration of LLM-based, transformer-based, and rule-based extraction methods enables both broad semantic coverage and structural precision, supporting robust analysis of collaborative inquiry. Grounded in Knowledge Building theory, the graph operationalizes key principles in a concrete and visible way. It supports Idea Diversity  by representing each distinct contribution as a node, allowing multiple perspectives and ideas to coexist and be explored. Community Knowledge emerges as individual contributions are aggregated into shared structures, making collective understanding explicit. The principle of Rise Above is facilitated by revealing higher-level conceptual patterns and connections across id eas, encouraging learners to synthesize and move beyond isolated facts. Symmetric Knowledge Advancement is supported by enabling students to view, build on, and refine one another’s contributions through the evolving network of relationships. In this 441 way, the tool functions as both an analytic lens and a pedagogical scaffold, making the dynamics of collective knowledge construction visible and actionable for learners and educators. Figure 1 Interactive Knowledge Graph of Salmon Lifecycle Discourse The empirical example presented here is illustrative, demonstrating the analytic affordances of the approach rather than providing a full evaluation. Accordingly, this work is best understood as a design-oriented contribution. Despite its potential, classroom application depends on effective teacher facilitation and integration into lesson plans, underscoring the importance of involving teachers as co-designers. Teacher perspectives and classroom-based feedback were not systematically included, representing  a key limitation. Future work will incorporate co-design with teachers, qualitative user studies across diverse contexts, and evaluation of both technical validity (e.g., reliability and coverage of extracted triplets) and pedagogical impact. Further development will also focus on improving adaptability across topics and supporting flexible, context-sensitive use in applied settings.

This study demonstrates the feasibility and educational promise of leveraging knowledge graphs to analyze and support Knowledge Building discourse. By automatically extracting relational triplets from student posts and rendering them as interactive networks, the tool makes the invisible architecture of collaborative inquiry explicit and actionable. Students and educators gain real time insights into how ideas emerge, intersect, and evolve — insights that were previously obscured by the volume and complexity of textual contributions. The integration of multiple NLP methods ensures robustness across varied linguistic styles, while the Pyvis visualization offers an intuitive interface for both teacher and learner engagement. Ultimately, by coupling advanced graph  analytic techniques with foundational Knowledge Building theory, this research paves the way for smarter, more responsive learning environments that transform communal idea advancement from an implicit promise into an explicit, navigable reality.
