# Unmasking Visible and Invisible Challenges Faced by Neurodivergent Students in Higher Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We investigated the barriers experienced by neurodivergent students across multiple universities. Employing a conceptual framework grounded in diversity, equity, inclusion, neurodivergence, and socio-emotional viewpoint, we identified (a) systemic barriers to accommodations, (b) reasons for social isolation, and (c) the need for faculty training as themes. We offered recommendations, including streamlining accommodation processes, implementing faculty development programs, and creating peer support systems. The findings offer a pathway toward more equitable and inclusive environments for neurodivergent students, advancing academic success and well-being.

## Introduction

The number of neurodivergent learners entering academia has steadily increased, yet many institutions have made little to no progress in providing adequate support to these individuals. In particular, s ome institutions lack meaningful resources  for students with Autism, ADHD, and other cognitive  conditions. We aimed to raise awareness of this often-overlooked population within diversity, equity, inclusion, and belonging initiatives and to help educators take the first step toward implementing effective support measures. College poses substantial academic and social challenges for neurodiverse individuals  (Dwyer et al., 2022). Research (Clouder et al., 2020; Spaeth & Pearson, 2023) indicates that these students face unique learning challenges due to their cognitive differences and negative feedback from their social environments. For example, individuals with ADHD are estimated to have received 20,000 more negative signals by th e age of 10 compared to their neurotypical peers (Jellinek, 2010). These negative signals often take the form of diffi culty following instructions, struggles with time management, or pe rceived inattentiveness (Bukhari et al., 2024; S. E. Connolly et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). From an early age, students with ADHD, for example,  may repeatedly hear phrases such as “pay attention,” “stop interrupting,” or “why can’t you just focus?”  contributing to a cycle of diminished self-esteem and heightened self-doubt. These early experiences of discouragement ca n shape neurodivergent students’ perceptions of their abili ties and self-esteem (Bacon & Bennett, 2013; Holthe & Langvik, 2017).

The consequences of these repeated negative experie nces persist into the college years, where neurodivergent students often struggle with fear of  judgment and a lack of institutional understanding  (Evans et al., 2023). In higher education, this manifests in lower rates of requesting accommodations, greater f eelings of imposter syndrome, and heightened anxiety about mee ting academic expectations  (Evans et al., 2023; Geyer, 2021; Syharat et al., 2023). Additionally, research  suggests that neurodivergent students are at highe r risk for mental health challenges, including suicide, compared toneurotypical students (Geyer, 2021). Addressing these issues requires targeted interventions and support systems in higher education to foster inclusive and  equitable learning environments for both neurotypical and neurodiverse students. Despite growing awareness of the challenges faced by neurodivergent  learners (Clouder et al., 2020), there remains a lack of  comprehensive, evidence-based strategies to support them effectively.  This study represents a broader investigation conducted across multiple universities to bridge this gap. By using questionnaires and interviews with students who have official diagnoses and those  who  self-identify as neurodivergent, this study seeks to understand the specific needs and experiences of these learners. The findings aim to reduce stigma, enhance teaching and learning practices, and create a more welcoming and inclusive environment for neurodivergent students.

This research question was addressed: What barriers and challenges doneurodivergent students encounter in higher education learning environments?

Our inquiry leading to this research The concept of unmasking resonates strongly with post-structuralist thought  (Peters & Burbules, 2004), particularly in its focus on uncovering the inequities embedded within institutional structures and discourses (e.g., Darvin, 2015; Kaomea, 2003). Post-structuralism emphasizes how power operates subtly through norms  and language, often marginalizing certain groups (Gill, 1984; Stoddart, 2007) while presenting a façade of equity and inclusion. In the context of higher education, inst itutions may strive to create inclusive environment s through diversity initiatives (Barnett, 2020; Sadlier, 2024). Yet, the experiences of neurodivergent students may reveal significant challenges that occur behind the scenes. By sharing their struggles, these students can unmask systemic barriers, social stigma, and the inadequacies of ex isting support systems, calling into question the n arratives of inclusivity and accessibility that dominate academi c discourse. Drawing on their voices, this research shows the dissonance between institutional intentions and liv ed realities, offering a critical pathway toward ge nuine inclusion and equity.

Through a post-structuralist lens, this research examines the stru ctural mechanisms that worsen the inequities. Rather than assuming neurodivergent stu dents should adjust to existing academic and social  norms, we argue for reexamining those norms. By making visible the hidden struggles  of neurodivergent students, we call into question whether higher education is trul y designed for all learners  or whether it continues to privilege neurotypical ways of thinking, learning, and interacting.

## Conceptual framework

This study’s conceptual framework integrates diversity, equity, inclusion, neurodivergence, and the socio-emotional perspective to provide a holistic understanding of the challenges faced by neurodivergent students in higher education. These three components were chose n because they each contribute to a critical dimens ion of the neurodivergent student experience: structural b arriers (diversity, equity, inclusion), cognitive and behavioral differences (neurodivergence), and social-emotional well-being (socio-emotional learning). While broad fields on their own, we focus on the specific aspects of each  that are most relevant to our study: systemic accessibility (diversity, equity, inclusion), cognitive diversity in learning (neurodivergence), and social belonging (socio-emotional perspective). Together, these dimensions allow for a more comprehensive analysis of how neurodivergent students experience and navigate higher education. The critical role of social identities and individua l differences in shaping experiences and opportunit ies is recognized by diversity, equity, and inclusion initiatives (Gallegos et al., 2022; Ng & Kalrsfeld, 2018). While differently abled individuals bring unique  perspectives and strengths, they also face significant problems in diverse situations (Austin & Pisano, 2017). This highlights the importance of creating equitable and inclusive settings that nurture well-being and unlock potential. Inclusion is essential in understanding how institutional structures shape student experiences. In the contex t of neurodivergent students, we focus on instituti onal accessibility and inclusion efforts, which examine whether policies and accommodations are designed to meet the needs of neurodivergent learners or if they merely reinforce existing systemic inequities. While inclusion frameworks often address race, gender, and socioeconomic disparities, neurodiversity remains an underexamined dimension within these initiatives (Gallegos et al., 2022). We highlight  how policies, faculty practices, and campus culture either support or marginalize neurodivergent students. This framework particularly focuses on neurodivergent individuals, examining their behavior, decisionmaking, and social interactions through insights from neuroscience and cognition. By exploring diversity-related experiences such as empathy, prejudice, and stereot ype threat, this perspective deepens our understand ing of the interplay between social, cognitive, and cultural factors in learning (Cole et al., 2011; Hodson et al., 2018; Plaut, 2010; Schachner, 2019). Neurodivergence encompasses a spectrum of cognitive  differences, including ADHD, Autism, and other conditions that shape how individuals process information, interact with their environment, and engage in learning. Prior research has shown that n eurodivergent students often struggle with rigid ed ucational structures (Butcher & Lane, 2024)  that fail to accommodate learning  differences, executive functioning differences, and sensory sensitivities. Additionally, societal expectations often require neurodivergent individuals to engage in masking behaviors, suppressing traits to conform toneurotypical norms (Pryke-Hobbes et al., 2023), which can lead to exhaustion, anxiety, and academic  disengagement. By centering neurodivergence within  this framework, we challenge the assumption that all stu dents should adapt to conventional academic models and instead advocate for learning environments that recognize the strengths and needs of neurodivergent individuals. Another essential component of this framework is the socio-emotional viewpoint, which highlights the importance of interpersonal interactions, social co ntext, and emotional intelligence  (Berger et al., 2011; Lane & Smith, 2021). Emotions significantly impact how we think, behave, and interact with others (Lane & Smith, 2021; Scholl, 2013). This paradigm emphasizes the need for safe and supportive environments where individuals can express their feelings, build meaningful connections, and develop healthy relatio nships (Delahunty et al., 2014; Taneja-Johansson & Singal, 2021). It also addresses the socio-emotional dimensions of inclusion by considering how societal structures and systematic injustices shape emotional experiences and overall well-being (Peralta et al., 2022).

While each of these dimensions provides a unique le ns, they are deeply interconnected  (see Figure 1). Institutional policies (diversity, equity, inclusion) influence both learning accessibility (neurodiver gence) and social belonging (socio-emotional perspective). For example, if accommodations are difficult to access (diversity, equity, inclusion), neurodivergent students may struggle academicall y (neurodivergence) and experience heightened stress and isolation (socio-emotional perspective). This integrated approach provides an understanding of neurodivergent student experiences, moving beyon d individual-level challenges to examine how structural, cognitive, and social factors collectively shape their academi c trajectories. By applying this framework, we aim to uncover not just the barriers neurodivergent students face, but also the institutional changes necessary to create a more inclusive and supportive higher education environment. ## Participants

Participants in this research study were required to be at least 18 years old, with an average participant age of 23. A total of 64 individuals from various states in the United States participated in the survey. Among them, 53 had an official diagnosis, while 11 self-identified as neurodivergent. The most commonly rep orted conditions were ADHD and autism spectrum disorder. Thirty-eight identified as female, 22 as nonbinary, three as mal e, and one participant chose not to disclose. Most participant s were enrolled in four-year institutions, with a significant number attending liberal arts colleges.

## Data collection

A survey was designed by the researchers to explore the experiences of neurodivergent students, with a focus on social, academic, and institutional dimensions. The survey included open-ended comment sections  inviting participants to share insights into their lived experiences as neurodivergent students in higher education, describe the support they received, detail the relationships they formed, such as those in clubs or support groups, and provide any additional comments in free form. The survey contained four thematic sections: (1) De mographics and neurodivergent identity (e.g., diagnosis/self-identification, i nstitution type, gender); (2) Academic experiences and challenges (e.g., ease of accessing  accommodations, faculty support, coursework barrie rs); (3) Social experiences and campus culture (e.g., peer interactions, stigma, sense of belonging); (4) Mental health and well-being (e.g., anxiety related to academic settings, emotional support structures). The survey was distributed through the discussion forums of EDUCAUSE and the POD Netwo rk, two well-regarded organizations in teaching and learning. It was also shared on social media by members of these networks. Data collection was conducted using the RedCap plat form. EDUCAUSE and the POD Network are respected organizations with strong reputations for  advancing quality in higher education. Their exten sive and engaged communities provide valuable platforms for exchanging innovative ideas. Both organizations all ow members to post research surveys for com munity participation and broader dissemination, whi ch facilitated sharing this study’s survey information.

Three participants consented to participate in interviews, each lasting approximately 45 minutes. Sample questions from the interviews included: “What has your educational experience been like as an individual?” “Have you disclosed your condition to your friends, facul ty, or fellow students? Why or why not? ” “Does disclosing your neurodivergence make things easier for you socially or academically?” and “What has your social experience been like at your institution?”

## Data analysis

An open and axial coding strategy was employed for t he open-ended responses  and interview transcripts  to identify concepts, categories, and relationships re levant to the study framework (Strauss & Corbin, 19 98).All open-ended responses were read multiple times to gain a deep understanding of the dataset. During this phase, we noted initial impressions and recurring ideas aligned with the study’s conceptual framework. One researcher conducted line-by-line coding to identify meaningful concepts and ideas within the text. Codes were developed inductively  to ensure that they emerged from the data rather than being impose d by preexisting assumptions.  Sample codes  included emotional states (e.g., anxiety), acceptance, time-consuming processes, (in)accessible help, challenges stemming from university regulations, and dissatisfaction with social life. Once the coding was completed, we engaged in discus sions to refine the codes, ensuring that they were consistently applied across the dataset and accurat ely captured participant experiences. Any discrepan cies in coding were resolved through discussions and consen sus-building. If conceptually similar codes emerged, they were merged to prevent redundancy; for example, codes related to bureaucratic obstacles and complex paperwork were grouped under the broader theme of barriers to accommodations. To determine which codes were significant, the rese arch team employed multiple criteria. First, frequency was taken into account, and a code was flagged as significant if at least 20% of participants referenced it across either survey responses or interview tran scripts. In addition to prevalence, conceptual rele vance played a role in determining significance, with particular attention given to codes that aligned with the study’s conceptual framework. Even if a code appeared less frequently,  it was retained if participants described it in particularly strong emotional or impactful terms.

Following the coding process, the research team engaged in theme development by grouping codes into broader categories. The identification of themes was informed by both data-driven and theory-driven approaches. In the data-driven approach, commonalities in participant respo nses were used to establish initial themes based on recurring experiences and concerns. For instance, codes related to financial and procedural barrier s were gathered under the initial theme Barriers to Accommodations. Similarly, codes describing feelings of loneliness, judgment, or stigma were initially categorized under Social Isolation and Exclusion. This stage allowed the identification of central themes. In the theory-driven approach, the codes were mapped onto the study’s conceptual framework, ensuring that the final themes were not only reflective of participant experiences but also  grounded in existing theoretical perspectives on inclusion, neurodivergence, and socio-emotional learning. The final themes were selected based on their abili ty to capture the most critical challenges faced by neurodivergent students. Each theme was supported by multiple data points across different participants to ensure reliability.

## Findings and discussion

Theme 1. Neurodivergent students face significant barriers in accessing accommodations due to financial, procedural, and privacy challenges For many students, the transition to college can be both exhilarating and difficult (Christie et al., 2008), particularly for those who  require official accommodations because of disabili ties or mental health conditions (Connor, 2012). Unfortunately, securing these accommodations ofte n involves a difficult and tedious process, complicated by financial burdens, lengthy procedures, and concerns about privacy and autonomy. The financial cost of obtaining accommodations is a major barrier for many students. Specialized equipment, assistive technologies, and additional r esources can be prohibitively expensive. Moreover, obtaining a formal diagnosis, which is required to access accommodations, poses a substantial financial barrier, especially when insurance does not cover diagnostic costs. These unexpected expenses can strain students ’ already limited finances, further complicating their ability to access necessary support. One student explained: “Neurodiversity is mostly ignored at my school, which tends to focus solely on BIPOC [Black, Indigenous, and People of Color]  diversity; help is only available if forced by docu mented accommodations through [the] disability office. This is problematic as adult dia gnosis of Autism Spectrum Disorder is prohibitively expensive and is not covered by insurance.” Securing accommodations often requires students to navigate a complex and bureaucratic system. This process typically involves meeting with coordinator s or advisors, discussing personal challenges, and presenting documentation, a daunting and repetitive task that must be completed every semester. For many students, this can lead to significant stress, detracting from their academic focus. One respondent highlighted these challenges: “I did not pursue accommodations when I transferred to my second institution either. To do so would have required meeting with the ADA [Americans with Disabilities Act] coordinator, then my PCP ($$$)  (indicating the cost associated with visiting a Pri mary Care Physician), then with the ADA Coordinator and my advisor, followed b y each of my instructors. This process would have to be repeated each semester.” In seeking accommodations, students are often requi red to disclose confidential information about thei r disabilities or mental health conditions. While such disclosures are necessary to obtain support, they can feel like an invasion of privacy. Concerns about potential stigmatization or discrimination may deter students from sharing sensitive information (Eccles et al., 2018), further limiting their access to help. As one participant noted: “It has been difficult to receive help without sacrificing my privacy and autonomy.” Theme 2. Neurodivergent students face social isolation and exclusion due to communication challenges, institutional barriers, and misunderstandings Neurodivergent students often feel singled out by t heir peers due to a range of issues that limit thei r ability to engage socially and feel integrated into the group.  These students frequently struggle to make friends  outside of the classroom. One participant shared: “Sometimes the way that my peers talk about neurodiv ergence and disability in general makes me very uncomfortable and feel singled out.”.

Difficulties initiating and maintaining connections  can arise for neurodivergent students  (Cox et al., 2021), as they may struggle to read social cues, communicate nonverbally, and keep conversations going. Finding friends who share similar interests can be challenging, contributing to feelings of isolation. As one student noted: “There is definitely a difficult aspect with socializing and making friends outside of classes.” Higher education institutions often operate within a framework that unintentionally excludes neurodivergent individuals. For example, rigid atte ndance policies may penalize neurodivergent student s who require breaks or accommodations due to their uniqu e needs, adding stress and leading to further exclu sion. One participant explained: “I often feel stressed and overwhelmed by the amount  of (doable) work that I have which inhibits my ability to do things in a timely fashio n, I have difficulties consuming excessive amounts of written content, online content, and paying attention adequately in class.” Neurodivergent students may also miss out on valuable social interactions. As one student shared: “There are also a large number of students who dismiss people with executive function as lazy, who think autistic people are being rude when they refuse to participate in the draining process of masking, and other instances like this that educ ation could help alleviate. I am tired all the time and I don’t know how to express this to people who do not have my lived experience.” These challenges often lead to increased social anx iety as neurodivergent students fear judgment and struggle to understand social norms. Their nervousn ess may prevent them from interacting with classmat es or participating in social activities.

The masking process, which involves suppressing neurodi vergent traits to conform toneurotypical norms, can lead to exhaustion  and suicidal ideation (Miller et al., 2021; Pryke-Hobbes et al., 2023). While institutions may claim to foster diversity and acce ssibility, our research suggests that these student s experience systemic barriers that remain invisible to those who do not share their lived experiences. ## Implications for research and practice

The findings underscored the critical need for higher education institutions to evolve in supporting neurodivergent learners. One of the most pressing issues highlight ed by this study is the systemic barriers that neur odivergent students encounter when seeking accommodations. Fin ancial costs, bureaucratic processes, and concerns about privacy and autonomy make it difficult for these students to access the support they need. Therefore, institutions should streamline accommodation processes and ensur e they are less intrusive and more accessible. This could involve creating clearer, simpler pathways for obtaining accommodations (T. Connolly et al., 2024) and providing financial assistance to reduce the costs associated with formal diagnoses. Policies should also be revised to protect the privacy of neurodivergent students (Kelly, 2024), ensuring that they feel comfortable seeking support without fear of stigmatization or discrimination.

D espite their supportive intentions, many faculty members lack the training to e ngage neurodivergent students in the classroom. This study calls for formal training programs focused on neurodiversity to be integrated into faculty development curricula. Training should  focus on recognizing cognitive differences and creating inclusive teaching practices that cater to a wide r ange of learners. Faculty need to be equipped with strategies to design flexible courses, foster inclusive classroom  environments, and understand the unique challenges  faced by neurodivergent students.  It is also vital to raise awareness about neurodive rgence in academic communities (Welton & Dettmer, 2023) to ensure that faculty are not only receptive toneurodivergent students’ needs but are proactive in seeking out resources and support for these students. Faculty should be encouraged to eng age in discussions about neurodiversity  (Accardo et al., 2024), collaborate with disability services, and foster an environment of empathy and inclusivity.

Social isolation emerged as a key theme in the find ings, with neurodivergent students reporting difficulties in forming social connections and navigating campus life. Higher education institutions must prioritize fostering social inclusion by creating safe spaces where neurodivergent students can connect with peer s with similar experiences. Peer support groups, mentorshi p programs, and social clubs tailored toneurodiver gent individuals should be developed and promoted.  Interventions that educate all students about neurod iversity and the importance of empathy and inclusion could reduce social exclusion and foster a sense of belonging on campus. Peer-led workshops or seminars focusing on neurodiversity (Brownlow et al., 2023), for example, could go a long way in normalizing neurodivergent experiences and a ddressing common misconceptions. This would not onl y support neurodivergent students but also develop a more inclusive campus culture that benefits all members. While this study provides valuable insights into the experiences of neurodivergent students, there is still much to explore. In addition to revealing barriers that neurodivergent students have in higher education settings, the study can advance socio-emotional viewpoint (e.g., Berger et al., 2011; Lane & Smith, 2021)  and inclusion and belonging studies (e.g., Gallegos et al., 2022; Ng & Kalrsfeld, 2018). Future research should study specific strategies that can support  neurodivergent students  in different institutional settings. Longitudinal s tudies examining the academic success and well-being of neurodivergent students w ith access to specific support interventions, such as peer mentoring or faculty training, would provide a clear picture of the impactful practices. More research is needed into the intersectionality o f neurodivergence with other identity factors such as race, gender, and socioeconomic status. By exploring how these multiple aspects of identity shape the experi ences of neurodivergent students, researchers can better understand the unique challenges marginalized groups face within the neurodivergent community.
