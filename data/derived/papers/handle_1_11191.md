# ‘A Black Woman in Geoscience, is Like a Unicorn’: Black Women’s Educational Experiences in The Geosciences

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Empirical research concerning social identity in geoscience education has primarily examined single categories of social difference, centering analysis of either race or gender (Mattheis et al., 2019). To expand the current scope of geoscience education research, I use Black Feminist Epistemology (Hill-Collins, 2022) to center and explore Black women’s educational experiences. Using autobiographies and semi-structured individual interviews with Black woman geoscientists, I explored educational practices and relationships that played a critical role in shaping their experiences in geoscience. Black women participants described their position in and navigation geosciences education as in/hypervisible, non-human, mythical objects using the metaphor of the Lone Black Unicorn. In this paper, I center the narratives of Lone Black Unicorns as a disruption of the persistent underrepresentation of Black women’s voices in academic sensemaking.

## Introduction

To be a black woman in the geosciences is to be a unicorn-rare, beautiful and leaves people to wonder. It also means standing out, being a lone pioneer at times and it gets tiring carrying the weight of other's hopes. But also builds strength. It is inspirational. (Cali, Geoscience Autobiography) Before I met Cali, she wanted me to know about her mythification – or what it means ‘to be a unicorn: rare, beautiful, and to [be] inspir(ing) others to wonder.’ As she responded to my national call for Black w oman geoscientists who were willing to share their educa tional journeys, the unicorn became the central org anizing construct of her autobiography. There, Cali wrote about what it meant to be ‘a lone pion eer” who ‘some times gets tir[ed] of carrying the weight of others’ hopes’. I had to know more, so I invited Cali to a follow up interview and she gracefully accepted. We spoke for hours..

Cali was the first of the participants to bring the  language of the Black woman geoscientist as a unic orn to my attention, but she was not the last. As I spoke with other Black Women geoscientists they would explicitly equate Black womanhood to being a unicorn, and/or i nvoke the central organizing tenets of the Lone Bla ck Unicorn mythology as Cali explained: isolation, rac ialized-gendered weight, hypervisibility and invisibility, beauty, and success in the face of being  made to feel out of place.  The unicorn metaphor is  both accurate and false. The purpose of this (re)telling is simple —  historically marginalized people tell stories as a way to unsettle what is known and create spaces for imagining liber ation from oppr essive norms (Hill-collins, 2022; Toliver, 2021, Mckittrick 2020). In order to unsettle the ta ken for granted discourses surrounding Black woman geoscientists as unicorns, I write to explore two research questions: (1) What does the discourse of  “Lone Black Unicorns” reveal about  Black women’s positioning in and experience of  geoscience education?, and (2) How do Black Woman geoscientists navigate and agenticaly respond to the Lone Black Unicorn discourse?

## Background: Black women in geoscience education research

Geoscientists investigate  a broad variety of topic s including the distribution of natural resources, impacts of pollution on our atmosphere and waterways, earth’s changing climate, and even debates concerning the time and location of the origin of humanity. Though the fiel d incorporates a diverse array of methodologies, and disciplinary knowledges, it has remained the least demographically diverse of all science engineering mathematics and technology (STEM) fields (Bernard and Cooperdoc k, 2018). For decades governmental and disciplinary organizations have contributed millions of dollars to fund and stimulate the diversification of the field yet despite these efforts, little has changed (Callahan et al., 2017; Marin-Spiotta et al., 2020). This demographic exclusion is reflected in geoscien ce education research trends. Historically, scholar s in geoscience education have attributed the lack of demographic diversity to a leaky geoscience education pipeline (Marín-Spiotta et al.,2020). The use of this pipeline meta phor draws on disciplinary ways of being to describ e social phenomena: natural resources (in this case  humans) are taken from the earth, sequestered and r efined. If some of the yield – in this case predominantly women and people of co lor– is lost in this process, that is to be expected. A growing body of literature interrogates  the pipeline metaphor as insufficient for assessin g the historical and systemic construction of the "pipeline", and posit that the discipline itself merits interrogation (i.e.: Stokes et al., 2015; Marín-Spiotta et al., 2020). These, more recent, articles ask: if by design, only some scientists persist through the pipeline – those who conform to dominating social norms — then what is the pipeline for? In order to improve demographic marginalization in the  geosciences, our disciplinary research needs theori es and methods that are attuned to the social, historical,  and ideological contributions to student sensemaki ng in geoscience education. Pursuing alternate ways of be ing in geoscience education research ha s the potential to denaturalize the limited scope of what it has  meant to become a geoscientist. In a response to the need for new disciplinary know ledge,  Mattheis and colleagues (2018) proposed intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1991) and Black Feminist Epistemology (Hill-Collins, 2022) as guiding frameworks for geoscience education research concerning social  identity.  While some empirical studies have answe red the call for an intersectional approach (i.e.: Mattheis  et al., 2018;Núñez et al., 2020;  Mattheis et al.,  2022), extant studies in geoscience education that use these frameworks include few, if any, Black women’s perspectives. Black women remain hidden, even in the empirical studies that employ theories for and by Black women. The emergent push toward alternative ways of being in the geosciences need to elevate Black women’s experiences. The  absence of Black women in geoscience education  research,  is an injustice on two accounts. First, Black women are students in geoscience settings but  their approximate absence in the literature erases  their presence. The inability to render minority g roups as subjects is likely a limitation of the exi sting paradigms in geoscience education research which is focused on g eneralizability and rely heavily on quantitative me thods (Mattheis et al., 2019). This pattern of exclusion is a form of representational injustice. Additionally, the exclusion of Black women from our understanding of geoscience education is a form  of epistemic injustice. The exclusion of  Black women in geoscience education  is often explained away using statistics  or irrelevance (Mattheis et al., 2019; Marín -Spiotta et al., 2020)  — this line of reasoning adheres to a positivist epis temological paradigm. Dominating orientations to research aim to create s cientific descriptions of reality by producing obje ctive generalizations, follow s trict methodological rules, and to distance researc hers from the values, vested interests and emotions generated by their class, race, sex, o r unique situation (Hill-Collins, 2022). This approach cannot recognize or make sense of minoritized people, and is ill suited to address the concerns about demographi c representation for which the methods have been invo ked. Centering Black women in geoscience education research to disrupt injustice will require an ideological and methodological shift. Black feminist theory When Black women are perpetually non-subjects we are  positioned as  incapable of geological sense making and our exclusion is seen as natural. Dr. Patricia Hill-Collins (2022)  argues that Black women have an epistemological way of navigating the world th at is based on their racialized and gendered standp oint. Criteria for substantiated knowledge, in alignment with Black Feminist Thought, is characterized by lived experience as a criteri on of meaning, the use of dialogue in assessing knowledge  claims, the ethic of caring, the ethic of personal accountability, and Black Women as agents of knowledge (Hill-Collins, 2022).  Black Feminist Thought is rooted outside of the logics of colonization and is necessary for the praxis of research with Black women. Pipeline logics, universality, and objectivity that characterized ge oscience education research are linked to the histo ry of colonization and extraction that shaped the field (Monarres et al.,2022). Black Feminist epistemology,  and methodological approaches are well suited to disrupt the ongoing demographic homogeneity of the geosciences. I ground my work for and with research with Black w omen in the Black feminist epistemological tradition and outside of the post-positivist inheritance in geoscience education rese arch. Of note, my use of ‘I’, ‘we’, and ‘our’ in this work is meant to show  that the ideas I share are not just my own but inst ead is around on experience as a criterion of meaning, the use of di alogue in assessing knowledge claims, the ethic of caring, the ethic of personal accountability. These criteria in formed all aspects of the study design, choice of qualitative methods and analysis.  This work demonstrates that  shifting our gaze beyond the dominant paradigm whi ch focuses on normative populations in the field allow s Black womens’ ways of being in the field to emerg e. For example, some number Black geoscientists have publish ed narrative guides and commentary for anti-racist practices based on analysis of their own educationa l journeys (i.e.: Woods et al., 2023; Morris, 2021;  Scarlett 2021). These Black scholars draw attention  to the impact of systemic and structural barriers t hat have yet to be named as a part of the disciplinary culture. This w ork builds on their contributions by uplifting more  stories and opening up space for  educational research to interrogate  taken for granted disciplinary norms. Positionality I am a Black woman geoscientist and educational sch olar  who lives in the double-bind (Ong et al., 2011)/ intersection (Crenshaw, 1991) of racialized and gen dered experiences in the academy.  I trace my inter ests in Black Women’s relations to Earth to my homeland, Ja maica, where our national flag is a representation of  how Blackness and the history of struggle (Black) is ti ed to a knowledge of nature (green) and our relatio nship to the Earth’s resources (gold). We wa ve our flag as hard and as often as we can and take  pride in what these colors represent. As a daughter of  this tradition, my ide ntity is inextricably linked to my desire to know E arth, and the people who live here, in a way that is non-extractive. Though  this narration, of Black woman geoscientist as unicorn, might be new to some, I  am  quite familia r with this fable as I, too, was and am  a Black w oman geoscientist. While I was in the field, I was regul arly complimented and celebrated as a unicorn when  others remarked on my puzzling presence in the field.

In relation to where I enter this work, it is imper ative that this work  acknowledges and explores  (f or me, participants, and the reader) the nuances of be ing simultaneously marginalized and agentic.  I am aware that others regard me, and others like me, as Black unicorns:  part mythical creature and part  spectacle; to be spoken of and about, but never spoken to.  Through my research, I uplift and center Black women as knowledge producers. Being a multiply marginalized person allows me to see the dominant power system more clearly  – an exercise of agency despite and because of my intersections. I d esired to invite 10 participants to this study, 9 w omen have joined me thus far, and I, the tenth unicorn, threaded our experiences together. Not all is to be shared, but I write to amplify that which is already known.

## Study design and methods

The present analysis is part of an ongoing study which started in Spring 2023, and is open to Black women in the United States who have earned post secondary degree (s) in geosciences. The larger study asks  (1) What  early experiences promoted Black women’s  interest in the geosciences and development of posi tive geoscience identities? and (2) What supports, both in and out of postsecondary geoscience classrooms, impacted Bl ack Women’s thriving in the field?  In order to address  these research questions I iss ued a national call to Black woman geoscientists. Interested people completed a demographic survey, then submitted a brief geoscien ce autobiography (5-10 paragraphs) chronicling what they have perceived  as positive and negative educational experiences as Black Women in the Geosciences. Participants who completed an autobiography were invited to a 90-minute semi-structured individual interview via-Zoom. The interviews explored participants' sensema king about being Black Woman Geoscientists (ie: “ What does being a Black Woman geoscientist mean to you?”) and was responsive to the specific events and themes shared in their autobiography (ie: “In your demographic survey you mentioned that your relationship to the geosciences was [participants response], could you tell me more about that relationship?). Providing multiple opportuniti es for participants to reflect on experiences that are important to them, and revisiting themes from the autobiograp hy during interviews allowed layered data collectio n (i.e.: Leyva, 2021) and supported nuanced understanding about the intersectional nature of their lived experiences. This research design centered the experiences of Black women and created space for dialogue as Black feminist praxis. A total of 9 women have participated. I purposively  focus on Kendra, Cali, and Jordan for this analysi s because of their explicit use of the  unicorn metaphor as a part of their sense making of the racialized and gendered aspects of geosciences education.

In order to respond to the research questions for t his study, I approached each data source inductivel y. Of importance to answering both research questions,  which explore Black women’s positioning and agenti c navigation in geoscience spaces, the use of dialogu e and ethic of caring from BFE guided flagging of i nstances where Black women participants describe their interactions with others and expressed emotional connections (ie: joy, frustration) within geoscience education setti ngs. First I applied an open (i.e.: “social interaction”, “stereotypes”, “curriculum”) and axial coding (i.e.: “isolation”, “visibility”,“counterspaces”) to ga in a sense of the nature of each data source. Based on these open  and axial codes,  I developed four overarching the mes concerning Black women’s positioning and navigation: (1)  being out of place, (2) (in/hyper) -visibility, (3) carrying weight of being a Black woman, (4) engagi ng in heterogeneous collectivity within each participant’s narrative as well as between participants.

## Participant profiles

Cali Cali no longer works in the geosciences, but spoke often about the ways that she carries what she lear ned with her as transferable knowledge. Her affiliation with a K-12 bridge program created opportunities to meet and be in community with an intergenerational group of Black scientists while taking courses with a cohort of Black STEM majors. Through a bridge program, Cali experienced racially affirming academic and social support that  led her to declare a major in geology. Cali went on to work  multiple jobs  in the oil and gas industry where people constantly questioned her position as a geoscientis t. Cali found joy, hope, resistance, pressure, and fulfillment during her educational journey and responded to my call for participants as a way to give back to the next generation of Black women geoscientists.

Cali’s autobiography responses were rich and detailed. She took a holistic view of every part of her postbaccalaureate journey and included key programs, da tes, and people that contributed to her trajectory as a geoscientist. In her sensemaking, Cali used  the unicorn metaphor three times. Being a unicorn w as central to the way that Cali wrote about her experiences.

Kendra Kendra is an interdisciplinary scientist and scholar who has recently embraced her title as a geoscientist.  Despite her recent acceptance of the term, Kendra’s exposur e to the field happened in middle school and contin ued into highschool through extra-curricular programs. She enrolled in an HBCU where  ex celled in her STEM courses, and her decision to become a scientist was solidifi ed when she participated in a post baccalaureate in ternship experience at an environmental organization. In wri ting about her academic journey she spoke about her frustration with being in classrooms where no one l ooked like her, and advocating on her own behalf wh en professors and staff members expressed doubt in her ability to succeed. Though Kendra did not invoke the unicorn metaphor i n her autobiography, she used the metaphor as a part of her opening remarks for the interview.  Lik ening herself to a unicorn was the first thing she shared with me apart from her name, pronouns, and professional affiliations. Jordan Jordan is a first generation scholar who immigrated  to the United States from a predominantly Black co untry in order to become a geologist. Unlike Cali and Kendra  who were introduced to geoscience through formal extracurricular activities, Jordan was introduced t o the field by a close family member who is a field  scientist. Jordandescribed her first geology course where the  instructor opened her mind to the geosciences. In our interview, Jordan spoke about the importance of bei ng authentic to who she is – she talked about her physical appearance, style, music choices, and other ways that she stayed grounded in spaces that often reinforced the old white male centric culture of geoscience spaces.

Jordan spoke often of her independence, self assure dness, and commitment to authentic expression as a way to ‘represent every kind of geoscientist’. She evoked the unicorn metaphor once, during her indivi dual interview, as a way to describe what it is like to navigate predominantly white conference spaces. Analysis The Lone Black Unicorn as anachronistic, visibly out of place This section of analysis is an exposition of themes  concerning Black women’s positioning within geosci ence education as (1) being out of place, and  (2) (in/h yper)-visibility. Jordan, Kendra, and Cali discuss the un icorn metaphor in every stage of a geoscience educational  journey: recruitment, retention, instruction, ment orship, engagement in professional settings, and in employm ent.  Their use of the metaphor relays information about structural, ideological and social space. The Black  unicorn metaphor conv eys how being Black woman in geoscience is to be a living example of what is per ceived to be anachronistic – impossibly out of order or out of place in the context of the discipline.

I'm kind of the unicorn in our group, in that my expertise spans [multiple topics]…they [speaking abouther peers] have a hard time figuring out what exactly it is I do.  (Kendra, Individual Interview) Within the first few minutes of meeting Kendra, she  linked being a unicorn to her interdisciplinary interests. Over the course of her career, Kendra de veloped expertise in a range of disciplines to ensu re that her work gives back to the Black community through rese arch, teaching, and scholarship. As evidenced by th e ways her colleagues have a hard time ‘figuring out exact ly what [she] do[es]’, Kendra’s interdisciplinary r esearch interests violate a disciplinary norm and Kendra’s colleagues expect her to align with only one physic al science tradition. Beyond being out of alignment with disci plinary norms due to the commentary of her peers, K endra highlighted the ways that departmentmental and inst itutional expectations conflicted with her personal  values. Her superiors have advised her to focus on writing, res earch, tenure and promotion, but Kendra discussed teaching, mentorship, racial diversity and family as her priorities. Kendra’s description of being a unicorn is about being out of place in her department, and in academia.

In response to these normative ideas of developing expertise and what it means to navigate an academic career, Kendra spoke often of the art of  ‘finesse’ or ‘giving [her] opinion in a way that they hear it, and they still value it’ which is required to navigate predominant ly white male spaces. Kendra mentioned that she nev er felt herself in academic settings saying ‘I do that [fin esse] all the time. I do it in emails. I do it in m eetings. And sometimes it works.’. A part of being out of place as a Black woman geoscientist has meant that Kendra chooses her battles carefully, and has intentionally built a strong community outside of her department, as a matter of self preservation.  Kendra’s characterization of being a  unicorn is ideological and epistemological as she questions what it means to know geoscience, how that is communicated, and who gets to do that work. While Kendra’s interdisciplinary navigation of the field made her a unicorn,  Cali’s invocation of metaphor was tied to her geology degree. She mentioned that  in every setting, people have expressed s urprise when they learn of her affiliation with geology. Fo r example, when she was employed by an oil company,new colleagues tended to guess every department but geology saying ‘You work in HR? Are you in business? Are you an engineer?’. People wondered why a person like he r, a Black woman, would be associated with a geolog y educational background and had a hard time figuring  her out. This disbelief is likely related to the p ublic’s lack of familiarity with the field as a whole as well as the persistent racial and gender demographic underrepresentation of Black women in industry. Cali’s characterization and re sponse to being a unicorn  reflects Black womens’ perceived incompatibility with normative ideas of geology as well as the ways that geology is regarded as a niche subject.

Normative disciplinary practices associated with geology degrees also created tensions for Jordan. In her autobiography, she listed field camp as the most negative educational experience for her as a Black woman.  She specifically mentioned the compounding  impact of being a first generation scholar on the a ccessibility of field camp, which was required for her geology degree (i. e. Poslett &Núñez, 2022). Reflecting on the impact  of field camp, Jordan shared ‘stressors during [field camp] were planning for a menstrual cycle during field work, making sure I could physically handle the hiking and making sure I would financially be able to afford weeks in the field.’ The disciplinary expectations of wealth, ability, a nd gender performance were all at odds with Jordan’ s lived intersectional identity. Trip leaders did not consi der the financial, cultural, and gendered barriers to full participation in the field course, and women as wel l as first generation students in these settings ma de their own accommodations. Jordan, Kendra, and Cali’s use of the unicorn metaphor highlights disciplinary specific barriers to full participation for Black women geoscientists.

You are noticeable. You are almost isolated in that. Oh, everybody notices when there's a Black girl in a geoscience space. We're not that common, you know, a black woman in geoscience, is like a unicorn.

(Jordan, Individual Interview) Jordan’s invocation of the unicorn metaphor was a p art of her sense making about being both “noticeable” and “isolated”. Being “the only one” m akes Jordan a unicorn. Her description of being vis ible were reflections on being in disciplinary conference spa ces where being a Black woman has meant being under   “a huge magnifying glas s”.  She describes this hyper-visibility saying  “We are probably the only one in  our department. We're probably the only one in our rese arch area in geoscience.”  A sense of being watched  leads to a performance that Jordan finds problematic for the ways she feels pressure to “overcompensate”  and to be  “this articulate person that always has to say the right thing.” To be a Black woman in the geosciences mea ns to be seen and to be scrutinized for being present in spite of low demographic representation in the geosciences. Jordan noted that her expertise and knowledge gets minimized and downplayed in ways that were linked to her deviation from the rugged white male geoscie ntist archetype saying “ it was almost like they in stantly thought that I would be incapable of my role,  because I was a Black female in that position.” The  pressure to perform is out of alignment with Jordan’s expressed values of authenticity and ‘unapologetic Black woman[hood]’ so she affirms herself often. Jordan focuses on aut henticity and  intentionally presents herself in ways that 'don’t typically look like ‘Oh, you do field work’ or ‘you do lab work’. Jordan’s characterization of the unicorn metaphor is relational because of the ways it is defined in interaction with others but is also centered on her desire to define geosciences for herself.

Cali further nuances the visibility by highlighting  the benefits of Black women in geoscience spaces saying ‘I enjoy being adored as a unicorn because black women geologists do not exist’. Like Jordan, Cali invokes the idea that Black woman geoscientists a re anachronistic,  which in some ways afforded her an elevated status. In Cali’s retelling of her educational journey, bei ng a Black woman was connected to financial resourc es and opportunities including access to the community bas ed programs and support t hat spurred Cali’s interest and persistence. The financial  and academic resources she described were linked to demographic underrepresentation in STEM. Being ‘adored as a unicorn’, however, did not come without a price for Cali. During our indiv idual interview, she spoke often of feeling like her path was chosen for her, and that the pressure she felt and still feels to be the change that the field needs subsumed her ability to define her path for herself. Cali’s ador ation as a unicorn in the context of retention programs was at odds with her agency. After invoking the ways that being a unicorn was be neficial, Cali shares the often cited reasons to increase retention and persistence of Black women i n the geosciences when people  say things like “ th ey are black and we need more representation, its for the culture, would make the ancestors proud, inspiring the future, free money”. Cali insists that “there needs to be m ore” critiquing the ways that Black women and girls  are made to bear the weight of improving field demographics in exchange for financial reward. Cali instead posits that “these things are great by products if you are pass ionate about it”, because she “left [home] to study  math and science because it was a free, good education. No p assion involved”.  The holistic desires and needs o f Black women geoscientists are left out of the push for rec ruitment and retention, and relegates Black women a nd girls to  inputs and outputs of an educational system. Cali ultimately left the field in order to regain a sense of agency. The isolation of Black woman geoscientists extends to classroom spaces. At multiple points during Kendra’s journey she encountered instructors, peers, and staff who doubted her ability to succeed. She  noted the racialized and gendered aspects of peer inter actions as she was usually the only Black woman, and Black person in these classes. Twice during her journey, Kendra mentioned racialized group work dynamics to her ins tructors and it was unaddressed. The color-evasive and meritocratic values espoused by faculty and students in this course setting were at odds with her lived experience as a  Black woman student. Of these experiences Kendra n oted ‘I had to navigate those obstacles as a female of color in those classes which shouldn't have been the case.” Kendra felt well prepared for the content of the courses, but not the course culture. She responded to this reality by tapping into resources outside of the class structure like office hours, and her parents. Institutional culture was an additional source of isolation for Kendra once she completed her coursework. Kendra recalled an interaction with a staff member who questioned her decision to start a family while  she was an advanced doctoral student. In her reflections about why that experience was a memorably negative moment in her educational trajectory, Kendra responded “How d are you put your limitations on what you think I ca n do? …you think I can't walk and chew bubble gum at the same time.”  Her expectations for herself, in this case becoming a parent, were not aligned with normative constructs of what it means to perform “serious” (i.e.: masculine; childless) academic pursuits. After bein g questioned about her decision making, Kendra redu ced the opportunities for her white colleagues to judge and criticiz e her work, her presence, and her priorities as a B lack woman geoscientist. Her experiences inform her mentorship of underrepresented students in her department  ‘I'm not doing to them what was done to me’.

Cali, Kendra, and Jordan used the unicorn metaphor to describe Black womanhood as a state of embodied anachronism. All three women detailed the inability  of others to reconcile their identities as Black w omen in geoscience and shared the ways that they respond to protect their identities. Together they make sense of the ways that hypervisibility can be a dehumanizing process that suppresses the needs of living breathing Black  women geoscientists. Black women feel the pressure to perform in academic spaces, and can be judged and criticized for disrupting limiting ideas about what it means to be  a geoscientist.  The mere presence of Black women becomes a spectacle, and navigating that space requires Black women to respond in varied ways. The Lone Black Unicorns bear the weight of black womanhood in heterogeneous collectivity Though the unicorn metaphor served as a shorthand f or the ways in which Black women’s positioning in t he geosciences is a response to ideological, epistemol ogical, structural, relational, and agentic aspects  of their underrepresentation, the metaphor also illuminated the ways that Black women come together, as an expression of the Black Feminist ideal of heterogeneous collectivity (theme 4; Hill-Collins, 2022) in order to carry the weight of being a Black woman  in geosciences (theme 3).  In response to Black Womens positioning in the geosciences, we gather and write new narratives.

‘They believed in unicorns.’ (Cali, Autobiography) ‘we go together now… like I water you, you water me’ (Jordan, interview) ‘we kind of decided, yeah, we're doing this. We were like locked in.’ (Kendra, interview) As an undergraduate, Cali was a member of a well re sourced STEM bridge program. Throughout the program she received targeted academic support, and  navigated her coursework as a part of a cohort of Black students who attended an HBCU. When Cali later enrolled in a masters program at a PWI, no such formal cohort and social network of support existed. She spoke ab out Black women staff members  ‘ dedicated to our s uccess and gave encouragement in a way that felt like a be loved mom or auntie … they believed in unicorns.’  Their offices were ‘a safe space to discuss anything’ and when Cali felt as though she would not finish her degree, one of the staff members ‘insisted on calling me Dr [Cali] until I believed it was a possibility.’ The belief in unicorns that Cali shared was tied to holistic and responsive support from Black women. In addition to the staff members who provided socia l and programmatic opportunities for support, Cali pledged a Black sorority, joined a religious organi zation, and was introduced to her future spouse thr ough her connections with Black woman graduate students outs ide of her department. In her engagement with heterogeneous collectivity, during her masters, Cal i built a community, was empowered to finish her th esis, and laid the groundwork for her to build a  life outsid e of the disciplinary expectation of in dustry or academic career paths. In all  her artifacts, Cali spoke about paying it forward to the next generation of Black women geoscientists, so that they too might navigate their educational journeys  in ways that allow them to explore their passions. She felt that sharing her story was a part of what it meant to realize that future. Like Cali, Jordan sought the companionship of other  Black women scholars as peers and as mentors at every stage of her academic journey. One such siste rhood happened as an undergraduate on the first day  of an upper level calculus class where for the first time Jordan saw another Black woman. They supported each through the course  and took several subsequent physics and  math courses together, even though they were study ing for two different STEM majors. Similarly, when  Jordan recalled her time as a doctoral student, she descri bed ‘rely(ing) more on external things’ like national w riting groups and disciplinary conferences for a se nse of community.  Jordandescribes the moment she  met another Black woman geoscientist saying ‘ immediately, when we met at the  workshop, we were like we go together now, and so w e started everyday zoom calls where we would just work together.’ In talking about the emp owerment that came from building an academic commun ity with other minoritized scholars, Jordan used two me taphors: ‘you just kept stirring the pot’and ‘like I water y ou, you water me’. The relationships that sustained Jor dan to finish her doctoral degree were academic, so cial, authentic, and reciprocal.

Jordan co uld simply enjoy sharing her research with others. NABG let Jordan know that a reality outside of the normative  gaze is already possible within geosciences: ‘When  I went there I was just like this is probably a way other pe ople of different races feel like when they go to t hese conferences like they just feel like they fit in.’ NABG lessened the dehumanization that happens as a result of Black women’s hypervisibility and performance and eliminated the feeling of  being under a magnifying glass’ at the predominantly white disciplinary conferences.

Kendra’s connection to a collective is the root of her identity as an interdisciplinary geoscientist. When Kendra noticed a Black woman on campus, she looked up her affiliation on the university website and introduced herself.  That first meeting has blossomed into a formal academic relationship with multiple Black woman scholars on their campus. Now, they write together, apply fo r grants together, advise students together and celebrate wins as members of the collective approach and achieve t enure. When I a sked her about this group of Black woman scholars Kendra said ‘It was not intentional. But... once we kind of decided, yeah, we're doing this. We were like locked in’. As a collective, Kendra and her Black w oman colleagues ‘define geoscience for [them]sel ves’. In community with other Black women, Kendra is able to  create a vision for geosciences that disrupts the rigid disciplinary thinking that is linked to Black women's continued underrepresentation. Kendra mentions a lessening of the need to ‘finesse’ in discussions with he Black women research group and in  her mentorship relationships with historica lly underserved students. In speaking about her own  approach to mentoring the predominantly Black and Latin* students who Kendra remarked ‘ I'm a little bit more authentic because I want them to not get a fluff answer so th ey can make an informed decision.’ Kendra  is able to give council  in ways that disrupt the isolation that ch aracterized her own academic journey. Kendra was no t alone in this approach. Cali and Jordan likewise reflected on their academic journeys, and spoke about their active  role in bettering the field through mentorship. Forming het erogeneous collectives is an ongoing liberatory pra ctice that is engaged in, by, and for Black and other historically underrepresented groups in the geosciences. In collectives, unicorns are no longer anachronistic, they are right on time.Contrary to the individualistic and white male centric articulations of what it means to be a geoscientist, Black women engage in heterogeneous collectives for their own empowerment and liberation.

## Discussion and conclusion

In  the present study, the metaphor of the 'Lone Bl ack unicorn' emerged as a nuanced symbol used by Bl ack women geoscientists to articulate their experiences navigating geoscience education.  Engaging in Back feminist theory and method, specifically through centering B lack women as agents of knowledge, honoring our liv ed experiences, and engaging in qualitative inquiry as  a dialogic process,  allowed for an articulation o f Black womens’ geoscience experiences in our own words. This theoretical and methodological framing elucidates how the Lone Black Unicorn metaphor shaped the racial-gender intersectionality of Black women's experienc es in geoscience education. The Lone Black Unic orn metaphor additionally highlights the challenges  and contradictions of visibility and invisibility —being simultaneously hyper-visible due to demographic characteristics of the field while being dehumanize d and mythologized within the academic and social  spaces. Black women gather collectively as a way to navigate the limiting nature of our positioning. One of the ways Black women are systematically excluded from the geoscinces is through research that does not consider Black women or our knowledge prod uction. This study, however, contributes to the dis course on demographic representation within the geosciences by offering an expanded perspective on the experiences of Black women.  In exploring narratives of Lone Black unicorn, Cali, Jordan, and Kendra highlighted the ways that engagement in the discipline (including field camp,  group work assignments, and advising relationships) was inherently intersectional.  Black women often sough t external resources or gathered together to compen sate for these intersectional deficiencies and are reimagining the limiting structures within geoscience education, through Black feminist thought.
