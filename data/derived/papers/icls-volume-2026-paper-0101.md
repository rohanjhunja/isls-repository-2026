# Dialogue, Design, and Reflect: Partnering with Teachers to Transform Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teachers have the potential to be transformational agents of change. And yet, fewer accounts from practice illuminate the process of teachers becoming transformational agents. In this paper, we present a composite counterstory developed with six high school teachers to demonstrate how they and their colleagues evolved as transformational intellectuals through their participation in professional activities over the years, including inquiry-based teacher professional development (PD) sessions, co-designing a justice-oriented computing program, and reflecting on their experiences. Drawing from thematic qualitative analysis of video recordings of five reflective sessions with teachers at the end of a three-year-long codesign work, we answer: How did teachers develop as transformational intellectuals through co-designing a justice-oriented computing curriculum alongside inquiry-based professional development and reflective practice? The counterstory highlights themes that emerged from the analysis, showing that teachers developed trusted relationships, agency, and solidarity through critical historical analysis and reflectivity.

Computing solutions are shaping our everyday lives in unprecedented ways. From facial recognition software to recommendation systems aiding humandecision-making across criminal justice, healthcare, and finance, examples of computing solutions interacting with the sociocultural and sociopolitical milieu abound  (e.g., Benjamin, 2019; Crawford, 20 21). This has led to the urgent need to focus on justice-oriented and equity frameworks to support computing learning and teaching (Kafai & Proctor, 2022;  Vakil, 2018). A “critical turn” has meant a move away from the dominant view of computing concepts as value-free or divorced from any connections with people’s social, cultural, and political realities  (Ko et al., 2025). Computing learning has been reframed towards liberatory educational goals, one where learners get to see how computing solutions perpetuate and amplify existing inequities within communities and societies. Such a call relies on teachers to support learning of critical computing within classrooms  (Ko et al., 2025). Teachers will need to become transformational intellectuals, who make the pedagogical political and political the pedagogical (Giroux, 2018; 2024). Teacher education and preparation efforts should enable  critical consciousness development among teachers, just as teachers  support among learners in classrooms. However, most efforts in the teacher education space have focused on aiding teachers with narrow and often disconnected disciplinary learning (e.g., Yadav et al., 2016; Webb et al., 2017). Such an instrumental approach to disciplinary education, particularly visible within computing and engineering education, minimizes teachers’ role to content deliverers instead of enabling critical consciousness development among learners (Giroux, 2018; Malazita, 2022). However, the more recent expansive take on teacher learning, as one happening within design spaces and not just within classrooms, teacher PDs, and education programs, holds promise to engage teachers in various opportunities over time and examine their learning across spaces (Potvin, Boardman, & Scornavacco, 2023). Collaborative design or co-design is one such approach, as a part of the participatory design framework, where teacher learning takes the form of learning by designing (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Penuel, Roschelle, & Shechtman, 2007; Philip et al., 2022). Rooted in Scandinavian principles of democratic society, teachers design tools that are of consequence for their professional lives, such as curricular materials and teacher PD sessions. Co-design efforts across STEM education have been successful in supporting teacher agency development and empowering teachers while supporting teacher learning (e.g., Severance et al., 2016; Kelter et al., 2021). However, very few studies have looked at teacher learning across different learning spaces such as classrooms, teacher PDs, and co-design efforts. Such longitudinal engagement has the potential to not only compound learning for teachers 888 but also enable them to develop as transformational intellectuals, especially when the same teachers are engaged in different activities. What it may lead to is not just more of the same but a transformed opportunity for teachers to develop trusting relationships and work towards solidarity, joy, and possibilities for the future. It is particularly important to study teacher learning and community building across spaces as teachers are being attacked for what and how they teach (e.g., Giroux, 2024; Santo, Phelps, & Yadav, 2024). This study is situated within a justice-oriented introductory high school computing program, Exploring Computer Science (hereafter ECS). For this paper, we analyzed the five reflection sessions a group of experienced high school teachers a fter co-(re)designing the latest version of ECS  for three years  (Jayathirtha, Chapman, & Goode, 2024; 2023). Inspired by Freirean  praxis, of iterative cycles of action and reflection  (Freire, 1970), we partnered with teachers to read critical scholars, Henry Giroux and James Malazita, to collectively reflect on the process and the products we designed. The first three authors, also curriculum designers and researchers, conducted qualitative thematic analysis of the session transc ripts (Clarke & Braun, 2017) to answer the research question: How did teachers develop as transformational intellectuals  through co-designing a justice-oriented computing curriculum alongside inquiry-based professional development and reflective practice? To protect the teachers, especially the ones who practice equity pedagogies, we developed composite characters and counternarratives (Cook & Bryan, 2021; Solorzano & Yosso, 2001), drawing from the data, to highlight the themes from the analysis and keep the focus on the systemic issues rather than focusing on the individual teachers.

## Theoretical framework

While studies have focused on learners developing critical computing literacies, there are many fewer studies that have taken a closer look at the teachers’ roles as being and becoming transformational intellectuals. We will therefore focus on theories that help us articulate the teachers’ side of the same coin. Teachers as transformational intellectuals Contrary to the expectations from teachers, educational reforms barely display any confidence and trust in teachers to be agents of change and provide intellectual leadership (Giroux, 2018). Teachers, for the most part, are relegated to the role of transmitters of fixed figments of knowledge or implementers of pre-set curricula, without any critical investigation or design efforts (Giroux, 2013; 2018; 2024). Teaching as a profession is organized by the managerial model, which disempowers teachers and reduces teaching to implementing pre-designed curricula and focusing on tests. Such approaches not only deskill and devalue teachers but also make invisible the intellectual work teachers do within classrooms (Giroux, 2024).

Approaching teachers as transformational intellectuals not only resists the deficit-view about teachers and teaching but brings attention to the complex nature of teaching and makes visible the intellectual work teachers do within classrooms (Giroux, 2018; Philip et al., 2016). As opposed to the technocratic and instrumental approach to teaching, the focus on the intellectual work of teachers surfaces how teachers navigate questions such as what they teach, how they teach, and towards what ends they teach (Giroux, 2024; Philip, Bang, & Jackson, 2019). Further, teaching will be enmeshed in the social, cultural, and political contexts in which they teach, bringing pedagogy in conversation with the political (Giroux, 2018, 2024). This place s teachers at the center of educational reform, removing them from the passive, peripheral role ascribed to them otherwise. Expansive roles of teachers outside classrooms While teachers’ role as transformational intellectuals focuses on the pedagogical approaches within classrooms, teachers’ participation in other professional activities which study teacher learning hold promise to enable our understanding of how teachers become transformational intellectuals. For instance, teachers facilitating PD for new teachers has been studied as an opportunity for continued learning outside of classrooms  (McGee et al., 2018). Yet another site for teacher learning has been design contexts (e.g., Kelly et al., 2019; Kelter et al., 2021). Drawing inspiration from Scandinavian participatory design methods, approaches like collaborative design or codesign involve various stakeholder groups in designing the very product that will impact their lives. Participatory collaborative design (co-design) with teachers not only enables teacher agentic participation but also creates locally sustaining models while empowering teachers to shape their professional spaces (S everance et al., 2016; Severance & Lee, 2025). Such efforts have the potential for educators to develop collective capacity to identify contradictions to their experiences in classrooms while resisting deprofessionalization of teachers and simplification of teaching  (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; P hilip et al., 2022). If teachers were to engender critical consciousness among students and empower them to bring their disciplinary learning into conversation with the social realities, teachers would need to have similar power and ability to ask questions about how knowledge gets produced in the field, how it gets circulated, and how they relate to the broader social and societal issues (Giroux, 2024). Teacher learning over time, as they engage in empowering design opportunities, needs further exploration. 889 An example of co-designing with teachers: Exploring Computer Science (ECS) program ECS is an introductory high school computing program, launched in 2008, to support equity-focused computing education (Goode, Chapman, & Margolis, 2012; Goode, Margolis, & Chapman, 2014). ECS demonstrated its commitment to sustaining computing education in high school contexts by focusing on teachers and offering a two-year teacher PD in addition to the year-long curriculum. The curriculum comprises five units: HumanComputer Interaction, Problem-solving, Programming, Computing and Data, and Robotics, with an additional elective unit--electronic textiles. Furthermore, the program developed a facilitator model where experienced ECS teachers were mentored by the second and the third authors to become PD facilitators to support new teacher development (McGee et al., 2018). Between the two-year teacher PD model and the facilitator development model, the ECS program has fostered a teacher community across the US and Puerto Rico over the last decade. Teachers built relationships with one another and with the program as they participated in the teacher PD and facilitated PDs regionally and nationally.

Starting in Fall 2022, the ECS program was redesigned by a group of twelve ECS teacher-facilitators to support teachers and students to critically engage with computing (e.g., Jayathirtha, Goode, & Chapman, 2023; Jayathirtha, Chapman, & Goode, 2024). Twelve of the twenty-four facilitators who participated in the Summer 2022 ECS Annual Facilitators Workshop (hereafter called co-design teachers) volunteered to be part of the multiyear co-design work. The demographics of the co-design group reflected those of the larger facilitator group: ten identified as cis-female and two as cis-male. Among the women, four identified as Black, one as Middle Eastern, and five women and one man identified as White, with the other male-identifying teacher having a mixed race. Between Fall 2022 and Spring 2025, the co-design teachers reviewed shared resources, brainstormed ideas for curriculum and teacher PD, co-authored new lessons, peer reviewed and revised them, and reviewed external feedback to revise the overall program for final publication in Spring 2025 (Jayathirtha, Goode, & Chapman, 2023; Jayathirtha, Chapman, & Goode, 2024; Jayathirtha et al., 2025). Our positionalities Good qualitative research requires that researchers acknowledge and constantly recognize their position and influence on the  research design, methods, and  power relations in the researched space (Solorzano & Yosso, 2002). As a research collective with varied roles —as original curriculum designers (Goode & Chapman), teachers-facilitators (Elmeligui, Griggs, Crocker, Ottina, Yanek, Parr), and researcher-facilitator (Jayathirtha)— we brought different degrees of relatedness and perspectives to the analysis. The decade-long relationships between the curriculum designers and the  teachers enabled trust but  also created the potential to interpret facilitator’s distance from program implementation and its history helped create spaces for analysis and research. Jayathirtha identifies as a cis  woman researcher from India. Goode and Chapman identify as White cis women from the US. Fourth through ninth authors are a subgroup of co-design teachers who collaborated to author this manuscript. They have 5-10 years of experience teaching the program, 2-10 years of facilitating teacher PDs for new teachers locally and nationally, and three years of co-designing ECS. Co-authoring this paper meant crafting the composite characters and counternarratives presented below.

The study was guided by the research question: How did teachers develop as transformational intellectuals through co-designing a justice-oriented computing curriculum alongside inquiry-based professional development and reflective practice? Drawing from Paulo Freire’s praxis (Freire, 1970), the first three authors hosted a series of five online reflection sessions at the end of the co-design process. All eleven co-design teachers were invited to join the sessions (one of them passed away during the second year of co-design). Six of them accepted the invitation based on their availability in Summer 2025.

The reflection sessions functioned simultaneously as consciousness-raising praxis (Freire, 1970)  and collaborative analysis among the author s. Authors 1-3 structured sessions around theoretical readings, while Authors 4-9 engaged both as participants reflecting on co-design experiences and as co-researchers beginning to theorize those experiences, reflecting epistemological commitments to experiential knowledge as legitimate scholarship (Solorzano & Yosso, 2002). In an age when equity-focused teaching is under attack, teachers were invited to reflect on the co-design experience and the products as they reviewed works of James Malazita (2019,

2022) and Henry Giroux (2018, 2024), whose works influenced our understanding of the political and economic

dimensions of computing teaching and learning. Each of the five  sessions entailed engaging with a reading followed by preparing digital synthesis cards to summarize key takeaways and connections with co-designing. 890 Each session was 60-minutes-long and involved sections to discuss key takeaways from the reading, connections to the program, and roles as co-designers. We video-recorded the sessions and annotated video transcripts based on the initial AI-generated transcripts, which totaled up to 65 pages. Further, the first two authors took detailed field notes  during the reflection sessions to supplement the transcripts. Co-designed curricular materials and the PD design were reviewed in preparation for these sessions. Teachers were actively encouraged to bring examples from the program when preparing their synthesis cards, an online slide for each card, to include their in-session reflections and discussions.

Collaborative thematic analysis The first three authors adopted a qualitative, inductive, and thematic analysis of the session transcripts while drawing from the co-designed products as a secondary data source when necessary (Clarke & Braun, 2017). The first author read through the auto-generated transcripts for errors while watching the recording to capture gestures and facial expressions, aligning with the preparation of multimodal transcripts for video analysis (Derry et al., 2010). The first three authors went through the transcripts at the end of every session asynchronously, independently commenting in response to the research questions. The three authors discuss ed and identif ied individual codes, group them to generate themes  and shared them with teachers during the next session, inspired by critical approaches to participatory research (Campos & Anderson, 2021). Teachers reflected on these themes at the beginning of each session and elaborated on them during the sessions. T hey engaged as active meaning-making agents throughout the process, questioning the focus and representation of themes for broader audiences. At the end of the five reflection sessions, three key themes emerged as processes that enabled teachers to become transformational intellectuals: teachers developed trusted relationships, agency, and solidarity  across their experiences co-designing a justice-oriented program and facilitating teacher PDs for it (Fig. 1, left).

## Composite counterstorytelling as a methodology

Because ECS is a popular equity-focused introductory high school computing program in the US, we required a method to protect our individual identities while preserving our collective narrative. We employed composite counterstory methodology to shift focus from individual participants to systemic issues  (Cook & Bryan, 2021; Solorzano & Yosso, 2001). Drawing from critical race theories and research methodologies, the first two authors developed composite characters by taking elements of intersecting stories and teacher interviews from previous years of collaborative work. These characters were then placed in social, historical, and political situations based on the teachers’ discussions to reflect the emerging themes and their roles as co-designers. This methodological choice aligns with our theoretical commitment to teachers as transformational intellectuals by centering collective wisdom and systemic issues over individual narratives that risk reinforcing deficit perspectives or exposing individual teachers to professional vulnerability. Based on the emergent themes, the first author wrote individual stories, which was an important step in creating composite characters. Composite characters were constructed to represent the multidimensional and intersecting identities of participating teachers.  Given that stories are understood and constructed from exchanges, experiences, and observations, we wanted to ensure we captured the essence of each individual and their voices before creating composite characters. Inspired by critical methodologies, the first and second authors jointly developed counterstories by overlaying these themes in relation to the research question in the voices of composite characte rs. Composite stories are fictional narratives drawn from the teacher reflection sessions  and co-design products. While we have used the data to creatively stitch a coherent story, the dialogues, settings, and the thoughts of the composite characters were directly drawn from the transcripts of the reflection sessions, minimally edited to maintain the narrative flow. Emphasis on parts of the dialogue, as indicated by BLOCK letters, mirror teachers’ tone. Co-designing justice-oriented lessons and activities meant teachers br inging computing concepts in direct conversation with students’ lived experiences, their sociocultural backgrounds, and their sociopolitical realities, which Malaika called the “whole child pedagogy.” We see this in the opening vignette from Malaika’s classroom, where her students realized how their habits produce data for the software corporations to perpetuate and accentuate existing harms. In another instance, Nuray reflects on her experience co-designing programming projects within E CS, where she identifies how she had to unlearn the normative approaches to teaching programming and instead imagine new learning opportunities for learners to meaningfully connect concepts to their experiences. As seen in Figure 1 (right), these co-designed lessons invite learners to examine how social and cultural value systems may get encoded into programmed systems that then perpetuate ine quities. Such a collaborative redesign work required teachers to have difficult conversations at the intersections of sociocultural dimensions such as race and gender, and computing education within the PDs just as they are expected to facilitate those discussions w ithin classrooms to support the development of a trusting learning community within their classrooms to enable their students to critically engage with computing.

As teachers identify the crucial role they play in classrooms, supporting learners to become critical agents within computing classrooms, teachers recognized how pedagogy is always enmeshed in the political world. Juxtaposing Nuray’s comment on the “new fe eling like we’re a bit under attack” and Malaika’s comment on “Welcome to the concept and the feeling of being Black in the United States,” we see a reflection of the ongoing crisis in education, especially how public school teachers are under attack, and at the same time a historical account of oppression on the marginalized communities within the society. While Nuray was proud of creating a 894 curricular resource that could support teachers teaching equity-focused computing programs such as ECS, her concern surfaced the ongoing censoring of content within classrooms and attacks on teachers (Giroux, 2024). At the same time, in Malaika’s sharing, not only an invitation for solidarity is visible, so is the trusting space that existed during these sessions where Malaika, a Black female teacher could reframe other teachers’ experiences of the “new crisis” as “Black teachers’ ongoing reality.” The trust in the space is connected to the decade-long engagement and relationship building between these teachers as they participated in ECS PDs as new teachers, grew into facilitating PDs, sometimes with each other, and more recently co-designing a justice-oriented program. From reading texts such as the Stuck in the Shallow End (Margolis et al., 2017) during the PD to the engaging with an array of materials inspired by Cultural Competency in Computing program (Washington, 2020) during the co-design session, teachers had several opportunities to, as Michael said, to work through the ebbs and flows, develop camaraderie, and most importantly trusting relationships. The exchange between Nuray and Malaika also reveals how intersecting positionalities shaped teachers' experiences of political resistance and their pathway to solidarity. While Nuray, as a woman from a historically marginalized ethnicity and minority relig ion, experienced political attacks as 'new,' Malaika's positioning as a Black woman teaching in a rural context meant she had long navigated such resistance. Malaika's invitation, 'Welcome to the concept and the feeling of being Black in the United States,' was made possible by the politicized trust built through years of collaborative work (Vakil & de Royston, 2019). This moment exemplifies how the codesign process created conditions for truth-telling across differences, where teachers could name power dynamics and build solidarity not through sameness, but through recognizing how systems of oppression differentially impact their professional lives.

Just as teachers discussed their own learning across the PD and co-design sessions, what also emerges is how teachers learned to make the political more pedagogical. Instead of traditional approaches to teacher education, as Nuray shared about sessions whe re they “hand you the talking stick or they give you this book to read,” teachers were related to as critical agents and were trusted to develop into transformational agents as teachers and PD facilitators. As teachers discussed, placing trust in teachers’  intellectual capacity to “do the teaching,” and having the PD focus on the objectives of the content and not on the abstracted-out content, was a significant step in creating learning contexts across spaces. Further, Nuray’s comment on not giving a “talki ng stick” also points to the emphasis on inquiry for knowledge building, which was helpful in learning to facilitate “difficult conversations,” where the political becomes pedagogical within justice-oriented computing classrooms. What also become evident across teachers' reflections is how making the political more pedagogical required a shift in epistemological stance, from viewing teaching as knowledge transmission to understanding it as creating conditions for collective inquiry and meaning making. This shift was not accomplished through directive training but through sustained opportunities to experience inquiry-based approaches as  designers and learners themselves, then reflect on those experiences as they designed for others. Teachers' capacity to envision and enact liberatory pedagogies emerged from living through them, analyzing their features, and adapting them for their own contexts. The co-design process thus functioned as an extended pedagogical modeling, where the ‘how’ of working together became inseparable from the ‘what’ of justice-oriented computing education. Teachers' development as transformational agents were fundamentally shaped by opportunities for critical historical analysis alongside their design and reflection work. From reading texts like Stuck in the Shallow End during PD to engaging with critical reading materials during co-design experience, teachers repeatedly encountered historical accounts of inequity in computing education. This historical consciousness enabled teachers to situate their current experiences within longer trajectories of oppressio n, transforming individual struggles into collective understanding of systemic patterns. Across the teacher PD, the co-design work, and the reflection sessions, teachers weaved their experiences while recognizing the pedagogical and the political as interconnected. From classroom practice to teacher PD to design sessions to reflective spaces, as each context provided different opportunities to enact, imagine, and critically examine transformative practices. The themes emerging from teachers' reflections extend and complicate existing literature on teacher learning within co-design contexts. While learning sciences has focused on the co-design processes  (e.g., Severance et al., 2018), this multi-year work with teachers highlights the equally crucial parts outside of co-design through which teachers continue to experience professional growth. The inquiry-based dialogic nature of the PD in addition to repeated engagement with critical topics enabled teachers to grow together into a trusting community while learning to push the traditional disciplinary boundaries (Jayathirtha, Chapman, & Goode, 2024). Further, the co-designing of a justice-oriented program provided opportunities for teachers to agentically shape their professional practice by designing lessons and activities that bring to life new imaginations of justice-oriented computing for students and teachers.
