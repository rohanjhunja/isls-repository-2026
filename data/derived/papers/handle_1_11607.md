# Fostering Reflective Practice for Addressing Social Justice in Education: Experiences with Preservice Teachers

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In Canada, social justice and reflective practice have been crucial elements of preservice teacher education. However, recent challenges have underscored the need to rethink how teacher education can effectively address inequities and injustices within the education system. The aim of this study was to examine the focus and levels of preservice teachers (PSTs) reflections in a social justice-oriented foundation of education course. Analysis of 13 portfolios indicated that intentionally designing courses allowed PSTs to promote diversity, question privileges, and be aware of injustices, together with articulating a commitment to action.

## Introduction

Developing reflective practitioners has been a key goal in teacher education (Chye et al., 2021) and serves as a foundational competence connecting all other compet ences for teaching (Gadsby, 2022). Building on a widespread recognition of the importance of reflective practice, preservice teacher education programs adopt and implement varied approaches.  This study focuses on a preservice teacher educatio n program at a Canadian University where cultivating critical reflection was a core goal of the program. Specifically, our ana lysis focuses on a course that adopted social justice orientation. Social justice-oriented courses are distinct in that they challenge mainstream ideas and practices, aiming to unearth patterns and relationships (Sensoy & DiAngelo, 2017).  In such courses, reflection becomes a key learning strategy, encou raging PSTs to recognize and address personal biase s that may influence their perspectives on critical issues pertaining to social justice in education. However, engaging in critical reflection on complex social justice issues presents significant challenges. While higher education courses aim to provide relatively safe environments for exploring these topics (Han et al., 2017), PSTs often struggle to confront complex pers onal and societal issues, including privilege, iden tity, oppression, racism and injustice (Sensoy & DiAngelo, 2017). This study therefore aimed to examine students’ experiences of reflective practice in a social just ice-oriented educational foundations course. Our resear ch is guided by the question: What are the focus and levels of preservice teachers’ reflections in a social justice-oriented educational foundations course?

Reflective practice in teacher education Teacher education programs incorporate reflective p ractice using different theoretical lenses, with predominant frameworks based on the works of Dewey and Schön (Collin et al., 2013). Whereas Dewey (1933) described reflection as a way of thinking based on evidence and rationality, Schön (1987) understood it as a way of knowing through and in the actions of professional practice s. Such perspectives provide limited opportunities to leverage towards equity and social justice approaches in education (Martin-Kerr et al., 2022). On the other hand, approaches grounded in critical and transformative reflection encourage PSTs identify, examine, and change deeprooted assumptions and practices (Han et al., 2017). In Canada, social justice and reflective practice h ave been crucial elements of preservice teacher education (Hill et al., 2020). However, recent challenges have underscored the n eed to rethink how teacher education can effectively address inequities and in justices within the education system (Hill et al., 2020). PSTs should be encouraged to go beyond simply conforming  to existing practices and, through critical reflec tion, examine how dominant ideologies, narratives, and be haviors shape their understanding of the teacher's role, considering cultural and h istorical contexts (Kincheloe, 2004). This study, therefor e, aimed to examine the contents and levels of PSTs reflection evident in PSTs portfolios in an educational foundations course.

## Method

Data for this study were collected from preservice teachers (PSTs) enrolled in an educational foundations course at a Canadian university. In this course, PSTs engaged in a variety of reflective learning activities, culminating in the development of a portfolio designed to showcase their learning outcomes. The portfolios of 13 PSTs were analyzed in this study. Data analysis was an iterative process of segmenting the data, examining underlying meanings, and comparing and contrasting reflections within and across portfolios.

## Results

Results indicated that PSTs reflected on social justice issues related to both educational and societal contexts. More specifically, we identified and discussed four main themes from analysis of their portfolios. Valuing and celebrating student identities A major theme that emerged from the reflections of PSTs was the recognition and celebration of diversity within societal and school contexts. PSTs emphasized the i mportance of valuing, respecting, and honoring dive rse cultures, languages, ethnicities, sexual orientations, and abilities in both educational and broader societal settings. For instance, PST002 wrote: “As teachers, it is crucial that I do not devalue any part of our students’ identities— including their linguistic resources.” It is evident from most portfolios that prospe ctive teachers recognize the growing diversity in schools and the necessity of ensuring equitable access to learning for all students. Acknowledging one’s privileges Many PSTs demonstrated an awareness of the privileges they hold due to aspects of their social identities. These privileges, described as unearned advantages, often  stand in contrast to the barriers experienced by i ndividuals with differing identities. For instance, PST002 reflected on how their identities facilitated her transition to teaching abroad despite her lack of experience and knowledge of the local language and culture: “When I arrived in Korea, I had never lived in another country, could not spe ak any Korean, and had never taught before. However, my nationality, Western education, and first language granted me the privilege of securing a teaching vis a.” While recognizing these unearned advantages is an essenti al step toward advancing equity and social justice,  critical reflection necessitates a deeper examination of the  biases shaped by these privileges. PSTs need to consider how their privileges influence their worldviews, decisions, and actions within educational and societal contexts. Recognizing past and present injustices PSTs also reflected on both historical and ongoing injustices faced by various groups, with a primary focus on the experiences of Indigenous peoples. These reflection s ranged from newly gained awareness to the expansi on of prior knowledge, underscoring their growing understanding of discrimination, biases  and injustices. PST009 expressed how they felt pertaining to such injustices: “I was in disbelief over the systemic negligence tha t made it impossible for Indigenous peoples to have the same human rights and freedom of choice as those who are nonIndigenous.” Many participants articulated specific instances of  injustice, such as the underrepresentation of Indigenous peoples in media and politics, inadequate access to essential services like clean water, and the ongoing violation of their rights. PST0 13 noted the shift in their awareness:  “It is like that feeling when you learn a new word and suddenly, you see the word everywhere even  though you were not aware of it before. ” While the reflections predominantly focused on the injustices  faced by Indigenous peoples, PSTs also recognized the discrimination experienced by other marginalized groups, such as the LGBTQ+2 community. Promises of commitment to social justice action PSTs’ reflections showed  commitment to engaging in social justice action in both societal and educational contexts. Their reflections outlined promises and intentions to participate in discourses, advocacy efforts, and activism aimed at challenging and disrupting unjust  systems and structures. PST009, for example, emphasized the necessity of aligning beliefs with action:  “If there's one concrete belief I hold that has been  bolstered by this course it is this: I must walk the walk as well as talk the talk. ” As future educators, many PSTs pledged to decolonize and indigenize their curriculum and pedagogy. While some expressed  general intentions to integrate these approaches into their teaching, others propos ed relatively specific strategies, such as incorporating social justice resources, teaching about social justice is sues, and creating spaces for the use of Indigenous  languages in classrooms. These reflections demonstrate a promising willingne ss among PSTs to engage in meaningful social justice actions.

## Discussion

The intentional design of this course and reflection prompts facilitated PSTs’ engagement with issues including diversity, privilege, historical injustices and indigenization. While honoring diversity, under standing privileges, and recognizing inequities are critical steps towards social justice, it is equally important for PSTs to examine how these privileges shape their perspectives on injustice and inequities. Additionally, it is imperative that critical reflection moves beyond general notions of diversi ty to a more critical engagement with the structura l and historical contexts that perpetuate inequities. Given the cultural and racial demographics of Canadian schools and society, it is imperative for PSTs to critically reflect on the inequities and injustices faced by marginalized groups.
