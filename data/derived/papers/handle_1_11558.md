# Designing Chatbots for Place-based Environmental Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Place-based education facilitates understanding of the links between human activities and ecosystems. We outline the design process to integrate local knowledge into Large Language Models (LLMs) chatbots to promote environmental education. We find that chatbot responses vary with chatbot profiles in discussing climate change’s root causes and economic impacts. Our chatbots ask more questions to connect to students, compared to a default LLM. We discuss how to design LLM-based technology to support learning about place.

## Introduction and background

Place-based education—engaging learners in the environment, culture, and experiences of local community—can facilitate understanding of the interrelations betw een human activities and ecosystem health (Jordan e t al., 2009; Zangori & Cole, 2019), wellbeing (Sampson & Gifford, 2010), and pro-environmental behaviors (Duerden & Witt, 2010). We designed chatbots using Large Language Models (LLMs) to facilitate dialogues about place. The technology is embedded in a four-week, high school environmental science curriculum (grades 11-12). Students construct models of climate change’s impact on marine ecosystems, co llect community interviews and environmental data, and interact with different chatbots to refine the ir models. The chatbots roleplay as local stakeholders, such as a civil engineer  or a fisherman, to integrate place-based perspectives into chatbot design. We report on the chatbot design before classroom implementation. We ask: How do the chatbots leverage placebased dimensions in answering questions about local environmental issues? Our chatbot design is informed by place-based education (PBE) frameworks, which connect schooling’s discourse with the local environment, community pra ctices, and environmental justice (Bowers, 2001; Gruenewald, 2003; Smith, 2002; Sobel, 2004). In a review of 149 articles, Yemini et al. (2023) built on Ardoin et al. (2012) to categorize four PBE dimension: political-economic (i.e., investment in a place), socio-cultural (i.e., awareness and agency to engage in pro-environmental actions), biophysical (i.e., environmental concerns and physical connections with places), and psychological (i.e., environment’s impact on identity, values, and beliefs). We leverage LLMs to develop chatbots to facilitate learning about place. A challenge in using LLMs to simulate perspectives is that the models may include superficial place-based details (Kim et al., 2024). To address this challenge, we connect the LLM (GPT4-o) to external databases (e.g., using Retrieval-Augmented Generation; RAG; Lewis et al., 2020). We buil d on prior research, which found that LLMs connected to different databases with updated climate reports outperformed a default LLM (GPT-4) in accuracy (Vaghefi et al., 2023).

## Methods

Our chatbots represent the perspectives of a civil engineer, an influencer, a college student, a kelp researcher, and a fisherman. These profiles were co-designed with three high school students, four undergraduate  students, two formal and five informal educators (expertise in ou tdoors education  and science communication) over eight months. We focused on representing the chatbots through locally situated experiences (Nguyen et al., 2025). For this, we created a corpus of interviews with commun ity members embodying the chatbot profiles and created a database consisting of the interview transcripts, local news and scientific articles, and videos to illustrate learning concepts. The co-design and interview procedures were approved by the university’s Institutional Review Board. We leveraged Retrieval-Augmented Generation (Lewis et al., 2020) with Lang Chain, so the chatbots could reference the database in response generation  (Figure 1). We prompted the chatbots to “ask questi ons about climate change’s impact in communities” and “keep it conversational”. To answer the research question and evaluate the effectiveness of our design (before im plementing the chatbots with students), we compared 138 responses generated by (1) our chatbots to those (2) created with a default LLM (GPT-4o; API calls in September 2024; 23 responses per chatbot profile). We used the same prompts for both conditions. The prompts focused on climate change’s impact on marine ecosystems, politics, economics, and health in several in-land and coastal cities in Southern California. We inductively developed a cod ebook through three discussions between the first three authors. We coded a data sample (20%) to generate initial codes, grouped codes, and built on PBE dimensions (Yemini et al., 2023; Ardoin et al., 2012) to rename the co de categories. The final codes  (subcodes in parentheses)  included climate change’s root causes (urbanization, wildfires), biophysical (biodiversity, ecosystem service, climatic change),  sociocultural (mitigation, health and wellbeing), economic, and psychological (connecting to students’ interests and experiences). We added a code for evidence, to evaluate whether the responses cited place-based, external resources. The first two authors separately coded an additional 16.7% of the data, achieved sub stantial inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s 𝜅 range.62-1), and split the data to code.

## Discussion and conclusion

Limited research has focused on the psychological dimension  of PBE —inviting students to reflect on the connection between climate change and their values, beliefs, and experiences (Yemini et al., 2023). Our findings illustrate the design of LLM-enabled chatbots to show different perspectives and  promote psychological links to students’ interests, experiences, reflection, and a ctions. Future work can examine students’ interactions with the chatbots and how the interactions promote learning about place. Researchers should carefully examine the balance between using chatbots to teach PBE and the resource demands to develop the chatbots.
