# Towards Consequential Arts: Educators’ Conceptions of the Arts’ Roles in Learning and Change Making

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The arts have great potential to contribute to learning and change making. However, many educational environments have not meaningfully invested in supporting consequential art. In exploring the potential of arts in both disciplinary and transdisciplinary learning, it is essential to consider educators' conceptions of the arts and how these conceptions might be shaped by sociocultural factors. This study analyzed 51 interviews with educators and found that 90.1% articulated views about the arts’ roles in learning and change making, which can be grouped into 20 conceptions, 5 of which were especially prominent.

## Introduction

As the arts have great potential to open possibilit ies in and beyond educational environments, our res earch explores educators’ conceptions of the arts’ role i n learning and change making. There is a growing sc holarly recognition that learning is both a process of tran sforming ourselves and a motivator of ethical change (Escobar, 2017; Rogoff, 2023). Change making education might refer to attuning l earners to relational responsibilities intrinsic to living (Boswell, 2024)  and empowering ethical decision making. Our researc h emerges from Learning in Places (LIP), a research and design pro ject that opens spaces for expansive and heterogene ous learning through codesign with educators and studen ts. In this way, this work is grounded in communiti es that support change making in educational spaces such th at young people both experience robust and “liberat ory forms of disciplinary learning” (Warren et al., 2020, p. 277) and feel agency to contribute to just and sustainab le futures (Vossoughi et al., 2023).  This paper explores educators’ conceptions of arts prior to their participation in the program. Our findings show that 90.1% of edu cators have conceptions of the arts’ consequential roles in learning and change making, which can be grouped in to 20 conceptions, 5 of which were especially prominent. This work lays the foundation for design research t hat builds with educators’ existing conceptions and  might open possibilities in arts both in schools and beyond.

## Background: The arts across educational environments

From time immemorial artistic processes have been  considered socially, culturally, and politically em bedded praxis. The arts play key roles in carrying and sharing histories, practicing cultural knowledge (Barajas-López & Bang, 2018), holding space for emotions and affect, building c ommunity, and blurring the line between reality and imagination (Barry & Keane, 2019; Selfridge et al., 2021). The arts are consequential acts of resistance and survival (Kelley & Monet, 2022), especially for those surviving the domination of the current global order (Lorde, 1973). Currently, the arts as included in educational se ttings have been acknowledged for motivating curiosity, supporting metacognitive skills (Lapum &  Hume, 2015), providing unique forms of communicati on (Anning & Ring, 2004), encouraging risk-taking (Halverson, 2021), developing creativity (Sawyer, 2018), and facilitating cross-disciplinary transfer (Weyer & Dell’Erba, 2022). While all these contributions are important, we might consider how only valuing conceptions of arts that are largely subser vient to narrowly conceived academic learning might limit the potential of the arts to contribute to liberatory and expansive possibilities. Most schools largely separate content into unconnec ted disciplines. Disciplinary conceptions of learning are so ingrained in our systems that decis ions to design learning environments in this way ar e largely taken for granted. Even in environments like LIP, w hich seek to move beyond siloed disciplin es, it is essential to consider our conceptions of disciplines and acco unt for how they might impact our teaching and lear ning. In disciplinary learning environments, the arts have l argely been excluded or positioned as a less import ant form of learning. Many schools do not offer even elective a rts courses and “decreased arts availability” corre lates with schools serving lower income communities (Elpus, 2022, p. 50). Furthermore, schools dedicate less time for educators to engage with professional development i n arts compared to other subjects, leading many conventional educational settings toneglect to pro vide intentional forms of arts instruction (Anning & Ring, 2004). When educational environments lack informed arts instruction, it discourages children from engaging in artistic activities (e.g. Willats, 2006). Lindsay (2021) showed that educators who are not provided opportunities to build confidence or knowledge in arts pedagogy can pass on negative disciplinary identities to their students. Some interdisciplinary educational approaches attem pt to integrate the arts with other disciplines, bu t in these settings the arts are often positioned as secondary to other forms of learning. A currently p revalent approach, STEAM, integrates arts into science, tech nology, engineering and math (STEM). These subjects  are associated with economic benefits: jobs in STEM fie lds are projected to increase by 11% from 2020 to 2 030 (Weyer & Dell’Erba, 2022). A common critique of STEAM is that the arts are u sed primarily to enhance the teaching and learning of STEM disciplines (Liao, 2016). In these environments, there is rarely intentiona l arts instruction or meaningful engagement with the expan sive potentials that live at and beyond the interse ctions of arts and STEM. This is particularly true among unde rrepresented groups where arts are often primarily celebrated as a method for increasing diverse engag ement in STEM (Roberts & Horn, 2024). This co-optation of arts might be both well-intentioned and a way of preparing a greater divers ity of people to  work in STEM industries which often directly and indirectly uphold systems of capitalism and coloniality (Fúnez-Flores, 2024). Our review of arts integration in both disciplinary  and interdisciplinary settings informs how we migh t approach expanding the possibilities of arts in tra nsdisciplinary learning environments. We hold that educators and all people are mediated by the social activity systems we live within (Vygotsky & Cole, 1978); we are all shaped by and shaping societal landscapes  of arts conceptions. Therefore, in designing for any learning environment that includes the arts, it is important to understand educators’ different goals and conceptions. Educat ors and their students learn through routine outdoo r wonderings, gathering and organizing their wonderin gs, and developing “ Should We” questions. Rather than focusing on science, civ ics, language arts, math, or arts individually, thi s pedagogical approach provides students with opportu nities to connect their interests, questions, and c ultural knowledge, supporting them to engage with disciplin ary knowledge in personally and culturally meaningf ul ways. ## Methods and analysis

In the summer of 2024, the LIP research team conduc ted 107 pre-interviews with educators and educational leaders in anticipation of the 2024-2025 school year. 51 interviews were available for this initial analysis, including 44 educators who work directly with stude nts, 4 whose roles are part time with students, and 3 who are educational leaders or professional development  providers. All educators were given three letter pseudonyms. The structured interviews aimed to prov ide insight into broad topics, including but not li mited to: “General Background, Motivation, and Interest” (3 r equired questions), “Field-Based Learning” (4 required questions), “Interdisciplinary Learning” (5 require d questions), and “Culture, Learning, & Equity”  (5 required questions). Across the interview, two questions exp licitly prompted discussion of art. This study incl uded the responses from the explicit questions focused on ar ts as well as any other moments when educators talk ed about arts unprompted. While we take an expansive approac h to understanding the arts (Warren et al., 2020), which entails resisting boundaries on what can be conside red an artistic practice, we only coded speech as s pontaneous art talk if it included drawing,  painting, making music, writing poetry, or dancing, unless the educator explicitly referred to another practice as artistic. ## Results and discussion

In the 51 educator interviews, 46 educators verbalized  conceptions of the arts’ roles in learning and change making (90.1%). Ninety-nine of these moments were “Emergent talk”. Among the 20 conceptions of the arts’ roles in learning and change making, 5 conceptions were present in over 25% of educator interviews (Table 2). Commonly reported conceptions often involve positio ning arts as in service of  school learning; educators discussed arts as “Aiding/increasing lear ning in other disciplinary areas”  (41.58%) saying things like “Art is a good way to explain science” (Ang). We found nine explicit intersections between “Agency to see, conceive, or observe the world in new ways” and the  “aiding/increasing learning in other disciplinary areas”. For example, Iri discussed how arts increase engagement in disciplinary school learning by affording opportunities to communicate in meaningful and exploratory ways otherwise not supported by schools: I think when you can cross things, especially like the arts, with things, that the engagement increases and it gives students more choice and opportunity for how they want to either learn or show their learning. And… you can hit more of your learning targets and goals as a teacher in one thing, rather than having to teach everything separately. This quote demonstrates that although Iri is in fav or of arts integration and recognizes the arts’ rol es in shifting both “how” learning occurs and is communicated, this might often be in service of traditional educational goals. Frequency of positioning arts in service of other d isciplines leads to arts’ role in learning being of ten limited to shifting “how” we learn, rather than expanding “what” we have opportunity to learn. “Agency and space to feel and express affect/emotio ns” can exemplify how arts are integrated into classrooms to compensate for areas where schools do n’t prioritize supporting students. For example, Jo y said “I think that arts is a good way of expressing like priority and emotion. ” Research has documented the importance of affect within learning (Sherry-Wagner, 2023), but in many ways calls for school to increase me aningful engagement with learners’ affect are yet to be appr opriately addressed (Mirra & Garcia, 2023; National Research Council, 2012). This issue is especially urgent among BIPOC commu nities as “Black, brown, or native peoples’ affects that have systematically be en excluded from curricula and pedagogies” (Zembylas, 2022, p. 346). In many cases arts are positioned to compensate f or the ways schools deny students’ affect, allowing students to form positive learning identities at school (Nasir, 2002) rather than meaningfully shifting the culture of schools such that affect and emotions are treate d as inseparable from learning rather than something ext ra. For example, another educator, Eli, said “I think t hat for some, if they're not academically inclined in the traditional sense, the arts can be a savior.” Inste ad of shifting the atmosphere of schools such that all students are welcomed and made to feel smart or academically inclined, art might be positioned to keep students invested in a system that is often not designed to support their needs and strengths. The final prominent conception, “Art as living cult ure,” shows us that educators see arts as a place f or learning with and through culture. In some instances, we found teachers practicing arts as forms of reviv al, while other educators used arts to bolster schools’  lacking meaningful engagement with students’ and t heir families’ cultures. Schools don’t consistently make  space for students' cultural ways of knowing and b eing; in many cases schools treat “culture as an overlay to learning” (Mirra & Garcia, 2023, p. 545). For example, “For Black History Month, we did black inventors, … I tu rned our assembly into Project Runway. I made a red carpet come down, and all the kids created using gl itter and construction and cardboard” (Kai). The pr actice of doing arts for assemblies is often engaging for kid s, teachers, and families, but only including cultu re and the arts during special events exemplifies approaching “culture as an overlay to learning,” rather than fr aming learning as intrinsically cultural. However, within  this group we also found six teachers who talked about arts as “Agency for resistance and resurgence.” [Planning music] forces me to push myself outside of that Western bubble of music, because that's what was pushed for so long… [I think] about the cultures that my students come from, and how am I able to pull music from that without devaluing or valuing one music or the other, knowing that we can all learn from those different cultural practices and music. (Mrs) While schools frequently foreclose decoloniality  and resurgence, there are exceptions when it comes to schools that are led by nondominant groups. Iri, an indigen ous teacher at a tribal school said, “We did a Sali sh arts salmon. Every fifth grader made their own and learn ed about that style of art.” Through arts, Iri’s class practiced an “everyday” act of resurgence, where the class “d isrupts the colonial physical, social and political  boundaries designed to impede [their] actions to restore [their] nationhood” (Corntassel, 2012, p. 88). In this example, when the school was already investing in decoloniality, arts were supported as a practice of resurgence.

## Conclusions and future directions

Our current lack of meaningful and expansive engage ment with arts in educational environments foreclos es learning possibilities for children. Educators larg ely don’t have time or access to pedagogical resour ces or professional learning to support developing, expand ing, or sharing their current conceptions of arts a nd approaches to arts in their classrooms. Despite wor king within systems that limit artistic possibiliti es, educators have diverse and positive conceptions of the arts ’ roles in learning and change making. This work calls for schools and design researchers to provide educators  with time, pedagogical resources, and professional learning in arts and recognizes the potential in supporting educators' expansive conceptions of art.  One design move that could facilitate this is creat ing opportunities for educators, students, their fa milies, and communities to enhance each other’s  conceptions of the roles of the arts in education and change making.  Our future research will explore if and how educators’ conceptions change af ter participation in LIP and how educator’s concept ions align and differ from their learning and change mak ing practices that engage art. We will also work wi th children in LIP to explore pedagogical conditions t hat impact how young people conceptualize and practice arts and change making. Our hopes are not for every educ ator or every student to practice or conceive of th e arts in all the ways described in this paper; rather  we hope for systems and programs to support educato rs and students conceiving and practicing arts in expansive, consequential ways.
