# Sustainable Interdisciplinary Collaboration among Early Career Learning Scientists

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract: We invite learning scientists to explore ways to foster interdisciplinary collaboration among early career scholars. Drawing on collaborative reflections, we examine our experiences highlighting challenges and potential future research to cultivate sustainable interdisciplinary collaboration among early career scholars in ISLS. We propose creating tangible entry points, committing to shared goals, weaving mentorship with open-ended inquiry, and supporting flow-like states of play as actionable starting points for building a more robust and inclusive infrastructure to support early career scholars.

## Introduction and theoretical framing

Interdisciplinary collaboration is essential to advancement in the Learning Sciences (LS) due to the field’s inherent diversity and the integration of knowledge across d isciplines (Markauskaite et al., 2024; Yoon & Hmelo-Silver, 2017). Interdisciplinary collaboration requires integrating knowledge, practices, perspectives, and cultures across disciplines and non-academic knowledge fields  (Markauskaite et al.). While the LS field emphasizes the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration (Sawyer, 2022), there is a need to better understand and support such collaboration among graduate students and early career learning scientists. These scholars face several challenges, such as their entry into professional fields, navigating disciplinary boundaries, publishing, building networks, and maintaining engagement with peers within and between institutions (Eley et al., 2012). Although interdisciplinary collaboration can enhance their development and pro fessional advancement, the lack of sustained mechan isms for collaboration may hinder their ability to grow in the field and contribute to its long-term development. Drawing on our experiences as early career scholars in the International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS), we explore how to cultivate and sustain interdisc iplinary collaboration. While extensive research has examined collaboration in formal education (e.g., Dillenbourg, 1999) and the workplace (e.g., Edmondson, 1999; Wenger, 1998) our investigation addresses the unique collaborative dynamics of early career LS scholars as they initiate and sustain collaboration with peers in an academic society, working across disciplinary, institutional and national boundaries. Addressing the complexities of interdisciplinary wo rk requires identifying the factors and tensions that shape productive engagement and team learning (Frickel et al., 2017; Markauskaite et al., 2024). By investigating these conditions, we aim to support early career scholars to intentionally foster inclusive and sustainable work environments which they can shape over time to bolster their own professional growth, and thus the growth and innovation of the LS. We emphasize “true” collaboration, characterized by  sustained joint engagement, mutual transformation of understanding, and collective knowledge-building (Jeong & Hmelo-Silver, 2016; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). We contend that exploring th is research area could provide early  career scholars with the tools and opportunities they need  to thrive, thereby supporting themselves and the l arger LS community. Thus,  we propose that early career scholars can be transf ormative agents, contributing to “world-making” by creating and implementing solutions to global challenges—a central focus in the learning sciences.

## Method

This poster shares a reflective inquiry into the co nditions that foster productive interdisciplinary e ngagement (Markauskaite et al., 2024) among early career lear ning scientists in ISLS. Formed after ISLS 2024, initially we aimed to sustain connections and collaboration through mutual support, feedback, and advice. As we reflected on our earlier experiences during our Zoom calls and WhatsApp messages, it evolved into a space for examining the recurring challenges and successes of starting and sustaining interdisciplinary work among the ISLS community. To analyze our experiences, we drew on Markauskaite  et al.’s guiding question: What are the conditions that facilitate productive interdisciplinary engagement?  We each  reflected on our experiences of participating in interdisciplinary teams at ISLS using the following prompts:  1) Describe an experience in which you attempted to take part in an interdisciplinary team within th e learning sciences community. 2) What tensions or challenges arose in that experience?  3) What new understanding or questions emerged, either  individually or as a group  in the experience? After individually writing responses to these promp ts, we conducted content analysis  of written and verbal reflection (individually and as a group) to explore factors related to our experiences (Mayring, 2015). We engaged in multiple rounds of reading each other’ s reflections, discussing similarities and differen ces in our experiences during structured peer debriefing sessi ons (Stahl & King, 2020).  Through the iterative review and discussions, we collectively identified emergent themes related to the conditions related to sustained interdisciplinary engagement among early  career scholars in ISLS. For example: the theme, “Creating tangible entry points for unheard voices” was built from the initial codes: access to resources, blockages, institutional rules.

## Results

Our findings present four themes that were drawn from our reflections. Creating tangible entry points for unheard voices (Alekh). As an early career learning scientist from India, I see the ISLS annual meetings as rare oppor tunities for interdisciplinary engagements towards  learning science research. With a limited number of researchers working in L S in India, the accessibility to material and human resources are at blockages. As my research area is that of maker education, research challenges take further heights since maker movement is still on the way to  gain traction, and yet to flourish in India as compared to the US and Europe. The institutional guidelines dd another layer of complexity in attending these m eetings with the challenges of funds, locations, proposal acceptance etc. Although ISLS annual meetings offer great opportunities for the unheard voices, gaining and sustaining acce ss to the conference and its networks remains contained and limited. These factors force me to question: How and what kinds of sustain able entry points can be created for unheard voices towards early career interdisciplinary engagement within the learning science community? A commitment to shared goals and strategies to achieve them (Danielle). I served on a committee tasked with helping learning scientists develop solutions to problems of practice, which provided a valuable yet challenging interdisciplinary experience. One of th e main tensions that arose was a lack of clarity and consensus around the committee’s aims and the strategies to achieve them. While we generated numerous ideas, both within the committee and through discussions with initiative participants, there was little agreement on what our primary focus should be or how to move forward collaborativ ely. Despite our best intentions and initial commit ment to the initiative, without a clear goal and strategies to meet our goals, our initiative lacked focus, and thus direction. I therefore ask: How can early  career scholars foster shared goals to sustain coll aboration, connection, and commitment to actionable strategies to achieve those goals? Weaving mentorship into collaborative growth (Jinzhi). My journey with interdisciplinary collaboration in LS has been marked by moments of both promise and challenge. I had an opportunity to organize a symposium bringing together diverse projects across disciplin es. The senior scholars on my project team guide d me through every step, helping me connect with different schol ars. The symposium with senior scholars facilitated rich interdisciplinary dialogue and positive outcomes, yet I discovered that shifting to such work with fellow graduate students through the ISLS without intensive leaderships from senior scholars proved surprisingly difficult. These experiences, particularly the contrast between the well-supported symposium and the more organic student groups, make me wonder: How might we balance the st ructure and support of senior mentorship with the openended peer-initiated collaboration to create sustainable interdisciplinary partnerships? Supporting flow-like states of play  in interdisciplinary problem solving  (Tom). Our group created this poster to address the complex challenge of supporting interdisciplinary collaboration among early career scholars in ISLS. I was drawn to this challenge because it f elt like a fun puzzle —an opportunity to play with ideas, be creative, and achieve something new. In r eality we faced difficulties, and this framing shif ted. We were often hamstrung, unsure of how to begin, struggling to elicit and integrate ideas and practices from our local work into this new setting. This contrasts with the more fluid and generative interactions that characterize the initiation and deep flow-like states of play, where people become immersed i n solving a puzzle or playing a game. This prompted me to reflect: How does the way early career learning scientists (re)frame and approach problems during interdisciplinary collaboration relate to creativity, flow, and the development of ideas and practices?

## Discussion and conclusion

Our groups reflections on the lived experiences of early career scholars in the Learning Sciences reveals four key themes. The themes, respectively, connect to ongoing lines of inquiry in LS such as 1) access, internationalization, and broadening participation, 2) joint problem spaces and agency  3) structured support/scaffolding versus openended inquiry, and 4) framing and affect. We invite colleagues to join us in taking up research in these areas with early career scholars in the LS community. In this poster, we build on Markauskaite et al.’s (2024) special issue which is itself an example of sustained inte rdisciplinary engagement and progressive idea impro vement by scholars in LS as they work across ISLS workshops and empirical projects. The research directions outlined above offer pathways to extend such practices by empoweri ng early career scholars to cultivate the opportuni ties they need, strengthen the ISLS community, and contribute  to 'world-making' by envisioning and implementing innovative educational designs through true interdisciplinary collaboration.
