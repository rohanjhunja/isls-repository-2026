# Inform, Empathize, Inquire: How Youth Use Participatory Storytelling to Engage With Social Issues

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We explored how youth created participatory stories—a genre of interactive fiction that logs players’ choices as data to be analyzed—to advocate for social change. We designed a workshop to guide 22 high-school-aged youth in creating participatory stories about social issues of their choice. Analyses of their vision statements, stories, and reflections, revealed three ways that youth used this medium: to inform players, to provoke empathy, and to inquire into human behavior related to their chosen social issues. This study shows the potential for participatory storytelling to amplify youth’s agency to take social action.

## Introduction and background: Storytelling and social change

Whereas research has examined youth’s creation of g ames and stories to engage audiences in reasoning a nd empathizing about social issues (Dasgupta & Hill, 2017; Lee & Garcia, 2014), few tools exist that allow youth to conduct socially engaged inquiry. We examine how yo uth created participatory stories, a genre of interactive fiction that logs player data, to both engage audie nces with social issues, and to generate player dat a to pursue personal inquiries into those social issues.

Participatory stories build on the emotional impacts and empathy-building qualities of stories (Junker & Jacquemin, 2017; Koopman & Hakemulder, 2015), immer sing players in a role, and prompting them to make choices that have narrative consequences. When stor ies are themed around social issues, these choices allow audiences to reflect on how their actions contribute to those social issues, and to even replay and observe different outcomes. Designers can furthermore create participatory stories that investigate particular questions, and analyze players’ data to gain insight into the answers. Suc h inquiry, performed by and/or for those impacted by a social issue, can be an act of social change, in that it can engage and highlight marginalized perspectives (Freire, 2018). To understand the potential of this novel medium as a tool for social action, we ask: (1) How do youth use participatory storytelling to address social issues that matter to them?

## Methods: Context, participants, and data

Workshop design The authors designed and facilitated—with 3 other colleagues—a virtual summer workshop (1 hour-long Zoom meeting/week for 4 weeks) that guided high school aged youth in creating participatory stories to address social issues of their choice. Through whole group and bre akout room activities, we introduced youth to the v alue of player data in games for social change, and facilitated their identification and selection of social issues (Day 1). We guided youth in using the free interactive fiction creation platform, StudyCrafter (studycrafter.com, see Figure

1) (Harteveld et al., 2017), offered written feedba ck on their ideas, and assisted them in recruiting players by

sharing links to their stories to members of our graduate programs (Days 2 and 3). Each story accumulated ~4-25 players by the end of the workshop (stories completed and posted earlier had more time to attract players). Finally, youth shared insights from their player data, and on their experiences (Day 4). Between sessions, we used Slack to prompt youth to share updates, assist them with technical issues, and offer feedback on their work.

To describe how youth used storytelling to address their chosen social issues, we first categorized youth’s chosen social issues in terms of their broader doma in (education, environment, etc.). Using an open co ding approach, three researchers then read and discussed each of youth’s vision statements, StudyCrafter stories, and final written reflections to identify salient or re curring themes (Corbin & Strauss, 1990). To achieve  inter-rater reliability, we used a consensus building process (Baxter & Jack, 2015), in which three researchers developed and iteratively refined categories through joint discussion of each set of artifacts. The result was three, non-mutually exclusive categories describing the spectrum of youth's purposes for their stories (see Table 1): to inform, to provoke empathy, and to inquire. ## Overview

Youth chose topics from their personal or community  experiences, which covered the domains of environm ent (e.g., climate change attitudes) (N=5), education (e.g., school budgeting priorities) (N=4), mental and physical health (e.g., supporting friends suffering from dep ression) (N=4), socioeconomic (e.g., healthcare acc ess inequality) (N=3), and technology issues (phone addiction) (N=1). All 17 stories sought to inform players about a social issue, such as by providing  information about its prevalence or impact (e.g., I want players “to real ly consider all angles of [an environmental] proble m [and] to understand the [impacts on all involved.]”). A subs et of these 17 stories had the additional goal of e ncouraging players to empathize, such as by allowing them to experience a situation from the perspective of someone impacted (e.g., I want players to feel “the emotions and tho ught process that a family with limited resources w ould go through.”). A further subset of stories also aimed to use player data to inquire into some question about the chosen issue (e.g., I want to know “When most people think  they should get [mental health] help,” and when to  “push others to get help.”). Categorized in terms of the main purposes that youth described, 12 stories sought mainly to inquire; 4 sought mainly to provoke empathy; and 1 story sought mainly to inform. Stories that inform and empathize While each of the stories sought to inform players of a social issue, only one youth in our sample, Emily, had ‘to inform” as her sole goal. In her story, the player is the town mayor, and needs to decide how to address the issue of increasing sargassum algae on its beaches while mitigating impacts on tourism. For Emily, awareness of the complexity of this environmental issue was the ultimate goal. As she writes, she wanted players “to really consider all angles of the problem before making a decision for them to understand the complexity of these issues.” Four stories had the additional goal of generating players’ empathy for an issue. Youth who created such stories expressed a desire for players to feel “str essed,” and “upset,” and to appreciate the unfairne ss of certain situations. These stories had players experience issues such as mental health stigma, inequitable resource access, and the lack or burden of power felt by people with  different societal roles (e.g., managing a school budget, deciding to help a friend with depression, considering unaffordable medical treatment). For example, Yuka wanted ICLS2022 Proceedings 1478 © ISLS her players to feel “conflicted by the difficult ch oices ppl make abt their health based on affordabil ity.” In her story, players make a family’s budgeting decisions, each of which is a compromise between the family members’ individual health needs (therapy, medication). Players then observe the unavoidable impacts of their choices (the father’s ER visit, the mother’s burnout, the daughter’s failing grades). Similarly, Angela sought to generate sympathy for those impacted by socioeconomic disparities. In her story, players follow two characters—one wealthy, the other in poverty—and make a series of choices for them (e.g., to take a job, to apply for college). By mak ing different decisions on replaying the story, it becomes clear that these choices are inconsequential to the characters’ outcomes. Thus, Angela sought to challenge the myth of meritocracy by showing that “no matter how much [th e characters] try, the economic disparity between t hem clearly hold the poorer one back.” By offering inconsequential choices, her design sought to give players the sense that there is no escaping the path determined by one’s existing resources. Stories that inquire Twelve of the youth’s stories were designed to gene rate data that would address a question about their  chosen social issue. For example, Sang’s story (see Figure  2) had players take on the role of an injured teen  who must decide whether to ask for medical treatment that his parents cannot afford. While this story aimed to inform players about healthcare equity, and to promote their empathy for people with limited healthcare access, it also collected data on players’ choices to better understand the h ealthcare choices people make when financially cons trained. Sang created a pie chart of his 25 players’ choices, and observed that 40% chose to go to the hospital while 60% chose to deal with the injury at home. He hypothesi zed that “people were valuing their family over the mselves and/or the injury not being serious enough to warrant a visit,” and wondered about the “severity of an injury that is necessary to warrant a visit to a clinic no matter what financial situation.” These observations prompted Sang to propose a follow-up inquiry that he might conduct through an iteration on his story: “I would make the injury more severe to see if there would be a more even split among players on whether they would go to the hospital or not. With a minor ankle injury, it’s easier for pla yers to think that a hospital visit isn’t necessary. But, a more severe injury would eliminate that variable and give much harder choices with bigger consequences.” Figure 2 Top: Scenes from Sang's story about healthcare inequities, with both closed and open-ended player choices. ## Discussion and Significance

This study illustrates the potential of participatory storytelling for engaging youth in acting on social issues that concern them. By having players make decisions in p articular roles and situations, youth creators used  this medium to raise awareness of issues, to provoke emp athy for those impacted, and to generate data for i nquiry. Youth’s analyses of their player data supported dee per explorations of their chosen issues, and also p rompted ideas for iterations to improve the clarity of their message, and new directions for inquiry. These findings suggest that participatory storytelling can amplify youth’s agency and voice to advocate for change. While some of our participants’ stories were unclear or incomplete, our findings suggest that with sufficient guidance a nd support, participatory storytelling can be an effective tool for socially engaged inquiry by all youth.
