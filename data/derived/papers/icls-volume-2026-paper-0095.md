# The Role of Gesture and Materiality In Co-Constructing Mechanistic Explanations

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite increasing scholarly attention to the role of the physical body in student sensemaking, few studies have explored how gesture and materiality factor into students’ engagement in scientific modeling and co-construction of explanations. In this paper, we present a qualitative case study that examines children’s explanation construction through modeling over multiple days. We draw on a conceptual framework that interweaves theories of embodied learning and new materialism with resource-based views of explanations and classroom modeling practices. Our analysis illustrates how gesture and interactions with materials can support children to reason mechanistically, communicate their thinking, and co-construct explanations by 1) activating sensorimotor resources that help children formulate and experience new ideas, and, in the case of gesture, 2) serving as a discursive and modeling practice that supports meaning-making and conceptual progress.

Researchers have increasingly framed students’ sensemaking and learning as embodied, that is, as linked to action, perception, and the physical body (Walkington et al., 2019; Jha & Price, 2022; Wilmes & Siry, 2024). However, few studies have explored the role of the body and materiality in students’ engagement in scientific modeling and co-construction of  mechanistic explanations (Mathayas et al., 2019). S pecifically, research that extends over longer time periods and treats gesture and materiality as c entral to explanation is needed. In this paper, we trace the development of a mechanistic explanation of seed travel through the lens of one student, Maya, as she interacts with materials and engages in gestural exchanges with her classmates. We share how gesture and interaction serve as socially situated modeling practices that contribute to the development of a shared explanation. Co-construction of mechanistic explanations We focus on students’ development of causal explanations via mechanistic reasoning. We define mechanistic reasoning as thinking that identifies the underlying entities and activities that cause a phenomenon and includes linking information about how those entities and activities interact with one another (Krist et al., 2018; Russ, 2008). We draw on fine-grained resource-based perspectives on explanation construction (and learning more generally) (Hammer et al., 2005). From this point of view, we are interested i n the resources students draw on to construct explanations, including how they come to see ideas as relevant and connect those ideas  in light of their (emerging) goals. Rather than only focusing on cognitive resources (e.g., those in “students’ heads), we draw on situated and embodied perspectives  to widen our lens. We consider explanations as co-constructed across interactional exchanges (Siry et al., 2012) and explore resources children call upon within the classroom environment, such as materials, charts, and pictures (Manz, 2012; Varelas et al., 2007; Tang, 2022). Scientific modeling In classroom practice, models have been used as a vehicle and instructional support for explanation development. Scientific models are external representations that help scientists predict and explain how the world works (Windschitl et al., 2008). They are composed of hypothesized relationships among objects, processes, and events that are repeatedly tested against evidence and refined in an iterative process (Constantinou et al., 2019). Historically, models used in the classroom have taken the form of paper and computer representations (NGSS Lead States, 2013),  with gesture typically positioned as a translanguaging tool for multimodal communication about models (Pierson, et al., 2020). New research is expanding notions of what constitutes a model and the kinds of modeling practices students engage in, for example by designing opportunities for students to use dance or theatrical storytelling to represent and support their learning (e.g., Reimers & Brady, 2019). Embodied learning: Gesture and interaction with materials In this study, we explore how gesture and materiality contribute to students’ development of explanations through modeling. Theories of embodied learning reject the Cartesian view that the mind is separate from the body, 830 favoring instead a view that all knowledge is grounded in actual or imagined sensori motor experiences (Gallese & Lakoff, 2005; Macrine & Fugate, 2021). This perspective is increasingly supported by evidence from neuroscientific studies that show that even “abstract” concepts such as number concepts and language processing (e.g., Bert eletti & Booth, 2015; Glenberg & Kaschak, 2002) are embodied, for example,  through evidence of activation of auditory and tactual systems.

In classrooms, students ’ gestures and interactions with materials are fruitful areas for better understanding and better supporting embodied learning. Gestures have been shown to support communication and meaning-making in several ways, including by linking abstract and perceptually available elements (Roth & Weltzel, 2001) and providing visuospatial inscriptions that recipients can use to refine their own ideas (Singer et al., 2008). Most  studies linking gestures to conceptual progress have focused on gestures used in discrete episodes (Eneydy, 2005; Reynolds & Reeve, 2001; Singer et al., 2008), however, classroom explanation development typically occur s o ver days or weeks. Many of these works conceptualize gesture as a stepping-stone to the development of more sophisticated explanations. In contrast, we treat gesture and interaction with materials as central to meaning-making, explor ing how they support communication and make emergent sensorimotor resources available for students’ modeling and explanation development. Purpose We present a qualitative case study that examines children’s explanation construction through modeling over multiple days. We seek to bring embodied perspectives more explicitly to bear on understanding children’s explanation construction in classroom science. As described above, we treated explanation as a collective, socially situated practice. We ask:

1) How can embodied learning support children to engage in mechanistic reasoning about seed travel?

2) In what ways can embodiment support students’ communication and co-construction of explanations?

This study is situated within a larger  design-based study of investigation and modeling in elementary school classrooms. The project involved a multi-year co-design partnership with a public school district in the northeast US that has a racially, linguistically, and socio-economically diverse student population. The case focuses on a 2nd-grade (student ages 7 & 8) class of 25 students taught by Ms. Lacey, an experienced elementary school teacher that was in her 5th year of working with the research team.

## Design and data collection

The case is drawn from the class’s investigation of seed dispersal. Children collected different focal seeds in their schoolyard, exploring how those seeds could get tonew places and grow into plants. After making predictions about how their seeds traveled, they worked in groups to conduct three investigations —using a tub of water to consider whether their seed could travel by water, a fan to consider whether their seed might travel by wind, and various cloths to decide if their seed could travel by sticking to animals. They made decisions about how to represent the travel process in the investigation, what to attend to as evidence, and what to conclude from the tests. A key conjecture was that these decisions and discussions would support students to develop mechanistic explanations of seed travel  that drew on and linked the  seed’s structure, the forces that act upon the seed, and other elements of the environment.

In earlier iterations of this investigation, we found that the maple seed predictably  elicited uncertainty about investigation results and motivated students to identify mechanisms for how the seed travels by wind. Students conducted the wind test by placing a fan on a desk and holding their seeds out in front of the fan. With this setup, the maple  seeds dropped almost straight down. The resulting tension between students’ experiences with maple seeds flying outdoors and the maple seed’s poor performance in the test appeared to support students to consider  consequential differences between their test and what happens outside, in turn  supporting them to consider new ideas about forces and starting height of the seed. The case focuses on the multi-day development of  Ms. Lacey’s class’s explanation for how the maple seed travels. We selected this episode because it  comprised a highly successful case of children working within the tensions between their investigation and the phenomenon to develop a mechanistic explanation. Further, we noted children drawing on multiple material and gestural resources as they constructed their explanation, and we observed that the gestures students used achieved various states of stability, refinement, and evolution over the three days of sensemaking chronicled here. Data sources included classroom video  of all whole group work and the maple seed group’s discussions, fieldnotes and class and student artifacts. 831

## Analytic approach

To guide our analysis, we referred to videos and fieldnotes spanning students’ testing of the maple  seed through to students’ final explanations of how the maple seed travels. We traced the construction of the class’s explanation through the lens of one student, Maya. We chose to focus on Maya  for several reasons: first, Maya was very invested in resolving the tensions elicited by the fan test; second, it was evident that Maya was using materials and gesture not only to communicate about her ideas, but also to better understand her own thinking and get new information; third, during whole-group discussion, Maya was involved in negotiations with her peers about which ideas to include in a model and how to include them. We moved sequentially though the data fr om Maya’s interpretation of findings after testing her group’s seed, through classroom discussions and retesting, through to Maya’s final development of a claim and explanation.

We began our analysis by tracing how the class’s co-constructed explanation developed across time and what gestures, talk, and interaction with materials co-occurred with explanation development. To do this, we made multimodal transcripts, recording students’ verbal discourse, gesture, gaze, interaction with materials, and explanatory progress. Drawing from Alibali and Nathan’s 2012 typology, we characterized gesture as representational (depicting aspects of their meaning literally), pointing, or metaphoric  (depicting conceptual metaphors). We also noted instances where gestures were echoed or reused. To describe students’ interaction with materials, we drew from Tang’s (2024) work on materiality in science learning, focusing primarily on interactions related to sensing and manipulating materials. For each instance where students interacted with materials, we recorded what material was used and to what end. Further, we tracked students’ conceptual progress using a finegrained description of the conceptual terrain developed for the project work (an outline of concepts students could attend to, draw on as resources, and connect in explanation). We attended to which of these concepts students drew on and when. We identified progress in students’ mechanistic thinking by tracking when they identified and chained entities and activities at lower scalar levels than the target phenomenon and when they connected lowerlevel interactions to the target phenomenon, drawing these foci from Krist et al. (2018) and Russ et al. (2008).

## Findings & Analysis

We found that interaction with materials supported students to reason mechanistically, communicate, and co-develop explanations of the phenomenon of maple seed flight. One artifact that was central to students’ gesture and explanation construction was a seed structure chart; other materials included maple seeds, a small electric fan, and projected pictures of the maple seed and its parent tree (Figure 1). Students constructed their explanation over three days of working with these materials: as they engaged in small group work to make claims after the fan test, participated in a whole-group claims and evidence discussion facilitated by the maple seed group, re-tested the maple seed by dropping i t from a second-story window, and engaged in a final whole-group discussion (Figure 1). Students’ mechanistic explanation of maple seed flight accounted for and linked the starting height and orientation of the seed, the relation of setup conditions to the triggering of autorotation, the role  of air resistance, the inverse relationship between the seed’s spinning speed and rate of descent, and the alternating descent and lift of the seed during flight.

By the end of the episode, Maya’s gesture and speech signaled a shift in her attention away from wind as the primary force acting on the seed and toward the role of gravity. Finding 2: Gestures served as sensorimotor resources that supported students to coconstruct an increasingly sophisticated, embodied explanatory model In the days following this  small group work, students engaged in whole-group discussions in which they used gesture to co-construct an embodied model of maple seed flight. We conceptualize the collection of  talk and gesture-building during these discussions  as a model because 1) students’ gesture and talk explicated a set of hypothesized relationships among the maple seed, its actions, and the forces that act upon it and 2) many of the gestures either remained relatively  stable over time or were explicitly built upon, or  refined. Further, we understand students' gestures as instantiations of modeling practice because we saw students making decisions about what gestures to include as part of their model and using gestures to think with (Gouvea & Passmore, 2017). Students’ mechanistic explanation became more sophisticated as students moved from identifying entities and actions to chaining entities and activities at lower scalar levels and connecting those lower-level interactions to the target phenomenon, with gesture serving a central role in this development  (Figure 2). Maya’s claim that the maple seed only flies downward became a site of contestation, as students intr oduced new ideas about the role of spinning and the importance of seed orientation. On this day, Maya accommodated some aspects of her peers’ contributions into her own gestural modeling, but not all. For example, while she incorporated spinning and upward movement of the seed into her gestures, she did not take up possible horizontal or fluid movement of the seed. As the shared model began to take shape, a key question for the class was how interactions between the two forces they had identified (wind and gravity) might influence the maple seed’s flight path.

On Day 3, after the class dropped the maple seed from a second-story window to gather more information, they engaged in a whole-group discussion in which they used gesture to refine and iterate ideas related to flight pattern and air resistance. Specifical ly, students gestured about how the maple seed’s flight path is characterized by an up-and-down spinning and gliding motion and reasoned about connections between the seed’s orientation at the time of falling, the triggering of autorotation, and the force of air resistance to keeping the seed aloft.

At this point, Maya may have felt there was not enough evidence to support the inclusion of wind in her tentative explanation (Windschitl, 2008). Further, as in the previous episode, we saw evidence that gesture provided sensorimotor resources for Maya and her peers to think with. For example, when Maya explained her ideas about the seed’s orientation contributing to its ability to spin and fly properly, she threw her arm up, looked up, and paused before verbally finishing her thought. The physical acts of throwing the seed into the air and looking up may have supported Maya (and Paulina who mimicked this gesture) to access resources related to imagining seed flight and experiencing air resistance. Maya repeated her action of throwing the seed into the air in the final whole-group discussion on day 3, during which she made more explicit connections to ideas about lift and air resistance. Our analysis illustrates how gesture and interactions with materials supported children to reason mechanistically, communicate their thinking, and co-construct explanations by activating sensorimotor resources that helped them formulate and experience new ideas. For example, in the first episode, embodied interactions supported Maya to shift her attention toward gravity as the primary force acting on the seed; we also saw others engaging with Maya’s ideas through mimicry.  Further, Maya and her peers used gesture as a discursive and modeling practice as they reasoned about and made bids for the importance of different forces and their possible interactions. These findings build on others’ work showing that gesture and interaction with materials contribute to discourse and sensemaking in science (Crowder, 1996; Mathayas et al., 2019). Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of embodiment for individual explanation construction and refinement of mental models. Our  work builds on and extends 836 previous work by positioning material and gesture as sensorimotor resources that become central to explanations and treating gestural work as a form of modeling practice. Activating and providing sensorimotor resources In this paper, we situated the  understanding that sensory and motor processes are implicated in cognition (Barsalou, 2008; Berteletti & Booth, 2015) within the frame of resource-based views of learning. We interpreted students’ gestures and interaction with materials as instances of sensorimotor resource activation for sensemaking. This conceptualization is consistent with claims that gesture changes thought (Beilock & Goldin-Meadow, 2010) and that gesture is thought (McNeill, 20 05, p.99;  Nathan, 2017). From this viewpoint,  we observed students spontaneously interacting with materials and gesturing in ways that led tonew verbalized or embodied ideas. For example, in the first episode,  Maya gazed at the seed in her hand, toward the fan, and back at the seed before changing her idea that the fan was not powerful enough to make her seed fly. By shifting her gaze, Maya guided her attention toward the material aspects of her test (Grant & Spivey, 2003): the seed and the fan. This attuned her to unsatisfactory elements of the test other than wind power, including the position of the seed when it fell. By moving the seed to different positions relative to the fan, she further engaged in material inquiry that provided resources she later leveraged toward explanation (Tang, 2022). We also saw mimicry, or repeating another students’ gesture, contributing  to student sensemaking. When Maya dropped the maple seed to show that “it doesn’t even need the fan” to fly, Faye mimicked her action by dropping her pencil. Most of the literature on mimicry identifies social functions of the practices, such as for affiliation and disaffiliation, empathy, and self-regulation (Chartrand & Dalton, 2009). However, this analysis suggests that mimicry can also serve a sensemaking purpose and that this  process occurs through activation of sensorimotor resources.

Gesture as an interwoven discursive and modeling practice In addition to supporting individuals to refine mental models (Goldin-Meadow & Beilock, 2010), a collection of gestures can act as a shared, refinable explanatory model. Throughout the case, gestures supported discourse and meaning-making by functioning as redundant to ideas expressed in talk, enhancing ideas  in talk, or expressing ideas not represented in talk (Crowder & Newman, 1993). For example, as Maya brought her arms down to signal the downward path of a falling maple seed, she foregrounded the role of gravity in her explanation. Devon and Ray used both redundant and additive gesture s to elevate wind as an additional force worthy of consideration. Their dissatisfaction with Maya’s representation of maple seed flight as only downward persisted throughout the case. Flight path became a site where gestures were put forward, contested, rejected, taken up and/or refined. As the case continued, Maya and her peers used gesture to co-construct an increasingly detailed explanation of how the maple seed’s structure interacts with the forces that act on it to determine its flight path. Students’ gestural contestations and proposed refinements pushed Maya to think about and articulate new activities and entities relevant to maple seed travel, such as the triggers of autorotation and forces other than gravity. These examples illustrate how gesture served as a discursive and modeling practice by supporting students to pose problems (Phillips et al., 2017), take up or disagree with othe rs’ ideas, and iterate their tentative explanations (Windschitl, 2008).

## Implications

Our analysis suggests the potential of increasing opportunities for sensemaking toward scientific modeling and the co-construction of explanations by: 1) designing for gesture and embodiment to emerge during meaningmaking discussions; 2) making time and space for students to use materials and embodied learning in small and whole-group settings; and 3) legitimating gesture as both a discursive and modeling practice within which to see students’ learning. Where most studies on the role of gesture in sensemaking position gesture as a primitive stepping-stone in a progression of complex symbolization that culminates in language and text  (Yeo & Nielsen, 2020), we  saw that students continued to use gesture s (even generating new  ones) through to the end of explanation development.  We thus argue for researchers and educators to consider  students’ gestures and interaction with materials as discursive and modeling practices  in and of themselves. Such a shift can contribute to calls for researchers to expand conceptualizations of modeling to highlight it as a diverse human sensemaking practice (Schwarz et al., 2022).
