# Queer and Trans Learning: Placemaking, Non/Dominant Imaginaries, and Educational Dignity Towards Resistance

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium, presented as a hybrid structured poster session, brings together work that helps us grow queer-trans perspectives within the learning sciences. Contributors examine a variety of spaces that work either with or against LGBTQ+ youth and adults. We think with prior work in the learning sciences and deploy queer theories, designs, methodologies, and axiologies to better understand learning. These papers are united by their engagement with queer people, places, and perspectives to advance understandings of learning in the current political climate. The papers help us understand how communities position LGBTQ+ people, for good or for ill. The studies here highlight how learning can unfold as oppressive or can unfold as joy, resistance, or resilience in the face of oppression. Together, these papers put queerness and learning into conversation to highlight the affordances of queer-trans perspectives for building actionable, equitable, and rigorous theories of learning.

## Introduction and background

This symposium brings together the growing area of work in the learning sciences that centers queer-trans people, places, and perspectives. We situate our work within a global context of rising hostility toward LGBTQ+ people. Politicians are targeting quee r, and especially trans, people by pursuing antagon istic and exclusionary rhetoric and policies, which seek to stoke hate and fear as well as actively worsen material conditions for LGBTQ+ people. The American Civil Liberties Union (2024) tracked 5 31 anti-LGBTQ+ bills in the U.S., with 207 of these bills targeting schools and education, including, for exa mple, curriculum censorship, school facilities bans, sports participation bans, and the forced outing of LGBTQ+  people by schools. Other examples of anti-LGBTQ+ bills include drag performance and expression bans and ge nder-affirming healthcare restrictions. In Mali, the Transitional National Council recently passed a law that makes homosexuality a criminal offense (Kojoué, 2024). In Canada, anti-trans legisl ation and policies that violate human rights have b een introduced or enacted in numerous provinces, despite strong opposition, with  two provinces (New Brunswick and Saskatchewan) fac ing court cases to challenge the policies and another p rovince (Alberta) likely to face a court challenge upon passing proposed anti-trans legislation (Johnson, 2024; Mason & Hamilton,  2024). Similarly, in April 2024, England introduced new age restrictions on gender-affirming health care for people younger than 16, c ontrary to medical consensus (McMillan, 2024). Across the world, discr iminatory policies and legislation for LGBTQ+ peopl e remain and are even growing (Bisset & Francis, 2023). Queer-trans and allied people are resisting this hateful violence, working to make educational spaces safer and more inclusive, and fighting to maintain access to genderaffirming care (1). In formal education spaces, which are one of the battlegrounds for queer-trans inclusion, school climate surveys indicate significant issues for students ’ sense of safety. In Canada, a national school clima te survey conducted first in 2008/2009 and repeated in 2019/2 020 found that there has been little improvement in  the percentage of students who felt unsafe at school du e to their sexual orientation or perceived sexual o rientation— 53% in 2008/2009 vs. 43% in 2019/2020 (Peter et al., 2021). In the case of gender identity and gender expression, the situation has become significantly worse, rising from 29% who felt unsafe in 2008/2009 to 41% in 2019/2020 (Peter et al., 2021). GLSEN ’s 2021 U.S. school climate survey found that 68% of  LGBTQ+ students felt unsafe at school because of their sexual orientation, gend er identity, or gender expression (Kosciw et al., 2022). These statistics highlight that learning settings are crucial spaces in which queer-positive interventions can be made. Learning is central to how people participate in either LGBT Q+ or anti-trans social movements, digital cultures, communities, and learning environments. M ost projects in this symposium discuss learning amo ngst LGBTQ+ people towards resilience and resistance. Pr oductively, we also include one project that captures h ow people learn to think against LGBTQ+ rights, which helps us better understand lea rning and queerness in this cultural moment. It is clear that learning sciences ideas can be productive in understanding how learning towards (or against) justice takes place. Curnow et al.’s (2019) framework of politicization covered four dime nsions of how people learn to become politicized—political concepts, practices, epistemologies, and identities. With this in mind, we consider how LGBTQ+ people learn towards educational dignity under these conditions, remembering that “dignity can be derived from productive partic ipation in the process of learning. Although perilo us, it can also be acquired from resistance to the inaccessibility of the opportunity to learn” (Espinoza & Vossoughi, 2014, p. 287). In LGBTQ+-affirming spaces, humanizing pedagogies tend to be leveraged, including centering students’ lives and identities, seeing learning environments as transformative places, fostering relationships a mongst learners, and focusing on community-building, among other values (Yoon et al., 2020). In this symposium, we build on growing work in quee r learning sciences (e.g., McWilliams, 2016; Shrodes and Paré, 2022; Uttamchandani, 2021). That work itself builds on scholarship from a variety of perspectives, including queer theory (e.g., Ahmed, 2006; Butler, 2006/1990), curriculum theory (e.g., Keenan, 2017), and critical methodologies (e.g., Fine et al., 2021), among others. Looking across this work highlights how queering learning can help us understand learning better and design m ore inclusive learning environments. We can queer learning in a variety of ways, including queering learning as disrupting both dominant norms  and the notion of “normal,” queering learning as an emancip atory project to redistribute power, and queering learning as prefigurative worldmaking to co-construct desired communities. In this symposium, we build on this prior work usin g queer theories, designs, methodologies, and axiologies to better understand learning. These pap ers are united by their engagement with queer-trans people, places, and perspectives to advance understandings of learning in this climate, and how learning can u nfold as oppression or as joy, resistance, or resilience in the face of that oppression. Together, these papers  put queerness and learning into conversation to highlight the aff ordances of queer-trans perspectives for building actionable, equitable, and rigorous theories of learning and designing inclusive and equitable learning environments. Blake Attending to relationships is one way scholars reco gnize expansive repertoires of historicized making practices (Vossoughi et al., 2016; Davis et al., 2020), learn ing in queer communities (Uttamchandani, 2021), and  in social movements (Vea & Curnow, 2022).  I build with this work to attune to making with a l ens of trans cultural production (Stryker & Currah, 2014; Galarte, 2015) to study an intergenerational and multiracial group’s relationmaking in a queer and trans communal clothing makin g program situated in histories of multidimensional struggles for gender self-determination (Stanley, 2014). In this queer design-based research project (McWilliams, 2016), I followed 18 participants across 9 months o f QTthreads Sunday Studios. Participation did not r equire explicitly identifying as queer or trans; many did use this language as we dynamically articulated our selves, individually and collectively. Through micro-ethnographic analyses of fieldnotes, memos, artifac ts, audio/video recordings, and interviews, I address the question: How does a lens of trans cultural production shift an analysis of relation-making in the queer and trans communal clothing making practices of participants in QTthreads? Trans cultural production expands analysis beyond c onnecting around technical endeavors to see our (relation-)making as a “site for analysis where knowledge by and about transgender phenomena and communities is produced” (Galarte, 2015, p. 520) through “creat ive action that transforms and continues the world”  (Stryker & Currah, 2014, p. 468). While working on compressi on garments for a mutual aid project, for example, participants (1) practiced skills of drafting and sewing while cre ating garments for folks  whoneed them, (2) confronted and worked to create relations to address the conditions in which these needs are not sufficiently met, and (3) invited each other into expansive play with gender, embodiment, and creative nourishment. This lens, and our work together, offers an orientation to designi ng for and studying making that foregrounds “how we  cando things together in ways we were told not to imagine” (Spade, 2020, p. 16). Public trans pedagogies resisting anti-trans carceral pedagogies Grace A. Chen, Ali R. Blake, Jasmine Y. Ma As New York City Community Education Councils (CEC) switched from appointing to electing councilmembers in 2021, billionaire-backed Parent Leaders for Accelerated Curriculum and Education (PLACE) (Strong Economy for All Coalition et al., 2024) organized fa r-right takeover efforts facilitated by scant voting infrastructure; only 2% of families voted in 2021 and 2023 (Zimmer, 2023). We have analyzed how participants in a PLACE-influenced council (CEC2) engaged racist carceral r elations, such as weaponized Whi te motherhood, by mobilizing emotional configurations (Vea, 2020) around childhood (Blake et al., 2023). In March 2024, CEC2 passed anti-trans resolution 248. Through critical discourse an alysis of public meeting recordings, we focus on how members of the public respond to 248 in subsequent CEC meetings. W e highlight the actions of a trans-led coalition organizing against 248, working to pass a resolution that affirms trans youth, and mobilizing voter outreach and recruiting  council candidates for the 2025 CEC elections. Spe cifically, we examine how they develop resources for trans-affirming politicization (Curnow et al., 2019) through the stories they tell in CEC public speaking sessions. Beyond t he far-right majority council, public meeting participants overwhelmingly show up in support of trans folks and to highlight how “TERF [trans-exclusionary radical feminist] logics are carceral logics” as “the policin g of gender is also part of a larger white supremac ist political project” (Alexander, 2023, p. 230-231). Whereas the council’s non-binding resolution tells a story to the public that denies transgender personhood, the coalition makes clear, month after month, that this is neither the only nor the popular story in the district; the popular publ ic story is one of gender self-determination bound up with all efforts toward liberation. Through this pedagogy of resistance  (Templeton & Dohrn, 2010), the coalition is organizing for learning in public that recognizes t he carceral logics of the council and shapes public  discourse toward solidarity.

Building the big gay wall: Queering safety and solidarity in Winnipeg Joe Curnow, Anny Chen, Erica McNabb In fall 2023, over 3,000 people marched on Manitoba ’s legislature in support of “parental rights”, an anti-trans slogan and social movement that calls for parents t o have control over their children’s (and other peo ple’s children’s) exposure to 2SLGBTQ+ content in schools, and for schools and public spaces to erase and wi thhold 2SLGBTQ+ content. The parental rights mobilization was networked to dozens of other similar mobilizati ons across Canada, but in Winnipeg, queer organizers were caught off guard by the scale and militancy of the march. In response, queer organizers mobilized large-scale rallies in support of queer and trans communi ty, and organizers paid special attention to ensuring safet y vis a vis policing, counter-protesters, and the overall harm of queer-and trans-phobic hate circulating across the province. This s pecialized approach to safety innovated new tactical repertoires for keeping people in liberati on movements safe. Over the course of the last year, this group emerged as the “Big Gay Wall” which provides marshaling through community-led holistic safety expertise, and which leverages abolitionist commitments to non-police and de-escalatory safety strategies. The Big Gay Wall is part of the ongoing Abolitionist Futures research project, where Winnipeg’s police and prison abolitionists have asked how “we keep us  safe” and how we design for a world where abolitio n is possible through a participatory action research pr oject. For this analysis we examine (1) How Big Gay  Wall innovated new practices, (2) How those practices we re embedded within queer and abolitionist political conceptual frameworks, and (3). How relationships e nabled the politicization of safety and  solidarity practices. Our analysis centers situated theories of learning as becoming (Lave, 1996), politicization (Curnow et  al. 2019), and learning ideology (Philip et al., 2018) and contributes to ongoing shifts in the field to address the political and ethical dimensions of learning (The Politics of Learning Collective, 2017, Esmonde & Booker, 2016). Togetherland: Designing a learning environment for childhood selfdetermination at a queer and trans-led after school program Harper B. Keenan Amid the current political firestorm over the use o f queer and trans pedagogies in schools and the bro ader treatment of LGBT people in public education, queer  and trans educators continue to design and facilitate young people’s learning experiences. I draw  from Davis, Vossoughi, and Smith (2020), who argue “that children ’s everyday forms of self-determination are much more than individual acts; t hey emerge from social histories and carry future potentialities that shape learning and intellectual life within, and sometimes beyond, the setting” (p.2). This paper offers findings drawn from data gathered  at a queer-and trans-led after school program in San Francisco, California, that I argue was designed th rough the lens of knowledge and community practices developed through histories of queer and trans social movements in the United States. In particular, I suggest that the program’s design drew heavily from the legacy of 1970s-era queer/trans social movements and their desire to reconfigure the role of the child in society – specifically, arguing that childcare was not the exclusive responsibility of mothers, that queer and trans people should be able to participate in children’s lives outside the confines of the heterosexual nuclear family, and that queer and tra ns communities had important contribution s to make toward the larger goal of children’s own self-determination and bodily autonomy (Bronski, 2018; Cohen, 2008). The central data for this qualitative study included 150 hours of observation, 11 interviews with program staff, and 6 play-based focus groups with children at one LGBT-led after school program at a public San Francisco elementary school. In this presentation, I offer a series of qualitative snapshots to illustrate how the program drew from queer and trans knowledge and experience to su pport children’s agency and collective self-determination through five key elements in the design of the lear ning environment: taking children seriously, “remix ing” the norm, respecting children’s agency, centering creat ivity and relational learning, and embracing affect ive knowledge. For example, the program’s emphasis on c reative learning extended to creative forms of gend er expression beyond a prescriptive male/female binary. Although these beliefs and practices support the autonomy and self-determination of all children, I suggest that their  enactment — combined with the queer and trans lived experiences of many staff — contribute to the design of a learning environment tha t is particularly hospitable to gender self-determination.

Identity, power, and belonging in the diaspora: Learning from South Asian Queers in Canada Rishi Krishnamoorthy, Palak Desai, Ananya Banerjee South Asian (SA) immigrants comprise the largest and fastest-growing visible minority community in Canada (Statistics Canada, 2023). SA immigrants trace thei r geographic relations to the Indian Subcontinent, the Caribbean, East and South Africa, and West Asia, speak numerous languages, practice a variety of religions, and have diverse migration histories (Banerjee et al., 2024). Yet education research treats us as a monoli th, making invisible our heterogeneous experiences with systemic discrimination. For example, diasporic queer South Asians often contend with experiences of alienation and is olation, finding ourselves out of line (Ahmed, 2006) within both SA (heteronormative) and queer (White-dominated) spaces (Paranthan, 2024; Patel, 2019). A dditionally, scholarship concerning SA communities in Canada is only beginning to recognize the intersectional powe r dynamics (e.g., caste, religion) that shape our relations with each other and the land (Upadhyay, 2024). This study is a response to Shrodes and Paré’s (2022) call to recognize the (in)visible asymmetrical powered rela tions that shape experiences of “culture, language (and) identity” within queer communities. We examine the relationalities, epistemologies, and practices that inform Canadian-based SA queer peoples’ identity-construction and belonging. Data for this study includes 25 semi-structured interviews with South Asian queer identi fied people across Canada. Interviews focused on participants’ experiences of, relationships with, and desires aro und belonging. Thematic analysis traced the power dynam ics that shaped who participants understood themsel ves to be, and how they identified as part of a community.  Grounded in the Rightful Presence framework (Tan & Calabrese Barton, 2023), we “make present” and situate these power dynamics in their historica l emergence to illustrate the tendrils of pre-colonial histories, colonial violence, and intra-community powered hierarchies that shape how SA queers position themselves and are positioned by others. Findings also identify the ways in which white supremacy and settler colonialism undergird conceptions of belonging and participants’ hoped for inclusive and just futures as part of their communities.

This work highlights the importance of frameworks grounded in learning about the self in r elation to the other, with particular implications for how we practice learning theory among queer folx. Trans imaginaries: Placemaking toward resistance and belonging with virtual reality art Dylan Paré To imagine oneself as queer or trans when the world claims you should not exist requires imagining worlds beyond or alternative to current life. By analyzing a film  project about trans youths ’ artistic visions for their futures, I explore how trans youths’ virtual reality art (1) challenges efforts to control marginalized people and (2) imagines utopic places where they make community, belonging,  and joy central principles. My collaborator and I created the film Creative Futures  in 2018, and it is a two-part documentary consisting of a linear film and a 360º immersive companion film in  which three transgender youth design their ideal fu tures using a virtual reality painting application (Paré & Windsor, 2018). Building on constructionist approaches (Papert & Harel, 1991) and the arts in learning sciences (Halverson & Sheridan, 2014), I highlight how the trans youth represent their identities and political stances through their art and stories within the activist learning context of  a public media project about critical trans joy and critical trans  politics (Shuster & Westbrook, 2024; Spade, 2015).  The youth raise issues of figurative and literal policing of marginalized (e.g. Black and trans) people’s everyd ay lives and the transmisogynistic, sexist beliefs about trans w omen’s bathroom use at school. Through my analysis, I aim to illustrate how critical approaches can transform constructionist learning p rojects toward socially jus t ways of creating and learning through technology-enabled art by centering intersectional trans imaginaries of our possible futures together. Queering design by designing queer spaces Suraj Uttamchandani, Eric Knee This project focuses on design, asking how we candesign for queer-supportive experiences, including both school and out-of-school experiences. We are informed by McWilliams’ (2016) notion that queer design (particularly in the learning sciences) consists of axiological principles that are ever-changing and sometimes even contradictory. We compare two datasets from a larger project on qu eer geographies of learning and leisure. The first is a focus group with LGBTQ+-identifying college students, in which participants articulated what “queer space” meant to them and how they knew they were in a queer space. We place this side-by-side with a documentary-style interview with Kyle, the co-owner of an LGBTQ+ bar in a conservative county in the United States. In th e interview, Kyle articulates design choices he made in constructing the bar, such as what furniture and  lighting were purchased, with an attention to how he hoped q ueer people would feel in the bar. Using a place-informed discourse analytic approach, we examined the focus groups and interview in terms of how they construct ed understandings of queer sites for learning and leis ure. Across the data, people responded differently to what symbolized queer space. One group of students noted  that the presence of a  Pride flag would help them see a space as queer. But a different group noted that they were skeptical of explicit markers of queerness because they were aware of spaces that proclaimed themselves to be queer-friendly but in actuality were not, preferring spaces they described as “implicitly queer,” meaning that actions rather than symbols queer a space. In the i nterview, Kyle discussed objects he placed behind the bar as symbols that would be meaningful to queer clientele, such as a lion wearing a crown to demonstrate the strength of the queer community and a masquerade mask, highlighting how queer people can “take off their masks” in a qu eer safe space. These highlight differences in what  and how queerness can be symbolized and conveyed through ob jects and decorations. These differences indicate tensions in how students view queerness and potential challe nges in designing queer-inclusive environments. With these tensions in mind, we revisit McWilliams’ principles for queer participatory design to grow them—and also contest them—in a variety of queer contexts.

Constructing a dehumanizing “truth”: Emotion, ideology, and epistemic practices in the Moms for Liberty podcast Tanner Vea Responding to a call for learning scientists to inv estigate learning in regressive social movements (E rickson, 2021), this analysis examines how knowledge claims about trans and queer people are advanced and warra nted in far-right movements. Alongside the rise of fascism, the foundations of a shared basis in truth are eroding. While it is understood that the contemporary instability in ways of knowing is partly a “political” problem (Barzilai and Chinn, 2020), existing approaches to researching post-truth trends may underemphasize their powered, emotional, and ideological dynamics. This nexus provides the conceptual focus of this paper. “The Joyful Warriors Podcast” from the far-right movement organization Mom’s for Liberty (M4L) circulates harmful yet influential ideas driving book bans, fights against inclusive curricula, suppression of history, and attacks on educators. Turn by conversational turn, its participants build a view of the world in which innocent children and the United States itself are under urgent threat. Informed by studies of global fascism (Berezin, 1997; Finchelstein, 2022), this paper focuses on 10 episo des of “The Joyful Warriors Podcast” published betw een January 2023 and August 2024 where LGBTQ people, tr ansgender people, or “gender ideology” are referenc ed in the episode title. I show how participants use e pistemic practices (e.g., asking questions, using e vidence, evaluating claims) interwoven with emotional (Vea, 2020) and ideological dynamics (Philip et al., 2018), with consequences for the dehumanization of LGBTQ+ people. How people develop community-specific norms about the relation between epistemic practices, emotion, and power is a learning problem. I argue that taking these fringe epistemic practices seriously and understanding the ir functioning are required steps toward interrupti ng them in educational settings and public discourse more broadly.
