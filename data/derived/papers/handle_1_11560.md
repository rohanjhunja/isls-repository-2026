# Unveiling Learning Dynamics in Nursing Educational Escape Rooms: Insights from Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) Analysis and Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA)

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While educational escape rooms (EER) are quickly integrated as widely accepted learning strategies in nursing education, the need to take a learning science approach into its mechanics emerges. Using linguistic inquiry and word count (LIWC) analysis and epistemic network analysis (ENA), this study highlights the learning dynamics among 31 first-year nursing student teams in an escape room themed around sepsis. The results reveal the temporal and cognitive dimensions of students’ interaction in escape rooms.

## Introduction

Scholarly interest in educational escape rooms (EER s) has surged over the past five years, with two systematic reviews and meta-analyses on EERs in nursing published this year (Gonzalez-de la Torre et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). While the literature has advanced in reporting escape room design, implementing structured prompts, and adopting more rigorous research methodologies such as quasi-experimental studies, little attention has been given to analyzing the conversations occurring within the se rooms. Most qualitative studies on EERs rely hea vily on post-hoc student interviews (Morrell et al., 2020), which may not accurately capture the dynamics and  events in the room. This reliance risks distorting the percei ved effectiveness of EERs. Adopting a learning scie nces perspective and employing advanced data analysis te chniques—such as text mining  (e.g., linguistic inquiry and word count (LIWC) analysis) and epistemic network analysis (ENA)—can provide deeper insights into how EERs influence students’ learning experiences. Thus, this study aims to investigate students’ learning dynamics through LIWC and ENA, further understanding how escape room shapes their learning experiences.

## Methods

The participants are first-year nursing students taking a simulation-based introductory course in nursing. A total of 69 students at a public university in the southe rn United States were selected. The participants ag reed to the audio and video recordings of their escape room experience and collected their academic performance records. The escape room for this study was themed around se psis and designed by a teaching faculty with 15 yea rs of experience. Participants were grouped in teams of two or three and given fifteen minutes to escape. Two data analysis techniques were used in this stud y: LIWC and ENA. LIWC explored the linguistic features of students’ conversations based on the LI WC-22 dictionary (Boyd et al., 2022). While the output included dozens of columns, only those with acceptable internal consistency with at least Cronbach’s a of.6 were considered. For ENA, the data was coded according to the collaborative problem-solving framework by Liu et al.

(2015) and Zhang et al. (2022) (see Table 1) and analyzed using the ENA web tool (version 1.7.0) (Marquart et

al., 2018).

## Results

Several significant correlations were observed across diffe rent categories (Table 2), indicating low likelihood of these results being due to chance. While some correlations are not surprising due to similarities in the dictionarybased approach of LIWC (e.g., Cognition and Cognitive processes), others provide insights into the interaction dynamics. For example, the correlation between Cognitive processes and Present focus indicates that students were analyzing their status quo of problem-solving in the escape room. The correlation between  Negative tone and Physical shows that students were sharing clinical informati on about the patient, which was negative. The negative correlation between Past focus and Present focus implies the clear distinction between the discussio ns on the past assessment and the next steps. The output from ENA corroborates the temporal dynamics of students’ conversations, as indicated by the heavy weighted line between PI and PN (0.25) (see Figure 1). Students provided information about their patient assessment results and prompted next steps, actively engaging in clinical discussions. ID co-occurred with EO (0.30), PI (0.45), and RI (0.43), indicating that they mainly checked the validity of the information or the opinions, not di rectly engaging in the problem-solving process itself. A MannWhitney test showed that the epistemic networks of students (Mdn = -0.455) and instructors (Mdn = 0.440) were significantly different (p <.001), with students with more diverse and weighted nodes. ## Discussion and conclusion

This study demonstrates the combination of  LIWC and ENA to evaluate whether educational escape  rooms (EERs) achieve their intended purpose  of student-led collaborative problem solving. By analyzing real-time conversations, this approach provides a detailed un derstanding of how students interact, construct mea ning, and engage with the designed learning objectives. The f indings should be considered in the specific contex t of this study, which involved first-year nursing students engaging with a sepsis-themed escape room. Scenarios involving more emotionally charged topics, such as patient de ath or miscarriage, could produce significantly dif ferent linguistic patterns and interaction dynamics. There fore, generalizations should be made cautiously, em phasizing the importance of tailoring analysis methods to the unique characteristics of each escape room scenario. Future research adopting a learning sciences perspe ctive can further inform  EER design by addressing key factors such as the role of competition, inclus ion of distractors, team sizes, and the duration of  gameplay. Although they were not examined in this study, thes e elements likely influence collaboration, engageme nt, and learning outcomes. By integrating these considerati ons, researchers can contribute to the development of more effective EERs that meet diverse educational needs and contexts.
