# The Language Between Us: Using CHAT to Explore a STEM Coach-Researcher Collaborations

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examined how collaborative language empowers STEM coaches' agency through researcher-practitioner partnerships. Using Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) to analyze the collaboration between one STEM coach and researcher, data from professional learning sessions, videos, and artifacts were analyzed through thematic analysis. Findings revealed that empowering language functioned as a mediating tool in transforming traditional hierarchies and encouraging collaborative partnerships.

Though teachers and researchers are closely connected, teacher communities and researcher communities often do not overlap, leaving the two guessing at the needs of the other (Ko et al., 2022; Penuel et al., 2007). It is essential then to create successful collaborative spaces that emerge when researchers move beyond traditional roles that favor researchers as authorities over knowledge, instead adopting participatory approaches that amplify the voices and agency of STEM coaches. The goal of these approaches is then to bridge research knowledge and its practical application among teachers while empowering STEM coaches within these collaborations, (Giamellaro & Siegel,  2023; Yamagata-Lynch & Haudenschild, 2009). A key observation in collaborations between researchers and practitioners is the emergence of a shared language during collaboration, characterized by ideas, viewpoints, perspectives, and values that are mutually beneficial (Crain-Dorough & Elder, 2021; FarleyRipple et al., 2018). In this project, we examined how the language used by researchers in collaborative spaces empowers STEM coaches' agency indicating coaches' active involvement in the partnership and their product as opposed to deferring to the  researchers' goals. Through active language decisions from the researcher, we argue that the usage of empowering  language posits the STEM coach as an expert and provides more opportunity for agency and power sharing between  the pair. We explore this through the question: How does language function as a mediating tool to empower STEM coaches' agency in researcher-coach collaborative partnerships?

## Theoretical framework

Cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT) provides a framework for analyzing communities and facilitating professional learning collaborations by emphasizing system dynamics and promoting connections within the systems (Engström & Sannino, 2021). CHAT analyzes participant interactions within their context to identify tensions and contradictions in systems. It analyzes how mediators such as subjects, objects, tools, community, and division of labor facilitate systemic transformation (Bloomfield & Nguyen, 20 15). Third-generation CHAT expands this framework by analyzing the interactions among multiple activity systems, which frequently lead to the creation of new tools, rules, and objectives (Yamagata-Lynch, 2010). Deliberate attempts to connect systems of researchers and coaches can result in significant transformation, as participants redefine their activity systems and seek new objectives that mutually benefit both communities.

## Design and methods

The Hearing Each Other’s Voices (HEOV) project bridges research and instructional practice by bringing together STEM coaches and researchers to design professional development that integrates field-based evidence into sustainable practice, combining researchers’ expertise with practitioners’ experience. In its second year, the project included twelve participants; this paper focuses on two: Clarissa, a Midwest-based STEM coach in an urban elementary school who works with teachers to embed inquiry-based STEM  instruction, and Gloria, a researcher from a research-intensive institution. Their balanced, mutually respectful partnership provides a case for examining how language in coach– researcher collaboration supports agency and empowerment. A planned school visit to provide contextual grounding was not permitted due to district restrictions. Data sources included 2770 videos from online professional learning sessions and an in-person two-day Writing-Intensive that combined structured writing with informal reflection, culminating in a co-authored research practice brief (RPB). Transcripts were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s (2012) thematic analysis. Themes were first derived deductively from Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) mediator categories (tools, rules, division of labor) and then refined inductively through recurring patterns. For example, “tool” codes captured language that redirected institutional constraints, while “rules” codes reflected references to district norms shaping participation. This dual approach clarifies how themes map onto CHAT constructs and how these mediators shaped both independent and collaborative roles within the partnership.

Two themes were identified that highlight how language served as a powerful mediating tool in navigating systemic constraints and fostering coach agency. In the first theme, Transformative Rule Renegotiation Through Trust, a key tension arose from Clarissa’s inability to host a  school visit. Clarissa expressed, "I really wanted to be part of this project because I knew my experience could help,  but I was terrified that my district would find out and there would be consequences. This created a contradiction for Clarissa that wrestled with her desire to contribute pedagogically to  the project and her need to protect her  p rofessional standing However, Gloria's strategic mediation transformed this tension. Drawing from her digital  portfolio experience, Gloria stated: "we can create a digital portfolio that protects your identity while showcasing your incredible contributions”. These quotes show how language could serve as a mediating artifact to increase  Clarissa's agency, transforming her from constrained participant to empowered contributor. “This project has made me excited for new opportunities in having conversations and creating materials for teachers through a new lens. Hearing from a researcher and being able to collaborate has been very tremendous help” (journal quote). Therefore, we observe that the tensions associated with the rules (i.e. District level) that were identified by Clarissa were overcome by using language as a mediator. Which led to evidence of trust building language helped her find confidence  and empowered her to do the work through trusting the researcher.

In the second theme Empowering Language as Mediating Practice, a tension that was recognized was Gloria's use of language functioned to mediate and increase Clarissa’s agency during creation of the research practice brief during the writing intensive. Gloria’s empowering approach was evident when she stated to Clarissa during the writing intensive, "Your insights are exactly what this research needs." The collaboration produced language that bridged research-practice communities, enabling Clarissa to maintain practitioner authority while engaging with Gloria in the creation of the research-practice brief. As Clarissa noted, “I often have the passion and knowledge of what needs to be happening or what needs are within the educational sector but sometimes I am not able to get the words together and this makes that all possible. I really felt my confidence increase as well as excitement for where this work will go and what other opportunities may arise (reflection artifact).” Gloria's strategic use of language empowered Clarissa to transform her role from constrained participant to active contributor. Through their collaborative partnership, Clarissa gained the confidence to continue creating materials for teachers and students. This experience demonstrates how empowering language practices strengthen agency not only within research partnerships but also in classroom spaces, ultimately benefiting coaches and classroom practice.

These findings challenge traditional researcher practitioner hierarchies by showing that strategic language use fosters sustainable partnerships centered on practitioner agency. This requires rethinking research relationships, making empowering communication the foundation for equitable educational research that generates knowledge and supports practitioners. Language-mediated interventions and recognizing the coach’s contributions transformed tensions between institutional regulations and research participation into collaborative opportunities. Gloria’s linguistic choices reframed Clarissa’s constraints as legitimate concerns needing creative solutions, not barriers. Implications from this study  suggest that researchers should design partnership protocols to prioritize linguistic practices that honor practitioner  expertise, acknowledge tensions, and create reinforcing mechanisms. Future iterations of this project will explore whether these patterns hold across larger samples and varied partnership contexts, as this dyadic case offers depth but not breadth.
