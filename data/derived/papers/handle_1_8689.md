# AI Voice Tutor Usability Study: Understanding Impact on Math Self-Efficacy and Metacognition Among Middle Schoolers

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We present CAI, a voice-based intelligent tutor that helps middle schoolers learn prealgebra concepts. Users interact with CAI by solving math problems presented by the AI-tutor, each with a varied mode of narration and type of feedback. We describe the design, methods, and findings from our testing with middle schoolers. We provide insight into how voice-based intelligent tutors can impact motivation and metacognition, yet also require personalization and complementary video-based instruction to improve their performance.

## 1. Background

Previous research demonstrated discrepancies betwee n human and Al-based tutoring systems in their gran ular feedback and their ability to target relevant and frequent student responses (Kolchinski et al., 2018). Our research aims to understand how designing a voice-based AI tutoring system might close the equity gap in accessing human tutors. Current online learning platforms offer video-based lessons, yet these are limited in interactivity. Thus, AI tutors can enhance learning by providing personalized feedback, adapting to student responses, and enhancing AI tutors' presence and motivational support among middle schoolers. We prototyped an AI-tutor named CAI to have two primary tone-based narrative styles and five types of instructional feedback. As a pilot study, we explored the research questions through user testing with five middle schoolers employing CAI: (1) What impact does an AI tutor's narrative styles (standard vs. motivational) have on middle schoolers' math self-efficacy and metacognition? Which style do they find more compelling? (2) What impact does an AI tutor's types of feedback have on middle schoolers' math self-efficacy and metacognition? Wh ich type do they find more effective? (3) What are the techniques for designing features of an AI tutor?

2. Prototype design

We used Voiceflow, a conversational app prototyping tool, to prototype AI-tutor named CAI. Because the ability to learn through multiple channels concurrently increases the chance of remembering knowledge compared, we applied the cognitive theory of multimedia learning (Mayer et al. 1998). Accordingly, we integrated voice, text, and visuals to explain math concepts. For tasks, we  created three scenarios employing a combination of  two components: speech style and feedback type (Melis & Andres, 2005) (See Appendix A).

## 3. Methods

We conducted the usability testing with five middle schoolers based on convenience sampling—demographics in Appendix B. A demographic survey included questions on math s elf-efficacy. Following the survey, four prealgebra math tasks were given; in each, CAI explained a pre-algebra mathematical topic followed by a question. All math concepts and questions were provided at a similar difficulty level. Based on the students' responses, CAI provided five types of instructional feedback: know ledge of result (KR), knowledge of correct result (KCR), answer until correct (AUC), instruction-based elaboration (IBE), and extra-instructional elaboration (EIE). Task 1 to 3 served as treatment conditions with differing AI-tutor's narrative styles and types of feedback; Task 4 served as a control condition where the students underwent  a video-based instruction (Khan Academy) followed by a math question provided on Google Quiz form. After c ompleting all tasks, the students took a post-exper iment survey to provide feedback on the AI tutor's design features. An overview of the research is described in Table 1. We conducted the Friedman test on motivation and m etacognition. To add qualitative understanding, we analyzed each task’s observation notes by color-coding and creating affinity diagrams. ## 4. Results

The testing highlights can be viewed here: videos 1 and video 2. For motivation, the Friedman test showed no significant result. In the qualitative analysis, we  found four main themes: (a) metacognition, (b) mot ivational moments, including persistence and resistance to learning, (c) learning process, (d) CAI's positive and negative performance. Motivational moments were observed in all four tasks, and persistence in learning was most salient in Task 1. As Task 1 included AUC feedback, students were given a choice to take multiple attempts, and all took multiple attempts. For metacognition, the Friedman test was not statistically significant. However, in descriptive statistics, we found that AI-tutor conditions had a more significant impact on metacognition than the control. The observation data revealed that metacognition was mo re frequently observed in AI-tutor conditions (T1=2 8.1%, T2=34.6%, T3=29.2%) than the video-based (20%). In Task 2, students showed metacognition most frequently and two students attempted to fill in the knowledge gap by scrolling up to the explanation. A few design implications were found. For narratio n, three participants preferred the motivational narrative. For feedback type, four identified instr uction-based elaboration as the most effective. It provided information on "what to do to get the correct answe r" and guided them with a formula that "makes it ea sier to solve problems.” In the post-experiment survey, they thought the AI-tutor was helpful in terms of pacing learning (M = 4.4, SD=0.55), explaining solutions (M = 4.4, SD=0.55), and providing feedback when they got questions wrong (M = 3.8, SD=1.79). Regarding the positionality of AI-tutor in comparison to video-based instruction and human tutors, they rated 2.8 (SD = 1.30) for CAI’s similarity with human tutors and 3.4 (SD = 1.14) for whether they learned better compared to video-based instruc tion. In the interviews, the pros of AI-tutors incl uded the opportunity to ask questions, interact, and receive feedback based on progress. In contrast, what was found to be a deficit in AI-tutors (the limitations of speech recognition) was found to be a pro in video-based instruction.

## 5. Discussion

The findings revealed that the video-based instruct ion and CAI provided complementary benefits rather than counteracted. As a pilot study, we discuss two main issues for prototype redesign. (1) Personalization of AI-tutor: This study showed high variance between the participants in motivation and metacognition, reflecting individual students' confidence, math ability, and preference. User control over personalized AI configuration is suggested: allowing users to control the speech rate, tone, pa use time, as well as AI’s involvement level and int ervention frequency. (2) Strengthening AI-tutor’s impact on m etacognition: With reflection time and instruction-based elaboration feedback, students showed improved metacognition. They also indicated that an AI-tutor’s respect for learning pace and explanations of solutions to be e ffective. Additionally, we found potential in AI tu tors' "nonjudgmental" support in learning math. Thus, AI-tuto r redesign should define and validate design featur es that affect metacognition. The study’s limitations were the sample size, short experiment time, and students’ attention span. As we confirmed methodologies, we aim to address the limitations with a larger sample in future.
