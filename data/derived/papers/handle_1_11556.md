# Beyond School: On Lingering Effects of Mathematical Learning Difficulties in University Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Mathematical skills are crucial in our knowledge society. Consequently, poor mathematical skills significantly impact academic, but also life prospects more generally. However, little is known about mathematical learning difficulties (MLD) in university students. Here, we conducted thematic analysis on anamnestic interview data of 16 students presenting at a service facility for students with special needs. Results indicated three major themes. First, all participants reported significant number-related key deficits with basic mathematical skills and arithmetic operations. Based on these, the second theme reflected considerable daily struggles, in particular regarding money usage and time management. A final theme represented severe emotional and behavioral consequences, such as shame, mathematics anxiety, and avoidance behavior. Taken together, our findings underscore the impact of untreated MLD on the academic, but also personal lives of students with considerably daily struggles as well as lasting emotional and behavioral consequences – and call for appropriate MLD intervention for adults.

## Introduction

Mathematical learning difficulties (MLD) describe a variety of challenges individuals face in acquiring and applying even basic mathematical skills  (e.g., magnitude understanding)  and arithmetic operations (cf. Karagiannakis et al., 2014). Studies estimate that 3 to 7% of children and adolescents are affected, despite typical cognitive abilities and adequate learning opportuni ties (Haberstroh & Schulte -Körne, 2019). Typical difficulties include impaired basic mathematical skills such as magnitude understanding and basic arithmetic operations. For instance, Meier et al. (2021) observed that subtraction and division were specifically challenging for children with MLD. Moreover, children with MLD frequently employed inefficient problem-solving strategies. Without adequate treatment, MLD persists into adult hood (e.g., De Visscher et al., 2018)  with adults with MLD exhibit ing difficulties similar to those observed in children. Moreover, when unidentified or/and untreated, MLD may affect not only academic performance, but also mental wellbeing (e.g., Devine et al., 2018). In adults, MLD also have negative effects on employment, financial decision making, but also act ivities of daily living such as managing time and measurements (Vigna et al., 2022). And only recently, it was observed that even university students with MLD still face considerable  challenges in performing up to their potential in tertiary education (Kaufmann et al., 2022).

However, the majority of scientific research so far was devoted to study MLD in school children, leaving its behavioral and emotional impact on adults in genera l and on university students in particular underexplored. This lack of knowledge also means a dearth of appropriate asse ssment instruments and intervention  approaches for adults with MLD (Haberstroh & Schulte-Körne, 2019; Kaufmann et al., 2020). Given the severe consequences of untreated MLD for individuals’ academic,  employment but also more general life prospects, this qualitative study aimed at identifying consequences of MLD in university students.

## Methods

To further our understanding of consequences of MLD in adultho od, a thematic analysis according to Braun and Clarke (2006) was conducted on the anamnestic interviews of sixteen university students (14 females, two males, mean age 25.13 years ± 4.77). Participants were recruited between 2022 and 2024 at a university service facility for supporting students with special educational ne eds. Coming from different academic disciplines, all sought help due to severe academic and personal distress associated with poor mathematical skills. Braun and Clarke (2006) define thematic analysis as a method for identifyin g, analyzing, and documenting patterns (themes) in data. Two clinical psychologists followed the steps recommended by Braun and Clarke (2006) as independent expert raters: i) Familiarization of data: Data of the anamnestic interviews was transcribed, summarized, structured in a table, and read through repeatedly. Additionally, significant phrases were marked, and relevant points noted for subsequent analysis. ii) Generation of codes: Relevant segments (e.g., lack of number sense, counting strategies) were color-coded usi ng MAXQDA, a software for computer-assisted qualitative data analysis. iii) Combining codes into themes: By collating codes into potential  subthemes and gathering relevant pieces of data for each theme, a comprehensive overview of th e findings emerged.  iv) Reviewing themes: Coded text was re-checked to ensure validity.  v) Determine significance of themes: The identified subthemes were the categorized into clearly defined overarching themes that provided insights into the impact of MLD for university students. vi) Report findings: Analyzing identified themes and interpreting them in the context of our research question.

## Results

Thematic analysis of the interview data identified three primary categories of themes and subthemes re lated to MLD: i) number-related key deficits, ii) daily struggles, and iii) emotional and behavioral consequences. University students with MLD reported number-related key deficits consistent with DSM-V criteria, including impaired number sense, arithmetic fact me morization, fluent calculation, and mathematical re asoning (APA, 2013). Many indicated to struggle with uncertainties in basic numerical skills such as reading, transcoding, and memorizing numbers. Participants also reported deficits in basic arithmetic operations. In particular, many highlighted substantial challenges with mental calculation and difficulties with adding/subtracting across tens. A relevant number of participants admitted inefficient problem-solving strategies, especially counting strategies. In addition to academic difficulties (e.g., time issues in exams, need to change study program because of mathematical difficulties), a second theme reflected significant daily struggles, which impact on everyday life of adult participants. In particular, almost all of them reported to experience significant everyday challenges related to money usage, time management, estimation skills, and transferring mathematical concepts to real-world scenarios (e.g., How often do I need to go skiing for a season pass to be worthwhile?). Participants often expressed an inability to verify change they receive and having to “trust the cashier” instead. To avoid challenges with cash transactions, many of them resorted to exclusively pay by card. Difficulties with time-related tasks often begin with reading  analogue clocks. Consequently, participants reported difficulties in time planni ng and estimating time. Additionally, participants mentioned facing problems with estimating quantitie s, prices, and physical distances which impair their daily life.

Finally, there were severe emotional and behavioral consequences. Despite significant struggles in school, none of our participants were diagnosed or treated for MLD during childhood. Reasons included parental reluctance to seek testing and the belief that they  just needed to "try harder." Many students compens ated with strong cognitive abilities, diligence, and extra st udy efforts, allowing them to complete secondary ed ucation and enroll in university. Nevertheless, their difficulties had emotional and behavioral consequences based on negative learning experiences such as investing significantly more time than their peers, yet achieving poorer grades, being told they were lazy or unintelligent, etc. This resulted in increased psychological strain affecting their c urrent functioning and wellbeing. Also, participants exhibited a tendency toward s negative core beliefs, such as “I am simply too stupid for this”. The emotional impact associated with these negative learning experiences, including repeated and lasting feelings of shame, frustration, mental block when confronted wi th numbers, math anxiety, etc. and the associated negative beliefs  typically continue to affect them  in adulthood. These experiences led participants to adopt avoidance behaviors, such as refraining from discussing number-related topics due to fear of embarrassment, as well as compensatory strategies, such as excessive use of calculators.

## Significance

Our findings highlight that university students wit h MLD face persistent daily struggles due to their numberrelated key deficits. This limits participation in everyday life beyond academic and occupational settings, supporting Kaufmann et al.’s (2022) earlier findings. However, our results extend beyond academic and occupational barriers, highlighting broader difficu lties in daily activities.  Importantly, these difficulties do not disappear upon graduation; rather, our findings sug gest that they intensify over time, leading to an i ncreasing burden and significant emotional and behavioral consequences. Repeated experiences of failures and negative feedback led to  avoidance behaviors, shame, and math anxiety (Foers ter et al., 2001; Jansen et al., 2016). Participants often avoid math-related situations, yet their difficulties often remain unavoidable, causing significant emotional distress and reduced daily participation.  While emotional impacts of learning difficulties a re well-documented in children (Alesi et al., 2014; Nelson & Harwood, 2011), research on university students with MLD is still scarce.

Our findings underscore the urgent need for better assessment to ols and interventions tailored to adults. Current diagnostic methods fail to capture everyday  mathematical difficulties (e.g., money usage, time management), and the lack of support contributes to  emotional distress and avoidance behaviors. Develo ping targeted interventions is crucial to improving participation and quality of life for university students with MLD.
