A q uantitative analysis comparing 39 families’ pre-and post-experience interviews revealed significant learning gains (Cohen’s d = 1.57) after their 20-minute AR-guided experiences. Complementary case studies illustrate how the app’s AR features fostered family conversations using an analytical framework examining conceptual, affective, connecting, and perceptual talk. The case studies illuminate how the AR elements supported engage ment in scientific meaning-making. The study’s implications include four design conjectures for using mobile AR to deliver microlearning experiences that promote science talk and learning in rural communities  underserved by traditional science institutions.

Investigating learning-on-the-move and socio-technical pedagogy Our design-based research study was developed by university learning scientists in partnership with computer programmers and developers, pollinator scientists, and educators from two outdoor learning settings. Together, we seek to understand how augmented reality tools deployed on mobile devices support rural families’ science learning (e.g., conceptual learning and science talk).  Rural families were selected as the study population because they are underserved by traditional science education institutions (e.g., zoos, museums) yet have rich resources for learning in their community outdoor spaces.

We ask two related research questions. First, how did the Backyard Explorers App support families’ knowledge gains (related to pollinator ecology and biology) in an outdoor informal learning setting? Second, how did the Backyard Explorers App support two families’ science talk during their visit to the Children’s Garden? Learning-on-the-move to support intergenerational science talk Our framework draws from sociocultural perspectives within the  learning-on-the-move (LOTM) literature. LOTM is a social learning process where people make sense of new information collaboratively as they move their bodies within and through community-oriented places (Bang, 2020; Marin, 2020; Taylor, 2017; Zimmerman, et al., 2023). LOTM asserts that mobility is key to learning and making meaning. LOTM can include small and large motions such as pointing, posturing, walking, wheel-chairing, biking, dancing, and engaging in play. Marin and Bang (2018) applied a LOTM lens in their  analysis of a Native American family’s experience during forest walks. They found that while “walking, reading, and storying land” learners were “coordinating attention and observing, finding evidence and generating explanations, and creating a story about the perceptual field” (Marin & Bang, 2018, p. 111).

In informal learning settings, families are supportive of children’s science talk; however, parents do not always know how to support youths ’ science learning  (Gleason & Schauble, 1999). As such, we leverage augmented reality (AR) to provide families with mobile science learning supports while they engage in LOTM activities. We build on prior work with mobile technologies and LOTM in the outdoors for informal science learning including woods and trails (Rogers et al., 2004; Xu et al., 2024; Zimmerman & Land, 2022), gardens and parks (Huang, Lin, & Cheng, 2010; Land, et al., 2020; McClain, et al., 2026; Zuiker & Wright, 2015), historical sites (Kennedy et al., 2021; Souropetsis & Kyza, 2025), and watersheds and ponds (Georgiou & Kyza, 2018; Zimmerman & Weible, 2018). Educational scaffolds and prompts were designed to support families in talking together because meaning-making conversations were identified in the literature as a central learning process in out-of-school-time (OST) learning (National Research Council, 2009). 670 Our work builds on the prior LOTM findings  by designing a n AR-supported LOTM experience with intentional design conjectures that (a) focus learners’ attention on key place-based phenomena, (b) encourage observation of community spaces, (c) support the development of scientific explanations with evidence from observations and shared intergenerational experiences, and (d) ends with a learner-curated artifact or collection of photographs  that document their visit. We operationalize these design conjectures with a microlearning approach (Taylor & Hung, 2022) where science content is broken down into small conceptual pieces and short activities that are implemented in discrete, compact digital units as learners move through a community space. We take a holistic view of meaning-making that occurs during science talk (Land & Zimmerman, 2015) to include important diverse elements.  Science talk in out-of-school-time settings has been conceptualized as having five components (see Allen, 2003): strategic elements on how to use the technologies or museum exhibits supporting learning,  perceptual (observational) noticing of key phenomena, affective (emotional) reactions to the phenomena, conceptual engagement and reflection  with the big ideas, and meaningful connections to families’ prior experiences, values, cultural narratives, and shared activities. Like many prior studies of informal education, our team (e.g., Land & Zimmerman, 2015; Li et al., 2024) often used the coding framework offered by Sue Allen

(2003) that considers the strategic, perceptual, affective, conceptual, and connecting talk that makes up meaning

making conversation. By using this framework across the iterations of our design-based studies, we refine design conjectures and principles about how different kinds of OST interventions, such as our mobile app,  support various kinds of learning.

Socio-technical pedagogy Our design perspective is informed by socio-technical pedagogy  (Schmidt et al., 2024) to  support families’ science learning-on-the-move. The affordances of mobile AR allow our team to provide opportunities for learning in outdoor community spaces (Kawas et al., 2019; van Kraalingen, 2023). Our place-based mobile AR app was not developed for a single specific location. Instead, it was designed to encourage learners to observe flora, fauna, and their interactions in outdoor places they are familiar with, such as their own backyards, neighborhood parks, or community gardens. In this way, families build on their prior experiences in familiar  places with an app that provides digital augmentations of scientific processes and concepts otherwise not directly visible to them. Our socio-technical pedagogy consists of the following elements (Land et al., 2022) that were embedded into our app that prompt families to look at and talk about their surroundings through a scientific lens or to make personal connections to the place-based elements they are seeing:

1. AR filters or AR animations, which serve as visualizations and representations of science phenomena

– these foster observations and add immersion to the learning experience (Georgiou & Kyza, 2018),

2. Digital images or text-based resources tied to place while LOTM,

3. Question prompts or action prompts to support meaningful conversation, and

4. Learner-curated photo or video artifacts (i.e., photo-journaling) to channel attention through

discussing or annotating photos with observational criteria. By bringing LOTM, AR, and socio-technical pedagogy together, our study extends existing research. We analyze how AR influences microlearning experiences by  integrating mobility, place-based meaning making, scientific understandings, and family talk  in outdoor informal settings. This conceptual intersection is underexplored in prior work, particularly in out-of-school-time learning settings.

## Method

This analysis is part of a larger multi-year design-based research (DBR) study (Sandoval & Bell, 2004), called SPACES, which supports the iterative design of multiple mobile AR applications and the refinement of theory related to informal science learning. This study focused on the third iteration of the Backyard Explorers mobile AR app, which guided families on a LOTM experience lasting approximately 20 minutes.

The study took place in the Children’s Garden at the Arboretum at Penn State, which includes gardens, forests, and over 100 acres of natural lands. Eligible participants included at least one parent or legal guardian and one child between the ages of 5 and 12. Families were recruited via the Arboretum’s website and social media, as well as from on-site visitors. Most participants resided in rural counties. Upon arrival, adults provided written consent and completed a demographic survey; children gave oral assent. All participants consented to audio and video recording, with the option to opt out of sharing images publicly. As a DBR study conducted in a naturalistic outdoor setting, this work prioritizes ecological validity over experimental control. Variations in weather, participant engagement, and group dynamics introduce d inconsistencies in learning experiences and data collection quality. All names used are pseudonyms, and participants’ faces are blurred for presentation. Families also had the option to stop at any time or refuse to answer 671 any question, in keeping with a participant-centered research design. Each family received an e-gift card after participating. While the lack of a control group limits causal claims, our iterative approach allowed for a close examination of intergenerational talk and learning processes in a real-world setting, which can yield practical insights into how AR tools support authentic family interactions. Technology: Backyard explorers app The Backyard Explorers content focused on pollinators and plants with AR features that provided resources relevant to the local (i.e., Appalachian) habitat that may not otherwise have been visible to learners (Dunleavy & Dede, 2014). Learners engaged in multiple microlearning moments to find and observe pollinators and flowers in places meaningful to their families. The app highlighted insect behaviors for families to focus on while observing pollinators and provided opportunities to photograph insect polli nators. The AR elements in Backyard Explorers included an interactive filter showing how some pollinators perceive ultraviolet (UV) light beyond human vision, and an interactive 3D model of a bee that appeared in the setting when learners looked through the iPad screen.

A total of 43 families (69 adults and 79 children) consented and participated in Iteration 3 of the Backyard Explorers study. After excluding four families with incomplete data, the final sample included 39 families (n=130; 62 adults and 68 children).

The data collection protocol began with children and adults participating in a brief pre-experience interview. Four interview questions were asked to gauge scientific understanding: 1) What do you know about how pollinators and plants interact? 2) How do pollinators find flowers? 3) What are pollinators looking for on flowers? 4) How does pollination happen? Families then used the Backyard Explorers app for a 15-25-minute technology-guided experience with iPads that were preloaded with the app. Post-experience interviews followed, using the same four questions.

During the experience, one family member wore a GoPro camera mounted on a hat, and the iPad screen was recorded during use. After the visit, the families’ two video streams were merged so researchers could simultaneously analyze each family’s iPad screen, their talk,  and the objects at the Arboretum they observed. Recordings were transcribed and then verified by research team members. Consequently, the data sources included 39 merged video recordings, 39 demographic surveys, 39 transcripts of family interaction, and 39 matched pre-and post-experience interviews.

The case study families were strategically sampled from the 39 families based on their high level of engagement with the Bee Vision AR feature and the 3D bee model in the app. Additionally, the final families selected were clearly visible in the GoPro camera  footage in order to make the comic-strip data representation featured in this manuscript. These families were selected as illustrative cases and demonstrated high learning gains.

## Data analysis

To evaluate the families’ conceptual learning, we developed a content-focused rubric with levels ranging from 0 to 4 and rated their pre-and post-interview responses. A score of 0 represented incorrect or no response, 1 was a partial understanding, 2  represented emerging understanding, 3 was understanding (including concept and vocabulary), and 4 represented advanced concepts and terminology. Content validity of the rubric was established by two university pollinator scientists.

To assess the families’ learning gains, composite scores were calculated for participants’ responses to the four interview questions at both pre-and post-test (n = 39). Two researchers independently coded 20% of the pre-and post-interview transcripts and achieved 89% interrater agreement.  Discrepancies were adjudicated by four team members, and the coding rubric was clarified to address those discrepancies. The revised codebook was used to code the remaining 80% of the dataset. A paired-sample t-test was conducted after confirming the scores were normally distributed via a Shapiro-Wilk test.

## Case study 1: Family talk and interaction in a bee vision activity

This case illustrates how the Backyard Explorers app supported informal, family-centered science learning by blending perceptual and affective engagement with children’s imaginative talk. In the episode, Maya and her two daughters, Lily (7-year-old) and Ava (10-year-old), explored the Bee Vision activity. During this interaction, Lily held the iPad, Maya stood beside her reading the text aloud, and Ava, wearing a GoPro hat, sat across from them. When Maya explained that bees can see ultraviolet light to locate pollen and nectar, Lily immediately concluded, “That’s how they find it” (Figure 1, line 1). When prompted to “swipe left and right” on a marsh-marigold photo (line 2), Lily revealed the bee vision view, and both she and Maya exclaimed, “Ohhhh” (line 3), in surprise. Their enthusiastic reaction to the visual contrast between human and bee vision marked an affective high point of their visit and sparked further conversation. Maya reflected that she did  not know that bees saw flowers differently (line 4), which led Lily to declare that she wanted to be a bee (line 5), indicating imaginative engagement with the science content.

As Lily swiped to a dandelion image (line 13), Maya noted how the color was not as pretty from a human perspective for a bee (line 14), modeling perspective-taking and continuing their imaginative talk. Ava asked for a turn with the iPad to explore the image, indicating sustained interest and collaborative exploration. When looking at the zinnia (lines 15-16) Maya observed that only the tips of the petals glowed for bees and said, “I wonder why” (line 17), prompting Lily to lean in for a closer observation of the image (line 18), signaling continued curiosity and ongoing engagement. In sum, the app’s AR visuals and prompts scaffolded the family’s exploration of pollinator biology, transforming abstract concepts  (such as ultraviolet vision) into a shared and visible  experience. Through conceptual and affective science talk, Maya and her daughters gained biological insights while engaging with the app and garden space. Their interactions illustrated how the family’s conversations and mobile scaffolds worked together to support conceptual development in the outdoor learning setting. Their shared perceptual observations of the AR images, reflective questions, and imaginative talk sustained their science conversation about pollinators throughout the experience.

## Case study 2: Exploring wing movement with a 3D bee model

This case illustrates how Sophie and her 7-year-old son Noah examined a 3D bee model that appeared virtually in the garden space when viewed through the app interface.  Noah, wearing a GoPro hat, shared the iPad with his mother as they read and interacted with the AR model.  Sophie remarked that the bee looked “cool” and “neat” (Figure 3, lines 1-2) and encouraged Noah to “walk around it and see” (line 2), to which he responded, “That was really cool” (line 3).

Figure 3 Sophie and her 7-Year-Old Son, Noah, Explore the 3D Bee Model on the Backyard Explorers App, Part 1 675 Prompted by the app, Sophie asked what Noah noticed about the bee’s wings (Figure 4, line 4), engaging in perceptual talk. He responded, “The bee’s wings were attached to that thing” (line 5), referring to a part of the bee’s body, and after looking closer (line 6), he observed that the wings were “going that way” (line 7), indicating their direction of movement.  Sophie described the motion as “going around” (line 8) and then read aloud from the iPad, explaining that bees move their wings in a figure-eight pattern (line 9). Noah replied hesitantly, “Yeah, but it was going around” (line 10), to which Sophie invited him to look again (line 11). As they re-examined the model together, she embodied the motion by flapping her arms and adding a playful “shoo shoo” sound (line 12), then commented on how fast the wings were moving (line  13). Noah then made a connection to his knowledge of hummingbirds (line 14), “hummingbirds wings go like that”, which was confirmed by Sophie, “They do” (line 15).

The app’s prompts encouraged Sophie’s and Noah’s exploration and guided observation. With the support of his mother’s revoicing of the app’s information, Noah examined, revisited, and refined his understanding of bees. Their interaction highlights how the app scaffolded learning through multimodal engagement and parent-child dialogue. Our quantitative findings illustrate how the Backyard Explorers app supported families’ knowledge gains related to pollinator ecology and biology in an outdoor informal learning setting,  with a large effect size even during a 20-minute experience. This finding expands the research base on microlearning (Taylor & Hung, 2022) by showing how mobile technology-delivered microlearning experiences can lead to meaningful gains in family science learning during out-of-school-time experiences. Families coordinated their learning with the AR technology across the space for meaning making while visiting the Children’s Garden, relying on perceptual, conceptual, and affective talk during the Bee Vision activity and conceptual, connecting, and perceptual talk while engaging with the 3D b ee model. The accounts from the two qualitative case studies suggest that AR can engage intergenerational groups in conversation about important scientific phenomena while also making meaningful connections to families’ shared knowledge and history. 676 Together, the case study analyses and knowledge gain results demonstrate the potential of mobile AR in outdoor settings to support families during LOTM (Bang, 2020; Marin, 2020) within designed informal education interventions. Families walked through the Children’s Garden learning about how the flora and fauna  in their community, seeing ecological interactions and connections related to pollination. In the second case, the family used movement to understand how a 3D bee model simulated a figure eight wing motion, as they walked around the model superimposed into the Children’s Garden space. By using her arms to simulate a bee’s wing movement, the mother supported her son’s biological understandings. The qualitative cases illuminate  the specific learning processes that likely contributed to the significant learning gains observed across the family groups. Across both cases, the forms of science talk (e.g., perceptual noticing, conceptual sense-making, affective engagement, and connecting talk), represent discourse patterns through which learners refine and deepen their understanding. This suggests that these forms of talk may influence families’ conceptual development; more research is needed to understand the depth and breadth of this emerging pattern. Conceptually, these findings extend LOTM theories by linking movement to types of science talk and conceptual thinking during intergenerational learning interactions within a blended AR technology –outdoor garden–family setting. We expand on Marin and Bang (2018) findings about the connections between movement, perception, land-water, and intergenerational talk to add AR technology as another LOTM layer that supports family learning about science and personal connections related to outdoor spaces. Returning to our orienting design conjectures related to using microlearning strategies to support a LOTM intergenerational learning approach, we found that digital content, AR simulations, and filters focused learners’ attention on key place-based phenomena, as evidenced by the knowledge gains and the conversation in the first case study on the Bee Vision activity.  The digital content, question prompts, and short activities encouraged observation of the Children’s Garden as a community space  as illustrated by both case studies. The second case study shows how prompts and AR tools supported the development of scientific explanations grounded in observations on-site and shared intergenerational experiences. (This manuscript did not focus on the learner-curated artifact and collection of photographs that documented the families’ visits; we will address that in future work.) Methodologically, our study also demonstrates the value of carefully coding qualitative data from learners’ interviews during OST experiences, rather than on implementing  school-like pre-and post-tests or similar instruments. Given the informal nature of the learning experience, aligning data collection procedures with the experience itself was an important consideration for our team and partnering site. Our qualitative coding method detected knowledge gains within a 20-minute experience, demonstrating that conversation-based methodological approaches that quantify family talk can be effective. Relatedly, our team used the Allen (2003) framework to categorize talk in situ for our qualitative case studies. The framework allowed us to examine how families used different types of talk in specific situations, enabling distinctions to be identified in how meaning making occur red during our sociotechnical pedagogy. The four types of science talk (e.g., perceptual, conceptual, affective, and connecting) were useful categories to understand how families made sense of engaging and interactive AR elements embedded in the app. If others conducting similar work in LOTM, outdoor education, and/or mobile computing use this type of framework, the learning sciences field can further develop design knowledge about the types of talk various intervention features support.

Finally, given that rural communities are often under-resourced in informal learning institutions (e.g., museums), our findings suggest that mobile AR used in outdoor settings may be a productive way to support science meaning making within rural communities’ outdoor spaces. Community-based educators, ecologists, and learning scientists can collaborate to design materials for rural families’ OST experiences, building on the design principles and conjectures from the Backyard Explorers project and the related work that we cite here. In doing so, this work contributes design knowledge about how specific AR features, prompts, and microlearning structures can support learning-on-the-move and intergenerational science talk in informal outdoor settings.
