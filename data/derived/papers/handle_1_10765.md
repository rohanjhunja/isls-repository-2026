# Constraints and Boundaries for Collective Agency in Co-Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Co-design in education has emerged as an avenue for including broader perspectives into the creation of curriculum and learning environments, while also positively impacting learning and identity development. This kind of co-design requires designers’ collective agentic participation. In this paper, we look across several sets of data to build understandings of the ways collective agency emerges and is constrained within co-design. While research has pointed to the ways that participation in co-design has potential to increase feelings of agency in practitioners and youth, there is still a need to understand the ways communities of designers build and use collective agency. Without theories that describe the ways that co-design teams leverage their collective identities toward common outcomes, we fear that the power of this kind of work can fall short of its potential.

Major issues addressed Co-design in education has emerged as one avenue for i ncluding broader perspectives and experiences into the creation of curriculum and learning environments, w hile also positively impacting learning and identit y development (Pitt et al., 2021; Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2021; Yip et al., 2017). This kind of co-design requires designers’ collective agentic participation. Notion s of agency have continuously surfaced in research around identity in the learning sciences (e.g., Holland et al., 1998). We recognize that agency is often discussed in relation to action and engagement with regard to individual learners and the ways they navigate learning environments. However, in work guided by participatory collaborat ive design (co-design), we question the ways that  agency surfaces when groups are working toward common outcomes. Building from these perspectives, we begin from a definition of agency as the leveraging of identitie s toward action (Engeström & Sannino, 2010; Sannino  et al., 2015). As we ground our research in transformative notions of collaboration and the potential for co-design of educational interventions and environments to disru pt the hegemonic structures of schooling, we see a need to theorize collective agency (Gehring & Marx, 2023) as essential to successful partnerships.

In this paper, we look across several sets of data to build understandings of the ways collective agen cy emerges and is constrained within co-design. While research has pointed to the ways that  participation in co-design has potential to increase feelings of agency  in practitioners and youth (Severance et al., 2016; Yip et al., 2017), there is still a need to understand the ways  communities of designers build and use collective agency. Without theories that describe the ways that co-design teams leverage their collective identities toward common outcomes, we fear that the power of this kind of wo rk can fall short of its potential. We are interest ed in the kind of co-design that is grounded in efforts by diverse participants to leverage their experiences and knowledge toward the transformation of learning tools and environmen ts (Ishimaru et al., 2018). As such, we see agency – and particularly the development of collective agency – as an essential part and the goal of successful co-design. To ground our exploration of collective agency as a  construct, we look specifically at several co-design projects where youth and community members collabor ate in educational design, bringing their unique perspectives to the design process. To resolve the tension that exists between agency as a goal of co-design, while recognizing that expressed agency is ultimately a result of our sociocultural histories, we ask the question: Within co-design, what are the boundaries of context and activity that result in observable collective agency?

## Theoretical perspectives

We build from studies of collaboration in design-based research (Barab & Squire, 2004; DBR Collectiv e, 2003), research-practice partnerships (Farrell et al., 2021), parti cipatory and community-based design (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016), and youth participatory action research (Fine, 2008; Mirra et al., 2016), to define co-design as collaborations between stakeholders including youth, practitioners, and researchers, to shift the desi gn of educational spaces, interventions, and interactions. Co-design asks designers to take on new and different roles and relationships. Though youth voice may be amplif ied in co-design, it is still situated within the normative power dynamics of society broadly and, more specifically, public education (Collins & Bilge, 2020). The notion of collective agency challenges the idea  “that only individuals can act but treating group actors… as mere shortcuts for complex interactions among group members” (Gehring & Marx, 2023, p. 7) h as potential to reify problematic assumptions about wh ose knowledge and action counts. These authors “loc ate the source of collective agen cy in the formation of collective intentions throug h the adjustment of group members’ attitudes, in the organization of group decision processes, or in the transfer of resources to the group level” (p. 7). Gehring and Marx (2023) put forth that non-hierarchical groups can be a productive site of stud y of collective agency; however, we argue  that a sociocritical perspective requires an analys is of power that acknowledges the shifting hierarchies of groups.

In this paper, we look to non-normative approaches to agency that place the const ruct alongside Foucauldian notions of power as ever present and continuously in flux (e.g., Matusov et al., 2015; Sannino, 2015). We conceptualize agency as the enactment of identities in relation to the sociocultural and sociopolitical contexts in which they take place (e.g., DeJaeghere et al., 2016; Engeström et al., 2022; Gutiérrez et al., 2019). We further understand collective agency as dependent upon enactments of collective identities, which are formed in relation to others, to ecologies, and to sociopolitical cont exts. While we recognize that many settings in educ ation could be argued to have agency as the goal, we follow critical theories of learning to question the ways that agency has been co-opted for neoliberal pursuits in education (Matusov et al., 2015). As Freire (1980) noted, many classrooms have not historically required agency, instead rely ing on “the banking method” of education –unidirectional concepts of lear ning where the teacher imparts knowledge to youth. In this paper, we look to non-normative approaches to agency that place the construct along side Foucauldian notions of power as ever present a nd continuously in flux (e.g., Matusov et al., 2015).

Methodological approaches and data sources In this paper, we engage in a secondary analysis of  data from four research studies focused on co-design. Study 1 is a youth co-design project that focused on creating a museum exhibit to include Latine histories that designers felt were missing from the m useum’s public record (42 participants).  Study 2 i s a community design project focused on developing playgrounds in historically s egregated and underfunded areas of an urban center (110 participants). Study 3 is a youth co-design project focused on creating a digital badge system for a youth volunteer program at an Aquarium (25 participants). Study 4, located at a Hispanic-serving university, is an undergraduate student co-design project focused on redesigning curriculum (19 participants). Previous analysis had focused extensively on participant agency in co-design (Stamatis, Polman, & Hernandez, 2023; Stamatis et al., in review; Tierne y & Horstman, 2023; Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2024). Across the four studies, data was qualitative in na ture, including video recordings of design sessions  and interviews with design participants. In addition, d esign artifacts were collected for each of the studies. Analysis focused on the co-design process to examine participation, power, and  positioning in and across design sessions and included examining participants’ opportunities to impact the design process and products and reports (public or individual) of agency or lack of agency (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Collins & Bilge, 2020). We reanalyzed our studies to understand collective agency through the  constraints and boundaries that shaped opportunities for agency. From our original analysis we identified features of co-design processes and participant agency. We then compared these features  across th e four studies to create memos on tensions/ boundaries and collective agency that applied in al l research studies. Additionally, we examined momen ts of tensions that surfaced in the design studies, considering moments when designers voiced frustration or feelings of isolation. Seeking these kinds of tensions within o ur data, as well as their resolutions, helped us co nceptualize instances of collective agency.

Together these constraints and boundaries help define to what end collective agency emerges in design processes. We understand facilitator s have power inherent to their positioning. When participa nts buy into the process of co-design, there is potential for both individual and collective agency to emerge. While collective agency has been explored in different communities o f practice, co-design is an ideal setting for examining the construct because this kind of agency must consider the entire collective. In co-design, as opposed to classrooms, multiple outcomes are possible. The notion of colle ctive agency pushes us to recognize who counts and to move toward outcomes that honor the collaborators. We recognize that this does not guarantee equitable design, instead, the notion of collective  agency moves us to consider how design teams are co nstructed, who is invited in, and how power is distributed. If agency is the confluen ce of choice, power, and action, then notions of co llective agency push us toward Patel’s (2016) questions about the value of research. Here we consider: Agency for whom? To what end? And in what timescale? While agency is  always bounded by ecological, social, political, cultural, and ideological constraints of participants and set tings, attending to these boundaries means that des igned interventions and tools have more potential to reco gnize and attend to the needs of diverse and histor ically marginalized learners in ways that traditional education cannot.

## Emerging significance

While co-design projects have potential to transform hegemonic struc tures of education by creating space for more voices within the design of educational tools,  interventions, and environments, the field of the learning sciences continues to require new wa ys to conceptualize the processes for transformativ e work that co-designers cando together (Ishimaru et al., 2018; Farrell et al., 2021). The construct of collective agency has the potential to offer new ways of understanding collaboration toward change by creating an avenue through which we can better share experiences of power, privilege, and identity  as they are leveraged toward common goals. In this  work, we specifically examine collective agency in relation to constraints and boundaries that impact agency and the ways that collective agency occurs both in alignment wit h planned co-design processes and products and in disruption with planned co-design processes and products. While substantial re search has endeavored to define and characterize agency as a component of learning and design, collective agen cy remains largely undertheorized in relation to human action. This work seeks to contri bute to theorizing of agency writ large and specifi cally collective agency within co-design. Understanding that agency is always situate d (Holland et al., 1998) leads us to the question: Agency to what end? In this paper,  we examine the ways that collective agency emerges in codesign, while acknowledging that it does not negate  individual agency, but instead builds upon it. Aut hentic codesign cannot take place without recognition that e very member of the co-design team is essential for the collaboration to exist at all. By examining collective agency, we have potential to disrupt the notion that co-design is inherently good for teachers and students and in stead acknowledge that without those participants a nd their collective identities, the co-designed outcomes would not exist.
