# Critical Witnessing and Action: The Transformative Potential of Youth Storytelling through Climate Change Art

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research investigates how arts-based storytelling supports youth learning and agency within climate justice education. We view artistic practices as critical tools for fostering epistemic justice and decolonizing dominant structures that shape post-colonial science learning. Drawing on a case study of Arab immigrant youth, we examine how they used graphic novels to critically witness and respond to climate injustice in their community. Youth positioned themselves as active agents exposing vis ible harms while illuminating unseen systemic dimensions of environmental injustice. Their graphic novels make visible both explicit challenges and possibilities, showing observable environmental changes alongside hopeful pathways for action. Process and product collectively demonstrate capacity for empathy, critical analysis, and engagement across scales toward just futures. We argue that arts-based methods foster epistemic justice, amplify minoritized voices, and support justice-oriented science education. Implications include integrating multimodal artistic practices as sustainable, humanizing pathways for learning, activism, and systemic change.

Confronting global crises like climate change often leaves young people feeling overwhelmed, helpless, or hopeless (Ojala, 2011). For youth from historically minoritized communities, this burden intensifies through their direct experience of structural climate violence/injustice (Verlie, 2024). Science education approaches often perpetuate epistemic violence by silencing situated knowledge. Dominant pedagogy erases the lived experiences and voices of those most affected by climate injustice, its causes, impacts, and political dimensions (Verlie, 2024). This epistemic exclusion represents a critical barrier to meaningful climate change education, as it fails to center how minoritized students' socioscientific realities expose the limitations and perpetuation of violence in normative science education.

Yet, Freire argues that hope is an ontological need, an existential imperative essential to our survival and the struggle to improve our existence in the world (1994). To cultivate this necessary hope while addressing epistemic violence/injustice, we adopt  a lens of critical witnessing (Villenas, 2019), which requires explicit axiological commitments. Denial or silence in the wake of violence can be a secondary act of violence, while witnessing initiates processes that help reflect lived realities, making youth voices and experiences more visible, included, and empowered to engage in climate discourse (Verlie, 2024). Arts-based methods can facilitate critical witnessing by enabling students to challenge dominant discourses multimodally, recognizing their everyday testimonies and reclaiming long-neglected narratives (Halovsen & Sawyer, 2022; Hammond et al., 2018; Peters, 2015). Acknowledging the aesthetic value of student artwork means  “embracing the multiple truths” rather than “reducing the complex human world to one version of reality” (Tian, 2023, p. 59). Critically, art-based practices in science classrooms cando more than mitigate epistemic violence; they facilitate effective and embodied processing of scientific content and students’ critical reflection on ethical issues involved in science (Tian, 2023; Ruiz -Mallén et al., 2021).  Using a lens of critical witnessing (Villenas, 2019), we share a case study of two sixth-grade youth from an historically minoritized community who created a speculative graphic novel  addressing climate change and air quality. This approach allows us to engage with students’ voices and narratives, “co-conspiring” with them (Love, 2019) to amplify their stories. We ask: How do graphic novels enable critical witnessing of youths’ lived experiences with climate change and support their hopes and capacity for community-based action as agents for climate activism?

## Theoretical framework

Why critical witnessing matters in the study of learning about climate change Climate change threatens places and livelihoods worldwide, disproportionately affecting vulnerable communities such as people of color, Indigenous peoples, and ethnic minorities who face unjust and unequal impacts (Trott et al., 2023). However, dominant approaches to science education often perpetuate epistemic injustice by privileging White, Western epistemologies as the only legitimate forms of knowledge (Bang & Marin, 2015). Dominant 1822 approaches emphasize objectivity, value-neutrality, and universality, thereby separating science from society (Smith et al., 2022). As a result, they overlook the emotional, social, political, and ethical dimensions of learning and fail to account for how cultural and geographic contexts shape communities’ experiences of climate change (Zeidler et al., 2019). By avoiding the complexities and lived realities of climate change, students are denied opportunities to make meaning about how science intersects with urgent social challenges. This avoidance further marginalizes those most affected, narrowing what counts as legitimate knowledge and limiting possibilities for transformative climate justice education.

Critical witnessing is an ethical orientation toward being with and learning from the lived experiences of youth in context. It involves serving as both an “eye-witness” to what youth reveal and “bearing witness to what cannot be seen” (Oliver, 2004). In the study of learning, critical witnessing holds power because it means seeing and hearing the multiple truths of children, recognizing them as whole beings, and making visible the injustices they experience within systems of power and possibility (Pillow, 2019). It also requ ires engaging with young people’s everyday testimonies to surface and resist deficit narratives about minoritized youth and families (Dutro, 2009). As Verlie (2024) notes, “witnessing diverse people’s varied experiences is a first and foundational step towards affective climate justice.” This practice “involves listening to and believing someone’s account of their experience of violence, trauma, and/or injustice, and responding in ways that support that person” (Verlie, 2024, p. 1606).

Drawing upon participatory critical design ethnographic methods, we collaborated with a  teacher, and four 6th-grade classrooms of students and families to co-design and implement a climate justice unit.  Our project took place in an urban public middle school located in one of the top 10 most polluted zip codes in the country,  serving a vibrant Arab community with ongoing migration from the Middle East. 89% of the 90 participating students experience economic disadvantage. While English is the instructional language, we fostered a supportive space for authentic communication in native languages and multimodal expression. During the unit, students explored and mapped local climate experiences, monitored CO₂ levels in homes and schools, discussed vulnerabilit y and climate change across times and scales, and explored trees' role in mitigation through tree equity lessons. Finally, students created an action project focused on sustainable communities, presenting during the school's EXPO community night.

Data were co-generated through field notes, video/audio recordings, student artifacts, group interviews, and creative inquiry. This case study centers on a graphic novel by Lena and Sunny, who responded to a prompt by identifying air pollution as a key issue and imagining solutions. Our data analysis followed grounded theory procedures, including multiple stages of open coding and constant comparison (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). We iteratively analyzed image-making/art alongside textual data to identify emerging themes around reality, power, agency, hope, and possibilities related to climate justice. Lena and Sunny's graphic novel illustrates these themes in nuanced depth.

Our findings highlight how youth employed arts-based storytelling, specifically through a speculative graphic novel, to critically witness and respond to climate injustice in their communities. First, through their creative work, the youth positioned themselves as active agents who both exposed the visible harms of climate change and illuminated the often-unseen systemic and emotional dimensions of environmental injustice. In particular, their work makes visible both explicit challenges, such as community vulnerability, and implicit possibilities, revealing “what is shown” (youth-identified problems and solutions) alongside “what cannot be seen” (systemic constraints and aspirations for action). Second, their speculative work, in both process and product, further collectively reveal youth capacity for empathy, critical analysis, and hopeful engagement across scales of activity toward more just futures.

Below we provide an overview of the graphic novel followed by a discussion of our two key points. Overview of youth graphic novel-climate comics: Air Pollution! The graphic novel starts by introducing the protagonist, Bob the Builder, walking to school, puzzled by the lack of snowfall despite it being winter. When he encounters his friend Hadhad, they discuss the unusual weather, and Bob attributes it to climate change. The central issue youth explore is “how harmful climate change is,” coupled with the frustration that “no one listens” – “no one taking action.” Driven with this concern, Hadhad and Bob team up with two other friends, Ele and Josh Allen, hoping to brainstorm ways to “help the earth”  from pollution. They create an educational video illustrating the causes and effects of air pollution, such as greenhouse gas emissions and their impact on human and non-human well1823 being. Their proposed solutions include reducing car usage and shutting down factories. When the video goes viral, it sparks widespread community dialogue through viewer comments like “ That's so true,” “I never knew that,” and “I'll help too.” Bob and Ele amplify their message with posters throughout town, eventually catching the attention of the president, who implements sweeping policy changes such as factory closures and a transition from gas vehicles to green alternatives. The story culminates in environmental restoration, with clean oceans, revitalized ecosystems, and thriving communities, embodied in the hopeful message, “let’s make America a good place.” Through their sequential art, the youth position themselves as critical witnesses and active agents of dialogue, envisioning collaborative change through civic engagement. Youth participants expressed a strong desire for others to recognize how climate change directly affects their everyday lives. Their accounts reveal that this awareness is not abstract but grounded in embodied experiences of environmental injust ice. Their concern and care extend across species and generations, shaping their commitment to raising awareness and amplifying truths about the unequal distribution of climate harm.

For example, through their graphic novel, Lena and Sunny depict how air pollution functions as a tangible manifestation of environmental injustice, shaping both their health and sense of community well-being. Sunny describes their graphic novel as speculative and future-oriented, yet firmly rooted in the reality of their community, one of the “top ten most polluted” ZIP codes in the country. For her, storytelling becomes an act of resistance and truth-telling: “I have to, like, tell people. I have to, like, let my little cousins, like, let other people know about it. And like, spread out how, like, bad it can be.” This statement underscores how youth transform personal witnessing into collective advocacy, linking local experience to broader calls for environ mental justice. Their narrative also exposes how vulnerability is structurally produced through proximity to industrial polluters. Lena articulates this systemic awareness, emphasizing that clean air is a universal right: “for everyone actually, because, like, we need great, like, air to breathe.” She connects industrial emissions to concrete health outcomes, stating that “factories can, like, put out, like, CO2 and, like, it’s very bad for breathing air… can cause you to go to the hospital”, making visible the embodied consequences of pollution. In their graphic novel, for example, they illustrate a person coughing against a backdrop of smokestacks which serves as a visual metaphor for the health burdens their community faces (see Figure 2). Figure 2 Proximity to Industrial Polluters made Visible in“Air Pollution!” and Photos from Local Sources Finally, their reflections on receiving home air filters demonstrate both the immediacy of harm and the relief that comes with environmental care. As Lena explains, “since they like, sent it to our house, they like, the breathing air is so good. It’s like fresh.” However, it is clear that the air filters do not actually solve the problem as evidenced by the people coughing and going to the hospital in their novel, but only serves to superficially help communities. This moment encapsulates the youth’s acute awareness of environmental inequality and their vision of what just, breathable futures might feel like.

Shared responsibility and hope for collective thriving In this section, we explore how youth leverage their desire for collective thriving to speculate hopeful pathways to more just futures. Their speculative work, in both process and product, collectively reveal youth capacity for 1824 empathy, critical analysis, and hopeful engagement across scales of activity toward more just futures. Through their comic, Lena and Sunny urgently advocate for clean air as a fundamental human right, declaring, “we need clear AIR!! Now” and “We all have the RIGHT TO BREATH FRESH Air!” They emphasize the need for "actual good solutions to stop it immediate ly,” educating readers about actionable strategies at multiple levels. At the macro level, they recommend updating factories with green technologies such as solar energy, wind turbines to replace smokestacks, and electric vehicles, noting “we can stop using fossil fuels as much.” At the community leve l, they propose planting more trees after discovering through tree equity data that their area has fewer trees, “I want more trees. Imagine seeing like, going outside to see all green and beautiful.” At the individual level, they recommend reducing energy consumption and choosing electric vehicles. Critically, they acknowledge intersecting injustices and barrie rs, recognizing that electric vehicles are often financially inaccessible to vulnerable communities. They propose, “we can lower the price for people so they can afford it,” calling for subsidization to ensure equitable access to sustainable solutions. They also acknowledge that meaningful change requires engaging those in power through political alliances. In their comic, they propose using posters to “get someone's attention, for example, the mayor, who can tell the president to do something about it.”  Their lived experiences as minoritized youth inform a nuanced understanding of systemic barriers to justice. Drawing on their ethico-onto-epistemological perspective, they explore both the possibilities and limitations of action within their sociopolitical context: “I feel like, even protesting is gonna be a good idea but even protesting hasn't worked yet. Many people do it. Even for Palestine people have tried that.” Lena makes sense of government inaction by comparing the lack of intervention in her community's pollution to Palestine.

Despite legitimate doubts about government allyship, Lena and Sunny remain hopeful and stress that meaningful climate solutions require everyone “to gather up and do something about it” through community engagement, dialogue, reciprocal care, and intergenerational collaboration. Sharing their comic during the school’s community night amplified this impact. They observed, “When you started telling the story like that, a lot of people started coming, like, a lot. So that was good, because most of them started learning what the story is about.” They expressed a desire to continue spreading their message “to other students or like children, kindergarteners” through public presentations. This constructive hope (Ojala, 2011) is the driving force sustaining their engagement in climate justice activism.

This study suggests that arts-based storytelling, and in this case, specifically the creation of graphic novels, can transform learning about climate change into a site of critical witnessing, empathy, and justice-oriented action. Through their speculative  yet grounded narratives, youth like Lena and Sunny illustrate how artistic expression serves as both testimony and resistance, amplifying community voices often silenced in dominant climate discourses. Their creative process reveals how storytelling enabl es minoritized students to connect scientific understanding with lived experience, making visible the intertwined realities of environmental harm, systemic inequity, and collective hope.

By positioning themselves as both witnesses and change-makers, these young artists reimagine climate education as a participatory, humanizing practice rooted in relational care and social accountability. Their graphic novel not only exposes environmental i njustice but also articulates concrete, imaginative pathways toward collective thriving. The youths’ work challenges educators and researchers to recognize art as epistemologically legitimate, as a mode of inquiry and activism capable of nurturing affective, ethical, and intellectual engagement. Ultimately, integrating multimodal, arts-based pedagogies (Halvorsen & Sawyer, 2022) invites classrooms to become spaces of co-conspiracy (Love, 2019) and co-creation, where learning transcends content mastery to cultivate agency, empathy, and systemic awareness. Findings reinforce that justice oriented science education fosters constructive hope (Ojala, 2011), agency, and competence and underscores the need to center historically minoritized youth as experts. Practically, the study supports integrating visual and arts-based methods, and various technologies into co-designed curricula and encourages researchers and policymakers to partner with vulnerable youth in reciprocal ways by leveraging arts‑based methodologies. Future participatory research should directly address positionality and power of all participants and explore how youth artwork candirectly inform research goals and axiological progress, K-12 curriculum development, and sustained youth action. Educators are called to center youth voices, especially those from marginalized communities, as vital contributors to envisioning and enacting climate justice. In doing so, we affirm that critical witnessing through art is not only a pedagogical method but also a transformative act of hope; one that reclaims the right to imagine and build just, breathable futures for all.
