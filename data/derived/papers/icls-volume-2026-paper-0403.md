# Boundary learning in a research-practice partnership

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs) are collaborations between academics and practitioners that focus on problems of practice while engaging with research. Within RPPs, various forms of learning can be mediated by the social boundaries that separate partners. In this case study, we employed ethnographic and discourse analysis methods to understand the types of boundary learning that were afforded by an RPP. Our findings showed that boundaries emerged within practitioner and academic groups, and not just between them. Boundary learning was characterized by modest forms of coordination and reflection, rather than profound transformation. Our findings suggest that RPPs should be understood as sites of complex, multi-layered boundary work.

Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs) have been heralded in recent years as a new paradigm for evidence-based educational improvement. One way to understand how RPPs function is through the notion of boundaries (Farrell et al., 2022), which provide an analytic lens that helps conceptualize how individuals engage with one another inbetween and across the separate sites they inhabit. People known as boundary spanners  and artifacts called boundary objects can play a major role in these encounters (Star, 2010; Wenger, 1998). Social boundaries, which by their very definition entail encounters with new ways of experiencing and understanding the world, afford unique learning opportunities. Akkerman and Bakker (2011) identified four learning mechanisms afforded by boundaries. (1) Identification, (2) Coordination, (3) Reflection, and (4) transformative learning. In this paper we present some findings from a case study that investigated the boundary learning that unfolded in an RPP context. To accomplish this, we asked what meaningful sociocultural boundaries emerged in the RPP, and w hat types of learning occurred on, within, or across those boundaries?

## Context

This case study focused on an RPP that included two academic institutions and 21 Israeli schools. The central aim of the partnership was to drive pedagogical innovation and school improvement by empowering practitioners to address local problems of practice through collaborative, systematic inquiry, thereby building a sustainable culture of evidence-based decision-making and iterative design of pedagogical resources. T he partnership forged direct collaborations between academic faculty and school-based practitioners. Over the course of the 2022-3 school year, the first author collected a trove of ethnographic data from three sources: a primary school; the RPP steering committee; and a series of RPP-wide meetings that were attended by the entire RPP community. Data included video and audio recordings of meetings and conversations, semi-structured and unstructured interviews with practitioners and academics, and detailed field notes. Data was coded three times using various ethnographic and discourse analytic tools (Agar, 1986; Bamberg, 1997; Gee, 2014). Our analysis focused on three boundaries that emerged in the context of the  RPP, each mediating  a different learning process. Only one of the boundaries  spanned the researcher —practitioner divide, while the other two were intra-organizational: between educational practitioners at the primary school, and between academic faculty members from one of the academic institutions. In this section we offer a glimpse into  one of these boundaries and the learning processes it mediated.

Efforts at translation (Academic/Academic) Dan and Linor (Author 3) were colleagues at one of the academic institutions. Dan  valued innovation and was skeptical towards most academic research, which he viewed as overly theoretical  and oftentimes irrelevant to 2638 practitioners. Linor was a more typical academic, who devoted most of her efforts to publishing research articles, securing grants, and supervising graduate students. As the year unfolded, it became increasingly evident that Dan and Linor had different conceptions of the RPP’s purpose, and that  their divergent views were rooted in their contrasting professional identities  (innovator vs. scholar). This created a boundary that  they were required to navigate. In this section, we focus on an ongoing conversation they had surrounding the notion of research, which meant something different to each of them. Mapping the narrated events (what the interlocutors were talking about) in this segment revealed how Dan and Linor’s debate over the meaning of the notion of research was part of the broader misalignment between their professional identities, and thus emblematic of the boundary between them. Dan began by fleshing out the purpose he saw for the research we were conducting about the RPP  (culminating in findings presented herein): “it should have added value for the [school] principals” (303). Moreover, he believed that to have value, the research should emulate what happens in the high-tech sector, which Dan —the entrepreneur —admired, and sought to associate himself with (302-12). In response, Linor  offered a scathing evaluation of Dan’s proposal, and highlighted what she saw as a deep misunderstanding of the term “research.” Indeed, in less than 30 seconds, Linor repeated five(!) times that Dan’s questions were not research questions  at all (313-7). In this way, Linor  positioned herself as a gatekeeper, implying that research was something she understood as a scholar, but that Dan  clearly did not comprehend. As the year progressed, it became increasingly apparent that the transformative  boundary learning Dan had envisioned for the more traditionally-minded academic faculty members on the steering committee was unlikely to materialize. Nevertheless, the steering committee still had to find ways to work productively together. Ultimately this negotiation led to boundary learning that manifested itself through efforts at translation, a form of coordination that does not involve reconstructing a boundary, so much as establishing continuity across it by facilitating “movement between different sites” (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011, p. 144).

Our findings challenge  the common assumption that the primary boundary in RPPs is the one separating researchers from practitioners.  We found that boundaries also emerged internally: among faculty members and among educational practitioners. These findings suggest that the landscape of boundaries in an RPP can be layered and complex, and that RPPs afford intra-organizational boundary learning opportunities just as much as inter-organizational ones. We also found that notwithstanding the expectation for transformative learning opportunities, boundary learning tended to unfold in the form of small refinements such as perspective taking/making and efforts at translation.
