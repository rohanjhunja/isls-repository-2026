# Show Your Work: A Gentle Introduction to Jupyter Notebooks for K-12 Educators

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract

We are developing a suite of free, web-based, asynchronous introductory Jupyter notebooks designed for K-12 educators with little to no prior experience with programming or with computational notebooks. In contrast to typical “intro notebooks” which focus on technical features, the Show Your Work project builds on theory and findings from the learning sciences and science education to introduce computational notebooks as epistemic tools within specific school subject domains, guiding educators to explore the questions: What role can notebooks play in my specific classroom? What does it feel like to conduct an investigation using a Jupyter notebook? What epistemic and communicative practices are foregrounded and backgrounded by computational notebooks? These materials are likely of interest to ISLS members who work in computing education, computing-integrated STEM education, and/or teacher education, as well as to members who wish to learn more about computational narrative as a new area of focus within the learning sciences. Motivation Jupyter notebooks are a form of computational notebook, an artifact that allows multiple blocks of editable, executable code and its output to be interspersed with rich text in a single document in the spirit of Knuth’s “literate programming” (Knuth, 1984). The name, “Jupyter,” evokes three computer languages commonly associated especially with data analysis – Julia, Python, and R (Perez & Granger, 2015). Since its original release about a decade ago, Jupyter notebooks now support a wide variety of computational languages (Granger & Perez, 2021). While it is not the first example of computational notebooks, Jupyter has been widely adopted due to its availability as a free and open source tool, and the Jupyter infrastructure now forms the foundation of a variety of popular interactive computing environments such as Google Colab and Deepnote. Although they were originally developed as a scientific research and communication tool, there is growing interest in using Jupyter notebooks at the K-12 level. Several research projects have studied the integration of Jupyter notebooks into secondary and intro college instructional materials (Fleischer et al., 2022; Son et al., 2021; Vandusen, 2020). They are also becoming adopted as a curricular infrastructure including as part of Berkeley’s popular Data 8 “Introduction to Data Science” course activities (data6.org; data8.org), UCLA’s CourseKata high school mathematics curriculum (coursekata.org), and ProDaBi, a data/AI curricular package for secondary students (www.prodabi.de/en/). Most recently Jupyter Everywhere, a free, lightweight, simplified Jupyter interpreter interface that makes it easy for teachers and students to launch, edit, and share Jupyter notebooks without installing special software or services (jupytereverywhere.org). Jupyter notebooks, as a popular example of a broader genre of computational notebooks, are likely to be of particular interest to the Learning Sciences community for theoretical reasons. In a recent paper, Odden and Zwickl (2025) described Jupyter notebooks as an example of diSessa’s notion of computational literacy: highlighting their integration of the material dimension of computational literacy through its support for explicit executable code, the cognitive (epistemic) dimension through users’s ability to test, revise, and keep note of the discoveries they make as they examine the output of their code, and the social (communicative) dimension through the ability to share this blend of code with text, images, and other explanatory media with others. However, there is growing evidence that additional support is needed for Jupyter notebooks to be productively leveraged in K-12 classrooms. Most existing work describes a variety of technical and conceptual difficulties that teachers and students encounter when working with computational notebooks (e.g., Hanč et al., 2020; Johnson, 2020). While the potential for deep epistemic engagement through computational exploration and communication is high (Hüsing et al., 2024), without careful design or preparation notebooks are at risk of surface-level engagements in which students simply execute embedded code and read the output as if it were a printed text. T he need for teacher professional development Beyond the need to address the technical challenges for teachers that are already well-documented in the literature, it is also important to offer pedagogical and epistemological support when introducing new epistemic tools in curricular materials. Ample work has demonstrated that introducing even “authentic” tools in secondary classrooms is only valuable to the extent that they are understood by teachers and students to support sensemaking (Stroupe et al., 2019). For example, students who are asked to use agent-based models to explore the particulate nature of matter are only successful insofar as they identify some compatibility between their representation of the phenomenon and the affordances of the tool (Wilkerson et al., 2018). Inspired from Russ’ (2014) distinction between epistemology “of” science versus epistemology “for” science, here we might similarly articulate computing not only as a practice “of,” but also “for” scientific reasoning. Teachers play a critical role in helping students recognize the potential benefits of a given computational tool for advancing their understanding (Wagh et al., 2025). Thus to maintain disciplinary depth and support all students, teachers need to clearly understand the learning goals that a given activity or tool is meant to support, and be able to notice and respond to student thinking in service of those goals (Robertson et al., 2015). A first step to supporting such understandings is to examine how teachers, as learners themselves, might experience computational notebooks as a sensemaking tool. Even if teachers do recognize the ways in which computational notebooks might function as an epistemic tool, there are still possible ambiguities regarding exactly what aspects of the analysis of complex datasets they can support. This is because data investigations are naturally interdisciplinary, and therefore their role within the curriculum can be ambiguous (Finzer, 2013). There are a number of paths an investigation can take. For example, in our own prior work, we found that teachers might emphasize dramatically different aspects of a data visualization, for instance, focusing on scientific, social, or mathematical learning goals (Wilkerson et al., 2025); a practice consistent with broader findings suggesting that teachers significantly shape how materials are enacted in their classroom (Remillard & Heck, 2014). Thus demonstrating how Jupyter notebooks or other computational narrative tools might integrate into a variety of disciplines, with a variety of potential (e.g. computational, statistical, or content-oriented) goals is consistent with what we know about teachers’ data integration practices. Design principles To address the need for epistemologically and pedagogically oriented teacher professional development experiences, we are developing (with a cohort of approximately 30 pre-service and in-service teachers, using a judicious informant design approach; Scaife et al., 1997) a suite of computational notebooks intended to form the basis of an interactive, self-paced introduction to Python notebooks for K-12 classroom educators. Our rationale, building on the preliminary findings from research described above, is that many practicing teachers are being introduced to notebooks through their integration into curriculum, without time to familiarize themselves with not only the technical but also and more importantly with the epistemological and pedagogical dimensions of these tools. In a preliminary analysis of several existing “Introduction to Jupyter” notebooks developed both for practitioners and for educators, we identified an almost exclusive focus on their technical aspects. This included how a notebook can be run, edited, and what are some of its basic and advanced features. Several also included features that support pedagogical goals such as encouraging a tinkering disposition (“It is easy to break the code this way, but that is a normal part of coding!” from the YouCubed Jupyter introduction) or supporting the inquiry cycle (as demonstrated in CourseKata’s Jupyter introductory materials). Some existing introductory notebooks briefly addressed the epistemic role of notebooks in science, typically through appeals to authority. Building on these initial findings, our sequence of notebooks intentionally foreground the pedagogical potential of notebooks as an epistemic tool, as grounded within use cases across a variety of school subjects. Each notebook is designed to explicitly communicate about eight principles we identified as desirable in our review of existing introductory notebooks including the purpose of leveraging a computational notebook for an activity; providing a comparison to other tools that might be used in such an activity; modeling and encouraging documentation both of code and of users’ thinking; describing the planned use of specific or unique features of the notebook to aid in analysis; supporting specific code learning goals; supporting specific data learning goals; supporting specific content learning goals, and encouraging productive dispositions toward notebook use. Guided by these principles, our suite of notebooks provides teachers with repeated experiences in which they are expected to conduct inquiry using specific combinations of code, data, and content, and to encounter useful or unexpected findings in the process (see next section). As we make clear in our materials, our goal is not necessarily to advocate for the wholesale integration of notebooks into teachers’ work. Rather, as we state explicitly in our introductory notebook, we seek to “introduce [teachers] to what notebooks are, explore how they might be useful, and help [teachers] decide if they are right for [their] classroom.” (Intro Notebook). T he “Show York Work” suite of notebooks The “Show Your Work” sequence of activities is under active development, with five notebooks ready for sharing at the time of writing and an anticipated 10 ready by the time of the ISLS conference. When completed, the materials will include notebooks organized into four phases. Phase 1 provides a basic introduction and early data exploration experience using Jupyter notebooks. Phase 2 provides a variety of notebooks designed to introduce key code and data concepts that are most likely to be relevant to integrating Jupyter data explorations into particular school subjects such as chemistry, physics, statistics, and so on. For these first two phases, most materials are already well-developed and we are in the process of testing and refining them in collaboration with approximately 30 pre-service and in-service teachers. Phase 3 will include deeper dives into key computing and data concepts along with student case studies to encourage teacher noticing as mediated by Jupyter notebook interactions (e.g., Walkoe et al., 2017). Phase 4 will include materials to support teachers in adapting and creating their own notebooks for classroom use. This includes scaffolding their use of more traditional Jupyter documentation materials, and offering guidance on judicious use of generative AI tools, particularly those embedded in notebook development tools such as VSCode, whilst attending to the pedagogical and investigative principles introduced throughout the materials. These third and fourth phases are planned, though we anticipate that draft materials will be available for sharing by the time of the conference. Phase I: Introducing notebooks as computational narratives Our introductory notebook introduces teachers to the idea of a computational notebook as an “interactive lab notebook” in which they can keep, experiment with data and code, and communicate findings. The notebook guides teachers through the basic aspects of navigating, executing, and editing computational notebooks; diagnosing common errors and how to address them; and compares them to other epistemic tools used for data analysis in K-12 classrooms. Motivation Jupyter notebooks are a form of computational notebook, an artifact that allows multiple blocks of editable, executable code and its output to be interspersed with rich text in a single document in the spirit of Knuth’s “literate programming” (Knuth, 1984). The name, “Jupyter,” evokes three computer languages commonly associated especially with data analysis – Julia, Python, and R (Perez & Granger, 2015). Since its original release about a decade ago, Jupyter notebooks now support a wide variety of computational languages (Granger & Perez, 2021). While it is not the first example of computational notebooks, Jupyter has been widely adopted due to its availability as a free and open source tool, and the Jupyter infrastructure now forms the foundation of a variety of popular interactive computing environments such as Google Colab and Deepnote. Although they were originally developed as a scientific research and communication tool, there is growing interest in using Jupyter notebooks at the K-12 level. Several research projects have studied the integration of Jupyter notebooks into secondary and intro college instructional materials (Fleischer et al., 2022; Son et al., 2021; Vandusen, 2020). They are also becoming adopted as a curricular infrastructure including as part of Berkeley’s popular Data 8 “Introduction to Data Science” course activities (data6.org; data8.org), UCLA’s CourseKata high school mathematics curriculum (coursekata.org), and ProDaBi, a data/AI curricular package for secondary students (www.prodabi.de/en/). Most recently Jupyter Everywhere, a free, lightweight, simplified Jupyter interpreter interface that makes it easy for teachers and students to launch, edit, and share Jupyter notebooks without installing special software or services (jupytereverywhere.org). Jupyter notebooks, as a popular example of a broader genre of computational notebooks, are likely to be of particular interest to the Learning Sciences community for theoretical reasons. In a recent paper, Odden and Zwickl (2025) described Jupyter notebooks as an example of diSessa’s notion of computational literacy: highlighting their integration of the material dimension of computational literacy through its support for explicit executable code, the cognitive (epistemic) dimension through users’s ability to test, revise, and keep note of the discoveries they make as they examine the output of their code, and the social (communicative) dimension through the ability to share this blend of code with text, images, and other explanatory media with others. However, there is growing evidence that additional support is needed for Jupyter notebooks to be productively leveraged in K-12 classrooms. Most existing work describes a variety of technical and conceptual difficulties that teachers and students encounter when working with computational notebooks (e.g., Hanč et al., 2020; Johnson, 2020). While the potential for deep epistemic engagement through computational exploration and communication is high (Hüsing et al., 2024), without careful design or preparation notebooks are at risk of surfacelevel engagements in which students simply execute embedded code and read the output as if it were a printed text. T he need for teacher professional development Beyond the need to address the technical challenges for teachers that are already well-documented in the literature, it is also important to offer pedagogical and epistemological support when introducing new epistemic tools in curricular materials. Ample work has demonstrated that introducing even “authentic” tools in secondary classrooms is only valuable to the extent that they are understood by teachers and students to support sensemaking (Stroupe et al., 2019). For example, students who are asked to use agent-based models to explore the particulate nature of matter are only successful insofar as they identify some compatibility between their representation of the phenomenon and the affordances of the tool (Wilkerson et al., 2018). Inspired from Russ’ (2014) distinction between epistemology “of” science versus epistemology “for” science, here we might similarly articulate computing not only as a practice “of,” but also “for” scientific reasoning. Teachers play a critical role in helping students recognize the potential benefits of a given computational tool for advancing their understanding (Wagh et al., 2025). Thus to maintain disciplinary depth and support all students, teachers need to clearly understand the learning goals that a given activity or tool is meant to support, and be able to notice and respond to student thinking in service of those goals (Robertson et al., 2015). A first step to supporting such understandings is to examine how teachers, as learners themselves, might experience computational notebooks as a sensemaking tool. Even if teachers do recognize the ways in which computational notebooks might function as an epistemic tool, there are still possible ambiguities regarding exactly what aspects of the analysis of complex datasets they can support. This is because data investigations are naturally interdisciplinary, and therefore their role within the curriculum can be ambiguous (Finzer, 2013). There are a number of paths an investigation can take. For example, in our own prior work, we found that teachers might emphasize dramatically different aspects of a data visualization, for instance, focusing on scientific, social, or mathematical learning goals (Wilkerson et al., 2025); a practice consistent with broader findings suggesting that teachers significantly shape how materials are enacted in their classroom (Remillard & Heck, 2014). Thus demonstrating how Jupyter notebooks or other computational narrative tools might integrate into a variety of disciplines, with a variety of potential (e.g. computational, statistical, or content-oriented) goals is consistent with what we know about teachers’ data integration practices. Design principles To address the need for epistemologically and pedagogically oriented teacher professional development experiences, we are developing (with a cohort of approximately 30 pre-service and in-service teachers, using a judicious informant design approach; Scaife et al., 1997) a suite of computational notebooks intended to form the basis of an interactive, self-paced introduction to Python notebooks for K-12 classroom educators. Our rationale, building on the preliminary findings from research described above, is that many practicing teachers are being introduced to notebooks through their integration into curriculum, without time to familiarize themselves with not only the technical but also and more importantly with the epistemological and pedagogical dimensions of these tools. In a preliminary analysis of several existing “Introduction to Jupyter” notebooks developed both for practitioners and for educators, we identified an almost exclusive focus on their technical aspects. This included how a notebook can be run, edited, and what are some of its basic and advanced features. Several also included features that support pedagogical goals such as encouraging a tinkering disposition (“It is easy to break the code this way, but that is a normal part of coding!” from the YouCubed Jupyter introduction) or supporting the inquiry cycle (as demonstrated in CourseKata’s Jupyter introductory materials). Some existing introductory notebooks briefly addressed the epistemic role of notebooks in science, typically through appeals to authority. Building on these initial findings, our sequence of notebooks intentionally foreground the pedagogical potential of notebooks as an epistemic tool, as grounded within use cases across a variety of school subjects. Each notebook is designed to explicitly communicate about eight principles we identified as desirable in our review of existing introductory notebooks including the purpose of leveraging a computational notebook for an activity; providing a comparison to other tools that might be used in such an activity; modeling and encouraging documentation both of code and of users’ thinking; describing the planned use of specific or unique features of the notebook to aid in analysis; supporting specific code learning goals; supporting specific data learning goals; supporting specific content learning goals, and encouraging productive dispositions toward notebook use. Guided by these principles, our suite of notebooks provides teachers with repeated experiences in which they are expected to conduct inquiry using specific combinations of code, data, and content, and to encounter useful or unexpected findings in the process (see next section). As we make clear in our materials, our goal is not necessarily to advocate for the wholesale integration of notebooks into teachers’ work. Rather, as we state explicitly in our introductory notebook, we seek to “introduce [teachers] to what notebooks are, explore how they might be useful, and help [teachers] decide if they are right for [their] classroom.” (Intro Notebook). T he “Show York Work” suite of notebooks The “Show Your Work” sequence of activities is under active development, with five notebooks ready for sharing at the time of writing and an anticipated 10 ready by the time of the ISLS conference. When completed, the materials will include notebooks organized into four phases. Phase 1 provides a basic introduction and early data exploration experience using Jupyter notebooks. Phase 2 provides a variety of notebooks designed to introduce key code and data concepts that are most likely to be relevant to integrating Jupyter data explorations into particular school subjects such as chemistry, physics, statistics, and so on. For these first two phases, most materials are already well-developed and we are in the process of testing and refining them in collaboration with approximately 30 pre-service and in-service teachers. Phase 3 will include deeper dives into key computing and data concepts along with student case studies to encourage teacher noticing as mediated by Jupyter notebook interactions (e.g., Walkoe et al., 2017). Phase 4 will include materials to support teachers in adapting and creating their own notebooks for classroom use. This includes scaffolding their use of more traditional Jupyter documentation materials, and offering guidance on judicious use of generative AI tools, particularly those embedded in notebook development tools such as VSCode, whilst attending to the pedagogical and investigative principles introduced throughout the materials. These third and fourth phases are planned, though we anticipate that draft materials will be available for sharing by the time of the conference.
