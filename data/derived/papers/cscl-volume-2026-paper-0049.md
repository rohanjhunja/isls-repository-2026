# Exploring Shared Agency in an Intelligent Immersive Learning Environment for Collaborative Writing

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
An increasing number of studies on AI-student collaboration highlight the importance of learner agency in the Generative AI (GenAI)-powered learning environment. However, limited research has examined how student-AI shared agency can be assessed, and it remains unclear whether individual interaction with AI agents or peer collaboration with AI agents is more effective in developing such agency. This study explores second language (L2) students’ shared agency within an intelligent immersive learning environment for writing. Using WriteCube, a self-developed AR-and AI-enabled writing system, 75 Primary 4 students from two classes in Singapore completed picture-based composition tasks under individual-AI and pair-AI collaboration conditions. Findings revealed no significant performance difference between groups, suggesting a trade-off between interactional richness and task efficiency. Regression results indicated that interaction quality, rather than frequency or language proficiency, positively predicted shared agency, particularly in ensuring equity. The study offers implications for assessing and enhancing learner agency in GenAI-supported L2 classrooms.

Agency has become a central concern in discussion on Generative AI (GenAI) integration in teaching and learning (Darvishi et al., 2024). The notion of agency has been typically defined as the learner’s capacity to act intentionally and exert control over their learning (Bandura, 2002). While from the sociocultural perspective, agency is not merely a personal trait but also an inherently collective phenomenon, shaped by social, cultural, and contexts (Eteläpelto et al., 2013), and fundamentally emerging as t he result of inter-subjective processes. As the  use of GenAI increasingly destabilizes boundaries between humans and technology, it becomes critical to  examine learner agency by considering how they navigate, critique, and shape learning experiences with GenAI  (Code, 2025). This study seeks  to examine shared agency between students and AI agents, building on the concept  of collective human agency in studies of computer-supported collaborative learning (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014; Zhang et al., 2021), while also incorporating considerations of ethical engagement and critical awareness (Ralston, 2023). Student-AI shared agency, in this study, is conceptualized as the emergent and dynamic capacity of students to co-act with AI agents. There remains a lack of research on how shared agency can be assessed (Code, 2025). Moreover, it is still unclear whether individual interaction with AI agents or peer collaboration  with AI agents is more effective in developing Student-AI shared agency.

To address this gap, the present study was proposed and conducted based on WriteCube, a self-designed and developed writing system for Chinese as a second language (L2) learning. The system brings students into immersive augmented reality (AR) scenarios, empowered by AI agents. AR and AI together offer complementary strengths for designing L2 writing environments  (Wen et al., 2025). While AI support often remains decontextualized, lacking the authentic and multimodal cues needed to ground language use in real-world contexts, AR technologies provide authentic, multisensory, and context-rich environments that situate writing tasks in meaningful scenarios, enhance motivation, and reduce language anxiety (Yang et al., 2023). The system was implemented in two Primary 4 Chinese language classes from a school in Singapore. This study aimed to deepen understanding and empirically examine agency in the GenAI-powered learning environments.  The Theoretical foundations Code developed the Agency for Learning Framework to measure agency in learning context by considering both individual and environmental influences on learners’ ability to act (Code, 2020). As the agency of technology is acknowledged, technology does not only play a role in mediating learner agency. Code further proposed the updated framework about the Postdigital Learner Agency, “equipping leaners to navigate, critique, and shape the systems mediating their educational experiences” (Code, 2025, p.338). In this perspective, agency extends beyond individual action to include collective and relational dimensions. Collective agency emphasizes collaboration, coregulation, and shared goals, fostering mutual understanding and problem-solving (Lipponen & Kumpulainen 339 2011), while relational agency emphasizes the dynamic and contextual nature of agency shaped through interactions with both human and non-human actors (Siles et al., 2024). Building on this perspective, the present study proposes student-AI shared agency, highlighting the dynamic and contextual nature of agency emerging through interactions between learners and AI systems.

The integration of GenAI as a partner broadens the scope of collaborative L2 writing. Existing studies have examined how students individually use GenAI in collaborative writing contexts (e.g., Kim et al., 2025), as well as how human peers work with GenAI to mitigate limitations in language proficiency that may hinder effective interaction with AI agents (e.g., Michel et al., 2025). Human peers bring contextual sensitivity, affective support, and stronger benefits for long-term retention (Yang et al., 2023). Despite growing research on AI-assisted writing, little is known about how factors such as students’ language proficiency, engagement level, and collaboration mode (individual-AI vs. human peer collaboration) shape student-AI shared agency in L2 writing. Addressing this gap is essential for understanding how human and AI collaborators jointly contribute to the development of shared agency in AI-supported writing environments. The following research questions guide this pilot study.

RQ1:  Do individual and pair writing modes differentially impact students’ writing performance and student-AI shared agency development?

RQ2:  What factors are associated with student-AI shared agency development in the intelligent immersive learning environment?

## Participants and setting

The study involved 75 Primary 4 students from two intact classes in a Singapore primary school, taught by the same Chinese language teacher (15 years’ experience). The two classes were selected because they followed the same curriculum and were taught by the same teacher, helping hold instructional conditions constant while implementing different collaboration modes. Class A worked in the individual-AI condition (N = 37). Class B worked in the pair-AI condition (N = 38), with pairs randomly assigned within the class.

## Procedure

Students completed a 45-minute technical familiarization session followed by a 60-minute WriteCube activity. WriteCube renders task images as AR scenes and supports composing through two interaction phases: (1) a touchpoint phase for fine-grained descripti on via short dialogues and role-play, and (2) a chatroom phase for developing coherent paragraphs when additional support is needed. An AI agent prompts idea elaboration, recommends expressions, and provides feedback. In a final stage, the AI generates a draft composition from students’ inputs and students iteratively revise and complete a self-reflection checklist.

trained raters; final scores were averaged. We tested condition differences via one-way ANCOVA with pre-writing as covariate. (b) Shared agency: a post-survey adapted from a collective agency scale (Zhang et al., 2022) and extended with items reflecting reflective/ethical engagement with AI (Ralston, 2023). Qualitative inspection of dialogue logs suggests that many high-frequency exchanges reflected promptfollowing behavior, where students adopted AI suggestions with limited elaboration. For example, students often directly reused AI-provided expressions suc h as “眉头紧皱” in expanded descriptions (e.g., “ 丽丽低着头，眼 睛看着桌子，眉头紧皱 ”), or inserted idiomatic phrases like “ 心里像吃了蜜糖一样甜 ” with minimal modification. In contrast, episodes associated with higher perceived equality involved meaning clarification and integration, such as a student asking “尽管是什么意思？” and then applying it appropriately in the next sentence. Some students also treated the AI as a linguistic resource, requesting support (e.g., “ 你能给我一些好词好句 吗？”) before integrating the expressions into their narratives. Notably, explicit critique of AI suggestions was rarely observed.

The absence of significant performance differences between the individual-AI and pair-AI groups suggest a potential trade-off between interactional richness and task efficiency. Students working individually interacted with the AI more frequently and maintained continuous task engagement, which may explain their slightly higher writing scores. In contrast, pair-AI collaboration involved fewer but longer dialogue turns, reflecting additional peer negotiation. This pattern aligns with research suggesting that  early-stage L2 writers often move from imitative uptake toward deeper linguistic integration as they internalize scaffolding (Guirao et al., 2015). While peer collaboration may promote reflection and evaluation, such benefits may not immediately appear in shortterm performance measures. Regression results further indicated that interaction quality rather than interaction frequency predicted shared agency, particularly in the dimension of ensuring equality. Students who incorporated more meaningful expressions into their dialogues reported stronger perceptions of balanced collaboration with AI, whereas more frequent but surface-level exchanges were associated with lower perceived equality. Interestingly, language 341 proficiency did not significantly predict shared agency. This suggests that, compared with traditional peer collaborative writing where proficiency often shapes participation (Susanti et al., 2020), AI-supported environments may reduce participation disparities and allow lower-proficiency learners to engage more actively. Overall, these findings highlight that students’ perception of shared agency depends less on how often they interact with AI and more on how meaningfully they integrate AI suggestions into their own ideas. Future research should further explore instructional strategies that encourage deeper interaction with AI, including prompting students to question, reinterpret, or critique AI-generated suggestions. This exploratory study examined student-AI shared agency in an intelligent immersive L2 writing environment. The findings suggest that individual-AI and pair-AI collaboration produced comparable writing outcomes and similar levels of shared agency, while interaction quality emerged as a key factor associated with students’ perceptions of equal collaboration with AI.  Several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size and short intervention period may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle differences between conditions. Future studies with larger samples and longitudinal designs are needed to examine longer-term effects on language development and agency formation. In addition, while qualitative observations helped interpret quantitative patterns, a more syste matic analysis of student-AI interaction processes could further illuminate how shared agency develops over time. Despite these limitations, the study provides an initial empirical perspective on assessing shared agency in AI-supported learning environments and highlights the importance of designing learning activities that foster reflective and meaningful human-AI interaction. ## Literature review

The TAM conceptualizes perceived usefulness as the degree to which a user believes a system enhances performance, and perceived ease of use as the degree to which it is free of effort (Davis, 1989). TAM2 and TAM3 elaborate that output quality, job relevance, soci al influence, and system characteristics significantly shape these perceptions (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). In the context of GenAI, research consistently reports that students perceive AI chatbots as useful for improving efficiency, clarifying concepts, and simplifying coding tasks (Baidoo-Anu et al., 2024). Ease of use is often attributed to conversational interfaces, accessibility, and well-structured explanations (Vo & Nguyen, 2024). However, concerns regarding hallucinations, misinformation, and overreliance remain prevalent (Zastudil et al., 2023). Reliability has been shown to significantly affect trust and perceived usefulness (Weisz et al., 2022). While much of the literature focuses on higher education and general chatbots, there is comparatively limited investigation into customized K –12 educational tools and how their specific design features influence TAM constructs. Designing GenAI learning systems requires attention not only to functionality but also to scaffolding, cognitive load, and adaptability. Clear and structured explanations reduce extraneous cognitive load and support learning (Sweller, 1988). Furthermore, GenAI systems should encourage active engagement rather than passive answer retrieval (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023). Despite these insights, empirical work linking such design features to perceived usefulness and ease of use remains underdeveloped. This study therefore integrates TAM with concrete system features to better understand student acceptance. 344

## Research design and methods

A design-based research approach guided the development and evaluation of MyBotBuddy (Khor et al., 2025). The chatbot was informed by Vygotsky (1978)’s Zone of Proximal Development and implemented a scaffolding model that provided tiered support, beginning with conceptual explanation, followed by examples, and culminating in diagnostic feedback (Khor & Chan, 2025). The system incorporated retrieval-augmented generation aligned with curricular materials, as well as a secure code execution sandbox to analyze student submissions and return structured feedback. Responses were segmented into manageable sections to reduce cognitive load, and safeguards were implemented to ensure age-appropriate and curriculum-aligned outputs. 49 secondary school computing students  aged 15 to 16 participated in the study. Students completed a 30-minute Python programming task involving ISBN validation both before and after a one-hour intervention using MyBotBuddy (Khor et al., 2024). During the intervention, students were encouraged to use the chatbot to refine their code, troubleshoot errors, or explore alternative solutions. Quantitative data were collected through pre-and post-test scores graded with a standardized rubric. Three cases were excluded due to technical issues, resulting in 46 valid paired datasets. A paired-samples t-test was conducted to assess differences in performance. Qualitative data was collected through an open-ended survey administered after the intervention. Thematic analysis following Naeem et al. (2023) was employed, with codes deductively organized under TAM constructs of perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use.

The paired-samples t-test showed no statistically significant improvement in students’ programming performance from pre-test (M = 6.26, SD = 3.42) to post-test (M = 6.87, SD = 3.10), t(45) = −1.176, p =.123, 95% CI [−1.65,.43], suggesting that short-term interaction with MyBotBuddy  did not yield measurable gains in performance. This indicates the need for further refinement to strengthen its objective instructional impact. In contrast, qualitative findings reflected generally positive perceptions. Many students highlighted the chatbot’s support in understanding programming concepts, and several spontaneously compared it with ChatGPT. While some perceived both systems as comparable, others preferred MyBotBuddy for its clearer explanations. Thematic shaped perceived usefulness, while the effort required to use the chatbot influenced perceived ease of use. These themes informed directions for further system improvement. Perceived ease of use Students generally agreed that MyBotBuddy  was easy to use, emphasizing its convenience, clarity, and well-structured responses. MyBotBuddy’s responses were described as “comprehensive and simple” (S28) and “concise yet easy to understand” (S9). They noted that the step-by-step breakdowns helped them grasp algorithms without having to read extensive code. The use of uncomplicated language, highlighted keywords, and clearly segmented sections reduced cognitive effort and made key ideas easy to identify. Howe ver, preferences varied. While some students valued concise responses, others appreciated more detailed explanations. This divergence suggests that adaptability is central to perceived ease of use. Students may expect a GenAI learning tool to provide adjust able levels of explanation to suit individual needs. Although overall perceptions were positive, t he lack of consensus on specific improvements indicates that perceived ease of use may depend less on a single optimal design and more on the system’s capacity to accommodate diverse user preferences. Perceived usefulness Students’ perceptions of MyBotBuddy’s usefulness centered on three main aspects: reliability, academic benefits, and system capability. Reliability was particularly salient. Although most students found the chatbot generally accurate, for instance, “much better and well-structured compared to other AI models” (S30), occasional logic errors in generated code raised concerns. While some noted that the system could correct itself after further interaction, accuracy remained a key expectation. Students viewed reliable, high-quality responses as es sential for supporting learning, and inaccuracies were seen as potentially confusing or detrimental. These findings suggest that strengthening output accuracy is critical to enhancing perceived usefulness, particularly for an educationoriented chatbot intended to outperform general-purpose tools in domain reliability. Students also reported substantial academic benefits. MyBotBuddy  was perceived as helpful in refining code, identifying and explaining errors, guiding program design, and suggesting alternative solutions. Students appreciated how MyBotBuddy would provide “clear step by step explanations” (S42), “gives the explanation of the code and constraints and the approach and what the variables do” (S30), and even provide “useful tips and 345 revisions to help me realize what is wrong and how to improve” (S49). This suggests that the scaffolding approach of the chatbot were valued  for learning, particularly by beginners, and supported conceptual understanding and encouraged creative problem-solving. These features contributed directly to their  perceived usefulness as a programming learning aid.

However, perceived usefulness was constrained by limitations in scope and system performance. Because MyBotBuddy was restricted to programming-related queries, some students viewed it as overly specialized and less versatile than broader AI systems. They expressed a preference for tools applicable across subjects, suggesting that cross-disciplinary utility influences perceived relevance. Students also expected a system that could handle complex inputs and adapt to varied prompts. Enhancing technical robustness and expanding functional scope may therefore strengthen perceptions of usefulness and support deeper learning engagement. This study aimed to identify the determinants of students’ perceptions of MyBotBuddy  using the technology acceptance lens  to provide insight s into MyBotBuddy’s current strengths and limitations  and suggest improvements. One of MyBotBuddy’s greatest strengths as mentioned by the students is its clear, detailed, and step-by-step guidance when explaining programming concepts and code that facilitates  students’ understanding of programming concepts. Th is feature forms the basis of its perceived utility to students – the bot’s ability to effectively scaffold students’ programming knowledge. This aligns with previous literature that highlights how GenAI chatbots’ ability to explain learning materials clearly and thoroughly influence students’ opinions on these tools (Shoufan, 2023) as it simplifies their studying process by providing relevant, easily accessible, and understandable information (Kayali et al., 2023).

Output quality, or students’ belief that MyBotBuddy performs its role as a tutor well, is theorized in TAM as a primary driver of perceived usefulness (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). When MyBotBuddy delivers clear, specific, and accurate explanations, students infer that it effectively fulfils the role of a programming tutor and, in turn, find it more useful. As earlier shown, our qualitative comments foreground explanation quality  and align with this mechanism. This suggests that refining the chatbot’s scaffolding and accuracy can raise perceived output quality and thereby strengthen perceived usefulness (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). Another strength of MyBotBuddy  is its concise, uncomplicated and well-structured answers. In line with Sweller’s (1988) cognitive load theory, these features could remove obstacles, such as complex jargon or overwhelmingly long text, that may interfere with students’ learning by competing for limited cognitive resources in understanding them. This reduces the cognitive load students face when interacting with MyBotBuddy and frees up cognitive resources for internalizing the content learned and problem-solving. Qualities of students’ experience while using a learning tool have been proposed to influence their perceptions on how easy it is to use in the TAM3 model (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). The model theorizes that user-friendly system characteristics encourage students’ feelings of control while using it, allowing them to feel more competent and perceiv e it as being easier to use. Thus, it suggests that the simplicity of MyBotBuddy ’s answers support s students’ confidence to use MyBotBuddy.

The above strengths both refer to specific characteristics  of MyBotBuddy’s generated responses, suggesting that the extent of its perceived usefulness and ease of use heavily relies on the quality of its responses. The implication is that the chatbot’s perceived usefulness and ease of use can thus be maximized by ensuring that it generates high quality responses which are clear, thorough, detailed, simple, and neatly structured. To further improve MyBotBuddy‘s utility and ease of use, it is imperative to ad dress its key weaknesses, namely its slow response speeds, issues with reliability, and narrow field of expertise. An interesting finding was that students preferred more general chatbots when studying and suggested that it may be more useful in aiding their learning than a speci alized chatbot. Instead of using a general chatbot, MyBotBuddy intentionally focused on teaching programming to lower risks of AI hallucinations or propagating bias as it has the added advantage of being able to control the data that it was trained on to ensure its reliability (Yeung et al., 2023). Educational chatbots designed to teach a specific field of study, like the Anatbuddy chatbot by Arun et al. (2024), were more factually accurate than their general counterparts like ChatGPT. Students’ view of the subject-specific nature of MyBotBuddy as a limitation was thus surprising. This preference may reflect students’ preference for convenience: instead of switching between different learning resources according to each subject, students may prefer seeking help from a central source. The literature supports this, suggesting that students were driven to use ChatGPT while studying due to how it saves time and effort in studying (Jishnu et al., 2023). As theorized in the TAM3, learning tools which are seen as being less effortful to use may be found more useful, thus expanding the scope of MyBotBuddy’s knowledge may improve its perceived usefulness to students (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). Alternatively, this preference may be attributed to students’ perceptions of MyBotBuddy’s relevance. Students may judge a learning 346 tool’s relevance according to its overall applicability to their studies, rather than to a specific subject. A learning tool which has academic benefits extending beyond a single subject but to one’s overall studies may be considered more relevant to their learning and hence seen as more useful (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). Based on this preference for an integrated platform, it would be beneficial to expand the scope of the tool’s knowledge or include additional resources related to students’ studies to maxim ize its perceived usefulness. The extent of MyBotBuddy ’s perceived usefulness may thus be tied to its ability to effectively meet students’ specific needs and learning goals. This highlights the ongoing challenge for educational GenAI tools to stay relevant and effective by continuously updating and integrating the latest advancements in the field (Chukwuere & Handoko, 2024). MyBotBuddy’s weakness in consistently generating accurate answers is closely tied to students’ perceived usefulness of it, as often a lack of consistent accuracy in AI-generated answers undermines their trust in the tool’s value to effectively assist them (Weisz et al., 2022). I naccurate information could breed misconceptions, which would hinder students’ success in programming and cause students to grow wary of MyBotBuddy’s usefulness as a learning tool (Shoufan, 2023). Students’ concern over the accuracy of MyBotBuddy’s responses may reflect the influence of perceived output quality on the perceived usefulness of a tool, as theorized in the TAM3 (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). Students who received inaccurate generated answers may negatively view how well MyBotBuddy  performs its role as a tutor, leading them to find it less relevant to their learning and thus less useful to them as a learning tool. The perceived usefulness of an educational chatbot can thus be enhanced by targeting output quality, ensuring its responses are both reliable and accurate. The non-significant quantitative findings of the test scores in this intervention as mentioned could be due to the short nature of the task; also as with many educational interventions, students need time to get used to learning in a new approach  and short interventions could indicate tool-fit more than learner growth (Arun et al., 2024). Furthermore,  human-AI inquiry seeks knowledge building through argumentative dialogue, which is inherently iterative and time-consuming relative to didactic methods (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023).  S till the qualitative findings suggest that several students did learn through their scaffolding provided in their interaction with MyBotBuddy.

The present study examined students’ perceptions of MyBotBuddy, a GenAI programming chatbot, through a technology acceptance lens to inform system improvement. Qualitative findings indicate that perceived usefulness was shaped primarily by response quality, demonstrated expertise, and accuracy. Perceived ease of use depended largely on how concise, simple, and well-structured the responses were. Students’ suggestions for improvement focused mainly on enhancing output quality and interactivity, highlighting these as central levers for strengthening acceptance. Improving feedback clarity, reliability, and responsiveness may enable students to engage in deeper, more meaningful dialogue with the chatbot, moving beyond passive answer retrieval toward more collaborative human–AI problem-solving. However, the study relied on an open-ended survey and a relatively short interaction period, which may have limited students’ depth of engagement and shaped their perceptions. Longer-term implementation could provide richer insights into how perceptions evolve over time. Another limitation of the study is the lack of an alternative condition to compare the effects of the intervention. Nonetheless, the findings translate theoretical constructs of technology acceptance into practical design recommendations to support more effective and engaging educational GenAI tools.
