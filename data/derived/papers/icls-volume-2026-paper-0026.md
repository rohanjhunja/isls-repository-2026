# From Frustration to Creativity: Exploring how Neurodiversity Affirming Learning Contexts Transform Uncertainty into Strengths

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how neurodivergent learners experience and respond to scientific uncertainty in informal learning contexts. Drawing on data from a one-week HOMAGO-inspired circuit-bending course with a Science, Technology, Engineering, Arts, and Mathematics (STEAM) curriculum grounded in the concept of glitch’n, we analyze the case of a neurodivergent student to explore how different forms and sources of uncertainty emerge and are navigated. Findings show that while uncertainty can trigger frustration and disengagement, when reframed through inclusive design features such as low-stakes entry points, iterative tinkering, and instructor validation, it becomes a catalyst for persistence, creativity, and productive struggle. Also, neurodivergent students’ cognitive strengths can be leveraged when learning environments legitimize exploration, ambiguity, and non-linear outcomes. The study contributes to research on equity in STEAM by demonstrating how uncertainty, often viewed as a barrier, can be positioned as a productive resource for broadening participation and supporting diverse ways of knowing.

Scientific uncertainty reflects both the limits of current data and methods in explaining a phenomenon and the inherent nature of science itself (Chen et al., 2024; Manz, 2018; Starrett et al., 2024). In the classroom, this uncertainty creates a critical tension for learners; It can serve either as a productive invitation to explore and refine explanations (Warshauer, 2015; Watkins & Manz, 2022) or as a destabilizing force that leads to frustration and disengagement (Park & Ramirez, 2022). These varied responses reflect students’ dispositions toward scientific uncertainty navigation (DSUN) – the characteristic cognitive, emotional, and behavioral responses to uncertain moments (Jordan, 2015). DSUN shapes engagement and outcomes by either fostering persistence or amplifying frustration. A positive disposition toward uncertainty navigation is therefore essential for scientific sensemaking, or the process of resolving uncertainty to construct understanding (Berland & Reiser, 2011; Odden & Russ, 2019). This navigational tension is salient for neurodivergent learners, students whose neurological variations (e.g., autism, dyslexia, synesthesia, giftedness) influence cognition and engagement (Clouder et al., 2020; Stenning & Bertilsdotter Rosqvist, 2021). Neurodiversity perspectives position such variations as valued forms of humandiversity rather thandeficits (Armstrong, 2015; Chapman & Carel, 2022; Cook, 2024). Neurodivergent learners often bring distinctive strengths such as creativity, pattern recognition, and deep focus (Dwyer, 2022; Pellicano & den Houting, 2022), qualities that flourish in environments where uncertainty is embraced. Yet, traditional science classrooms often minimize uncertainty, emphasizing right answers over reasoning (Starrett et al., 2024), reinforcing the view of science as settled and certain (Covitt & Anderson, 2022). This limits sensemaking and perpetuates epistemic injustice, undervaluing knowledge and experiences (Fricker, 2007). When classrooms minimize uncertainty or treat it as an error, opportunities for sensemaking are curtailed, and neurodivergent students risk further marginalization (Armstrong, 2015; Chapman & Carel, 2022). Instead of sparking curiosity, uncertainty may amplify frustration and disengagement. This underscores the need to examine how dispositions toward uncertainty navigation interact with neurodiverse ways of knowing to shape learning. Recent work in science education provides robust frameworks for teaching with uncertainty, distinguishing its types, sources, and the conditions that make it productive, such as relevance, timing, and complexity (Chen et al., 2024; Manz, 2018; Watkins & Manz, 2022). At the same time, neurodiversity research critiques deficit framings and highlights structural and epistemic barriers that constrain neurodivergent students ’ 177 contributions in schools (Chapman & Carel, 2022; Cook, 2024). Despite these advances, guidance remains limited on how learning environments can intentionally design uncertainty to amplify neurodivergent learners ’strengths while minimizing relational or epistemic harm. This study addresses that gap by examining how uncertainty can function as a pedagogical resource for neurodivergent youth in STEAM learning. Using the Hanging Out, Messing Around, and Geeking Out (HOMAGO) framework (Ito et al., 2010), we explore how learning environments can transform moments of frustration into opportunities for sensemaking. In doing so, we position uncertainty navigation as both central to scientific inquiry and a lever for broadening participation in STEAM. Neurodiversity as an asset in STEAM education Recent neurodiversity research positions neurological differences such as autism, ADHD, dyslexia, dyscalculia, synesthesia, and giftedness, among others (Clouder et al., 2020; Stenning & Bertilsdotter Rosqvist, 2021) as natural and valuable variations in human cognition. Research has centered deeper understanding of neurodiversity around the neurodiversity paradigm, which offers a critical framing of neurological differences around “those who fall outside neurocognitive norms as ‘neuro-minorities ’marginalised by a ‘neuronormative ’organisation of society in favor of the ‘neurotypical’, rather than as a matter of individual medical pathology” (Bertilsdotter et al., 2020, pp. 58). Despite the neurodiversity paradigm, scientific and educational institutions often operate within a medical model that frames neurological differences as pathologies that require correction, exception, or accommodation (Armstrong, 2015; Xia et al., 2024) rather than as an integral baseline for social and cognitive understanding (Dwyer, 2022; Reutlinger et al., 2025). This entrenched deficit-based approach contributes to systemic exclusion and epistemic injustices (Fricker, 2007) and represents a profound missed opportunity for scientific advancement, given the wealth of cognitive strengths neurodivergent individuals possess. Research highlights that neurodivergent strengths align closely with success in STEAM fields. Autistic individuals often excel in systemizing, mathematics, and pattern recognition (Mottron, 2011); dyslexic individuals demonstrate strong visual–spatial reasoning vital in astrophysics and engineering (Goldberg, 2023); and those with ADHD frequently display heightened creativity and novelty-seeking (Xia et al., 2024). Recognizing these abilities as assets is both an ethical imperative and a strategic necessity. Centering such strengths enables neurodivergent learners to pursue STEAM careers that match their capabilities, reducing underemployment and skill underutilization (Shattuck et al., 2012). By valuing diverse cognitive profiles, STEAM education can foster innovation, deepen problem-solving, and create inclusive environments that fully harness the talents of learners. Uncertainty in science learning Uncertainty is an inherent feature of science learning, and rather than treating uncertainty as a barrier, recent scholarship frames it as a pedagogical resource for scientific sensemaking (Chen et al., 2024; Starrett et al., 2024). Uncertainty emerges from distinct sources (Starrett et al., 2024) – insufficiency (when information is lacking to make learning progress), ambiguity (when multiple interpretations are possible, but it is unclear which is correct), incoherence (when pieces of information do not fit together into a consistent explanation), or conflict (when competing claims or perspectives contradict one another). For uncertainty to be productive, it must be desirable, or rather, it must be timely, relevant, and appropriately complex. It should be challenging enough to provoke sensemaking, yet not so overwhelming that it leads to frustration or disengagement. Uncertainty can be conceptual, epistemic, procedural, or relational. Conceptual uncertainty occurs when students struggle to explain phenomena or reconcile ideas from everyday experience with scientific models; epistemic uncertainty arises when learners question how knowledge claims can be justified or validated; procedural uncertainty relates to a lack of clarity on what steps to follow (Chen et al., 2024), and relational uncertainty addresses issues of belonging and identity within the learning environment (Hartner-Tiefenthaler et al., 2018; Jordan, 2015). While research has established the value of navigating uncertainty for scientific sensemaking, especially in formal learning contexts, there remains a gap in understanding how neurodivergent learners engage with and benefit from this process as part of STEAM learning and in informal spaces. HOMAGO as a design ecology for navigating scientific uncertainty The HOMAGO framework (Ito et al., 2010) conceptualizes youth participation in informal learning across three overlapping modes – Hanging Out, Messing Around, and Geeking Out. In this study, these modes are viewed as a design ecology that structures how different forms of uncertainty emerge and become resources for learning. Hanging Out emphasizes social interaction and identity formation. It primarily surfaces relational uncertainty-related questions such as “Do I fit here?” or “Is my perspective valued?” which is particularly salient for neurodivergent learners often experiencing epistemic injustice (Chapman & Carel, 2022). By legitimizing informal connections, hanging out mode builds relational foundations crucial for sustaining persistence through 178 later struggles in STEAM learning.  Messing Around legitimizes exploratory tinkering and iterative trial-and-error (Ito et al. 2010; Allen et al., 2020), where conceptual and procedural uncertainties, often stemming from insufficiency or ambiguity, are most visible. In HOMAGO, these moments are reframed as productive exploration, aligning with strengths-based perspectives on neurodiversity that highlight persistence, attention to detail, and pattern recognition as potential assets in STEAM learning (Dwyer, 2022). Finally, Geeking Out refers to deeper, sustained engagement in interest-driven expertise. At this stage, epistemic uncertainty becomes central as learners work to interpret phenomena and evaluate explanations through evidence and reasoning (Odden & Russ, 2019). Purpose of the study Building on this framework, the study examines how neurodivergent learners navigate scientific uncertainty within HOMAGO-designed contexts. While prior work shows uncertainty can support learning and agency (Chen et al., 2024) and that HOMAGO broadens participation in informal STEAM (Ito et al., 2010; Allen et al., 2020), less is known about how neurodivergent learners engage uncertainty in these spaces. Informal STEAM learning, such as those guided by HOMAGO, has been shown to create more accessible environments and opportunities for students to start learning processes at whatever point best meets their own needs (Jensen et al., 2023). Focusing on Nick, a neurodivergent student building a circuit during a two-week summer camp, the study explores how informal STEAM environments can transform uncertainty from a barrier into a resource for sensemaking. It aims to inform the design of more inclusive STEAM learning environments. The following questions guide the study:

This study uses an interpretive qualitative case study design (Miles et al., 2014) to examine how uncertainty functions as a resource for sensemaking among neurodivergent students. A case study approach enabled close took place in a hands-on classroom at a U.S. public university during a two-week STEAM summer camp for neurodivergent youth (ages 14–20). The class included 20 students working in small groups of four, with five instructors providing support; red cups at each table allowed students to signal for help. Our analysis centers on Nick, a 16-year-old whose visible and verbalized engagement with uncertainty made him a compelling case. His interactions offer insight into how some neurodivergent learners might navigate scientific challenges, aligning with prior single-case approaches in neurodiversity research (Van Goidsenhoven & De Schauwer, 2020). Data included approximately12.5 hours of classroom video on musical circuit bending, researcher field notes, and instructional materials (lesson plans and student artifacts). Analysis proceeded in two stages. First, inperson observations and team discussions identified Nick as a focal case. We then reviewed and transcribed videos, mapping episodes where he encountered uncertainty and coding by type (conceptual, epistemic, procedural, relational) and source (insufficiency, ambiguity, incoherence, conflict), as well as how these were taken up within HOMAGO. Second, we conducted interaction analysis (Miles et al., 2014) using: (1) open coding of uncertainty, emotional responses, and social interactions; (2) focused coding to trace how uncertainty was introduced and negotiated; and (3) memoing and constant comparison to synthesize patterns. Study curriculum: Circuit bending as STEAM Circuit bending, the creative short-circuiting of low-voltage, battery-powered devices, centers experimentation and the emergent, often chaotic behaviors that arise when components are used beyond specification (Collins, 2009). Rather than prioritizing theory before practice, circuit-bending is inherently exploratory; students manipulate components, listen for changes, and reason backward from unexpected outcomes. This shifts attention from fixed schematics to open-ended discovery, placing authentic engineering cognition at the forefront. To support this, we selected robust, low-power CMOS components (40106 Schmitt inverter, 4040 binary divider, 4021 shift register, 4051 multiplexer) that enable wide experimentation without failure, maintaining the circuit as a stable medium for exploration. The Arts dimension is epistemically central, not decorative, through “glitch’n,” where sonic deviations and irregular rhythms are treated as valid outcomes rather than errors. Using filtering techniques on simple digital pulses, students generated patterned sounds reminiscent of early 2000s “clicks and cuts” music. This aesthetic of failure (Cascone, 2000) repositions circuits as sites of subversion and expression, cultural technics that mediate subjectivity and collective engagement (Stiegler, 1998). By embracing noise and 179 unpredictability, glitch link scientific sensemaking with neurodiverse sensemaking, fostering embodied, crossneurotype collaboration that resists normative notions of “correct” communication (Thorn et al., 2025). A brief vignette: Nick’s journey in a musical circuit bending STEAM course Nick, a 16-year-old autistic (1) learner, was engaged in a weeklong informal STEAM program focused on building generative music from circuits. This program was part of a two-week STEAM-based training for neurodivergent youths. On day two of Nick’s week in the course, the instructor (one of five) presented the task: “Create your own mini radio station”. To complete the task, students had to individually watch a series of short videos that provided the procedure for achieving the task. While the instructor was still giving instructions, Nick was already working on his circuit, deeply focused on the activity. Later, when he encountered his first problem, he quietly called one of the instructors over, stating, “I think my battery is dead,” and the battery was replaced. Nick continued to work, but his challenges mounted. The circuit would not oscillate like the model in the video he was using, and his initial frustration began to escalate. He expressed his uncertainty to an instructor, “Firstly, I can’t make it to oscillate; almost oscillating pretty freaking bad.” Later, as his frustration grew, he began to speak to himself while still focused on his work, “What am I doing wrong? Christ. Fed-up. Doing this every freaking moment. Oh gracious, I’m losing my mind.” He placed his hands on his head, embodying his exasperation. Despite this visible frustration, Nick persisted. While other students took a 10-minute break, he remained in his seat, continuing to work on the circuit with unwavering focus. When a peer tried to engage him in conversation by asking, “Are you autistic like me?” Nick briefly acknowledged the question with a simple distracted “yes” before returning to his circuit. Finally, after a long period of sustained effort and three instructor personalized supports at different times, a breakthrough occurred. A sound emanated from his circuit that was different from the model, but uniquely “groovy” to his ear. His frustration vanished, replaced by a joyful exclamation, “What the hell? Look how groovy it sounds!” Later, in a group discussion, he shares a video of the sound with an instructor, laughing and exclaiming, “How this is funny!” This vignette encapsulates the core tensions of our study. It shows how an encounter with scientific uncertainty, which initially led to frustration, became a catalyst for deep engagement, productive sensemaking, and creative discovery for a neurodivergent learner. This aligns with research emphasizing tool use, setup, and experimentation as central to scientific practice (Chen et al., 2024; Manz, 2018). His trial-and-error tinkering demonstrates how students manage procedural challenges through iterative refinement and resilience (Jordan, 2015). Nick also encountered conceptual uncertainty, as shown when he exclaimed, “What am I doing 182 wrong? It’s exactly the same…, ” indicating difficulty explaining why the circuit’s behavior diverged from the expected model. Such uncertainty can be generative, prompting deeper engagement with underlying principles rather than simply reproducing steps (Chen et al., 2024). At times his uncertainty became epistemic, as reflected in statements such as “I can’t make it oscillate. Almost oscillating pretty freaking bad,” where doubt concerned the adequacy of his methods and results. These moments mirror scientific practice, where learners justify claims, evaluate evidence, and take epistemic ownership (Berland & Reiser, 2011; Manz, 2025; Odden & Russ, 2019). Relational uncertainty also surfaced during peer interactions at multiple levels. First, by publicly admitting moments of not knowing, Nick risked threatening his competent image (Jordan & McDaniel, 2014), particularly within a classroom of students spanning a wide age range (14–20). Such diversity can heighten concerns about appearing less knowledgeable in front of peers. Beyond competence concerns, relational uncertainty also emerged through identity-related interaction. For example, when a student asked, “Are you autistic like me?”, Nick responded briefly and returned to his task. While the student’s question may be interpreted as an attempt to establish identity alignment and community boundary (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020), Nick’s quick withdrawal suggests an adaptive strategy for maintaining focus. The example further illustrates how identity and belonging can momentarily surface in classrooms. In both instances, Nick appeared to prioritize task engagement by drawing on autistic sociality which is characterized by self-directed engagement and hyperfocus (Schneid & Raz, 2020). This neurodivergent strength likely allowed him to manage relational and epistemic demands while maintaining productive engagement with the task.

The study also shows how insufficiency, ambiguity, conflict and incoherence (Starrett et al., 2024) surfaced in Nick’s participation, which were productively transformed through HOMAGO design. Procedural insufficiency highlighted the need for accessible resources, while incoherence in “wiring exactly the same” risked unproductive struggle if unresolved. Instructor reframing repositioned this as exploration, aligning with neurodiversity perspectives that treat variation as generative (Armstrong, 2015; Chapman & Carel, 2022; Cook, 2024). Nick’s shift from frustration to diagnosis underscores the importance of timing and manageable complexity in making uncertainty productive (Chen et al., 2024). Once supported, his persistence became assets for sustained inquiry. Similarly, the instructor reframing the mismatch as the “beauty of circuit bending” redirected uncertainty toward innovation, moving him into geeking out and highlighting the role of timely instructional intervention. As Nick navigated these uncertainties, his participation unfolded along a HOMAGO trajectory. He began by hanging out, where informal interaction supported comfort with the social and material environment (Ito et al. 2010). Such low-pressure entry points are especially important for neurodivergent learners, enabling sensory and relational acclimation that supports later experimentation and self-directed STEAM learning (Jenson et al., 2023). This foundation became evident when his circuit failed. Rather than using the red cup help system, he voiced frustration openly, making uncertainty visible to peers. While such moments carry relational risk, the initial hanging out phase appeared to foster psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999), allowing him to stay engaged in messing around and approach problems through iterative tinkering rather than as personal failures (Kafai, 2006).

Our findings further reveal that hanging out can also function as a reflective recovery phase. During the instructor-facilitated reflection at the end of the work period, students were prompted to discuss questions such as “What are examples of things that don’t work but have a cool effect?” In doing so, instructors turned moments of frustration from the messing around phase into a socially supportive space, normalizing experimentation and uncertainty. These structures supported persistence by providing inclusive pathways for neurodivergent learners to engage deeply with the task (Ito et al., 2010; Pellicano & den Houting, 2022). Nick’s “glitchy sound,” reframed by the instructor and accepted by Nick himself, as valuable rather than broken, exemplifies how uncertainty can become a resource for innovation. Rather than abandoning the task, he continued iteratively testing and adjusting his circuit, eventually entering the geeking out phase where he deliberately tinkered with the circuit and began naming the sounds produced. This progression illustrates how sustained engagement through uncertainty can lead to deeper learning and exploration (Ito et al., 2010; Kafai & Peppler, 2011). Finally, the STEAM curriculum, rooted in the concept of glitch’n, reframed error as a generative resource rather than a deficit. This orientation emphasized both epistemic and aesthetic dimensions of learning, where discovery was as much felt as reasoned (Gendlin, 1997). Material contingencies such as the sensitivity of radiophonic circuits to transmission power, tuner precision, antenna angle, and distance, required students to navigate conditions similar to those faced by professional engineers. While instructional videos simplified these complexities, their brevity also lowered barriers to entry and supported sustained curiosity. In this way, sensemaking emerged not only as conceptual understanding but also as sensibility, positioning uncertainty as a creative resource. Nick’s experience thus illustrates how the curriculum fostered epistemic and aesthetic ownership, aligning with Deleuze and Guattari’s (1987) notion of “minor science” and Pellicano and den Houting’s

By integrating neurodiversity research with HOMAGO-informed design, this study advances a strengths-based approach to STEAM inclusion that treats neurodiversity as a pedagogical asset rather than a deficit. Flexible, low-stakes, and multimodal entry points, combined with timely instructor support, foster psychological safety and allow neurodivergent learners to channel cognitive strengths into sustained inquiry. These findings highlight the potential of informal STEAM environments to broaden participation by valuing diverse ways of knowing and innovating. From this study, four key principles emerge for designing inclusive STEAM learning environments:

1. Early diagnostic support: instructors should provide opportunities for students to self-diagnose problems

and ensure resources are available to move from insufficiency toward solution to sustain engagement.

2. Reframing incoherence: educators should treat mismatches and “failures” as opportunities for deep

systems-oriented reasoning, validating frustration as part of learning, and encouraging exploration.

3. Making uncertainty desirable: educators should calibrate task complexity and timing, so uncertainty is

bounded enough to sustain persistence yet still open-ended to foster tinkering, sensemaking, and inquiry.

4. Legitimizing non-linear outcomes: through creative reframing, educators can position deviations,

anomalies, and “glitches” as valuable contributions, fostering engagement and innovation. Together, these principles highlight that designing for uncertainty, rather than minimizing it, is key to leveraging the cognitive strengths of neurodivergent learners and enabling them to flourish in STEAM domains.

## Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis centers on a single focal participant, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Rather than offering broad claims about all neurodivergent learners, the case provides a detailed illustration of how uncertainty may be navigated within HOMAGO-structured STEAM environment. Second, the study was conducted within a specialized summer program designed for neurodivergent youths, where instructional support and classroom norms may differ from typical classroom settings. Third, interpretations of uncertainty and sensemaking are based on observed interactions and discourse rather thandirect access to participant internal reasoning through interviews. Future research could examine similar processes across multiple learners and contexts to better understand how instructional design shapes students’ engagement with uncertainty in STEAM learning.
