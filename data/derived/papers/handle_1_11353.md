# Connecting Identity and Classroom Content: The Role of Network Visualization in Eliciting History Students’ Funds of Identity

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Centering students’ Funds of Identity (FOI) in the classroom is important for moving towards asset-based frameworks for learning. However, it can be challenging to identify the FOI of an entire class and use those FOI to design instruction. Motivated by this challenge, we explore how network visualizations in which students create a network that displays connections between themselves and classroom content can simultaneously elicit and connect students’ FOI to instruction. We conducted this work with five middle school World History classes taught by a single teacher, who built a collective network visualization about Greek Mythology. Using an iterative process of analytic memoing, coding, and content analysis, we identified three ways in which the network visualization activity connected to students’ identities: 1) they could share about themselves (particularly personality descriptions and practical FOI); 2) they could connect to content from their history class; and 3) they could connect to their peers.

## Introduction (major issues addressed)

It is well established that in order to foster inclusive classroom environments, school curriculum and practices should both reflect and build on students’ backgrou nds, identities, and interests (McIntyre et al., 2001). Funds of Identity (FOI), a  theoretical approach coined by Esteban-Guitart and Moll (2014 a), offers a way of conceptualizing students’ identities, consisting of  bodies of beliefs, ideas, skills, and abilities gr ounded in experiences, as assets in a classroom spa ce. Yet, challenges remain regarding how to design classroom activities grounded in an FOI approach, particularly around how to elicit the funds of identities of a full cla ssroom. Established methods for surfacing students’ FOI are robust, but often localized and ethnographic (e.g. requiring home visits or individual interviews with students), introducing practical challenges for doing so in a classroom setting (González et al., 2005). Additionally, it can be difficult to know what to a ccept and what to discard as a fund of identity (Oughton, 2010). Therefore, we explore the problem space of how classroom teachers candesign and implement activities that both 1) draw on and reveal aspects of students’ FOI, and 2) help to bridge those FOI with the c urriculum. Specifically, our work is situated in a middle scho ol history classroom (described in methodology) learning about Greek Mythology.

We explore the promise of network visualizations for s upporting both our stated goals. A network visualization is a collection of nodes (circles that represent the entities or “things” in a network; see Figure 1) and edges (lines that represent “connections” between n odes) that is used to understand relationships between id eas. Important here is the ability of network visualizat ions to display connections through node-edge pairs, or two nodes that are linked together by an edge.  We focus here on a specific kind of network, that we call a “targeted identity network”, in which  students can share their FOI as they relate to a particula r content area  through connecting themselves (as a node in the network) to content that they are learning about in the classroom. Our use of the word connection is two-pronged here. Students could make connections between nodes using edges in the network (referred to in this paper as “creating an edge” or “network connections”), and then those connections may have helped them to ex perience broader feelings of  connection with peers and content. We aim to explor e how an activity using a targeted identity network, in which students created network connections to characters in Greek Mythology, both elicited students’ FOI and supported students to make connections between themselves, their peers, and content from their history class.

## Theoretical framework and literature review

Culturally responsive/sustaining pedagogies position the diversity that students bring to the classroom, including their heterogeneous practices, backgrounds, and identities, as an asset (Paris & Alim, 2017; Ladson-Billings, 1995). With a goal of designing classroom activities that center student identity, we draw on Funds of Identity as a culturally responsive pedagogy  that recognizes, affirms, and builds on the assets, skil ls, and lived experiences that students bring into the classroom (Moll & Gonzalez, 2004; Esteban-Guitart et al., 2019). Funds of Identity (FOI) refer to the “historically accumulate d, culturally developed, and socially distributed resources that are essential for a pers on’s self-definition, self-expression, and self-understanding” (Esteban-Guitart & Moll, 2014 a, p. 31). These resources can be geographical (e.g.  land, regions), practical (e.g. interests, hobbies), cultural (e.g. symbols, social  categories), social (e.g. friends, family), or ins titutional (e.g. structures). Stemming from a Funds of Knowledge approach (FOK; González et al., 2006), which includes all of a learner’s home cultural resources,  Funds of Identity include the subset of a learner’s funds of knowledge that matter to them personally. In our work, we assume that if a student shared something about themselves, then that thing “mattered to them”, at least to the extent th at it was relevant in the given interactional momen t. We are actively grappling with the theoretical and methodo logical challenge of knowing when something that a student shared in a network is an interest (be it fleeting or sustained), when it is part of their identity, or when it functions as both (Cruz-Gonzalez et al., 2025). Rather than attempting to set hard boundaries on identity versus interest, we embrace designing for both as valuable for student learning and classroom experience (Esteban-Guitart, 2019; Walkington & Hayata, 2017).

Designing instruction that aligns with students’ Fu nds of Identity requires that classroom teachers be able to both elicit and understand their students’ FOI and connect them to instructional design. In a literature review that synthesized Funds of Identity literature, Hogg & Volman (2020) identified four partially overlapping ways that FOI approaches have been applied pedagogi cally, including 1) learning about students’ FOI and using that information to design classroom learning, 2) co-constructing artifacts; 3) integrating popular culture FOI; and

4) student exploration of their identities through identity artifacts. In our work, we integrate the first two threads,

exploring how students can simultaneously construct an identity artifact while also engaging in classroom content learning. We expand the idea of an “identity artifact” from something that an individual creates about themselves (e.g. self-portraits, significance circles, drawings, or objects; Subero et al., 2017; Saubich & Esteban-Guitart, 2011; Esteban-Guitart & Moll, 2014b) to something that the class can create together. Specifically, we use network visualizations as a way for students to display their collective iden tities and visualize trends in identity across the whole class (Neri, in preparation; Stiso et al., 2023). Following Harris’s (2024) call to design opportunities for students themselves, rather than teachers, to frame classroom activities with the personal, social, and cultural knowledge and experiences that are mea ningful to them, we designed an activity in which students could actively connect themselves to the content in their history class.

## Study context

This work took place as part of a larger study at a public school in the rural, southeastern United States across five eighth grade World History classes (taught by the same teacher) and one sevent h grade English Language Arts class (taught by a different teacher). The school district has 17 schools that serve about 7, 000 students. In the district, between 75% and 80% of students qualify for free and reduced-price lunch. They have a well-established 1:1 digital device program, ensuring all students h ave access to devices during the school day and at home. The district is known locally and nationally for its innovative educational approach with district foci on project-based learning, STEAM (Science, Technology, Engineering, Art, and Math), and computational thinking. This work builds on these existing district priorities.

Participants in this study were 89 students from across one honors section and four traditional sections of eighth grade World History. An additional 53 students  participated in the activity but did not consent to participate in the research; their data was removed from the networks. As reported by their parents/guardians, 34 (38.2%) students were boys/male, 52 (58.4%) were girls/female, and 3 (3.3%) did not report gender; 22 (24.7%) students were  Black, African, or African American, 50 (56.2%) students were White, 1 (1.1%) student was Hispanic or Latino/a, 11 (12.4%) students were two or more races; and 5 (5.6%) did not report race/ethnicity. The classes participated in three network visualization activit ies centered around Ancient Greece  that were co-designed by the research team and the classroom tea cher, including a Greek Mythology network  (the focus of this paper), a Voting Network in which students learned about democracy in Athens, and a Greek Trade Network. In this paper, we analyze the Greek Mythology network activity, in which students across class periods created a shared network visualization that displayed connections vi a node-edge pairs between themselves and the Greek Mythology stories and characters that they were studying. We selected this activity to focus on because it was most explicitly designed to focus on student identity. The activity was supported by the Net.Create software, an online network visualization tool that allows fo r real-time collaborative creating and editing of networks (Figure 2; Craig & Danish, 2018). Students were assigned to work in pairs and prior to this activity, each pair was assigned a different Greek Mythology story to read.  The teacher made these assignments based on which gods/stories he thought the students might relate t o after reading six-word stories that the students wrote about themselves. Students were provided with a starting network that was seeded with nodes from the Greek Mythology stories that they had read in class. These included gods (e.g., Zeus, Hera), creatures (e.g., Nymphs, Satyrs), humans (e.g., Pan, Midas), places (e.g., Athens), and things (e.g., Golden Fleece). Students added themselves to the network (as “humans”) and were tasked with creating connect ions between and among nodes already in the network, and themselves. They could also add new nodes. Students could use “edge types” to indicate the kind of connection they were making (e.g. interacts with; v isits; connected) and could use a “notes” field to explain the connection. Students across all five class periods worked on the same network visualization. Figure 1 depicts the final network.

Researcher positionality As a research team, we approached this work with a shared commitment towards supporting and res pecting diverse learners and wanting to honor their lived experiences, but w ith differences in prior experience and our approaches in doing so, which required discussions of our goals and interpretations regularly. We are  a team of graduate student researchers, professors, and non-profit researchers that holds diverse identities, including a white woman, a white and Indonesian woman, a Latin American woman, a white man, and a  queer/bisexual Asian nonbinary/gender nonconforming person. We come from various geographical areas (the Northeastern United States, Alaska, a Latin American country, Native Ha waiian/Indigenous lands/Pacific Islands) and  cultural and religious backgrounds. Given our own varying lived experiences, we recognize the complexities of identities and intersectionalities that students hold. Yet, we also see ourselves primarily as outsiders to the community in which we conducted this research (one resea rcher had worked with the school district for three  years at the time of the research and had visited the school previously). As  such, we worked closely with the classroom teacher  to co-design the activities, and continually reflected on our positions, interpretations, and potential biases as we engaged in analysis and categorization of student identity. These reflections led  to the decision to discuss all our coding decisions as a research team as a way to check and negotiate our assumptions. Data sources Data sources included 1) pair interviews with the s tudents in the focal class periods that took place after the conclusion of all the activities; 2) classroom vide o recordings of the focal student groups working on  the Greek Mythology activity; and 3) the completed Greek Myth ology network (all class periods; Figure 1). During  data collection, we selected two focal history class per iods to video record (one honors and one traditional class). Across those classes, we captured video of  four focal groups of stu dents working on the Greek Mythology network. In the pair interviews, students interviewed each other using a  set of researcher-written questions that asked them to reflect on the activities, what they learned, and sharing their interests during the network activities. They lasted between 1.5 and 2.5 minutes per student.

Analysis We took the student interviews as a starting point for identifying how students saw the Mythology Netw ork activity as related to their identities. In our first pass of the interviews, we wrote analytic memos (Saldaña, 2011) in which we noted that students seemed to be talkin g about identity in three ways: through sharing abo ut themselves, through making connections to their peers, and through making connections to the content. To verify these three themes, we conducted a round of coding using the themes as codes at the level of an utterance (Grbich, 2013). Three researchers independently coded all of the interviews, obtaining a percent agreement of 85.4% on 20% of the data. We discussed and resolved all discrepancies, and one researcher reviewed the remaining coded data and made final decisions.

Next, we conducted a content analysis of the network that the students created, identifying how the nodeedge pairs that the students created mapped onto FOI categories, and how that sharing may have facilitated broader connections to their peers and the content. We used  this same coding scheme for the video data to identify how the content of the focal groups’ talk while creating the network included sharing their FOI and connecting to peers and the content. Throughout this process, we notice d that many of  the node-edge pair connections that students shared did not seem to fit into any of the establis hed FOI categories (e.g., “we are both very moody ”; “caring”; “funny”); therefore, we added a category called “personal description”, which included traits or self-views of oneself. We also noted when students created a node-edge connection between themselves and another node, but a reason was not given, as “unspecified sharing.” Over three rounds, three researchers coded all the network and video data independently and discussed discrepancies to reach 100% agreement. ## Findings

Student reflections on identity and sharing in the network activities In their interviews, students (n=30) talked about being able to share things about themselves (23 times; 20 students), being able to connect with a peer (30 times; 18 students), and being able to connect to the content they were learning about in history class (33 times; 14 students) during the network visualization activities. In terms of sharing about themselves, some reasons that students liked telling others about themselves included: “I could express myself” (Student 28), “people need to know people who they are and not by their looks and stuff” (Student 29), “people could see from a different perspective  on who I am as a person” (Student 42), “I felt lik e I actually mattered in the project” (Student 50), and “I got to share the good parts about me” (Student 56). However, not all students felt as though their sharing was meaningfu l. For example, Stud ent 38 explained, “I feel like it was just stuff that people already knew about me. So honestl y, no, I didn't really enjoy it because it just was n't anything interesting about me.” Student 48 cited being in a school environment as a reason why their sharing was limited: “I mostly didn't really share too much on anything else. Because it's what, school. I'm not giving you my life story.” While a majority of students enjoyed having the oppo rtunity to share things about themselves in history class, these students’ reflections raise questions about  how school settings shape the kinds of identities students are willing to share and suggests it was valuable for them to have agency in limiting this. In terms of connecting to their peers, students talked about how the network activities supported them to make connections with their peers in three key ways: they could connect directly to their partners; they could explore their own connections with others through the conte nt; and they could use  the network to understand trends in identity across students in all the history periods. As Student 43 explained to his partner: “And you know, at first I didn’t really think that before this pro ject, I didn’t think me and you really had much in common, right. But as we went through it, I learned a thing or two about you. And I’m sure you learned a thing or two about me, which sort of brought us together.” For the Greek M ythology activity, the classroom teacher assigned students into pairs based on what mythology character he thought they might relate to.  This enabled students to make connections with both their assigned god and each o ther. For example, Student 41 explained, “The reaso n we were picked for Dionysus was because he was very energetic, energetic and social person just like us. So it really sticks out of my mind because he was almost he was like us and we had similarities with each other.” Finally, viewing the networks at an aggregate level supported students to understand their classmates better: “it also helped to see, like, what the majority of our class connec ts, how they connect to it with th e different weights” (Student 50). Weights here refer to the thickness of the edges between two nodes, with a higher weight indicating a stronger or more important connection.

Lastly, students reflected on the ways in which the  network activities supported them to connect to th e historical content. They found that “It helped us learn about different  Greek Gods and how we're connected to 'em” (Student 26), “it helped you connect more with history and also h elp you connect more with the network” (Student 33), and “they helped me wonder how I conn ect with like all the like gods and goddesses that they had back then” (Student 47). They also cited specific connections that they were  able to make. For example, Student 58 explained “Something that interested me was how people were connected to Zeus. To Zeus, because he was like on top. He was like the biggest.” Biggest here refers to node size, with a bigger nod e indicating that i t has more connections. Taken together, students’ perspec tives suggest that through network visualizatio n activities, they could share about themselves, make connections to their peers, and explore connections to the content. FOI in the Greek Mythology network Based on these themes identified by the students (sharing about themselve s, connecting to peers, connecting to content), in this section, we take a deeper dive into what students shared during the Greek Mythology network activity (both in the network and their conversations). Specifically, we explore how what they shared mapped onto FOI categories, and how that sharing functioned to establish connections to their peers and the content. In the network, students were able to share informa tion about themselves by adding edges between themselves and Greek characters and writing “notes”  that explain ed their connection. In doing so, students primarily shared personal descriptions of themselve s (60%; see Figure 3); practical FOI (activities/interests) (21%) and social FOI (15%) were also present. Their personal descriptions ranged from comments ab out their appearance (e.g. “I’m beautiful”; “both have blue eyes”), to personality traits (e.g. “kindhearted and mysterious”; “very competitive”; or “selfish”). The kinds of practical FOI that students shared also varied, ranging from “kind to animals”, to “likes to break windows”, to “I lov e beef”. The social FOI that students shared were primarily facilitated by making a connection in the network t o their peers or their teacher and explaining in th e notes that they were “best friends”, or “teacher and student”.  Students also shared social FOI when they expresse d the importance of family or shared that they had siblings. However, institutional, geographical, and cultural FOI were rarely shared as connections in the network.  This raises questions about the kinds of FOI that a re prioritized by centering certain content, whether those priorities were due to the content or  the context (e.g., as some students didn’t want to share in school), and how we might elicit additional FOI. These network connections also served to help students to make explicit connections between their own FOI and the content in their history class. In the network, students (across all class periods, n = 89) primarily used personal descriptions and practical activities to connect to  the content. That is, students identified interest s (e.g. Student 33 connected their node to Aphrodite and wrote in the note, “we both enjoy music”) and personal traits (e.g. Student 78 connected their node to Prometheus and wrote in the  note, “peaceful”) that they shared with particular gods. Sharing social connections sometimes helped them to  connect to content (15/28 times)  and occasionally helped them connect to their peers (8/28 times). For example, students identified with gods for whom family was important or who had siblings (social co nnections that helped them relate to content) and  also connected themselves directly to friends (connecting to peers but not directly to content). Some of what students ta lked about made it into the final network, while other convers ations did not result in additions to the final network. For example, Student 50 tended to narrate what he was putting in the network ou t loud to his partner, saying things like “I’m gonna link myself to Midas. Because he wa nted money. And I want money.” and “ I linked myself to Nike.” By talking about his connections in the network, he was able to make broader connections with his partner. When he said that he linked himself to Nike, his pa rtner, Student 41, responded “I did too,” making explicit the shared interest between them. In other cases, students had in-depth conversations with each other about their identities and interests and how those related to t he content, but they did not put those connections into the network. For example, Student 60 and 38 talked about how much Student 60 loved sharks: she had two shark toys and her grandmother got her shark slippers for Christmas. Following that conversation, Student 60 announced, “I have a connection to Poseidon. You would never be able to guess why”, prompting Student 56 to ask “why”, Student 38 to laugh and say “dude”, and Student 60 to share again “I love  sharks.” However, Student 60 did not connect herself to Poseidon in the network. In cases like these, even though the network visualization did not directly facilitate the connection, the network activity enabled students to share about th emselves in ways that were relevant to the content.

Looking across the data, different students approached the activity and sharing their identities in different ways: some put lots of identity connections in the networ k and didn’t really talk; some  both talked and  put in identity connections; some only talked and only mad e a few connections in the network, while still oth ers made a lot of connections in the network between histori cal characters but not necessarily to themselves. ## Discussion and significance

Taken together, students’ reflections and their wor k in the Greek Mythology network suggest that the n etwork visualization activity supported them to 1) share p ractical FOI and personal descriptions about themselves; to 2) connect to the content  in their history class; and 3) to connect to their peers. This suggests t hat network visualizations hold promise as a method that can elicit students’ FOI in relation to content learning in a classroom setting, when home visits or individual interviews can be ch allenging. Specifically, students were able to select an “edge type”, add notes,  and view both individual data points and aggregate levels, all of which facilitated the creation and understanding of their connections. In this way, the network functioned as both an indi vidual and collective identity artifact (Subero et al., 2017)  and as a way for students to explicitly frame and make sense of classroom content through the lens of those identities (Harris, 2024). We see connecting to peers and content as important starting points for facilitating positive disciplinary identities (Bell et al., 2017) for students, as they can lead to feelings of belonging in a discipline or classroom community. The different patterns in participation suggest that yo uth had agency in what they wanted to share and how, which was mediated by the content and by t he larger school setting in which the activity was situated. Future work can consider  how, in what contexts, and to what ends w e may bring cultural, institutional, and geographical FOI into the classroom, as these were the FOI that were rarely shared by students. This work also raises questions around how personal descriptions (such as personality traits and ideas about appearance) fit into or complement existing FOI literature. We hope that the work can support conversations in the field around how to elicit and center student identity in a classroom setting.
