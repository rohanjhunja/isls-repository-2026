# Comparing Subjective and Objective Estimates of Effort and Performance on Adaptive v. Non-Adaptive Computerized Tests of Statistics Knowledge

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We examine associations between subjective (self-reported) and objective (derived from behaviors or performance) indicators of effort and ability and whether the format of a computerized assessment (adaptive v. non-adaptive) moderates these associations. Participants (N=219; Mage=19.83) were randomly assigned to one of two conditions in which they completed either an adaptive or a non-adaptive version of a 20-item computerized assessment. They then self-reported their effort and the number of items they believe they answered correctly on the assessment. Self-reported effort was not significantly correlated with the behavioral indicator. However, self-reported ability was positively correlated with participants’ test score, even after controlling for test condition. Test condition appeared to moderate the association between subjective and objective estimates of ability with participants with higher ability in the nonadaptive condition estimating a greater number of items answered correctly. Further work should examine whether effects of test condition differ for certain student subgroups. computerized assessment, adaptive test, test mode, self-evaluation, effort, subjective v. objective estimates

## Introduction

There are many advantages to computerized adaptive exams from a quantitative standpoint, but little is  known about how they affect test-takers' experiences. Computerized adaptive exams have grown in popularity over the years since as they allow for more efficient assessment by using fewer items or less time to achieve the same level of measurement precision (Weiss & Kingsbury, 1984), can be scored promptly, and are usually thought to involve more secure test administration (Klein & Hamilton, 1999). As a result of the shorter testing time and the approximate matching of item difficulty to the test-takers’ ability, they are thought to deliver a better test-taking experience than more traditional computerized linear fixed-form (“non-adaptive”) exams. However, matching of items based on students’ ability on adaptive exams may cause confusion for certain test takers, leadin g them to assume they have less aptitude than on test scores would in fact suggest. Past research on the effects of different test formats on test-takers (i.e., “test mode”) has largely focused on the influence of test mode effects on learners’ performance with respect to computer versus paper-and-pencil administration modes (Clarina & Wallace, 2002). To the best of our knowledge, few studies have examine d whether test mode effects differ on adaptive compared to non-adaptive computerized. Given that more and more tests are delivered on computers, particularly because of the COVID-19 pandemic's shift toward online and distant evaluation (Middleton, 2020), this seems a pressing question. Aside from test mode, certain factors unrelated to the construct or ability being tested may influence test-takers’ score (Haladyna & Downing, 2004). According to the demands-capacity model of test-taking effort (Wise & Smith, 2011), students who have high ability may exert low effort on test items if they perceive items as too difficult. Thus, understanding how aspects of s tudents’ subjective experience of test-taking interacts with mode effects is important, particularly in an adaptive test, where test-takers are matched to items based on performance.

Research Aims The present study seeks to examine the association between participants’ self-reported and actual effort and ability on two versions of a computerized test. We expected  to find that such associations may differ based on  the test mode condition (adaptive v. non-adaptive).

Subjective effort (Self-reported mental effort) A four-item self-report measure was administered af ter participants completed the 20-item test of stat istics knowledge. Responses to the items (e.g., "I tried hard to answer the questions correctly.") were provided using a 1-5 Likert scale (1 = Strong disagree, … 5 = Strongly agree). Subjective ability (Estimated number of items answered correctly) Following the 20-item test of statistics knowledge,  participants were asked, " Out of the 20 questions you completed just now, how many did you answer correctly?" Responses options ranged from "0-None of them" to "20-All of them".

Objective effort (Response time effort) We used response time effort (RTE; Wise & Kong, 200 5) to indicate responses that appeared motivated and effortful. Across all questions, fewer than 25% of all responses were submitted in under 30 seconds. T hus, any response that took under 30 seconds was marked as unmotivated. Participants with more than 50% of responses marked as unmotivated were flagged for removal.

## Results

As mentioned previously, we were interested in exam ining the associations between self-reported estima tes of effort and ability in relation to more objective es timates.  We first sought to establish the extent t o which these variables were correlated with one another (RQ1). Controlling for test mode (adaptive v. non-adapt ive), selfreported mental effort (based on an average scale s core) was not significantly correlated with the beh avioral indicator of effort used in this study (RTE; r =.13, p =.057). The behavioral indicator of effort (RTE) was not significantly correlated with any of the other measures. Instead, participants’ self-reported effort appeared to be positively and weakly correlated with both the participants’ estimated ability (after completing the 20-item test; r =.17, p =.014) and their test score (based on the theta estimate; r =.13, p =.047). Participants’ estimated ability was positively and moderately correlated with their test score (r =.54, p <.001). Next, we were interested in determining whether these variables significantly differed on the basis of test mode (RQ2) using independent samples t-tests. We found that participants in the non-adaptive test condition (M = 4.19, SD =.93) tended to report exerting more mental effort than those assigned to the adaptive test condition (M = 3.81, SD =.75; t = 3.28, p =.001, d =.437). By contrast, participants in the adaptive test condition (M =.80, SD =.14) tended to have higher estimates of behavioral effort as indicated by RTE than those in the non-adaptive condition (M =.75, SD =.13; t = 2.93, p =.004, d =.395). When comparing estimates of ability, we found that participants in adaptive test condition (M = 11.34, SD = 3.87) tended to report receiving a lower number of items correct out of 20 compared to participants in the non-adaptive condition (M = 9.75, SD = 3.59; t = 3.12, p =.002, d =.425). Comparing test scores (theta) appeared to reaffirm participants’ self-evaluations with those assigned to the adaptive test condition (M = -.18, SD =.79) receiving lower scores than those in the no n-adaptive condition (M =.07, SD =.61; t = 2.70, p =.008, d =.359).

Given the differences in each of the estimates of effort and ability based on test mode, we next wanted to determine whether test mode moderated the association between the subjective and objective estimates of each. Two separate ANOVA models were tested with the subj ective estimates of effort and ability as outcomes (i.e., self-reported mental effort, estimated number of it ems answered correctly) with predictors including t he respective objective estimate (i.e., RTE for effort, theta for ability), treatment condition (i.e., ad aptive or nonadaptive), and an interaction between the two. In t he model predicting self-reported effort (RQ3), there was a significant effect of treatment (F(1, 215) = 12.67, p <.001, partial eta2 =.056) but no effect of either RTE  (F(1,

215) = 1.70, p =.194, partial eta2 =.008) or an interaction of treatment and RTE (F(1, 215) =.22, p =.638, partial

eta2 =.001).

We followed a similar procedure to examine variati on in participants' self-evaluations of ability (RQ4). There were significant main effects of test score (F(1, 215) = 97.17, p <.001, partial eta 2 =.311), treatment condition (F(1, 215) = 4.09, p =.045, partial eta2 =.019), and the interaction of the two (F(1, 215) = 5.14, p =.024, partial eta2 =.023). The interaction term indicated that participants with higher test scores in the adaptive condition tended to report answering fewer question s correctly than those with higher test scores in t he nonadaptive condition. Given the matching of items based on performance in an adaptive test, this is to be expected.

## Discussion

The results provide information about how associations between subjective and objective estimates of effort and ability differ based on whether a computerized test is adaptive or not. Self-reported effort did not have a significant relationship with behavioral indications of effort,  according to our findings. Even after controlling for the test format, individuals' self-reported ability was positively correlated with their test score. Participan ts with higher ability in the non-adaptive condition estimated a g reater number of items answered correctly than thos e in the adaptive condition, suggesting that the test condit ion moderated the relationship between estimated nu mber of items answered correctly and test score.

Self-assessment requires one to accurately monitor and appraise their effort and ability (Andrade, 2019) and can vary from person to person (Baars et al., 2014win). According to previous research, certain demographic factors (e.g., age, gender, parental education), th e classroom learning environment (e.g., prior educa tional experience, interactions with teachers, engagement in learning), and the assessment context (e.g., low-or highstakes) all influence self-assessment accuracy. Student background factors (e.g., subject matter experience) and ICLS2022 Proceedings 1515 © ISLS current behaviors (e.g., course involvement) are hypothesized to influence the inaccuracy and overconfidence bias of self-assessment, as well as effort and ability on a specific task (Ober et al., 2022). Furthermore, there is evidence that female and underrepresented students in STEM m ay tend to underestimate their effort and ability o n evaluative tasks (Jacobs, 2005). This is regarded a s one possible barrier that inhibits individuals of underrepresented groups from pursuing STEM careers (Museus et al., 2011). As such, future work in this project domain will examine the extent to which the effects  of test mode are moderated by student background characteristics. Such findings in this area could contribute to a growing understanding of how assessment contexts contribute to differences in the accuracy of studen ts’ self-assessment and the implications of using i t for instructional practice.
