# Preservice Teachers’ Dialogic Talk Moves and Beliefs About Control in a AI-Driven Virtual Reality Classroom Simulation

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents two studies investigating preservice teachers’ dialogic teaching practices using an immersive, large language model (LLM) –enhanced virtual reality (VR) classroom simulation environment. Study 1 examined 23 preservice teachers’ perceptions of authenticity, situational interest, and self-efficacy during VR-based classroom debates and analyzed the talk move pattern using hidden Markov model. Participants perceived the simulation as authentic and engaging but relied predominantly on superficial probing questions rather thandialogic moves that deepen students’ thinking. Study 2 explored whether repeated simulation experiences combined with peer feedback influenced beliefs about classroom ownership of ideas and activities among 22 preservice teachers. Mixed-effects modeling indicated significant belief shifts toward student ownership of ideas, moderated by engagement with the simulation. Collectively, the findings suggest that AI-driven VR simulations provide authentic, scalable environments for developing dialogic competence and reshaping teacher beliefs toward more student-centered instructional practices.

Contemporary educational paradigms —from responsive teaching (Hammer et al., 2012) to dialogic pedagogy (Alexander, 2004)—challenge the traditional view of teachers as the sole authoritative voice controlling classroom discourse (Nystrand et al., 2003). Instead, effective teaching and learning hinge on productive classroom dialogues among teachers and students that promote collaborative sensemaking and shared knowledge construction (Mercer et al., 2019).

Within dialogic pedagogy, teachers intentionally employ discursive “talk moves” —utterance-sized instructional strategies analogous to chess moves —to scaffold students’ thinking and reasoning (O’Connor & Michaels, 2019). These talk moves “open, deepen, and widen dialogic spaces” (Wegerif, 2007, p. 233) that serve specific learning goals. The present study focuses specifically on teachers’ talk moves for fostering students’ ability to make cogent arguments, a “foundational disciplinary practice” (Lee, 2017, p. 91) emphasized across national education standards, including the Common Core State Standards (Common Core State Standards Initiative, 2010).

However, a major challenge lies in the fact that preservice and early-career teachers often struggle to enact dialogic pedagogy effectively. Prior research shows that novice teachers tend to rely on the Initiate – Respond–Evaluate (IRE) pattern (Chen et al., 2017), limiting opportunities for student-student interaction, critical reflection, and reasoning. They are also less likely to challenge students’ claims (Kim & Hand, 2015) and tend to dominate classroom talk (Wang et al., 2014). Despite its importance, dialogic teaching training is rarely emphasized in teacher preparation programs (Wei et al., 2018), as it involves nuanced social interactions that are difficult to reproduce in traditional settings.

To address this issue, teaching simulations have been developed as approximations of professional classroom practice (Duchatelet et al., 2022). These simulations —ranging from role-plays to mixed or virtual reality (VR) environments (Stürmer et al., 2024) —a llow teachers to engage in realistic instructional conversations. However, current approaches face a fundamental trade-off: human-based simulations offer richer interactions but lack scalability, while automated systems are more accessible yet lack responsiveness and authenticity. Thus, a critical gap remains between these two extremes. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) —AI systems capable of generating contextually relevant and coherent human-like dialogue (Naveed et al., 2024)—offer a potentially transformative solution to this dilemma. Early studies have explored teacher–LLM interactions in domains such as foreign language teaching (Ji et al., 2024), mathematical modeling (Yue et al., 2024), and natural sciences (Dai et al., 2024). Yet, these studies have relied on text-based interactions, lacking the immediacy and embodied experience of authentic classroom dialogue.

Study 1 VR classroom dialog simulation The classroom dialog simulation system was designed to resemble classrooms in German secondary public schools (see Figure 1). In a grid arrangement, thirty virtual students (agents) with different physical characteristics were seated. Numerous items in the classroom could be interacted with, such as the blackboard and presentation display. The classroom setting was deemed realistic and authentic by participants with similar demographics in other research utilizing similar immersive VR classroom systems  (Huang et al., 2022). During interactions between the teacher and virtual students in the classroom, the speech of the teacher was transcribed automatically by a speech-to-text software—the local port (Gerganov, 2024) of the Whisper small model. This transcription served as the user prompt and was sent as input to an LLM  instance operating on the same local machine. Specifically, we used gemma3:4b implemented within the Ollama framework (Ollama, 2024). The LLM instance was instructed to speak in the voice of the last student the user spoke to or the students whose names the user calls on. The students’ ability and prior knowledge can be configured. The generated text response was converted into spoken audio by voice models trained on real human  voices, known as voice clones, using a local neural text-tospeech engine (Rhasspy, 2024). VR classroom affords three essential requirements  for conducting experiments regarding dialogic teaching competences in this project: recreating  authentic situations that approximate real-life classroom situations, configuring and controlling the classroom situation for variable isolation and standardization, and collecting process-based data without intrusion to the teaching process. They also reported high situational interest (M = 3.53) and self-efficacy (M = 4.19) in moderating classroom debates after the simulation. We then examined the category of talk moves used by participants. The least frequent category of talk moves (revoice 5.27%; focus 4.38% of overall utterances) had the dialogic function of expanding students’ ideas and taking up students’ ideas. This i ndicated that participants rarely “work on and work with” students’ ideas to deepen the dialogic space. Mostly they stayed at simply initiating ideas, most notably asking probing questions (30.41%).

To dive deeper into the patterns of teacher-student interactions, we modeled the transition probabilities from categories of student talk to teacher talk moves using hidden Markov models (fitted with seqHMM and tna packages in R). We found that, no matter the students’ argumentation quality, the talk move that followed is highly likely to be a probing question (transition probability of 0.25 to 0.40). The probing question also had the highest initial probability, meaning teachers were most likely to use a p robing question to open the dialogue, but following the probing question, there are two distinctive patterns  (see Table 1). In the high probability pattern, teachers continue to make the same move of asking another probing question either with the same or a different student. While in the low probability pattern, teachers press the student to reconsider their reasoning and us e evidence, or they would uptake the student’s ideas.

These results demonstrated the effectiveness of using VR classroom as a dialog simulation environment to capture and identify patterns of dialogic teaching.

Study 2 According to Korthagen (2004), enduring pedagogical change requires transformation at the belief level. However, preservice teachers often enter teacher education with preexisting and often unsound beliefs shaped by years as students. Specifically for classroom discussions, preservice teachers often hold belief that they own the locus of control over students (Chen et al., 2017), this can be further differentiated as beliefs that teacher own the ideas (e.g., teacher decides what’s a good idea) or activities (e.g., teacher determines which activity to conduct) 533 instead of the students. Situated-learning research demonstrates that authentic, feedback-rich practice—such as VR simulations —can reshape such beliefs (Chernikova et al., 2020; Dieker et al., 2019). VR offers a unique opportunity for approximations of teaching practice (Duchatelet et al., 2022)  and has been found to improve various aspects of teaching (Huang et al., 2023), yet it is unknown whether engagement with VR simulation also induces belief change in classroom discussion.

Twenty-two preservice teachers were recruited from a block seminar on dialogic pedagogy in the bachelor's teacher education program at a public German university (Mage = 22.5 years; 38.1% female; Msemester = 5.0). During the seminar, the teachers participated in two rounds of VR  simulations with peer feedback in groups of three. Participants prepared a short five-minute lesson aimed at developing students' argumentative reasoning through a debate. After each simulated lesson, peers used an observational pro tocol adapted from Ambitious Science Teaching (Windschitl et al., 2018) to provide feedback. Before and after the seminar, teachers completed a questionnaire in which they rated their agreement with 16 statements regarding teacher control in the classroom. There were 4 items for each quadrant, based on combinations of ownership of activity (teacher/ student) and ownership of ideas (teacher /student). For instance, “During class discussions, as a teacher, I believe my main responsibility is to present information and conceptual relationships around a given subject.” (1 = “does not apply” to 4 = “applies completely”) is describing the belief that teachers control both activities and ideas. Engagement with VR simulation is measured with 4 items such as “I was enthusiastic abo ut the conducting a classroom  discussion in VR. ”(Vera et al., 2014)  on a 4-point Likert scale (α=.86).

A linear mixed-effects model (lme4 package in R) was fitted with time, belief scores about idea and activity ownership, engagement with VR, and their interactions as fixed effects, including a random intercept for each teacher.

The Time×IdeaOwner interaction was significant (F(1,128)=11.56, p<.001, d = 0.83), indicating a prominent shift in belief toward student ownership of ideas. But we found no change occurred for activity ownership, the participants continue to believe teachers should control the activity conducted. Moreover, we found that engagement significantly moderated the belief change in ownership of idea (Time×IdeaOwner×Engagement, F(1,128)=8.27, p=.005, d = 0.63); indicating that more engaged teachers showed a larger shift in belief toward student ownership of ideas.  With these findings, w e demonstrated that brief but well-designed simulation and feedback session may help preservice teachers meaningfully re-align their belief toward more student-centered instructional practices.

## Summary and implication

The findings from these studies hold significant theoretical implications for understanding how dialogic pedagogy can be developed through technologically mediated practice. Together, they suggest that immersive, AI-enhanced VR environments can serve as powerful “approximation spaces” for enacting complex pedagogical theories — particularly those emphasizing dialogic interaction, teacher responsiveness, and shared ownership of knowledge. Theoretically, these studies extend models of teacher learning that focus on practice-based education (Grossman et al., 2009) by demonstrating that dialogic teaching is not just a set of observable behaviors but a situated, belief-driven practice. The shift in preservice teachers’ beliefs toward student ownership of ideas supports Korthagen’s (2004) notion that lasting pedagogical change requires transformation at the belief level. The findings also illustrate that belief change is contingent upon engagement and reflection, aligning with constructivist perspectives on teacher learning that emphasize the interplay between authentic experience, cognitive engagement, and social feedback.

In terms of implications, the results underscore the pedagogical potential of AI-driven VR simulations as a bridge between theory and practice. These environments enable teacher educators to capture and analyze micro-level dialogic patterns while also influencing macro-level pedagogical orientations. Integrating such tools into teacher preparation programs could help novices internalize core principles of dialogic pedagogy—such as co-construction of meaning and epistemic openness —within realistic yet risk-free settings. More broadly, this work advances the theoretical understanding of how emerging technologies can support the development of dialogic agency, positioning teachers not merely as transmitters of knowledge but as facilitators of collective inquiry and reasoning.
