# Facilitating Cross-Community Knowledge Building Through Multimodal Artifact Creation

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster discusses an exploratory study in how student generated multimodal artifacts were used to facilitate cross-community Knowledge Building between two unique learning communities situated in Canada and Spain. Initial findings suggest that communities which drastically different (age, culture, geography, time, grade, subject of study); multimodality afforded nuanced idea expression, opportunities to connect on a personal level, and ownership of learning.

## Introduction

With education returning to in-person learning there is a unique opportunity to reim agine, reintegrate and reconnect pedagogy, technology, and collaborative learning. Specifically, various tools we u sed to facilitate connection during the pandemic helped broaden our understanding of community by bringing everyone c loser, regardless of time or geography. To explore the use of multimodality to foster collaboration within and between communities, this study sought to give students “a chance to tell their own stories  and create knowledge from their starting points about a topic” (Vivitsou et al., 2016). In collaboration with another school, students were afforded the opportunity of additional perspectives unique to a different culture, socioeconomics, and age group. As Scardamalia and Bereiter (2014) noted, “knowledge cannot be found in any  one person’s mind”, thus the primary objective was to answer the question of how may multimodal artifacts facilitate cross-community creative work with ideas?

## Methodology

This design-based research study was conducted over a 9-month period between September 2021 and May 2022. There were 82 participants, 22 Canadian grade 2 students (7-8 years old) and 60 Spanish grade 9 students (14-15 years old), situated in two research sites; one in Toronto, Canada and one in Ta rragona, Spain. The study was attached to the grade 2 inquiry subject, and science for the grade 9 students. Both grades were split into equal working groups (11 and 30) for easier classroom management. Roughly, 2 hours a week w ere spent Knowledge Building (KB) online and or in-person. Knowledge Forum (KF) was the primary platform on which both communities collaborated. Three community spaces or views in KF were used; two views local to each grade and one shared view. Flexibility was given to students in their modality of artifa ct creation throughout the study. Modalities included writing, drawing, typing, audio, and video recordings, curated online me dia, video conferencing, a website and Minecraft.

Design based research was deemed the most appropriate as this study sought to develop a framework for how multimodal artifacts could be used to facilitate cross-community collaborati on. Three design interventions were implemented to iteratively develop an understanding of the affordances and drawbacks of multimodality as it pertained to Knowledge Building. Qualitative data included interviews, audio and video recordings, virtual meetings, and KF notes (text, images, video, and audio). Recordings were transcribed for coding. KF notes were exported in CSV format for thematic analysis. Multimedia notes on KF wer e also downloaded for thematic analysis were appropriate. Quantitative data included KF analytical data collected related to frequency of reading, writing, and connections between notes and types of scaffolds used. To answer the  research question, the three interventions were compared. For each intervention, KF notes were triangulate d with observations, interviews and meetings notes to highlight advantages or challenges of each intervention.

## Results

The first intervention, established a baseline of cross-community online KB  between Canadian and Spanish students. The design problem they focused on – proposed by the grade 9s – why is our city so grey? This saw an immediate divergence in areas of focus from both groups. The grade 2s focus ed on their local community, trees, plants, and fungi – a micro lens to the question. They produced 194 notes that were primarily text-base d with some drawings. The grade 9s focused on education, lack of green spaces, urban mobility, pollution, and climate

– a macro lens to the question. They produced 90 notes, many of which were multi-authored and included a variety

of modalities (videos, images, text, and audio). They also produced at least one rise-above (a summary note, encapsulating related notes) per topic. This was reflective of typical KF activity, both in collaboration notes (avg. 1.20) and number of notes created (avg. 1.18). What happened outside of KF was inter esting, as students used Zoom to discuss cultural events that were significant to each group, and aspects of their daily life that was unique CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 373 in Canada and Spain. This had a positive influence on inter-community dynamics as  both groups began sending physical artifacts (custom bookmarks, photographs, drawings). The grade 9s even built an entire website that shared images of their school and types of trees found on school grounds. This sugges ted that social aspects of community were a crucial component in developing relationships, and further augment ed by digital and analog student generated artifacts.

The second intervention coincided with a change in topics for both groups. Grade 2s focused on salmon and human environmental interference and grade 9s focused on alternative energy sour ces. With each group spending two days a week working on Knowledge Building, one was dedicated to working w ithin their own community and the second day was spent building-on and contributing to the other community’s work. Multimodality was a crucial element as it aided in clearing up misconceptions or misinterpretations. One example, grade 9s were sharing their favourite recipes for preparing salmon, as they were learning English as a second language, the opted to supplement their KF notes with images of the dishes. In another case, the grade 9s utilized audio recordings to summarize key points in their rise above note so the grade  2s had the option to listen in addition to reading notes in KF. Based on the data and affordances of multimodali ty it would suggest that there was an improvement in understanding between two diverse groups, leading to increased col laboration. The average build-on notes increased to an average of 5.31 notes per student, or a 22%. In the third intervention, a new question was introduced, “ how would you design a new neighbourhood?”, this was in response to a news article the grade 9s saw in their local newspaper highlighting a new development close their school. This intervention lasted 3 months and built on wha t had occurred in the second intervention; use of multimodality to provide flexibility and clarity of discour se. An example was the heavy use of drawings to visualize various elements the grade 2s deemed necessary in a collaborative design of a new neighbourhood with the grade 9s. These would eventually be transformed by the gra de 9s class into 3D simulations in Minecraft. In doing so, we can see how multimodality has a strong resonance with students particularly if the modality can be tied to their lived experiences out of school. Furthermore, this example suggests that students can and will readily build-on, remix or transform the student ge nerated artifacts of their peers to synthesize or rise-above (Scardamalia, & Bereiter (2014), which can indicate the pushing the boundarie s of the community’s knowledge. To conclude, the integration of multimodality within KB, saw a positive shift in crosscollaboration as determined by the increase in build-ons. During the first interve ntion build-on notes averaged 6.65 per student, after the third intervention, build-ons averaged 12.87 per student, a 51.6% increase.

## Implications

First, by working in collaboration across communities it exemplifies a principle of KB, symmetric knowledge advancement in which “expertise is distributed within and between communities...to give knowle dge is to get knowledge” (Scardamalia, 2002, p. 11). Despite different topics of investigation, they were able to bridge the gap between subjects and effectively work together in building new knowledge in the form of designs and simulations that pushed the boundary of their knowledge while also allowing them to work on a potentiall y higher plane of understanding. Second, pervasive knowledge building is present, as seen in both communities drawing on their lived experiences and real-world events and bringing them into the discourse. L astly, extending on Yuan and Zhang (2019) on the use of boundary objects – primarily text-based – to enable cross-community KB, this study utilizes multimodal artifacts that include the sharing of both content knowledge and personal narratives. In doing so, students were better able to express their often-nuanced understanding of ideas in the most appropriate medium and afforded the chance to build deeper bonds with peers through stories alongside content knowledge learning.
