# Developing a Generative AI Framework for Analyzing Student Responses to Enhance Classroom Assessments

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study develops and tests a Large Language Model-based AI framework for analyzing written responses on assessment tasks, generating scoring reasoning, and identifying response uncertainties to provide feedback that supports learning. Using prompt engineering and aggregation methods, the proposed AI models achieved an overall accuracy of 85% when scoring 834 written responses. These findings demonstrate the framework’s potential to enhance the rationale of classroom assessments by providing timely, feedback to improve teaching and learning.

## Objectives and conceptual framework

Knowledge-in-use refers to students applying their knowledge with scientific practices to solve complex problems (Pellegrino & Hilton, 2012). Classroom assessments provide tangible evidence of this knowledge, enabli ng feedback to support learning (Harris et al., 2024). Timely, targeted feedback fosters cognitive growth by engaging learners to reflect on their knowledge, identify we aknesses, seek additional information, and revise t heir work (Nicol et al., 2006). Effective feedback should be based on analyzing student responses, making judgments about student performance, and providing clear, actionabl e feedback on areas for improvement to foster ongoi ng learning and growth. Yet, teachers have little time to provide such feedback. Advances in Generative AI (GenAI), notably Large Language Models (LLMs), facilitate au tomated analysis of written responses in assessment  tasks to provide meaningful feedback. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting (Wei, et al., 2022) enhance s LLMs by breaking responses into critical components, identi fying logical connections, and generating intermedi ate explanations to inform final scoring decisions. Thi s approach highlights weaknesses or ambiguities in individual responses rather than relying on predefined or prog rammatic outputs. It provides personalized guidance  that encourages learners to iteratively refine their answers to achieve their learning goals. This study aims to develop a GenAI-enhanced assessment framework to (1) analyze studen ts’ written responses, (2) generate reasons for scoring decisions and identify response uncertainty, and (3) assign final scores and provide feedback on weaknesses to support learning. This study explores two research questions (RQs): RQ1, “How can AI algorithms, leveraging LLM and grounded  in learning theories, be developed to analyze stud ent responses and identify weakness in written response s for knowledge-in-use assessment tasks?” RQ2, “How can uncertainty in student responses be identified to provide feedback on knowledge-in-use assessment tasks?”

## Method: GenAI-enhanced assessment framework

Our assessment tasks and rubrics were designed usin g a modified evidence-centered design approach (Harris et al., 2024) to measure middle school students’ knowledg e-in-use aligned with the Next Generation Science Standard (NGSS, 2013) (see Table 2). The rubric con tains three components, including (1) Disciplinary Core Ideas (DCI), (2) DCI and Scientific Practices (S P), and (3) DCI and Cross-Cutting Concepts (CCC), to analyze the understanding of knowledge-in-use. This paper reports the results of 834 student-written responses from one of the five Next Generation Science Assessment tasks, achieving an inter-rater reliability of 0.80 (He et al., 2024). Our AI models used Distilled Versions of GPT-4o-mini to provide the results of precision, recall, and F1-score to evaluate the accuracy by analyzing our AI model scoring predictions. For RQ1, we employed prompt engineering to guide th e LLM in analyzing student response structures and matching rubric components to the processed content of the responses step by step (He, et al., 2024). Our CoT approach involves (1) dissecting responses into the  three components, (2) checking logical connections  and alignment with the assessment question, (3) identifying possible incorrect statements, and (4) providing an overall assessment with scoring reasons. Using this approac h, we designed a prompting structure that integrate s “Instruction + Rubric + Examples of responses (5 ex amples) with CoT.” Our AI model scores responses as  “0” for incorrect and “1” for correct answers while als o generating scori ng reasons. For RQ2, we developed four distinct models by varying the prompts in specific ways. First, we adjusted the number of examples inc luded in some prompts. Second, we modified the instructions within the prompts to address two factors: (1) whet her a score is assigned when the student provides an intu itively correct but not explicitly articulated resp onse, and (2) whether the LLM focuses more on the structural anal ysis. For example, one of the four models exactly f ollows (He, et al., 2024) to perform structural analysis to comprehensively extract the key informa tion and key words. The final score is determined by the majority vote of the four models (Wang, et al., 2022). If at leas t three of the four models assign a score 0, the final score is 0. For example, if the model scores are 0, 1, 0, and 0, the final score for the case is 0. This approach also quantifies and identifies uncertainties in student responses whe n the models scores differ. In the above example, 25% of the student response is considered ambiguous.

## Results and discussion

The RQ1 results indicate that our proposed model for analyzing student responses and generating scoring reasons achieved the following accuracy: All (0.83), DCI&SP (0.87), DCI (0.78), and DCI&CCC (0.84). The RQ2 results, using the majority vote method to identify ambiguity in student responses, show that our model increased accuracy across all rubrics: All (0.85), DCI&SP (0.88), DCI (0.82), and DCI&CCC (0.86). Table 1 presents an example of results for DCI & SP. We reviewed student responses from the ambiguous gr oup and found them to be unclear and inconsistent within their paragraphs. This inconsistency led to low reliability in scoring for both human raters and AI. We propose using this uncertainty information to pr ovide feedback to students, alerting them about the ambiguity in the responses rather than AI to evaluate responses with a high risk of inaccuracy. ## Conclusions and future directions

Our GenAI-enhanced assessment framework demonstrates the pote ntial of LLMs to analyze written responses, generate scoring reasons, and identify uncertainty,  providing feedback that enhances learning. We are designing classroom studies to collect and analyze new respon se data with our AI models, exploring how timely fe edback influences learning across various student characteristics (e.g., performance levels, ethnicity, school location, and socioeconomic status). Our framework and methods can be applied  to o ther disciplines that use classroom assessment for teaching and learning, as well as other constructs like motivation, metacognition, and collaboration skills, which are influenced by causal variables that help diagnose students’ behaviors.
