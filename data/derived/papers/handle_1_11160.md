# Fused Representations: Linking Choreographic and Digital NetLogo Modeling through Intermodal Inquiry

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper reports from a summer camp, Dance to Explore Science, in which middle school youth, professional scientists and choreographers collaboratively engaged in intermodal inquiry using choreographic and digital NetLogo modeling to investigate a topic within the scientists’ research areas. Drawing on analysis of youth interviews, one group’s work towards preparing their final share-out, and their final share-out itself, we propose the construct of a fused representation—a distributed model developed by participants in which their physical choreography and NetLogo models expressed a scientific idea in interlinked ways. We present evidence of why we see their share-out as a fused representation, and illuminate how attuning to the relationships between their choreographic and NetLogo models led the group to construct a fused representation. In the discussion, we reflect on the importance of supporting the development of fused representations to leverage multiple modalities for scientific modeling and sense-making.

## Introduction

Supporting student sensemaking of scientific ideas and practices through computational agent-based modeling (ABM) has garnered increasing attention over the la st two decades (e.g., Wilensky & Resnick, 1999). AB M is a representational form to examine how phenomena in a  system can arise from simple rules followed by the individuals in it. While much of the research in AB M has involved using digital tools (e.g., Dickes & Sengupta, 2013; Wagh et al., 2022), there has also been atten tion to the use of computational physical or embodi ed and digital models as dual modes of sense making (e.g.,  Pierson & Brady, 2020; Zohar & Levy, 2021). This p aper reports from a design-based research project (Brown, 1992) in which we de signed a camp, Dance to Explore Science, built on the premise that bringing choreographic modeling  and digital ABM together can support generative science inquiry. Choreographic modeling involves creating sets of rules to govern or struct ure how dancers move and interact in a space. Digital ABM involves defining and coding sets of rules for digital agents to enact and interact with each other in a digital space. While we see these two forms of inquiry (choreo graphy and ABM) as almost isometric, translated in different m odes, our goal was to investigate how they might work to support each other.

In this paper, we characterize how processes of int ermodal inquiry generated the development of what we call a fused representation in which participants made sense of embodied, chore ographic models and digital ABMs as interlinked and interdependent in how they expressed a scientific idea. We present an analysis  of the intermodal representation a group of four youth age d between 9 to 13, one scientist, and one choreogra pher presented at the end of the camp as well as two foc al episodes from their creation of this representat ion and interviews with youth participants. Our analysis supports two findings: 1. Participants saw and shared their work as a distributed model that simultaneously consiste d of both their choreography being enacted as well as a NetLogo model running at the same time; and, 2. Not icing similarities and distinctions between their p hysical choreography and the digital N etLogo models led to a refinement of their distribu ted model. Finally, we reflect on the importance of intermodal inquiry and the gen eration of fused representations in enabling participants to engage in expansive scientific modeling (Schwarz et al., 2022; Wagh et al., 2021).

## Theoretical background

Digital ABM and science learning Even though ABM plays an important role in scientific research (e.g., Payette, 2012), research in using ABMs as a tool for science learning has mostly been done in  science classrooms (Yoon, Goh & Park, 2018). Littl e work has examined how to make this way of thinking accessible to youth, particularly underrepresented youth, outside of formal learning environments. As the gap widens between the understandings, tools and practices of scientists and the working science knowledge of the public, it  is critical that we expand opportunities for youth  to develop conceptual tools to leverage their own knowledge in  scientific inquiry. One way to address this is to design and examine science learning environments that allow yo uth to engage with scientists in informal environme nts in ways that are accessible to youth. This is particul arly important for youth who typically lack opportu nities for authentic science learning experiences and are less  likely to have access to professional scientists a nd science practices (Barton, Tan & Rivet, 2008).

Embodiment and scientific modeling: Connections between choreography and ABM ABM is a powerful representational infrastructure f or modeling (Wilensky & Reisman, 2006). Learners ar e invited to think about its constituent agents, thei r properties and the rules they follow. Embodied mo deling is a rich source of science learning in par tnership with ABM as it provides learners with a fi rst-person perspective inside of a system (e.g., Colella, 2000). ABM in th e form of embodied modeling offers opportunities fo r participants to raise questions about changing aspects of a system and act out those changes, exploring these ideas and raising new questions (e.g., Colella, 2000). Ch oreography affords similar opportunities for raisin g and exploring questions about how rules that govern the  coordination of moving entities can form new expre ssive formations (Vogelstein, 2020 & 2022). We draw on a fr aming of choreography as a form of research (Lerman, 2014; Vogelstein, 2020) in which rules for movement s, movement scores, are enacted by groups and itera tively refined and changed based on new questions that occur through the dancers’ choices and enactme nts. Research has shown that embodiment particularly through chor eographic modeling can provide opportunities for cr eative expression and divergent thinking that can further scientific modeling in unique ways (Champion, 2018). Both choreography and ABM provide opportunities for  multimodal perspective-taking in sense-making of a system. In ABM this is reflected in identifyin g the agents and their interactions within a system. In choreography it can mean considering the impact of physical connections with others and understanding your own role intrinsic to the choreography as always in  relation to an audience’s extrinsic perspective. B oth choreography and ABM require an understanding of the rules and relationships between agents and of the factors that influence the system. Those rules and relationships can be shaped by new proposals from participants at any point, shifting meanings, interactions and outcomes  within the system (Vogelstein, 2020). Choreographi c and agent-based modeling both require the use and considerati on of space and time. This expansive and inclusive perspective on modeling positions it as an interdis ciplinary tool for thinking and engaging with ideas, inviting it to be used as a tool for exploration, thinking, and practice.

## Context

The Dance to Explore Science camp was a 60 hour, tw o-week summer camp that invited 2 scientists, 12 yout h and 2 choreographers to engage in collaborative sci entific inquiry grounded in partnering scientists’ research topics (spinal cord injury & walking bi omechanics). The camp was hosted at a community the ater space in an African American neighborhood in a southern state in the United States. The camp design focused on introducing both adult and youth participants to a complex syst ems lens on scientific phenomena by building choreo graphic and digital NetLogo models for sense-making of scientific questions. In the first week, these two modalities were used to deconstruct the scientists’ research system s into agents, rules, and emergent patterns that co uld be represented physically and digitally (e.g., how neurons, astrocytes, and extracellular membrane (ECM) interact in the repair of a spinal cord injury). The second week focused on refining and investigating questions that surfaced in the first week (e.g., ho w donew neural pathways get made?) using choreogra phic and digital NetLogo modeling. This emphasized how choreographic and dig ital forms of modeling can be used in conversation with each other in cycles of scientific inquiry. Champio n facilitated the camp  along with our choreographic partners who acted as both leaders and participants in the i ntergenerational scientific inquiry. This emphasized that every adult and youth in the space was a learner, a researcher, a scientist, and a choreographer. For this analysis we focus on one of two intergener ational groups from the camp as an illustrative cas e of the development of a fused representation. This group spent the second week of camp working on thei r intermodal inquiry about questions relating to p artner scientist Eleana’s research on spinal cord i njuries. Part of their work entailed remixing a NetLogo “starter mod el” Eleana and the authors had made together prior to the camp as a way to introduce her topic. This starter model included the basic agent groups (neuron cells, cell-signals, & ECM) as well as the rules that governed how cells  moved through the environment to demonstrate when they found the ECM. During the first week of the camp, p articipants played with this model, running it on t heir computers to see how changing different variables (e.g., cell-signal range & different ECM shapes) might change aggregate patterns. In addition they also used choreographic modeling tools to interpret the code for the movement rules that governed cell movement in the starter model. While none of the participants had prior experience  with NetLogo (a few youth participants had some experience with Scratch), they were all able to gen erate ideas for editing and augmenting the existing  NetLogo code in the starter model. For example, one q uestion that arose for this group was how neural si gnal pathways could regenerate when an injury caused ECM to cut o ff existing neural pathways, this topic is known as neuroplasticity. As described in our findings, this  led the group to edit the starter model so that ne w neural pathways could be “drawn” by neuron “turtles” (mobi le agents in NetLogo) around injury sites. The grou p also represented this in the choreography  they developed. As we will demonstrate in our analy sis, thinking across modalities supported their inquiry on this particular topic. At the end of the two-week camp this group presented their inquiry in a final share-out in which they chose to share their  NetLogo model and choreography simultaneously, describing them as mutually elaborative.

## Methods: Data collection and analysis

Data consisted of daily field notes from the implem entation, video recordings of all group work and in terviews with participants, as well as photographs of artifa cts created. The focal group for this analysis incl uded six participants—four youth participants aged between 9 to 13, a local choreographer and a scientist. The four youth participants were girls of color and the adults were both white. Our analysis began with this group’s 2-hour work session at the end of the 8th day of camp. During this session, the first author was struck by the youth participants’ comfort with learning to code in NetLogo despite their limited exposure to it and the unique ways the group used choreographic language and enactments to engage wit h the code of their model. This observation led us to investigate the intermodality of their inquiry. We looked at this segment and identified moments that demonstrated their inquiry was connected across modalities (e.g., moments in which choreography supported the devel opment of code and code supported the development of chore ography). In this paper we present detailed microan alyses (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) of two of these moments:  Episode 1: The group “playing turtle” (Papert, 198 0) to code neuroplasticity; Episode 2: rehearsing their choreographic score. Analysis of the group’s inquiry process led us to analyze how they presented their final model at the end of the camp, which included close viewings of their final share out performance and youth comments about what they made in their interviews. Together, this analysis demonstrates both the intermodal nature of the fuse d representation this group created and how the dev elopment of a fused representation supported their scientific inquiry and learning.

## Findings

Our analysis revealed that the participants saw and  shared their work as a distributed model that incl uded their choreography as it was enacted as well as a running  NetLogo model. We call this distributed model a fused representation because of the ways in which the participants saw the two modalities as interrelated in expressing scientific ideas. In characterizing the processes t hat led to the generation of this fused representat ion, we found that attending to the similarities and di fferences between thei r physical choreography and the NetLogo model raised new questions that led to the refinement of their distributed model. Finding 1: Participants saw and shared their work as a distributed model that consisted of enacting their choreography while running their NetLogo model At the end of the Dance to Explore Science Camp, after youth and scientist participants spent two weeks engaged in intermodal scientific inquiry, participants shared their models with family and community members. The spinal cord injury group—which consisted of Naomi, Brandi, Olympia, & Briah (middle schoo lers), Eleana (scientist), & Alex (choreographer)—presented their choreographic and NetLogo models together. Part 1 entailed each member of the gr oup taking the microphone to share something about what they had m ade with the audience. They stood in a line and rea d from scripts that described aspects of their inquiry suc h as the topic, terms they lea rned (e.g., astrocyte), the ABM modeling environment (NetLogo), and their choreogra phy (Figure 1.1). In part 2 they played an animatio n they had drawn to take the audience inside the body (Figure 1.2). Then part 3 entailed showing their “healthy neuron” dance while their NetLogo “model” of healthy neuron activity ran on a computer that was projected at the bottom of the stage (Figure 1.3). Part 4 followed up with digital and physical transitions to create injuries  in both modalities which included Brandi drawing ECM in the  NetLogo model on the computer (Figure 1.4 left) and Eleana setting up “streamer[s]” on the stage as pro ps for the following (Figure 1.4 right). We focused on parts 3-5 during which the group presented their ideas as distributed across physical and digital modalities simultaneously. When given the o pportunity to share their inquiry, this group chose  to demonstrate the physical and digital components together, supporting our claim that they understood their inquiry across these modalities as consequently linked, or fused together. In addition to presenting their choreographic and digital inquiry in ways that highlighted their interconnectedness, youth participants also describ ed how these modalities were complementary and rela ted in their model in their interviews. For example, when  asked if the “coding and NetLogo dance you were doi ng, [were] related in any way?” Brandi shared: Yes, the– well, like our dance with, if we didn’t have our dance, our Netlogo wouldn’t be like– it would still make sense, it just wouldn’t be as clear. But if–  and if we didn’t have the NetLogo, our dance wouldn’t be as clear, ‘ cause it’s like helpful to  see like one perspective from another, like real life and computer. Taking the perspective of a viewer or audience member, Brandi described the clarity each modality offers the other, stating that although these modalities “ would still make sense,” on their own, “it just wou ldn’t be as clear.” According to Brandi, the NetL ogo model they created made more sense when accompa nied by the dance they choreographed and the dance made more sense with the Netlogo model because, “it’s like helpful to see like one perspective from another, like real life and co mputer.” Brandy compared  “real life and computer” as she discussed how choreographic and digital modeling environments mutually elaborated each other to explain ideas. The juxtaposition of “real life” and “computer”  is interesting as all modeling practices entail processes of selective abstraction such that no model is the phenomenon (or “real life”) itself, but rather a representation that foregrounds and backgrounds different aspects of the phenomenon. However, choreography and dance positioned as, “r eal life,” highlights both the scale and accessibility of person-scale and person-enacted movements in relationship to the physical distance between bodies and small-scale computer models. In choosing to keep these two modalities and scales together, Brandi and her collaborators l everaged the connections across these models to pre sent them together in order to clarify the ideas they developed.

While Brandi described these two modalities as mutu ally elaborative from an audience perspective, she also described their relationship from the perspective of someone engaging in processes of scientific inquiry: Author1:  Do you think each of them [choreography & NetLogo model] offer something different or are they just showing the same thing? Brandi: Well, different things because like some things you can’t really do in NetLogo, so you would have to do it with your body or expl aining with your words, but some things you can’t do in real life because it’s more like a computer thing than a person thing. Author1:  Do you have examples for that? Brandi: Oh, like how we couldn’t be doing-we can’t really like place an injury down and then, also we had less people than, but in  NetLogo you can just –  [code] 200 turtles and then 200 turtles would pop up.

Brandi described the limitations of each modality as a reason thinking across them was generative because, “there are some things you can’t really do in NetLogo [...and] some things you can’t do in real life.” One example Brandi gave was the number of entities that could interact  with each other. While the choreography was made f or their group of 6 dancers, they could easily and quickly p lay with hundreds of turtles (codable agents) in Ne tLogo, “in NetLogo you can just [code] 200 turtles and then 200 turtles would pop up.” Brandi also noted that there are some things that were easier to do “with your body” than  in NetLogo, because of how people can interpret ru les with fewer details than programmable agents (e.g. walk around any obstacle in front of you). Looking at how this group shared their inquiry and discussed the interconnect edness of choreographic and digital modeling provid ed evidence that they saw their work as a fused repres entation. Finding 2: Participants refined their fused represen tation by attending to similarities and distinctions between their choreographic and NetLogo models As the group engaged in intermodal inquiry, their d eveloping choreographic and NetLogo models served a s resources for sense-making of the science topic—spinal cord injury. Comparing their models in the two modalities brought up new questions and opportunit ies to refine their models. To make this argument, we present two episodes from the 8th day of camp in which the grou p worked on their final share-out model. In both episodes, the group explored questions about the generation o f new neural pathways that could go around injury s ites (neuroplasticity). By this point, the group had a near-final choreographic model to represent neuron behavior in a healthy spinal cord and were developing one to repr esent an injury that they called, “cell attack.” Wi th the help of Vogelstein, they had also started revising the N etLogo starter model to restrict neuron movement to  neural pathways by adding a purple diagonal for neurons to  move along. The two episodes presented in this ana lysis demonstrate how their neuroplasticity inquiry drew on connections across modalities of inquiry. Inquiry Episode 1: Noting a difference between rules for human neurons and digital neurons To represent the formation of new neural pathways, the group wanted neurons to be able to reconnect to pathways when injury sites cut off existing neural pathways.  However, it was hard to figure out a set of rules that would allow digital neurons to do this. While struggling to come up with rules for their code, Eleana (scientist) compared the ease of dancing this idea to their struggles developing NetLogo code (Figure 2). In particular, she noted a key difference between their behavior as neurons in the choreographic model and the digital neurons in their NetLogo model—an automatic cognizance towards their surroundings,  demonstrated in the ease in which she walked around an imaginary injury site (Figure 2.1) (“beca use when we were the signal, Right? We were  like, ‘Oh neuroplasticity, we just go around and go right bac k to,’ ((walked around an injury site))  but like it’s not gonna know how to get back”). The kinds of rules dancers can interpret (e.g., “go around”) are not the same kinds of rules digital agents can interpret (“It’s just gonna be like, ‘Oh is it black? ((looked down at tile)) No. Ok, ((sharp turn 90° to the left))  let me go over here,’ or something like that). Her words and enactment highlighted that creating a neural pathway was easy to do choreograp hically as a human agent, but more challenging to b reak down into many rules for digital agents in NetLogo.

They engaged in “playing turtle” as a way of thinking through rules that would allow the digital neurons to form new neural pathways. Alex (choreographer) got tape and marked a diagonal path of a few square tiles on the ground—“enough to feel this”—just like in their NetLogo model. In this modified space, four diagonal tiles were marked with a piece of tape to represent  a “patch” (in NetLogo language) of a neural pathwa y (represented as white rectangles in Figure 3). A fi fth tile was marked with a pamphlet to represent an  injury site that broke up the neural pathway (represented as a red rectangle in Figure 3). Participants took turns walking up the diagonal pat h to think through rules for a digital neuron tryin g to create a new neural pathway. On her turn, Naomi pro posed that, “some of them [the neurons] would go di agonal and stop or some of them would do something else.” She physically enacted her proposal through two tur ns—in one turn, she walked down the pathway and stopped a t the injury site (Figure 3.1-2) and in the next turn, she walked down the pathway and turned around (Figure 3.3-4). Naomi’s verbal and physical proposal suggested that what occurred in both modalities could (and maybe should) be modeled in NetLogo. Her first enactment reflected their current NetLogo model in which the neurons stopped at an injury site because the group had not yet encoded rules for moving around it, and her second enactment reflected their choreographic model in which the participants quite easily formed a path around the injury (Figure 2). This proposal opened up multiple possibilities for what a neuron could do on encountering an injury, suggesting that the group should consider multiple goals and outcomes for their code. Still figuring out what that could mean in NetLogo, Briah asked, “does that mean we have to make another breed?” This question again pointed out that things that could easily be done physically might have more nuanced implications when being encoded intonetLogo. It also served as evidence that our youth participants— in this case, the youngest youth participant in the group–were considering and thinking about this in their inquiry. Inquiry Episode 2: Noting similarities between stillness in choreographic and NetLogo models About an hour after the end of episode 1, the group  transitioned to rehearsing their choreographic mod el, which consisted of two dances: one of a healthy system and another a “cell attack” that represented what happens when there is an injury. In the injury dance, Briah, Nao mi and Olympia were neurons at the injury site that  sent out signals to indicate injury. Brandi, Alex, and Elean a were astrocytes that received these signals and m oved to the injury to block it. When enacting the choreographic  model this time, the neurons (Briah, Naomi, and Olympia) finished their signal choreography before all of the astrocytes (Eleana) could detect it. This meant that Eleana was unable to get to and block the injury before the music ended. Right after they finished this run of the injury dance, Briah asked, “what happens when we run out of chore ography to do?” Since the group developed the signa l choreography to be detected by the astrocyte dancers, Briah’s question brought a new idea to the group. The group responded to this in two ways. Author3 proposed that they could repeat the same choreography or create more so that they would not run out, addr essing Briah’s noticing as an error needing to be c orrected. In contrast, Alex reasoned about their choreographic model by relating it to their NetLogo model: I’m thinking about this model and once the injuries  happen ((pointed and walked towards the laptop with their NetLogo model))  the ECM is there, they just kind of, they all stop too. So if you’re like, you run out of choreography ((walked towards the area where tape had been on the floor to represent the neural pathway)) I think you also like  come to the edge of your path ((pointed towards the end of the prior tape path)). Right? Like the signal wouldn’t get through. While Author3’s response to Briah’s question propos ed a way to maintain a determined outcome (astrocyt es always detecting signals), Alex’s response proposed  that a single outcome might not always occur. He n oted a similarity between their two models: In the NetLogo model, the neurons stopped in response to an injury because of the ECM encoded in their model (“once the injuries happen ((pointed and walked towards the laptop with their NetLogo model)) the ECM is there, they just kind of, they all stop too”). Likewise, in their choreographic model, when a neuron ran out of choreography and had to st op, it could be because the signal didn’t get throu gh (“if you’re like, you run out of choreography ((walked towards the area where tape had been on the floor to represent the neural pathway))… Like the signal wouldn’t get through”). Instead of seeing an error that needed to be repaired, Alex leveraged the question to reason about the scientific system: neurons stopped because the presence of ECM caused the signal to not get through. The group continued to develop their representations of signal detection by refining their choreographic and NetLogo models. In their choreographic model, t hey incorporated a rule that a neuron whose signal did not receive a response would crouch down and die (See the crouching neuron in Figure 1.5). In their NetLogo model, they added a new parameter, “signal-range-amplified” that specified the rate at which the signal was amplified as astrocytes blocked the injury site.

## Discussion

In this paper, we analyzed  an informal learning environment in which youth, sc ientists and choreographers collaboratively engaged in intermodal inquiry using choreographic and NetLogo modeling to examine the scientist partner’s research topic. We argued that through th eir collaborative work, the group developed what we  call a fused representation —a distributed model that included an interconnected  choreographic and digital model to represent a scientific idea. Perhaps more important ly, we illustrated that t he participants themselves saw and shared their work as including two interlinked moda lities to represent a scientific system. We also sh owed how attending to relationships between their choreograp hic and NetLogo models raised new questions that ul timately enabled the group to refine their models and further their collaborative scientific sense-making. We see fused representations and this work more generally as pushing the field’s understanding of what computation and choreography can be, and the role each can play in the context of the other. In particular, we see the physical work that the group engaged in as bein g the bridge between their choreographic and their NetLogo modeling. Each modality readily lent itself to being represented physically (e.g., “playing turtle” to think through a NetLogo model’s rules in inquiry episode 1, and e nacting their choreographic model in inquiry episod e 2). Leveraging that bridge served as a resource for the  group to iteratively refine and interrelate models  across both modalities for scientific sense-making. It is noteworthy that the youth themselves saw their fused representation as a pedagogical tool and saw the two modalities as  offering distinct, but related, ways of engaging i n inquiry (e.g., Figure 1.0 & Brandi’s interview reflections). This builds on existing literature that has demon strated how embodied modeling can be an important resource in a n ecology of multimodal models in classrooms (Piers on & Brady, 2020). In particular, this case highlights h ow embodiment played a critical role in disrupting assumed hierarchies of experience and expertise as physical  ideas that fueled this group’s intergenerational inqu iry were initiated by both adults (Figure 2) and youth participants (Briah’s question). Moreover, comparing and contrasting their work across both modalities enabled the group to further their inquiry and sense-making as they iteratively refined their fused repr esentation. This finding is resonant with literature in scientific modeling in whi ch developing “interlocking” models supports modeli ng practice and conceptual sense-making (Manz & Georgen, 2023). In addition, juxtapo sing these two modalities seeded the beginnings of identifying non-deterministic outcomes in this system (inquiry epis odes 2.1 and 2.2) which has been found to be challenging for learners (Jacobson, 2001). Having access to the two modalities enable d participants to consider multiple possibilities for the agents in the system. Ultimately, our aim is that this work opens up opportunities for scientific modeling for all youth. We do not take for granted that this was a space where wa lking to demonstrate an idea (Figure 3) or running out of movement (Inquiry episode 2) were understood as gen erative provocations for the group’s scientific thi nking. Going forward, we are closely examining how the des ign features of this camp provided opportunities for young girls of color to express their ideas, verbally and  physically, and supported their peers and a scientist partner to notice these ideas as scientifically meaningful. We  see this intermodal inquiry and the generation of fused representations as a form of expansive modeling (Sc hwarz et al., 2022; Wagh et al., 2021) in which mul tiple modes of representations including embodied and arti stic resources are leveraged for scientific sense-making for all learners.
