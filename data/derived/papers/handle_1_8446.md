# Use of Crosscutting Concepts as Epistemic Heuristics

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Drawing from analyses of teaching and learning and a framework for epistemic thinking, I use four levels of epistemic thinking (practical, epistemic cognition, epistemic metacognitive knowledge, epistemic metacognitive strategy) and an implicit-explicit dimension to distinguish among uses of and supports for the crosscutting concepts. This work has implications for the design of learning environments that can promote students’ metacognition about the crosscutting concepts.

The issue addressed One critical aspect of engaging all students in sci ence learning is to prepare them to guide their own  scientific intellectual work (Nordine et al., 2021). Metacognition refers to students’ awareness and understanding of their own thought processes (New Oxford Americandictiona ry). Epistemic heuristics are rules for guiding one 's scientific work (Knorr Cetina, 1999; Krist et al., 2019), such as the tacit rules that scientists use to develop explanations for how and why phenomena happen (i.e., Anderson, 2018). However, the epistemic heuristics that guide this work in school science have generally not been made clear to students, which prevents many students from developing metacognition. The crosscutting concepts (CCCs) in the Next Generation Science Standards can be interpreted as a representation of these epistemic  heuristics (Anderson, 2018; Krist et al., 2019; Ri vet et al.,

2016) and there is agreement that the CCCs should be made clear to students, but there is a lack of consensus on

how and why to do so (Miller & Shelton, 2021; Nordine et al., 2021). I present an equity argument to explain why it is necessary to make CCCs clear to students and a two-dimensional model (implicit-explicit, practic alepistemic) with four levels of epistemic thinking to describe how to make the CCCs clear to students.

## Potential significance of the work

A lack of clear epistemic heuristics can prevent me mbers of marginalized communities from fully engagi ng in science (Cooper, 2020), which is an equity issue. T here is a need for clear teaching of epistemic CCC use so students can move toward self-regulation and direct their own scientific sensemaking. Teaching and learning that provides all students opportunities to learn how to use the epistemic heuristics will increase equity (Cooper, 2020; Fulmer et al., 2018; Nordine et al., 2021). Fick et al., (2020) introduced axes of support (practical-epistemic and implicit-explicit) with four categories of CCC supp orts. In this paper, a three-level epistemic thinking mod el describes three subcategories of epistemic CCC uses  that are learning goals. Each subcategory of CCC u se corresponds to specific instructional supports. Thi s new two-dimensional model provides a more detaile d framework for improving our understanding of the nuances of CCC use and how to provide instructional supports. Epistemic thinking and epistemic heuristics In general, thinking includes both the use of mental processes (i.e., cognition) and the knowledge and regulation of one's own mental processes (i.e., metacognition;  Barzilai & Zohar, 2016). In the context of science  teaching and learning, epistemic means related to the development of scientific know ledge. Epistemic thinking encompasses epistemic cognition and epistemic metacognition (Barzilai & Zohar, 2016). Epistemic cognition is a students’ ability to use a mental process to deve lop scientific knowledge, while epistemic metacogni tion is a students’ ability to monitor and control the use of a mental process to develop scientific knowledge. Epistemic heuristics are mental processes for guiding scientific intell ectual work (Knorr Cetina, 1999; Krist et al., 2019), such as the unwritten rules th at scientists use to develop explanations for how a nd why phenomena happen (i.e., Anderson, 2018). The crossc utting concepts (CCCs) in the Next Generation Scien ce Standards are statements of these epistemic heuristics (Krist et al., 2019; Rivet et al., 2016). In this paper, the term CCCs refers to their use as epistemic heuristics, w hich is important because how the CCCs are presente d in instruction may result in non-epistemic use by students (Andersen, 2021; Fick et al., 2020). For example, students may look for patterns without knowing how or why they are doing so, which is an example of non-epistemic use. On the other hand, students can be supported to know how to use the patterns CCC, why to use the patterns CCC, and how to self-regulate the use of the patterns CCC, which are different aspects of epistemic use. Describing CCC use with levels of epistemic thinking A two-dimensional model (implicit-explicit, practical-epistemic) with three levels of epistemic thinking can help science educators distinguish among different kinds of CCC use and provide appropriate instructional s upports. The practical– epistemic axis was described as a continuum, but specific criteria to meaningfully compare and contrast levels of epistemic CCC supports on the continuum were not provided. I have modified the practical-epistemic axis using a practical-epistemic continuum with four levels—pr actical thinking, epistemic cognition, epistemic metacognitive knowledge, and epistemic metacognitiv e skill—to resolve the issue. In addition to provid ing defined levels of epistemic supports, this model also uses a modified definition of explicit. The definition Fick et al. (2020) used overlaps with the extant definition  for epistemic thinking (i.e., Barzilai & Zohar, 20 16), which made the two axes interdependent. The new model uses a revised definition that removes the overlap and improves alignment with the educational psychology literature. These modifications are explained in more detail in the next section.

Defining the implicit-explicit and practical-epistemic continua Figure 1 is the new two-dimensional model of CCC us e. The vertical axis represents the implicit-explic it dimension. Implicit use of a CCC is use without knowing you are using that pa rticular CCC (Fick et al., 2020), such as when students use their intuitive ideas about CCCs (e.g., Goggins et al., 2021). However, in contrast with the prior definition of explicit as "including supports for students to know how and why to use the CCC..." (Fick et al., 2020, p. 854), I define explicit as including supports for knowing that you are using a particular CCC, removing the how and why component because the defined levels of epistemic thinking (e.g., Barzilai & Zohar,

2016) already account for that component. The lowest level of epistemic thinking, practical thinking, is use of a

CCC based on intuition. The other three epistemic thinking levels make important distinctions between epistemic cognition and two levels of metacognitive thinking.  Epistemic cognition refers to knowing how to use a CCC, while metacognitive knowledge refers to knowing why to use a CCC. In this way, how and why to use a CCC are addressed by epistemic cognition and metacognitive knowledge. Metacognitive skills are the ability to regulate one's own use of the CCCs (Barzilai & Zohar, 2016) and this highest level extends the Fick et al. (2020) practicalepistemic continuum to support students' agency and the engagement with multiple CCCs. Providing all students with greater opportunity to learn why they to use specific CCCs and develop self-regulation is an equity strategy because typic al instruction has denied such opportunities to stu dents from underrepresented groups (Cooper, 2020).

## Discussion: Interpreting the continua

Based on document analyses of a curriculum unit using the two-dimensional model (Andersen, 2022), I identified examples of the levels of epistemic thinking. Practical thinking is when students use their personal r esources to access CCCs in science. One support for practical thinking with the cause-and-effect CCC was Say, now that we have recorded our first set of data, we  have to wait. What do you think will happen to the different garbage materials over time? Why do you think that? Have students talk in partners about their predictions. Then tell students to write their predictions and reasons for their predictions... (NYU SAIL, 2019, Lesson 2-1 p. 11) In this example, the teacher is asking students about their ideas for what will happen and why, which is a practical thinking support for students to use the cause-and-effect CCC in their own practical thinking. Practical thinking is an opportunity for students to use their home-ba sed resources in science learning, which can increa se equity (Goggins et al., 2021). Later in the unit, we see practical thinking support for the same CCC that is made explicit by calling out the CCC.

How will you show what causes changes in the landfill bottles? Also, remind stud ents about the process of microbes, In our model, it is import ant to think about the process. The cause is microbes. The effect is decomposition of food materials. (NYU SAIL, 2019, Lesson 4-2 p. 11) Building directly on the knowledge that all student s bring to school helps to build science identity a nd increase engagement in science for students from underrepres ented groups, which is an equity strategy (Goggins et al., 2021). In this next example, we see epistemic cognition support for the systems and system models CCC. What we are doing today is different than a drawing you might do in art class. In art class, we might focus on making the drawing exactly like what  we see. In science, we focus on details that are important to our investigation. We use symbols and a key to represent components of the system. For example, in art class we would draw a real lo oking banana, but in science we could use a symbol of a circle to represent a banan a and a symbol of a square to represent the aluminum foil. (NYU SAIL, 2019, Lesson 2-1 p. 11) This example shows support for students to know how  to use the CCC as they identify the system compone nts (epistemic cognition). Andersen (2021) identified 290 examples of CCC supports through document analysis of the NYU SAIL (2019) curriculum, including supports for all four levels of epistemic thinking (practical, epistemic cognition, epistemic metacognitive knowledge, epistemic metacognitive strategies). These supports strategically facilitate learning experiences, or metacognitive experiences, in the curriculum including discussion that describes the goals, concepts, and processes that scientists use to produce knowledge (Chinn et al., 2020), such as the examples provided above. In other words, the te achers' guide included many explicit supports that teachers could use to facilitate students' epistemic use of the CCCs (Andersen, 2021), which is different from the findings of similar studies of teaching and learning that showed mostly implicit supports (e.g., Fick et al., 2021; Markwordt et al., 2022). Providing explicit supports that fac ilitate students' epistemic thinking broadens students' access to science and can make science teaching more equitable (Cooper, 2020; Goggins et al., 2021). ## Conclusion

Instructional supports can provide metacognitive experiences for the CCC uses represented by the eight regions in Figure 1. These supports are needed to help stud ents move from practical use to epistemic cognition  and eventually to metacognitive skill. Equitable science curriculum must provide intentional, strategic supports that allow all students to develop their epistemic thinking abilities, rather than relying on students to understand and internalize tacit norms, which has historically yie lded inequitable results (Goggins et al., 2021). Ep istemic thinking should begin with practical use of CCCs, t hen build to three levels of epistemic thinking—epi stemic cognition, metacognitive knowledge, and metacogniti ve skills. I hypothesize that students epistemic th inking moves through the epistemic thinking levels as they engage in learning experiences, or metacognitive experiences, that require them to use CCCs at corresponding leve ls of epistemic thinking. Those metacognitive exper iences are facilitated by teachers via curricular supports. Metacognitive experiences are relevant learning activities that develop the elements of epistemic thinking (Barzilai & Zohar, 2016). The eight distinct types of CCC use (Figure

1) suggest directions for future research to examin e how curriculum and teaching can strategically and

systematically support students' emerging use of the CCCs as epistemic heuristics. This work has implications for equitable science teaching and learning because without intentional supports many students will not take up these epistemic heuristics, particularly those who are members of marginalized groups.
