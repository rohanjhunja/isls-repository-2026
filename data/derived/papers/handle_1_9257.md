# Supporting Collaborative Online Science Education With a Transferable and Configurable Conversational Agent

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teachers in online science laboratories frequently struggle to provide timely discussion support to different groups. Collaborative conversational agents have been proposed to bridge this gap and foster productive talk in small-group discussions. Current implementations are usually highly configurable, but rather limited when modeling users’ intent in a transferable way. In this paper, we propose a new conversational agent that supports productive talk while addressing these requirements. Also, we discuss lessons learned from a pilot study in a science classroom with secondary students. Findings illustrate the agent’s potential to support productive talk among students during online science lab activities.

## Introduction

In online science labs, where students can interact with complex ideas experimentally, the use of chats during the activity is particularly diverse, sometimes productive and engaging but other time s also unproductive and distracting. The teacher can visit chats and provide guidance to each group individua lly. Unfortunately, this process is very time-consuming, and in many cases, teachers do not guide the dialogues at all. As a consequence of the lack of support, the quality of chats greatly varies. In some cases, for  example, the more verbal students dominate all the main contributions, or the students barely build on each other’s contributions. To fill in these gaps, conversational agents (CAs) have been regarded as a means to scale personalized feedback in collaborative learning (Sikström et al., 2022). In the last decade, several CAs have been grounded on Academically Productive Talk (APT), a framework originated from resear ch on classroom discussion practices and norms (Michaels and O’Connor, 2015). It comprises a set of guidelines and suggesti ons for “talk moves” to promote learning during classroom dialogues, for example, by fostering students' dis cursive engagement (Chen et al., 2020; Adamson et al., 2014).

To trigger such talk moves, most current collaborative CA implementations use vocabulary-based rules based on keywords and synonyms. With the main goal of supporting student discussions, these CAs usually intervene to make students explain the task’s concepts and their associations, expressed as a concept map (Tegos et al., 2017), or epistemic network (Nguyen, 2022). Unfortunately, CAs that rely only on vocabulary-based rules can be quite limited in terms of natural language understanding, which can cause some conversational breakdowns (Nguyen, 2022). The users' intent is rarely represented in order to decide on a n intervention. Further, a CA configured to one topic and language cannot improve its behavior based on chat data from  another topic and language. In other words, these CAs are not designed for transferability. In spi te of that, vocabulary-based rules have high configurability specially when compared to machine learning-based genera tive approaches. Practitioners can more easily determine what the CA should say and when, whi ch is highly demanded in educational settings. Here, we consider both transferability and configurability as key technical requirements that should walk together when designing CAs that aim to foster productive discussions in small-group settings.

In this paper, we propose a new CA that has its goal to stimulate productive talk in collaborative settings, named Clair, and describe a preliminary evaluation of our prototype in secondary c lassrooms. Clair’s triggering mechanism is described, and we briefly explain our approach to address such requirements. To assess the effects of Clair, we look at four initial outcome measures representing different a spect of productive talk as well as the students' feedback on their experience. Understanding the strengths and weaknesse s of our prototype provides insight to guide future improvements.

Collaborative learning agent for interactive reasoning (Clair) Some of the main challenges to developing effective CAs for collaborative learning include addressing "what to say" and "when to say" (Kumar and Rosé, 2010). A popular approach to the former  question is to rely on APT and define a set of talk moves that can be theorized as relevant to the CA's goal. Deciding "when to say," is where Clair’s design might contribute to existing approaches by addressing transfer ability and configurability at the same time. In this section, we provide some details on what interventions ar e performed by Clair, its triggering mechanism, and how we implemented our initial prototype. To extract quantitative metrics that help trigger Clair, we designed a set of dialogue variables (see Table 2), which can be interpreted as sensors for individual messages. To parse the message’s focus and intent, we trained multilingual machine learning classifiers to automatically code chat mes sages based on the scheme from Eshuis et al. (2019). In terms of transferability, we empirically found (de Araujo et al., 2023) that they work quite reliably in the topics of circuits and radiation and languages Dutch and Portugues e. Combined, these variables represent fruitful conditions for Clair to intervene, but extra variables can be considered in a future version Clair requires two levels of configuration. The first and higher level configuration is done by practitioners which consists of providing a simple list of topic keywords. The second level involves setting up rules and is not mandatory for teachers to fill out. It requires some knowledge about Clair’s dialogue variables. Because the dialogue variables can be considered transferable, the same default rul e set can apply for different topics and languages, without necessarily needing the teacher to set it up every time. To decide on "when to say," we designed a configurable algorithm using fuzzy logic rules to associate the dialogue variables (input) with the talk moves (output). The fuzzy inputs are modeled with three membership CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 418 functions to define their individual levels of “high”, “medium”, and “low”, extra cted using K-Means (k = 3) on past data. The fuzzy outputs are modeled with two membership functions, for “active” and “not active”. The fuzzy rules express a condition-intervention relationship that humans can interpret, operate, and draw logical connections. For example, the rule "if DOM is high and TSIM is high and TACC  is low then EXPAND_REASONING is active" translates into a condition where the message is focused on the domain, uses topic keywords, and the speaker has mentioned topic keywords much less often tha n the partner. This condition would be used to trigger, for example, “Could you elaborate more on this?”. To build the initial set of rules, we surveyed sixteen teachers. The majo rity of them use online science labs to teach. Teachers had to suggest rules (as exemplified earlier) that c ould potentially be relevant for each of the talk moves and provide an explanation of what they mean with the condition provided. We parsed and refined the results into distinguishable rules such that a higher number of talk moves is triggerable. Expert evaluation Still, defining effective rules and pre-assessing the system on a given topic and language require humans to be in the loop. Using data from students’ chats that we previously collected (without Clair), we generated the hypothetical interventions that Clair would have used. We then randomly selected three c hats and interviewed three teachers on whether they thought each intervention would have been appropriate. As a result, teachers mostly focused on issues as repetitiveness, bad timing, and the phrasing, which was some times considered too formal. Their feedback was incorporated into the design, but not all issues were completely solved. Pilot study The pilot study took place in a school in southwestern Brazil within the science curriculum of secondary students. In the classroom, each student on a laptop, dyads worked during four online lessons on Go-Lab (https://golabz.eu/) on an activity about the effects of light and temperature on photosynthesis. Stude nts had prior knowledge about the topic, and they had to solve an inquiry question collaboratively. The teacher was responsible for ensuring that the dyads' communication happened only through chat.

To assess Clair, we designed a within-subjects experiment to compare groups without Clair (control) and with Clair (treatment). The experiment had two phases. In the first phase (Lessons 1 and 2), students were able to chat with one another, but Clair was not active, such that we can control for spontaneous improvement in the discussions over time. With a small sample size, we needed to ensure we had comparable experimental conditions. Thus, after the first two lessons, dialogues were ranked on how many times C lair would have hypothetically intervened. Dyads were alternately assigned to one of the conditions. In the second phase (Lessons 3 and 4), the treatment groups had Clair. At the end, a user satisfaction questionnaire (5-point Likert scale) was administered. Only the students who participated in all lessons were considered for further analysis, resulting in 19 dyads. Analysis To assess Clair’s performance in making student discussions more productive, we have conducted a preliminary analysis on four outcome measures described below.

● Explicit reasoning measures the group’s percentage of messages coded as DOM and AR simultaneously.

When students are explicitly stating their reasoning when presenting ideas,  they enable clearer communication and understanding between each other.

● Off-task behavior, which measures the group’s percentage of messages that were coded as  OFF. When students engage in behavior that is not directly related to the topic or goal of the discussion, they are distracted from the main task.

● Topic similarity measures how often the group sent messages with TSIM as “medium” or higher. When students use keywords in their messages, they actively engage on the topic at hand. ● Participation imbalance, which measures the students’ difference in their proportion of contributions with medium/high TSIM. When the participation imbalance is high (e.g., student 1: 70%; student 2: 30%; difference: 40%) it is an indication that one student may be dominating the conversation. The outcomes are displayed in Figure 2. Non-parametric analysis of covariance (Quade’s test) showed no significant difference (p >.05) between the groups in Phase 2, using the outcom es in Phase 1 as a covariate. Nevertheless, Wilcoxon’s Signed Rank tests showed significant differences between phases in favor of the treatment condition for explicit reasoning (Z = 40, p =.038) and participation imbalance (Z = 1, p =.017), and a trend for off-task behavior (Z = 7, p =.066), suggesting that explicit reasoning increased, while participation imbalance and (potentially) off-task behavior decreased when Clair was present. When analyzing the chats, we noticed that on average, Clair intervened only six times and with three talk moves. Sometimes, Clair used two talk moves in an alternative order, so students found it quite monotonous and ignored interventions. By looking at chats individually, we noticed that the rules could have been better designed. The results from the user satisfaction questionnaire revealed that students  found Clair repetitive (M = 4.0) and robotic (M = 3.8), but still friendly (M = 3.9). When asked for improvement suggestions, students mostly focused on asking for features that Cl air is not currently designed to handle, e.g., “Clair, what should I do?”, “Clair, is my answer correct?” and were frustrated with a standard response that Clair provided (“Sorry I am not able to handle this question.”).

## Discussion and conclusion

In online science labs, teachers often struggle to provide timely dialogue support to multiple groups. To fill in this gap, CAs have been proposed to support productive talk among students. As we mentioned, current approaches can be quite limited in handling natural language understanding. In this paper, we first proposed Clair to address the technical requirements of transferability and configurability which are key to high usability standards. Then, in a pilot study, we assessed Clair’s performance and also feedback from students. Even though more evidence is still needed, future implementations of Clair could potentially benefit productive dis cussions. Clair had limitations, being quite repetitive and not using all the talk moves available, whi ch will be addressed in future studies. Furthermore, we believe future studies should pursue a more compre hensive analysis framework, e.g., considering principles of productive classroom dialogues and transactivity.
