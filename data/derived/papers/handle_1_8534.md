# Development of Students’ Science Identities in a Biology Classroom: A Learning Community Approach

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper describes the objectives, theoretical framework, design and outcomes of a research study conducted with 55 students in a Grade 11 Biology classroom in a diverse urban school in Ontario, Canada. Students worked as a learning community to explore and discuss authentic case studies and datasets, evaluate the credibility of resources, participate in scientific argumentation, and explore science-related careers. The year-long curriculum, co-designed with a teacher and guided by a theoretical model of Knowledge Community and Inquiry (KCI), engaged students in co-constructing a collective knowledge base, supported by technology environments, where students made connections across course topics, connected to real-world issues, and explored science careers. Through the Career Exploration activity, students learned about their peers’ interests and became more knowledgeable about various careers; through their discussions, students were motivated to further explore their own science identities. Keywords: learning community, collaborative learning, science identity

Keywords: learning community, collaborative learning, science identity Objectives A substantial research literature has addressed the benefits of a learning community approach, such as developing and advancing a shared collective knowledge base (B ereiter & Scardamalia, 2014; Slotta & Najafi, 2013)  and supporting inquiry-oriented discourse (Fong & Slott a, 2018). This approach emerges from the Fostering Communities of Learners model of a community in which students use real scenarios and are supported by deep disciplinary content, and where students share expertise with their peers (Brown, 1997). In a science classroom, students are engaged with their peers in a model of  a scientific community (Bielaczyc & Collins, 1999). Contributing to a shared and co-edited knowledge ba se, with a support of a technology environment, is fundamental for a learning community described in this paper (Fong & Slotta, 2013; Slotta & Najafi, 2018). By engaging in a model of a scientific learning commun ity, students develop collaborative skills, create new ideas and learning opportunities for their peers (Song, e t al., 2010). Through conversations during group wo rk and participation in authentic scientific activities, s tudents develop their science identities, including  specific topic interests, and potential career directions (Thoman et al., 2007). This study examines the context of a  scientific classroom learning community, and how students’ interactions with peers, materials and activities can foster the development of career identity. Our main research question: “How does learning in a collaborative environment supported by the KCI Model and technology contributes to the development of students’ career identities?“ Theoretical foundation The notion of identity is described as an individual’s sense of self within a group or a content, developed through interactions with others, which is evolving and can be regulated by culture and context (Leary & Tangney, 2012; Roeser et al., 2006). It has been shown that social context can improve an individual’s motivation -- not only by providing support, but also contributing to an individual’s interests (Thoman et al., 2007). Under the interactionist approach (Kim & Sinatra, 2018), it is the interaction between a student and the context that encourages or restricts the student’s formation of science identity. Others  suggest that interpersonal processes influence the  selfregulation of motivation (Thoman et al., 2007). For example, if a student feels that other classmates are interested in a science subject, they may also express a highe r interest – leading to the notion of an interest q uorum to be established (Nazari et al., 2017). The role of conversations has also been studied as a means of gaining value about topics, where students can socially verify and build their perception of an activity as interesting and valued (Nolen, 2001). Moreover, in collaborative activities, students jointly develop their understanding of the topic and achieve shared representations of it (Clark, 1996). Talking  with others about one’s educational interests and receiving social recognition during these conversations have been shown to increase interest in science careers, even more significant for women than men, in particular for women with a lower science identity (Jackson et al., 2018).

## Method

Our study explored how students’ interactions withi n a classroom learning community, including authent ic inquiry tasks, can impact students’ science identity formation. For the development of this year-long curriculum, ICLS2022 Proceedings 1541 © ISLS design-based research (DBR) methodology was applied: the curriculum was co-designed with a teacher of a Grade 11 Biology class consisting of three cohorts, total ing 55 students. Students worked as a class, in gro ups, and individually to explore case studies and datasets, evaluate the credibility of resources, participate in scientific argumentation, reflect in personal journals, and explore science-related careers. We applied the theoretical model of Knowledge Community and Inquiry (KCI), in which students construct a collective knowledge base and engage in scaffolded inquiry activities, with accessible l earning outcomes (authors, 2018). Throughout the se mester, students contributed their ideas to the emergent collective knowledge base and built on their previous ideas. Through the course, students explored primary and secondary resources (i.e., scientific journal articles) concerned with the topics of the five units of the curriculum: Genetics, Biodiversity, Evolution, Animal Systems, and Plants. We also added a cross-cutting diet and nutrition theme. Guided by the KCI model, a series of inquiry activities was designed, using a variety of technol ogy environments and materials to support students’ collaborative work. Among those activities, we designed a Career Exploration activity where students collectively built knowledge about and connections amongst a wid e range of science-related professions. Exploration  of science careers was a focus of at least one lesson in each unit, in which students contributed to the community Career Folder (on Google Drive), collaboratively editing Google Docs and adding details to the careers posted by other students and/or adding a document with a new career. Up to six students from three cohorts could contribute to each career page. If more than six students wanted to contribute to the career, they were encouraged to create another document with the same career. Working in g roups and discussing their choices, students gradua lly developed the Career Knowledge Base, which served a s a platform for further discussions and personal exploration of potential science careers and their connections to other careers. While current study is completed, we are planning its iteration and we are also aware of the study limitations. The DBR is a complex process which includes many interconnected elements. Brown (1992) described the difficulty of conducting design experiments in situ, rather than in the controlled conditions w here the designer has control over the task and oth er variables. When using the DBR approach, authors should provide descriptions of the contexts, theoretical framework, details of the design, and their impact on participation (B arab & Squire, 2004), included in this publication.  Besides, authentic learning contexts are unique to each classroom, which is why findings of design-based studies may have a limited generalizability, but offer insights for other researchers (Fong & Slotta, 2018). Data Sources Data included tables, documents and personal journals created by students individually, in groups and as a class. Co-constructed tables and documents to which studen ts contributed their responses represent a collecti ve knowledge base built during the school year. In the  Career Activity, students built a Career Knowledge  Base which served as a platform for further discussions and career explorations by students. Students researched and added to the career description, made connections to other professions, explored university/college programs that would support the career, researched the level of d emand for each career and the starting salary, and added interesting videos and articles. They also discusse d how various careers related to each of the curric ular unit. Students’ answers to science identity questionnaires were also collected. The questionnaires asked thr ee central questions: 1. What are some interesting career directions that were connected to this course, which you may not have considered before? 2.What is your current thin king about a career? Was it influenced by anything you encountered in this course, and if so, how? 3. Shou ld teachers make a stronger emphasis on career conn ections within their courses? How can we help students keep thinking about their career?

## Findings

Through exploring different careers and contributin g to the class Career Knowledge Base, students buil t their understandings of various careers in science and of  their own science identities. In their Career Page s (23 documents created), students identified connections to almost 100 different professions. The top three careers of interest were Neurologist (7 students contributed),  Oral Surgeon (6 students), and Forensic Scientist and DNA Analyst/ Pharmaceutical Chemist (5 students per each). The maximum number of words describing a career in a collaboratively created document was 1432 (the career of Neurologist). Students connected their careers of interest to many other careers (e.g., the career of Epidemiologist was connected to 19 other careers). Some of the career connections are shown in Figure 1. Also, 60 links t o various resources related to future careers were added by students. Pages on less prominent professions, such as astrobiologist, myrmecologist and genetic counsellor, were also created.

The Career Exploration activity helped students gai n insights into specific career interests or discov er careers of which they had not been previously aware. They also became informed about qualifications re quired to pursue certain careers. In the process of the analysis of students’ responses to the questionnaire, several main themes were revealed (Table 1). Selected students’ quotes regarding their participation in the activity are provided ICLS2022 Proceedings 1542 © ISLS in Table 2. The Career Exploration activity, including collective knowledge work and discussions about careers, was emphasized by a number of students as helping t hem see connections between different science field s, industry and other fields, such as business or law,  and become interested in exploring various careers. Students indicated that the section where they explicated college and university pathways for each science career was very valuable. At the end of the study, some students st ill had questions about careers, such as about work load for certain careers, career integration and job market,  changing career, and how technology will affect fu ture jobs. Collaborating with each other as a classroom learni ng community, through the exploration and recogniti on of their peers’ interests, social verification and building their perception of the activity as valued allowed students to develop their interest in the topics, which is beli eved to be a primary driver of science identity (Ma ltese & Tai, 2010).

## Scientific and scholarly significance

This study demonstrates the potential of a learning community approach achieved through a collaborative student work by progressively adding to and making use of a  collective knowledge base, through discussions in groups and as a class, in helping students to start develo ping interest to and knowledge of many science care ers, and contributing to the formation of their science iden tities. This study contributes to the literature on  the factors influencing the development of science identities a nd will be of interest to a broader educational com munity. Moving forward, we are interested in deepening our understanding of identity formation. Future work will explore the notion of Funds of Identity, and the various forms of intersections that may occur within a classroom learning community.
