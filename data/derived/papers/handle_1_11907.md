# Paired Students’ Sequential Actions Related to Their Productivity in a Computer-Based Collaborative Problem Solving Task

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigated paired students’ sequential actions and their relation to the productivity of collaborative problem solving (CPS). Logfile data from 166 Finnish sixth graders working in pairs to solve a CPS task in a computer-based assessment environment showed that continuous 2-and 3-action of chatting in sequences were likely to be key action sequences for productive CPS. However, continuous 6-action of chats and chats with other mouse clicking actions contributed to unproductive CPS.

## Background and aims

Our digitalized society increasingly requires learn ers to solve problems in various collaborative cont exts. Thus, collaborative problem solving (CPS) is widely recog nized in educational curricula globally as one of t he crucial 21st century skills to be mastered (Care et al., 2015). Computer-based environments for assessing CPS have been increasingly utilized in educational settings in recent years (e.g., Li et al., 2022). Such environments generate huge amount of time-stamped logfile  data documenting participants’ complete sequences of actions conducted (including clicking buttons, dragging objects, typing texts to chat) in the environment (Stadler et al., 2019). Similar to textual data, action sequences generated from logfiles data from computer-based assessment environments can be decomposed into n-grams (He & von Davier, 2015). For instance, a bigram is an individual bag of 2-action (i.e., two continuous actions in an action s equence representing a distinctive attribute). Usi ng a sequential data mining approach of n-grams, previous studies have uncovered patterns of individual participants’ action sequences to distinguish successful or unsuc cessful outcomes in computer-based problem solving  (e.g., Stadler et al., 2019, Ludwig et al., 2024).

To extend the individual problem solving to CPS  where participants work in pairs, this study explored patterns of action sequences related to CPS productivity. We focused on attributes of continuous 2-, 3-, 4-, 5-, and 6-action in sequences conducted by paired students. The following research questions are addressed:

1. How does  paired students’ productive or unproductive  CPS differ in their action sequences  in a

computer-based CPS task?

2. What are the most important paired students’ action sequences contributing to their productive CPS?

## Methods

Data were collected from 166 sixth-grade students (Age: M = 12.6, SD = 0.33; females: 91, 54.82%) in a Finnish urban area in 2019. The computer-based assessment environment developed in the ATC21 S project at the University of Melbourne was utilized. Four game-like tasks (Laughing Clown, Sunflower, Hot Chocolat e and Olive Oil, details see Care et al., 2015) were administered to paired students. Paired students’ CPS productivity was categorized based on individual students’ social and cognitive skill levels of CPS that were automatically calculated and extracted from the assessment environment (detail algorithms see Adams et al., 2015) based on CPS theoretical framework of Hesse et al. (2015). Pairs including at least one student with high social and high cognitive skills of CPS were categorized as productive in CPS (n = 32 pairs), otherwise unproductive (n = 51 pairs, details for the justification of categorizing productive and unproductive pairs see Li et al., 2022).

Logfile data of the Olive Oil task was utilized for sequential data mining (i.e., n-grams extraction, chisquare feature selection modeling with extracted n-grams and random forest tree modeling, see He & von Davier [2015] and Ludwig et al. [2024]) because the Olive Oil task is asymmetric (i.e., students in a pair have access to different resources on their computer screens). This resource interdependency ensures that the sequential process to solve the task is consisted of actions conducted  by both students in a pair. In the Olive Oil task, a ll 83 paired students conducted distinct 78 actions including clicking buttons, dragging to move object s and typing texts for communication (the contents of the texts typed were  excluded in the analysis). We extracted action sequences with continuous 2-, 3-, 4-, 5-, and 6-action (i.e., bigrams, trigrams, 4-, 5-, and 6-grams). Altogether there were 68 bigrams, 71 trigrams, 56 four-grams, 39 five-grams and 31 six-grams. After the extraction, chi-square feature selection modeling was applied with n-grams to find out different action sequences in paired students’ productive and unproductive CPS (RQ1). Random forest tree modeling  with 10-fold cross-validation was then applied with 20 selected n-grams with 70% data for the training set and 30% for the test set to identify most important action sequences predicting productive pairs in CPS (RQ2).

## Results

Different action sequences in productive and unproductive CPS (RQ1) Results showed that the occurrence of chatting in continuous 2-, 3-, 4-and 5-action in the Olive Oil task appeared to be the key for paired students’ productive CPS. Yet, the occurrenc e of chatting in continuous 6-action can be discriminative for either productive or unproductiv e CPS. Interestingly, continuous chat actions in se quences of 2-, 3-action were related to productive CPS but continuous chats in sequences of 6-action were related  to unproductive CPS.

## Discussion

Our findings are in line with the previous studies that interaction with paired partners by typing in chat windows is positive for paired students’ productivity in CPS (Li et al., 2022). Yet, the present study further finds out that continuous 6 chatting actions  are negative related to paired students’ CPS produc tivity. This is an important finding that efficient interaction with relatively short sequences of chatting with paired partners is often beneficial for the CPS productivity, but not the chats with long sequences. Thus, teachers should emphasize the importance of chats to students prior to the computer-based CPS tasks and offer personalized support when  paired students’ chats are noticed in long er sequences (e.g., 6 continuous chat actions).  Future studies could incorporate contents of chats into sequential analyses to understand mor e comprehensively about paired students’ action sequences in relation with their CPS outcomes.
