# Fostering Educational Intimacy: ILSSA Intergenerational Partnerships for Purposeful Community Building

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The International Learning Sciences Student Association (ILSSA) is the official committee of the International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS) for student and recent graduate scholars within the l earning sciences community. W e explore the importance of partnership and building community as ILSSA’s intergenerational leadership works toward a more just and equitable communal infrastructure. We write together to explore two questions: what does community mean to IL SSA, and how do we support that in ways that are equitable and just? We draw on our positionalities, definitions of community, and duoethnographic discussions of our past, present, and future initiatives using Uttamchandani’s (2020) educational intimacy as our theoretical lens. We position this work as our strategy toward creating more partnerships within the ILSSA board; connections with the communities we represent; embracing the political as inherent to learning processes; and establishing relational scholarly continuity for ILSSA. Our mission: Intergenerational partnerships for community As the official student, postdoc, and recent graduate committee of the International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS), the International Learning Sciences Student Association (ILSSA) is dedicated to supporting the intellectual, social, and professional development of those it serves in ISLS and across the field. ILSSA consists of a board of elected representatives of (primarily) students across the globe with varied areas of expertise. ILSSA functions to advocate for the interests of junior scholars at the society level, liaise between society leadership and general membership, and scaffold the professional, social, and intellectual development of learning scientists-in-training. In a recent board meeting, questions were raised around the association's current infrastructure for in-association communication –particularly whether our current (asynchronous, broadcast-style) communication platforms were accessible, relevant, and approp riate for the diverse lived experiences and needs of our learning sciences scholars-in-training. In short, we realized that ILSSA needed to question whether the means of our labor were congruent with the “ How,” “For What,” “For Whom,” “With Whom,” and “To what ends” (Philip et al., 2018) of ILSSA’s collective vision for early-scholars in the association. ILSSA’s mission statement operationalizes commitment through our charge and six consequent goals, of which one is to promote community, interdisciplinarity, and intellectual exchange among graduate students and postdoctoral scholars within the community. We extend on this goal, exploring how five generations of the ILSSA Board have defined and operationalized our commitment to community building and beyond as a method of establishing intergenerational partnerships. In this work, we investigate these ques tions of intergenerational discourses across five generations of ILSSA board leadership. We situate this symposium as both nuancedly 2424 connected to our conference’s theme, “Partnering with Purpose,” and timely, given the current US and global sociopolitical climate. If ever we were not convinced of the interconnectedness and centrality of politics within the work that we do as learning scientists and educational scholars (Phillip & Gupta, 2020; The Politics of Learning Writing Collective, 2017; Tivaringe & Kirshner, 2021; Uttamchandani et al., 2020; Learning Towards Justice Team, 2025; Vea, 2020), this work is being conducted in the midst of increased violence and erasure of minoritized bodies perpetuating a rhetoric of privilege, whiteness, patriarchy, and division. Many of our authors – the majority of whom reside in the United States – are experiencing the juxtaposition of this violence with a collective call for solidarity and community by those we represent. These juxtapositions prompt us to collectively investigate two critical questions: (1) What does community mean to ILSSA? —understanding the subjective connotations of community — and (2) How do we support community-building in ways that are equitable and just? Expansive literature demonstrates the subjectivity through which community is defined, as mediated by its intersections, including race, class, gender, geopolitics, and other critical lived experiences (Daniel et al., 2023; Dixon & Martin, 2017; Harris et al., 2019; Herrenkohl et al., 2019; Jadallah et al., 2024; Van Wart et al., 2019). We locate this subjectivity through Uttamchandani's notion of educational intimacy, as presented in the Journal of the Learning Sciences (2020). Framework: Embodying Uttamchandani’s educational intimacy Through research findings with Chroma, an LGBTQ+ interest and educational group, and the mediation of feminist and queer politics, Uttamchandani (2020) describes educational intimacy as “relationships that allow for inclusive and productive engagement in advocacy and learning while also mirroring desired future social configurations” (p. 52). He further notes the utility of this framework’s suitability, particularly toward the development and establishment of group-level goals for social transformation. Uttamchandani posits that educational intimacy’s presence within groups is operationalized when two criteria are met: when the group’s framework allows for respectful exchange amongst each other and disagreements are embraced toward transforming the group’s goals; and when relationships within the group demonstrate how group members would want to be treated within the context and community in which they are hoping to advocate. As we embrace this organizational dynamic within ILSSA’s context, we demonstrate educational intimacy through these two criteria and the three core tenets of how educational intimacy is evidenced within learning processes and discourses. Three core features denote the operationalization of educational intimacy within learning processes: prefigurations, feminist and queer politics, and relational activity; the politics and related discourses serve as the connection between these prefigurati ons and their consequent relational activity (Uttamchandani, personal communication, October 3, 2025). Uttamchandani locates prefigurations within the construct of prolepsis – capturing how anticipated desired futures drive present activity – distinguishing an emphasis on social movements within his definition of prefigurations. Here, prefigurations are organizational-level current activities toward achieving desired futures (Curnow, 2022; Uttamchandani, 2020). Relationality within this prefigurative context draws on the development and reorganization of relationships that support the desired futures for organizational forms of advocacy and change. While developed within the niche context of queer prefigurative and relational discourses among Chroma, Uttamchandani (2020) concludes his work by posing critical questions to the learning sciences, of which we specifically engage theoretically: “How is its [educational intimacy’s] character similar or different when different kinds of social change are the goal?” (p. 71). We embody the prefigurative using this symposium as the current activity that solicits desired ideal futures of ILSSA’s systematized intergenerational partnerships for community building. We mediate this prefiguration and desired future by embracing the current poli tical landscape that seeks to divide us, such as the travel incapabilities of many scholars regarding the 2025 and 2026 ICLS conferences. As border politics and fascism seek to divide us, we embrace this politics as a method of creating imagined futures for our intergenerational relationship and community building. This is our defiance and advocacy toward strengthening our organization and for ILSSA generations to come. Theoretical and practical contributions to LS and ILSSA communities We make important theoretical and practical contributions to both the learning sciences and ILSSA scholarly communities. Our first contribution is the theoretical innovation for learning sciences discourse, specifically advocating for broader applications of educational intimacy to apply to another group and social context. We borrow Uttamchandani's theoretical contribution, generated from a specific queered context, in order to demonstrate how educational intimacy can apply to other contexts concerned with advocacy and transformation. In our specific context, we engage with the notion of educational intimacy for social transformation towards strategic partnerships and community building. 2425 For our practical contributions to the ILSSA community, there are several goals. The first is to draw stronger connections between the board and its members, emphasizing the personhood and intimacy that brings each person to their respective roles as core representatives for the sub-bodies of learning sciences that each chair leads. The second goal is to reach into and connect more deeply and intimately with the communities that we represent, creating the space for us to experience this community, a sense o f safety, and belongingness. Thirdly, we aim to embrace the political as inherent to our work. This provides a framework for more strategic and intentional action in our initiatives, instead of ignoring what dictates and shapes the extent of our power and agency within our respective spaces. And finally, four, we aim to establish continuity of board writing initiatives as a scholarly representation of what brings us together to support this work (Lave & Wenger, 1991). These theoretical and practical implica tions, taken with a systematic approach to this work, provide an avenue for replicability and further applications of educational intimacy for groups and organizations that are concerned with the intersections of advocacy, social transformation, and partnerships for community building. Approach: Intergenerational collaborative duoethnographies As we explore the questions, what does community mean to ILSSA, and how do we support that in ways that are equitable and just, we employ a method of intergenerational collaborative autoethnography, specifically through duoethnographies. Burleigh and Brum (2022) define duoethnography as a juxtaposition of stories among two or more persons who have experienced a similar phenomenon. We tell these stories adopting educational intimacy through an intergenerational lens (Hernandez et al., 2015; Ropes, 2013), pos itioning our individual stories as located through shared experiences based on service in the same/similar roles across generations. We write together and share our stories through each leadership role of ILSSA, as a representation of intergenerational discourses. We each draw on our lived experiences, both personally and professionally, to inform our positionality in our respective roles and, more broadly, the learning sciences community. We then each define what community means to us, making explicit ties to the representative contexts and communities that we represent. We then expound on initiatives – particularly those working toward more just and equitable communal spaces –- through forms of educational intimacy as mediums of partnership among ourselves and within our broader, decentralized communities. Co-chairs: Leadership, advocacy, democracy, solidarity Morgan Vickery, John Louis-Strakes Lopez, Lisa Siciliano Students are the bottom of the academic food chain. We do the grunt labor that keeps the wheels of the academy turning while being paid in poverty-level wages under the mantra that “it will be worth it” eventually. Being an ‘academic-in-training’ in the learning sciences can be exhaustingly lonely at best and exploitatively dehumanizing at worst. Much of this work is reactive to the waves of hardship of those students who get siloed and assume their experiences are t oo niche to be empathized with. As co-chairs, we are not exceptions – our presence has been ignored and our work excluded from the learning sciences canon. Thus, instead of waiting for the gates to be opened, we open them ourselves. Our work as co-chairs designs against this future; we dream of community where these hardships are acknowledged, where insider knowledge is not gatekept from those who it is most consequential for; where institutional oppression can be named and fought against; where we can bask in our imposter syndrome; where we can learn to shamelessly promote the fruits of our labor, and where one can safely validate the ways we are not okay. This form of community requires labor beyond monthly meetings — it requires a politicized trust. ILSSA co-chairs wear many hats; we are performers, volunteers, managers, informants, translators, boundary-crossers, messengers, and advocates. We beg for labor that we have no means of compensating. We awake at 5am to reach members in multiple time zones. We bear witness to more-than-qualified soon-to-be-graduates facing unemployment. We join as a collective voice such that those with power take action beyond words. We formalize our own representation and democratize it. We ensure that student voices are present across the society’s committees. We are blessed-yet-cursed with insider information. We attempt to build mutual trust by yelling into an (often reactionless) digital void. All the while, we struggle to navigate the same system that we seek to educate our constituents around – and we do it anyway – because we know that community is what enables us to persist. It reminds us to care for the human at the heart of the research we dedicate years of our lives to discover. Lisa’s service (2023-2024) was marked by the creation of space that otherwise would not exist for those without representation in the rooms where decisions are made. Morgan’s (2024-2026) service can be defined by an insistent call for transparency around i nstitutional mechanisms and cultural norms that most impact junior scholars in the spirit of critical hope and ‘remembering the human’ behind every act of knowledge construction. 2426 John's (2025-present) service seeks to dismantle socially-configured walls and siloes toward a culture of being in-relation-with each other, focusing on improving initiatives such as the Mentorship Program, which he co-established with Golnoush (Lia) Haddadianduring his time as Membership Chair. Together, we represent, model, and sustain a humane space to safely be in-solidarity in the struggle. We form spaces of shared inquiry such that we create meaningful relationships between people who may otherwise b e socially positioned as diametrically-opposed. We construct a culture of humility, grace, and vulnerability, and hope our members carry on these values as they serve others. We put on our brave faces such that even one siloed student realizes they are not alone. Membership and outreach chair: Creating spaces of support and belonging Chaewon Kim Back in 2021, I was working as an elementary school teacher, deeply curious about how students learned from the games I designed, how I could make those games more engaging, and ultimately, how I could grow into a better educator beyond the role of a class room teacher. That curiosity, paired with a desire to explore broader questions of learning, led me to think about pursuing a PhD in the United States, although at the time I had no clear roadmap for how to make that aspiration possible. I quickly realized that my teaching experience alone would likely not be enough to secure admission into doctoral programs, so I knew I needed to engage in research. At that point, I had no awareness of ISLS, and even if I had, it would have seemed too prestigious and out of reach for someone like me. Instead, I began at the local level, presenting at regional conferences in South Korea, slowly building my confidence and my scholarly identity. One milestone I was so proud of at that point was presenting at a conference hosted at the University of Hawaii at Mānoa, where Korean, Chinese, Japanese, and Hawaiian educational researchers came together. That experience was transformative: I felt a sense of success and belonging that I had not yet experienced in academic spaces, and I believe it was instrumental in helping me gain admission to four PhD programs in the United States. These experiences really shaped how I see myself as a scholar because they showed me that academic communities can feel very different depending on how they are built. They can come across as distant and intimidating, or they can feel welcoming and empowering, and when I was serving as an outreach chair at ILSSA in my third year of my PhD program, I wanted ILSSA to be the latter. So when we discussed ILSSA’s mission to connect with scholars from East Asia, Africa, and Latin America, the first thing I thought of was that Hawaiian conference, which had been such a warm entry point for me into the larger learning sciences world. I imagined how meaningful it could be for students in those regions to discover ILSSA as something friendly and approachable, not distant or elite. With that in mind, I reached out to the conference organizers, and John and I put together a poster to share our work and promote ILSSA. Doing that was powerful for me —it felt like I was reaching back to help my past self, the version of me who once did not know where to start. In a way, it was healing because by supporting others, I was also affirming my own journey. To me, that is what educational intimacy looks like: creating spaces where people feel seen, supported, and encouraged to belong. Synthesis and discussion: Communities as intimacy, evolution, and advocacy As we weave together generations of partnerships demonstrative of our notions of community, guided by Uttamchandani’s (2020) educational intimacy, we revisit the questions we began with: what does community mean to ILSSA, and how do we support that in ways that are equitable and just? Toward the first question, three themes stand out across our stories as we describe our varying lived experiences that preempt how we define community: (1) community as a space in which people may experience intimacy, care, and belongingness; (2) community as ever-evolving, and 3) community as a form of advocacy. First, Blessed captures an intimacy and care through the philosophy of Ubuntu (“a person is a person through other persons”), providing a lens that captures many of the perspectives above: as he asserts, “Ubuntu offers a moral compass for what community should be – a place for empathy, care, mutual respect, and intentional inclusion of marginalized voices.” Second, as Grace and Jinho discuss, we recognize how community is ever-evolving, ever-changing, as our needs and various sociocultural, sociopolitical, and geopolitical contexts shift and interact, reciprocally, with our lived experiences: “community is not a static identity or geographic cluster, but something connecting across divides through relational care, shared vulnerability, and collective imagination.” Third, we recognize the centrality of advocacy in grounding and sustaining community – as intergenerational co-chairs, Morgan, John, and Lisa assert: “…we struggle to navigate the same system that we seek to educate our constituents around – and we do it anyway – because we know that community is what enables us to persist. It reminds us to care for the human at the heart of the research we dedicate years of our lives to discover.” As a highly interdisciplinary, cross-cultural space, our stories reveal how ILSSA provides opportunities to connect others within the learning sciences. Our mission: Intergenerational partnerships for community As the official student, postdoc, and recent graduate committee of the International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS), the International Learning Sciences Student Association (ILSSA) is dedicated to supporting the intellectual, social, and professional development of those it serves in ISLS and across the field. ILSSA consists of a board of elected representatives of (primarily) students across the globe with varied areas of expertise. ILSSA functions to advocate for the interests of junior scholars at the society level, liaise between society leadership and general membership, and scaffold the professional, social, and intellectual development of learning scientists-intraining. In a recent board meeting, questions were raised around the association's current infrastructure for inassociation communication –particularly whether our current (asynchronous, broadcast-style) communication platforms were accessible, relevant, and approp riate for the diverse lived experiences and needs of our learning sciences scholars-in-training. In short, we realized that ILSSA needed to question whether the means of our labor were congruent with the “ How,” “For What,” “For Whom,” “With Whom,” and “To what ends” (Philip et al.,

2018) of ILSSA’s collective vision for early-scholars in the association.

ILSSA’s mission statement operationalizes commitment through our charge and six consequent goals, of which one is to promote community, interdisciplinarity, and intellectual exchange among graduate students and postdoctoral scholars within the community. We extend on this goal, exploring how five generations of the ILSSA Board have defined and operationalized our commitment to community building and beyond as a method of establishing intergenerational partnerships. In this work, we investigate these ques tions of intergenerational discourses across five generations of ILSSA board leadership. We situate this symposium as both nuancedly 2424 connected to our conference’s theme, “Partnering with Purpose,” and timely, given the current US and global sociopolitical climate. If ever we were not convinced of the interconnectedness and centrality of politics within the work that we do as learning scientists and educational scholars (Phillip & Gupta, 2020; The Politics of Learning Writing Collective, 2017; Tivaringe & Kirshner, 2021; Uttamchandani et al., 2020;  Learning Towards Justice Team, 2025; Vea, 2020), this work is being conducted in the midst  of increased violence and erasure of minoritized bodies perpetuating a rhetoric of privilege, whiteness, patriarchy, and division. Expansive literature demonstrates the subjectivity through which community is defined, as mediated by its intersections, including race, class, gender, geopolitics, and other critical lived experiences (Daniel et al., 2023; Dixon & Martin, 2017; Harris et al., 2019; Herrenkohl et al., 2019; Jadallah et al., 2024; Van Wart et al., 2019). Framework: Embodying Uttamchandani’s educational intimacy Through research findings with Chroma, an LGBTQ+ interest and educational group, and the mediation of feminist and queer politics, Uttamchandani (2020) describes educational intimacy as “relationships that allow for inclusive and productive engagement in advocacy and learning while also mirroring desired future social configurations” (p. 52). He further notes the utility of this framework’s  suitability, particularly toward the development and establishment of group-level goals for social transformation. Uttamchandani posits  that educational intimacy’s presence within groups is operationalized when two criteria are met: when the group’s framework allows for respectful exchange amongst each other and disagreements are embraced toward transforming the group’s goals; and when relationships within the group demonstrate how group members would want to be treated within the context and community in which they are hoping to advocate. As we embrace this organizational dynamic within ILSSA’s context, we demonstrate educational intimacy through these two criteria and the three core tenets of how educational intimacy is evidenced within learning processes and discourses. Three core features denote the operationalization of educational intimacy within learning processes: prefigurations, feminist and queer politics, and relational activity; the politics and related discourses serve as the connection between these prefigurati ons and their consequent relational activity (Uttamchandani, personal communication, October 3, 2025). Uttamchandani locates prefigurations within the construct of prolepsis  – capturing how anticipated desired futures drive present activity – distinguishing an emphasis on social movements within his definition of prefigurations. Here, prefigurations are organizational-level current activities toward achieving desired futures (Curnow, 2022; Uttamchandani, 2020). Relationality  within this prefigurative context draws on the development and reorganization of relationships that support the desired futures for organizational forms of advocacy and change.

While developed within the niche context of queer prefigurative and relational discourses among Chroma, Uttamchandani (2020) concludes his work by posing critical questions to the learning sciences, of which we specifically engage theoretically: “How is its [educational intimacy’s] character similar or different when different kinds of social change are the goal?” (p. 71). We embody the prefigurative using this symposium as the current activity that solicits desired ideal futures of ILSSA’s systematized intergenerational partnerships for community building. We mediate this prefiguration and desired future by embracing the current poli tical landscape that seeks to divide us, such as the travel incapabilities of many scholars regarding the 2025 and 2026 ICLS conferences. As border politics and fascism seek to divide us, we embrace this politics as a method of creating imagined futures for our intergenerational relationship and community building. This is our defiance and advocacy toward strengthening our organization and for ILSSA generations to come. Approach: Intergenerational collaborative duoethnographies As we explore the questions, what does community mean to ILSSA, and how do we support that in ways that are equitable and just, we employ a method of intergenerational collaborative autoethnography, specifically through duoethnographies. We each draw on our lived experiences, both personally and professionally, to inform our positionality in our respective roles and, more broadly, the learning sciences community. We then each define what community means to us, making explicit ties  to the representative contexts and communities that we represent. We then expound on initiatives – particularly those working toward more just and equitable communal spaces –-   through forms of educational intimacy as mediums of partnership among ourselves and within our broader, decentralized communities.

## Synthesis and discussion: Communities as intimacy, evolution, and advocacy

As we weave together generations of partnerships demonstrative of our notions of community, guided by Uttamchandani’s (2020) educational intimacy, we revisit the questions we began with: what does community mean to ILSSA, and how do we support that in ways that are equitable and just? Toward the first question, three themes stand out across our stories as we describe our varying lived experiences that preempt how we define community: (1) community as a space in which people may experience intimacy, care, and belongingness; (2) community as ever-evolving, and 3)  community as a form of advocacy. First, Blessed captures an intimacy and care through the philosophy of Ubuntu (“a person is a person through other persons”), providing a lens that captures many of the perspectives above: as he asserts, “Ubuntu offers a moral compass for what community should be – a place for empathy, care, mutual respect, and intentional inclusion of marginalized voices.”  Second, as Grace and Jinho discuss, we recognize how community is ever-evolving, ever-changing, as our needs and various sociocultural, sociopolitical, and geopolitical contexts shift and interact, reciprocally, with our lived experiences: “community is not a static identity or geographic cluster, but something connecting across divides through relational care, shared vulnerability, and collective imagination.” Third, we recognize the centrality of advocacy in grounding and sustaining community – as intergenerational cochairs, Morgan, John, and Lisa assert: “…we struggle to navigate the same system that we seek to educate our constituents around – and we do it anyway – because we know that community is what enables us to persist. It reminds us to care for the human at the heart of the research we dedicate years of our lives to discover.” As a highly interdisciplinary, cross-cultural space, our stories reveal how ILSSA provides opportunities to connect others within the learning sciences. Our scholars describe different metaphors for this phenomenon, such as bridging others  (Jinho) and breaking barriers (Morgan, John, and Lisa), but they represent how ILSSA serves as a way for global scholars to connect. Scholars such as Adelmo facilitate these connections through mapping and connecting scholars across the Latin American region. Serving on t he board serves as a means of these connections. From these connections, scholars such as Chaewon become “ confronted with the reality that being a scholar in a global society involves far more than advancing individual academic interests, ” growing as scholars who advance equity and justice in the work they do. Yet, growth comes with growing pains. Our stories exemplify hardships experienced by many graduate students and early scholars, including experiences of isolation, overwhelm, labor exploitation, and cross-cultural communication; we notice how many of these hardships are exacerbated by our pursuit of ‘nontraditional’research agendas. Scholars like Yisha, who wish to study STEM education with non-traditional approaches (in her case, the arts), may experience isolation from peers as physical separation and research that may be “too niche to be empathized with,” as articulated by Morgan, John, and Lisa. In these ways, our stories reveal a key insight toward advancing equitable community-building in the learning sciences: a sensitivity to how the unique challenges of research in this specific academic community add additional hardships to more commonly-experienced pains of emerging researchers.

ILSSA provides an alternative to normative and bureaucratically-laden academic systems that expect us to tolerate isolation & labor exploitation in silence. It is an academic and professional space that refuses to deny the viscerally personal and human nature of being a junior scholar here and now. Extended, we seek to make these realities visible and undeniable to those who otherwise will make decisions about us, without us (inspired by Charlton’s 2004 disability justice work). As Morgan wrote in ILSSA’s 2024-2025 Annual Report shared with ISLS leadership: 2431 “...it is becoming increasingly difficult to act as if the issues facing established senior scholars are comparable to our own when, simply put, the stakes are different for us. Those of us ‘on the ground’ are expendable and the first to get cut all while our field continues to graduate more scholars than it could ever hire. For us, it’s a matter of survival. We don't expect our leaders and mentors to have all the answers; rather, we ask that they ‘read the room’ when vocalizing their complaints/concerns. W e ask that student perspectives be invited to meaningfully contribute (and be heard) future conversations where decisions are made that directly impact us. Nothing about us, without us.” Such radical calls to those in power beg the question – how do we operationalize such easy-to-dilute concepts as ‘equity’ and ‘justice’? This question can be extended to concretize what a community sustained by connection, visibility, reciprocity, and shar ed becoming (see Blessed, Lia, and Yetty’s commentaries) means. Each of these requires substantial lines of inquiry in their own right. In attending to this multifaceted notion of equitable and just community-building, we highlight the centrality of ILSSA as a space that creates and fosters the experience of our humanness, our need to connect with each other, and our need to see ourselves within each other. As we step into our elected roles each year, we inherit the legacies left by our predecessors and their embedded knowledge, practices, and desired futures all while actualizing a vision of our own. Our Africa and Middle East intergenerational partners, Blessed, Lia, and Yetunde, frame this: “through our intertwined stories, we prefigure a desired future where this learning community (ILSSA) is not defined by hierarchy or geography, but by connection, visibility,  reciprocity, and shared becoming. ” Our Asia and Pacific Region intergenerational partners, Nisumba, Rani, and Yoojin, elaborate: “together,  our stories represent an intergenerational journey across ILSSA’s Asia-Pacific leadership. What began as Nisumba’s vision for equitable visib ility evolved through Rani’s lived experience of inclusion and continues through Yoojin’s enactment of support and care.”  The persistence of these community tenets across generations, physical spaces, and times is not just a product of community action, but interwoven into the blueprints of the community itself. Here, educational intimacy is a central component to the community infrastructure and thus, a catalyst for the formation of relationships, inheritance of legacy, and actualization of cross-generationally desired futures. Educational intimacy: Theoretical expansions and considerations The other theoretical question we sought to engage in this discourse is Uttamchandani’s (2020) charge: “How is its [educational intimacy’s] character similar or different when different kinds of social change are the goal?” (p. 71). In revisiting the core components of educational intimacy’s presence and operationalization within discourse– prefigurations, feminist and queer politics, and relational activity (Uttamchandani, personal communication, October 3, 2025) – we make two important theoretical contributions for the theory’s innovation and expansion. The first is situating the importance of the intergenerational and historical in defining prefigurative activity; that is, we expand the notion of prefigurative activity to move into histories and generations of a conceptualized present. Present activity, then, refers to a specific moment in current time or in historical time. For example, Lisa notes that one of her major goals as the 2023-2024 co-chair (see Appendix) was to grow the board in number as well as in geographic diversity. As such, the present activities at that point in historical time served to denote the desired future of this numerical and geographic diversity – in the several years since her term, ILSSA has established a robust database of 512 people representing 250 institutions across 40 countries. This desired future at that point in historical time now represents prefigurative activity in the current time, as the board uses these activities to advocate for other desired futures.

Our second contribution expands the political beyond queer politics. Uttamchandani’s (2020) theorization was located within the specific context of youth queer activism. While we maintain this commitment to capturing queer politics, (as multiple contributors to this symposium embody queerness and its consequent politicalities), we expand this political to include the other representations of members across the organization, including geopolitics, transnational politics, racialized politics, neurodivergent politics, and intersectional politics. That stated, our expansion of Uttamchandani’s theorization requires careful navigation; we recognize that appropriating a framework designed for queer youth’s collective political sense-making beyond the intended context may inadvertently perpetuate damaging and queer-erasive rhetoric. Here, we adopt a theorization that maintains an explicit commitment to equitable discourse that is not erasive of queer theorizations – we maintain the aforementioned commitment to captu ring queer politics and do not seek to blur lines between contentions of equality and equity. Rather, we seek to adequately capture the myriad of lived experiences, intersectional representations, and their respective politics embodied by our authors and I LSSA as an organization, including queer politics, geopolitics, transnational politics, racialized politics, gender politics, and neurodivergent politics. 2432
