# Designing Writing Feedback with Large Language Models: Developing Young Adolescents' Conceptual Understanding of Effective Revision

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have the potential to deliver individualized formative feedback that supports the writing and revision processes. However, more research is needed to understand how to design prompts that generate effective feedback for young adolescents. To address this gap, this study illustrates an approach to integrating learning theory into prompt design to generate feedback that has the potential to improve young adolescents’ conceptual understanding of effective revision in a specific genre. Future directions for research are discussed.

## Introduction

Automated writing evaluation (AWE) systems that pro vide feedback on student writing are one approach to supporting writing development. However, evidence is modest that AWE systems improve the quality of students’ writing in the elementary and secondary grades (Gra ham et al., 2015). One reason is that students ofte n lack the skills necessary for effective revision (Roscoe et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2020). This highlights the need for systems that not only offer feedback to improve the quality  of writing but also build the conceptual understanding and skills needed to successfully implement feedback.

Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) show s ignificant promise for providing individualized feedback on student writing (Dai et al., 2023; Steiss et al., 2024). However, existing studies focus on exploring the potential of feedback to improve  the immediate quality of essays rather thandeveloping a conceptual understanding of effective revision. Furthermore, there is a lack of research examining the integration of writing-related learning theories into LLM prompt design and its impact on the quality of generated feedback. In this study, we address this gap by introducing a theory-driven framework for designing prompts in ChatGPT-4 that provide feedback to students on their argument essays in the seventh grade. Framework for designing LLM-generated feedback Adaptive expertise in revising Adaptive expertise refers to the ability to apply k nowledge flexibly and strategically to achieve goal s across various contexts (Hatano & Inagaki, 1984). Adaptive experts possess conceptual knowledge  of why and under which conditions certain methods should be applied or new methods devised. This conceptual knowledge can be developed through accumulated opportunities for learners to make connections between the specifics of a situation and broader principles within a domain (Walsh et al., 2023). Applied to the development of revision skills in writing, becoming an adaptive expert in revision requires a deep conceptual understanding of the generalized principles of effective revision (i.e., differences between content and surface-level revisions) and how specific revision strategies can be applied to achieve different writing goals (i.e., making a claim more convincing by adding more pieces of evidence). Effective feedback can support this process by highlighting the revision patterns (e.g., adding non-text-based evidence) observed between drafts. Then, the feedback further  links these revision patterns to broader genre-specific writing goals by demonstrating how these patterns advance or undermine those goals. Therefore, the LLM prompts in this study are designed to generate feedback that includes 1) identifying revision patterns shown between drafts, 2) connecting implemented revision patterns to genre-specific writing goals, and 3) connecting desirable revision actions to genre-specific writing goals.

## Sociocultural perspectives on formative feedback

Drawing on sociocultural theory, research on formative feedback asserts that assistance should take into account the learner’s current and potential levels of competence, or  zone of proximal development (ZPD) (Vygotsky, 1987). Applied to the development of writing skills, effective feedback should not directly tell students what to write but rather offer hints or prompts that provide appropriate scaffolding within their ZPD. In addition, effective feedback is non-directive and delivered in a supportive tone, which could pos sibly increase students’ motivation to review (Graham et al., 2018; Hattie & Timperley, 2007). Furthermore, meaningful learning occurs when abstract tasks of the school curriculum are situated in authentic contexts that are critical to them (Collins et al., 1991). Accordingly, feedback should also be grounded in authentic tasks that are critical to  school success— a genre that prepares students for the types of wri ting required for college readiness and success, su ch as argumentative writing.  Feedback that focuses  on the content of students’ writing, based on comparisons with expected genre-specific writing goals, has been shown to positively impact students’ writi ng performance (Graham, 2018). Therefore, feedback must align with and advance these goals. Finally, as young adolescents are still in the process of developing their reading skills, feedback must be accessible and understandable to them. To sum up, drawing on theory and research on writing developmen t, the LLM prompts in this study are constructed to generate feedback that contains four features: 1) scaffolded feedback, 2) supportive tone, 3) focusing on genrespecific writing goals, and 4) accessibility. According to the quality of the first draft, this student was assigned a revision goal of adding more explanation to strengt hen the connection between their evidence and the c laim. As shown in the feedback, it accurately identified a revision pattern that the student made minimal attempts to revise, with changes largely involving repeating information already present in the essay.  Making revision patterns explicit to students is a crucial step that has a strong potential to increase the student’s metacognitive awarenes s of noticing their revision process. The feedback then linked this revision pattern to t he assigned writing goals by explaining how merely repeating information did not  improve the quality of explanations. This connection  is expected to help the student understand why their revision strategies are ineffective. Then, suggestions for future revisions were provided, which would guide  the student toward implementing desired revision strategies to achieve the writing goals. Finally, the feedback was accessible and encouraging for seventh graders and provided an appropriate level of scaffolding through hints.

As demonstrated in the example, ChatGPT-4 has the potential for generating feedback that connects the specifics of students' revision patterns and desirable alternative strategies to broader writing goals, a key activity for developing a conceptual understanding of effective revision. However, since this study primarily focuses on constructing a theory-based framework to guide the prompt design of forma tive feedback, more exploration  is needed to integrate this framework with diverse pro mpting strategies (e.g., Chain-of-Thought prompting) to increase the quality of the generated feedback. More importantly, future studies should examine the effectiveness of the proposed feedback in developing students’ co nceptual understanding of revision —either through direct implementation with students or validation by teachers or writing experts.
