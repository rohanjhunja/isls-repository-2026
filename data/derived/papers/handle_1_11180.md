# Generative AI as a Mirror: Educators Exploring and Challenging Dominant Discourses about the Future of AI in Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in teacher education and the challenges educators face in gaining agency and understanding of AI implementation. Focusing on the concept of discursive closure, it investigates how educators envision the future of AI in education (AIed) and the implications of dominant discourses in their perceptions about the future, but also about its integration today. The study reveals educators' perceptions and the limitations of current narratives about AIed. It uncovers deterministic, trajectoristic, and simplistic binary views that tend to overshadow educators' roles and diminish their sense of agency in classrooms today. Additionally, through a speculative exercise employing a generative AI tool, the study showcases how educators interpret and make sense of these dominant discourses while envisioning educational futures, thereby exposing the normative assumptions ingrained in AIed today. The study emphasizes the need for educators' critical engagement with AI technologies to envision alternative discourses and shape more democratic educational futures for themselves and their communities.

## Introduction

Discourses about AIed promise to empower learners a nd educators in different ways, positioning generat ive AI technology as a propeller in the design of educational futures. Efforts aimed at improving people’s empowerment often involve funding agencies and governments exerting their influence by defining the concept of empowerment through projects, which may have clear or implied normative objectives (Andersen et al., 2023). These normatives often manifest in the form of discursive closure (D eetz, 1992), the elimination of alternative conceptualizations of power that center certain organizational ideologies over community-centered values and lived experiences. The availability of generative AI tools has led to a growth in dominant discourses that do not seem to contemplate the knowledge students and educators already hold about  the educational process. Questions like how educat ors incorporate AI in their classrooms and how they define the conditions of their environments are not being asked. A focus on the the ory, research, and practice of AI that leaves out t he voices and experiences of educators will not be able to capture how dominant discourses about AIed can be reconfigured to craft alternative discourses and envision more democratic futures (Eynon & Young, 2020). By examining the struggles and perceptions of educators regarding AI integration in education, we aimed to investigate the limitations they face due to the  influence of dominant discourses. This study seeks  to explore ways to overcome such limitations and engage in alt ernative discourses about AI integration, thus enha ncing educators’ agency and understanding. The findings w ill not only shed light on the challenges and possi bilities associated with the integration of AIed but also of fer valuable insights fo r teacher education programs and policymakers.

Discursive closure in the incorporation of technologies in education In promoting the incorporation of educational technologies by teachers, it is essential to leave behind approaches that solely focus on administrative, technical, or institutional aspects of technology integration (Ji menez, 2007) and engage in approaches that allow educators to ga in agency to find the power within themselves to tr ansform educational futures. However, imagining liberatory social futures is often impacted by normative practices of literacy and civic engagement (Mirra & Garcia, 2020) that leads educators to focus on attempting to repair or re-create systems that have historically marginalized people rather than allowing themselves to re-imagine new educational futures.

Current discourses in educational technology, including AIed, are shaped by sociocultural practices that may influence the experiences of individuals. These discourses are often disguised as grand narratives that convey culturally dominant norms and practices (Brown & Gi lligan, 1993) and create, transmit, and reinforce p ower structures (Vandijk, 2008). Given the obscure natu re of these structures, the growing presence of AI discourses in education presents more complex challenges, leading to limita tions in enacting alternative approaches to AI integration in education. This form of discursive c losure (Deetz, 1992) manifests itself in four diffe rent ways (Markham, 2021):

1.  Disqualification: This aspect of discursive closure refers to the notion that certain voices or perspectives

are considered less legitimate or qualified to cont ribute to a discussion, especially on technical or specialized subjects like AI. In the context of education, this can manifest when teachers feel that t heir lack of specialized knowledge in AI disqualifies th em from critically engaging with or questioning AI technologies and their implementation in educationa l settings. This feeling of disqualif ication can prevent teachers from asserting or dismissing their  perspectives and needs in the development and application of AI in education.

2.  Trajectorism: This characteristic involves the perc eption that certain developments, like the integrat ion

of AI in education, follow a predetermined trajectory. Teachers may feel that the march of technological progress in education is unstoppable and that they must adapt to it, regardless of their views or concerns. This sense of inevitability may lead educators to m erely reconfigure existing methods through technology rather than reinventing them.

3.  Technological Determinism: This is the belief that technology development is the primary driver of

societal change. In the context of education, this viewpoint can lead teachers to feel that the future  of education is primarily shaped by technological adva nces rather than pedagogical practices or ethical considerations. Such a deterministic view might obs cure the fact that technologies are socially shaped and, therefore, they can be hacked, altered, or rei magined. Occasionally, this may lead to utopian/dystopian views about the role of technology in our  lives, disregarding human agency in the process.

4.  False Dilemmas: This characteristic involves presen ting complex issues as having only two possible

solutions, often oversimplifying their implications. In educational contexts, teachers may face false dilemmas, such as choosing between traditional teaching methods and AI-driven approaches or between protecting student privacy and embracing data-driven educational tools. This oversimplification can limit the exploration of nuanced, multifaceted solutions that better align with educational values and goal s. This polarity often leads educators to see contrari es and contradictions, thus dismissing the opportun ity to engage in more nuanced understandings of AI integration. Engaging educators in exploring alternative discourses In making sense of educator’s own experiences with AIed, it is important to engage them in balancing or resolving competing discourses, oscillating between grand narratives and more nuanced personal reflections. In a study with university students, Tiidenberg et al. (2017) descr ibed how youth engaged in a journey from relying on  readymade interpretations of their lived experiences wit h technology to developing more granular, although contradictory, personal narratives about their soci al media use. By engag ing in self/reflection and experimentation, the participants were able to deve lop a more critical understanding of their current conditions. Similarly, by engaging in self/reflection, educators can move beyond surface-level understandings and simplistic generalizations about AI. Experimentation allows them to explore their own experiences and perceptions, leading to a deeper, more critical understanding of the role of AI in shaping their identity as educators. Teacher beliefs are very relevant regarding the att itudes of educators in their intention to either re sist or incorporate AI in their classrooms (Ertmer, 2005). In teacher preparation, there are discourses that f ocus on the notion that educational success will not be achieved without the incorporation of a  given educational technology (Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010). However, the process of incorporat ing an emergent technology is seldom a streamlined process. Raffaghelli and Stewart (2020), in exploring the development of data literacy skills among educators, argue that teachers’ development needs t o go beyond technological proficiency and involve t he comprehension of the implications of technology wit hin broader societal and ethical frameworks. Theref ore, teacher development programs need to foster an engagement with technologies that engage educators in critically understanding and utilizing the new technologies effectively. Adopting an ecological approach to examining AIed e nables educators to grasp the complexities and challenges involved. This approach includes develop ing shared visions, addressing socioeconomic contex ts, and aligning policies with educational practices, a s Raffaghelli et al. (2020) highlighted. Our study introduces an exploratory and speculative exercise, prompting educators to envision the future of AIed. This imaginative process aims to assess how prevailing discourses shape curr ent perceptions of AI amo ng educators. To facilitate this exercise, we employ an exercise where educators pro mpt generative images that depict future educationa l scenarios. These images serve as tools for educator s to identify potential biases in dominant narrativ es about AI in education and to stimulate the creation of alternative discourses about the future role of AI. The enactment of speculative visions of AIed that i ncorporate the experiences of diverse groups needs to affirm both teachers' and students’ identities as essentially human. To attain such level of humanization amidst claims that AI will revolutionize education, it is necessary to engage in speculative pe dagogies (Mirra & Garcia,

2023) that a) center informal, local relationships; b) take an iterative approach to learning; and c) engage in joyful

social dreaming to confront injustice. In this stud y, Mirra and Garcia’s (2023) speculative framework for youth participation was used to  imagine how educators can also speculate about disr upting the tensions they currently face while reminding them that they also have the right to participate in the design of educational futures with AI.

## Methods

Qualitative research employs a variety of thorough methodologies and strategies to comprehend individu als' experiences and viewpoints and explore the underlyi ng causes and motivations for their behaviors (Baze n et al., 2021). The present study employed a single case stu dy method to understand and explore the perceptions  and struggles of educators regarding AI integration in education. The research questions addressed in this study were as follows: RQ1.  How do educators manifest discursive closure regarding their agency in designing educational futures with AI?

RQ2. How does a speculative collaborative collage u sing generative AI influence educators’ perceptions  of dominant discourses in AIed?

## Context

For the study, a course titled "Introduction to Art ificial Intelligence in Education" (AIed) was designed at a Midwestern university. It is a one-credit online course exploring how artificial intelligence influences educational practices and prepares educators to contribute to t he evolving landscape of technology-enhanced learning responsibly. Within the scope of this course, stude nts also attended synchronous class sessions held v ia Zoom, with the first session taking place in the first we ek and the final session in the last week of the co urse. In the synchronous classes, students engaged in spe culative activities and participated in group discu ssions. A total of nine students were enrolled in the course. Followin g the approval of the IRB protocol, all students we re invited to participate in the study.

The study participants were practicing educators (N =6) and undergraduate students (N=3) with varying backgrounds, such as elementary education, English as a Foreign Language, and instructional design. Five of the practicing educators had between one to fiv e years of experience, while one of them had over 1 0 years of experience. The undergraduate students only had fie ld teaching experience. Regarding their former expe rience with AI, three out of six of the educators had used  AI tools in their teaching practice, but they only  mentioned ChatGPT and Grammarly as the tools they usually use. All undergraduate students manifested some level of familiarity with the same two tools.

The course consisted of four modules that sought to engage educators in examining the role of AIed with a strong emphasis on evaluating current discourses and enacting speculative, alternative discourses. T his study focused on the first module of this curriculum. In this module, participants first completed a discuss ion prompt through a digital collaborative collage on the Padl et platform, a collaborative visual platform: “How do you envision the future of AI in education?” This activity was followed by an introduction to Machine Learning using Google’s Teachable Machine. This tool allows users to train models using data such as photos, audio, and camera inputs. The purpose of this activity was to help ed ucators visualize how data and mathematical models were combined to create different outputs. This was rele vant as it provided a more nuanced understanding of  how generative AI models often reflect the patterns pre sent in the training data. Finally, educators engag ed in a synchronous collaborative collage where they used P adlet, a collaborative board application, to prompt  five images using a generative AI tool to describe the future of AIed.

## Data collection and analysis

The study utilized data from the first module of the curriculum, including a collaborative collage on Padlet where participants posted their views on the future of AI  and education. In the collage, they also used a ge nerative AI tool to create images based on prompts about education in the future. Discussions from an online board related to this activity and audio recordings from one of the two synchronous meetings—where participants speculated on future classrooms and teacher roles—were also key data sources. First, data was analyzed holistically by two coders  to identify potential themes. Then, deductive codi ng in co llaboration with a third coder was followed using the characteristics of dis cursive closure identified by Markham (2021). In deductive coding, codes were established before t he second round of data analysis and then used to categorize the data (Bingham & Witkowsky, 2022). MAXQDA software was used for the analysis of the data resources. To obtain a final codebook, researc hers agreed on the coding strategy and conducted th e coding separately. Periodic data analysis meetings among the researchers helped identify and revisit the emerging themes and categories together. ## Discursive closure in teachers’ perspectives of AI in education

Prior to conducting the collaborative collage, educ ators answered a question in the discussion board a bout how they envisioned the future of AIed in the next 25 years. The analysis of the educators’ responses to the discussions regarding the role of AIed was conducted deductively using the four characteristics of discursive closure described by Markham (2021). This section provides an account of the multiple ways in which discursive closure manifests in educators’ discourses during their initial responses to the question about their imaginations of the future of AI. Discourses of technological determinism The analysis of the discussion posts suggests a bel ief in the inevitable and linear advancement of AI as a main agent of change in educational practices. This beli ef tends to overshadow their roles, pedagogical req uirements, and ethical considerations. A common thread in their discourse is the notion that AI will fundamentally shape the future of education, as highlighted in a student's statement, “ I envision that the capacity of AI to create new environments could revolutionize the teaching of language, science, history, and mathematics.” This deterministic view of AI's transformative potential is often coup led with a diminished perception of educators' role s and influence, as evidenced by the observation from one of the students, “We will see more and more parts of teacher’s jobs be taken over by AI tech. It’s just a matter o f how much and when.” These views imply a straightforward trajectory of the technology towards increasingly a dvanced and unbiased AI tools, showcasing a belief in technology's predetermined progression and its capa city to enhance educational tools with limited huma n input. Nonetheless, while deterministic views of AI influence educators' discourses, they also reveal a sense of hope for the future, as one student expresses, “ What I hope for the future of AI in the classroom i s that teachers in 2040 can find joy in teaching once again.” Educators’ perspectives describe both dystopian and utopian beliefs that AI will drive education forward and help bridge social  disparities. However, these discourses rarely reflect the educators' own sense of agency in shaping the future of education. Discourses of trajectorism The analysis of discussion posts revealed that educ ators’ discourses align with the perception that ce rtain developments were inevitable and followed a predete rmined trajectory. For instance, one of the experie nced educators noted, “ The consequence of AI will be teachers developing m ore engaging and effective learning materials, meaningful performance assessments, and more personalized and accessible learning experienc es for students.” This statement implies a trajectory where AI leads to enhanced personalization in learning, suggesting an inevitable shift towards more engaging and effective teaching methods influenced by AI. One of the undergraduate students expressed, “ I think AI could really help teachers. Many teacher s are leaving, which is causing a high demand for them. I think that AI could relieve some stress.” This reflects a belief in the inevitability of AI as a solution to current  challenges like teacher burnout. It showcases an a cceptance of AI as an unstoppable force in education, one that teachers must adapt to for alleviating workload and stress. The following quote from one of the undergraduate s tudents summarizes how trajectorism manifests in their discourses: For example, as a future teacher, I see AI creating  all of our lesson plans and activities. We just will need to execute them to the students during sc hool. I also see AI grading all electronic homework, worksheets, tests, etc. I already have a class where the professor doesn't have to do anything but put the grade in Canvas because AI gra des our discussion posts. This will alleviate work from teachers and hopefully allow them to focus on the teaching happening in class. This perspective envisions a future where AI takes over significant aspects of teaching, from lesson planning to grading, positioning AI as an inevitabl e part of the educational landscape. Rather than se eing this reality as limiting their agency, the educator finds solace in the fact that they will have more time to focus on the actual teaching. This perspective implies a trajectory where teachers’ roles evolve to adapt to AI-driven changes, potentially limiting their ability to imagine alter native educatio nal practices beyond the reconfiguration of traditional methods.

Discourses of false dilemmas Teacher’s discourses also show evidence of false dilemmas by presenting complex issues in the integration of AI with simplistic binary solutions. For instance, one educator noted, “One of the main reasons for this is efficiency. We, as a society, are always trying to make things more efficient. For teachers, having lesson plans made for you or getting most of your ideas from an AI tool means  you can put your focus into other aspects of the j ob.” This statement presents a dilemma between using AI for e fficiency and focusing on other teaching aspects. This oversimplifies the integration of AI in education by implying that the primary benefit of AI is to free up teachers' time, without considering the potential complexities and nuances of how AI might impact the quality of teaching and learning.

Similarly, the following quote from an educator fra mes the issue of AIed as a binary choice between traditional systems and AI-driven approaches; The AI disruption in education is a consequence of the disconnection, the significant gap between the demands of the real world and the traditional e ducational systems. My hope by 2045 is that this gap is no longer relevant and that educators and educational institutions have responsibly and equitably embraced the power of AI.

This discourse simplifies the complex relationship between education and real-world demands, overlooking the possibility of integrating AI into education to complement and enhance traditional methods rather than completely replace them.

During the conversation, the educators were prompted about the potential loss of jobs or a higher number of students in their classrooms as a result of AI integration. One of them replied: “Yes, AI can replace many tasks but also improve how educators approach educational  challenges. If teachers do not change the way they  work and do not acquire the necessary skills, they will create a situation where administrators may think t hat way.” This view presents a false dilemma by suggesting th at educators must either adapt to AI or risk being replaced. This perspective reduces the complex issue of AI integration to a simple matter of adaptation versus obsolescence, neglecting the potential for a more nuanced approach that critically evaluates and selectively integrates AI in ways that align with educational values and goals.

Discourses of disqualification The discourses from the educators reinforce the not ion that they may lack specialized knowledge in AI.  For example, one teacher said: “as AI moves into the teaching role and educators move into supportive roles, so many emotional barriers to learning could be dismantled.  AI does not have bad days. Unfortunately, to the d etriment of students, teachers often do. ” This statement implies that AI, due to its lack o f emotional variability, is more suited to teaching than human educators, who are subject to emotional fluctuations. This perspective disqualifies the value of human elements in teaching, such as empathy and emotional intelligence, which are not replicable by AI. It suggests that AI's lack of emotional involve ment is preferable, undermining teachers' confidenc e in their unique human contributions to education.

Another student claims: “...as a future teacher, I see AI creating all of our lesson plans and activities. We will just need to execute them to the students d uring school.” This perspective creates a sense of disqualification among educators, leading them to f eel that their expertise and judgment are less valu able or necessary in these areas. It reinforces the idea th at teachers' roles are primarily executional, follo wing the groundwork laid by AI, thereby marginalizing their input in the creative and crit ical aspects of teaching and learning.

The themes identified during the analysis of the images and the conversations with t he students include a) the role of educators in the  imagined futures, b) the lack of alignment between utopian f utures and current realities, and c) the lack of pe dagogical ruptures. While th e generated images may have included robots, students wearing headsets, or manipulating large futuristic screens, the presence of rows of desks denounces the lack of pedagogical ruptures present in these imaginations of the future, as one of the teachers described during the live session: …it is interesting how the AI continues to show tra ditional classroom environments, even in a potentially future classroom… I think it might be related to what we've studied so far in the class, in the sense of AI as a generative process that learns from the data we feed it with. Still, the data d id not demonstrate that this technique alone allowed them to enact more critical or emancipatory discourses r egarding AI integration. This is why the workshop involved other activities which are not part of the analysis we conducted for this study such as a polarity mapping exercise and a speculative design task.

## Discussion

Our study has uncovered insights into educators' pe rceptions and interactions with AIed’s evolving landscape. One notable theme is their ability to navigate the polarizing nature of AIed: discourses fluctuated from claims that AI has an incredible potential to improve educational practices to discourses that emphasize the current challenges AI introduces to their teaching practices. Therefor e, they were able to move beyond polarized perspect ives to engage in a critical conversation about the nature of their discourses. Initially, educators exhibited a tendency towards d eterministic and trajectoristic views, perceiving t he integration of AI in education as an inevitable tec hnologically-driven process. These views, as seen in their discussions about AI leading to more pers onalized learning or replacing aspects of teaching,  demonstrate a discourse that potentially hinders the educator's role, creativity, and critical thinking. This deterministic approach suggests a limited scope for educators to enact alt ernative discourses about the ways in which AI coul d be integrated into educational practices.

Moreover, false dilemmas and disqualification in their discourses indicate a constrained narrative space. The tendency to frame the integration of AI in educ ation within binary choices, like efficiency versus  teacher burnout, oversimplifies the complexitie s involved in AI adoption. This not only limits the  exploration of more complex solutions but also potentially sidelines the educators’ values. Additionally, the sense of disqualification, where educators feel AI can perform their job bette r than they do, further exacerbates this closure. T hese discourses collectively demonstrate how certain dom inant narratives around AI in education can lead to  a discursive closure, hindering a more holistic and participatory exploration of AI's role in the future of education. This first module of the course allowed speculation to emerge as a form of rupture (Bene et al., 2023) in which educators used the very technology that promi ses to overtake their role in the classroom to elab orate challenging discourses that imagine alter native perspectives about AI. The educators were ab le to diverge from normative binaries where AI had to be accepted or a ctively incorporated, thus rupturing the discursive  closure that manifested in their initial imaginations of th e future. This emancipat ory rupture is not a technophobic cry against the incorporation of AI but a creative act in which educators begin to acknowledge and confron t the injustice of certain discourses (Mirra & García, 2023). For educators to engage in reflecting on their role in the integration of technology, it is essential they are first capable of acknowledging the way in which discourses manifest in their own practices and how these discourses affect them as a collective. By experimenting with generative AI technologies, educators were able to evaluate how the data that feeds these algorithms is embedded with dominant discourses.  They identified discourses that dismiss educators’ agency in enacting innovation in education while introducing unrealistic expectations regarding learners and reproducing systemic inequity.

## Conclusions

This study introduces an approach that supports the  understanding of educators' perceptions of AI with in educational contexts and offers opportunities for e ducators to evaluate the influence of dominant disc ourses in their perceptions by collectively exami ning the imaginations of a generative AI tool. This  collective endeavor allowed educators to share their hopes and concerns about the future and question the imposed narratives present in AI discourses. Still, it also led them to discus s their agency in th e integration of technology today. Future directions for this study include evaluating the ou tcomes of the speculative exercises in which educat ors are invited to reimagine emancipatory futures with AIed  within this curriculum. It is anticipated that man y of the dominant discourses will still prevail, given adults’ need to adhere to normative practices; however, we also expect this curriculum to highlight educators’ agency in cultivating their agency in designing educational futures.
