# Teamwork in Higher Education: Benefits and Barriers

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Industry and higher education have long recognized the importance of teamwork skills and the need for quality training. Although there is an array of pedagogical approaches to teamwork training, student perceptions on their effectiveness can influence implementation. This study employed a qualitative approach to investigate student experiences with teamwork and identified distinct clusters of experiences and learning theory principles associated with them. The findings have implications for improving teamwork pedagogy and enhancing students' learning experiences.

## Introduction

Cooperation enables us to share knowledge, improve skills, and  design and construct infrastructure and technologies. Teamwork is one form of cooperation t hat represents the working relationship between peo ple (Volkov & Volkov, 2007) and is important across several settings in today’s world. In industry, for instanc e, working effectively with others is no longer considered desirable, but rather essential (Riebe, Girardi, & Whitsed, 2016). It is thus expected that higher education institutions will provide quality teamwork pedagogy to train team skills and knowledge (Rapp & Mathieu, 2007). However, there are common challenges of teamwork approaches including student worries about grade distribution, teammate accountability, and balancing  other work loads (Clarke & Blissenden, 2013). To better understand teamwork benefits, struggles and potentially related theories, we investigated college students’ perceptions of teamwork in a project-based animal science course.

## Method

69 students in an online  undergraduate animal science course  were assigned to teams  to complete a three-phase project. Teams made a “roles contract ” then met throughout the course in group checkpoints to research and ultimately present on an animal science  topic. Afterwards, s tudents completed online written reflections about what they struggled with and what worked well regar ding teamwork.  We coded students’ reflections at the sentence level in the MAXQDA Analytics Pro data ana lysis software  regarding what worked well, student struggles, and learning theories (Thornberg, 2012). We calculated Pearson's correlations for the ten most common codes. These matrices were then analyzed using UCINET/NetDraw software (Borgatti, 2002). We used GirvanNewman cluster analysis (Girvan & Newman, 2002) to find clusters of codes and calculate betweenness centrality measures to determine the significance of individua l codes within the network.  We also generated descriptive statistics regarding the proportions of students who experienced a given code.

These included finding team meetings, collaboration, and logistics to work well for learning. When such experiences occurred, they aligned with the situated learning principle of community of practice or local team. The black cluster  (80% of students) included perceptions that equal workloads, role negotiation, and team motivation helped learning, co-occuring with the self-determination learning principle of being comfortable a team. The gray cluster  (65%) suggest that discomfort and stres s were experienced  at some point during  the teamwork process. The pink cluster (50%) shows difficulty with online discussion, miscommunication, irresponsible teammates, and a lack of role fulfillment. The red cluster (20%) suggests teamwork was associated with identity exploration, self-efficacy, and agency.

## Implications and conclusion

Our results indicate that teamwork duties, when organized and planful, were beneficial for students’ learning and promoted a sense of community.  Furthermore, successful role negotiation was relate d to positive learning experiences as well as motivation and comfort. The current course’s use of team contracts, peer evalua tion, student-instructor reporting, and regular team checkpoints seemed to have supported team accountability, cohesion, and distributive justice, aligning with past work (Riebe, Girardi, & Whitsed, 2016). With regards to struggles, students faced difficult y with negotiating team roles and dealing with “freeriding” teammates, aligning with previous rese arch (e.g., McCorkle et al., 1999). Interestingly, these struggles co-occurred with miscommunication and online discussio n, suggesting that poor communication may have been perceived as lacking accountability or en gaging in social loafing.  Furthermore, 65% of students experienced discomfort, difficulty, or found teamwo rk unenjoyable at some point during their t eam projects. Additional analyses revealed relationships between these struggles and the situated learning principle  of community of practice, and to a lesser extent, the positive experiences of communication and relations hips. This may indicate  that d ifficulty or distress was a smaller aspect of students’ more generally positive teamwork experience. Moreover, discomfort is considered a natural part of learnin g, particularly within team settings. In fact, guidelines for effective cooperative learning often espouse interactions that allow team members to challenge each other (e.g., Johnson, Johnson, & Smith, 1991).

In sum, despite its challenges for both students and instructors, team projects appear to be an import ant tool for students’ learning and cater to identified  needs of the job market. Some successful examples on how to support student teamwork shown herein are assignmen t scaffolding, instructor intervention when necessary, and clarity of roles and duty expectations within the team.
