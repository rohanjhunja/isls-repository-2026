# The Design, Implementation and Evaluation of a Human-Centered Design Professional Learning Community in a High School Setting

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study integrated participatory action research into a Professional Learning Community (PLC) at a high school to explore how teachers from various disciplines could integrate Human-Centered Design (HCD) in their teaching. Teachers valued students' development of empathy, collaboration, and creativity, but faced obstacles including time constraints and student buy-in. While teachers felt knowledgeable about HCD, they preferred incorporating individual tools into existing lesson plans rather than creating multi-day lessons.

## Introduction and conceptual framework

Human-Centered Design (HCD) is a problem-solving approach that uses design thinking tools to  identify the unmet needs of a population in order to collaborati vely and iteratively develop solutions (Brown, 2008). HCD focuses on empathy and iteration, requiring designe rs to communicate, collaborate, and test ideas and solutions with a wide array of stakeholders and communities. By utilizing HCD frameworks, individuals can tackle complex wicked problems that have no single solution, but instead require a careful balancing of stakeholder’s needs, wants and limitations to envision, integrate, and design effective sustainable solutions. Recent research has demonstrated that incorporating design thinking and HCD into cla ssrooms enables students to develop 21st centu ry mindsets necessary for workplace success (Shehab & Guo, 2021; Wrigley & Straker, 2015). Research demonstrates that incorporating design thi nking and HCD into classrooms enables students to develop 21st century mindsets necessary for workpla ce success (Wrigley & Straker, 2015). HCD provides authentic problem-solving contexts and features multi-disciplinary collaborations (Wright & Wrigley, 2019). Engaging studen ts in HCD activities is supported by constructivist  and experiential learning theories that emphasize the roles of prior knowledge, social interactions, and reflective practices in learning (Koh et al., 2015). However, bringing HCD to K-12 classrooms is challenging and requires teacher preparation. Professional Learning Communities, grounded in soci al constructivist principles, provide an environment where educators can share ideas, collaborate, and reflect on practices (Easton, 2015). Several studies have identified traits that contribute to successful PLCs, including supportive leadership (Thornton & Cherrington, 2014), organizational values (Lapoint, 2021), and p erformance feedback (Burns & Preast, 2017). Longitu dinal studies demonstrate that PLCs allow teachers to overcome initial difficulties with new subject matter and provide motivators for classroom transformations (Tam, 2014). Previous research has shown PLCs can effectively support design thinking implementation when teachers mainta in a willingness to learn, motivation for innovatio n, and openness to collaboration (Owen, 2016).

This study aimed to understand teachers' values, hu rdles, and expectations when integrating HCD into t heir practice through a PLC using participatory action research. The research questions were:

1. What values do teachers have towards HCD and its inclusion in classrooms?

2. What hurdles do teachers perceive regarding HCD classroom integration?

3. What expectations and challenges do HCD-focused PLCs face?

## Methods

During 2023-24, we partnered with a midwestern high school to s upport four HCD-trained teachers (the Core Team) and one administrator in developing a design thinking hub. The Core team was composed of teachers who have been teaching at this school for more  than twenty years and had developed high rapport with several o ther teachers and staff. The Core Team led the PLC initiative, taking responsibility for organizing sessions, facilitating activities, and maintaining engagement with participants throughout the program in and outside of the scheduled sessions. Twenty-one teachers and supporting staff from various disc iplines volunteered to participate in six in-person PLC sessions (1.5-3 hours each) over the school year. The Core Team r ecruited at least 2 teac hers from each discipline at their school and invited supporting staff such as librarians and social workers to take part. Researchers and the Core Team collaboratively devel oped the PLC curriculum, designing six sessions spanning October to April. This co-created curriculum was then implemented by the Core  Team who facilitated sessions covering introduction to HCD, reflection on teachers' personal hurdles to integration, classroom examples demonstrated by Core Team members, resource explora tion of common HCD tools, industry applications, and sharing of teacher-developed HCD lessons.

Following participatory action research (PAR) principles, the Core Team, serving as co-researchers, met weekly with the university researchers to redesign sessions based on participant feedback. In using PA R, we collaborated as researchers with participant s to co-construct new knowledge within these PLC sessions. In integrating PAR into our methodology, we looked to leverage the effects it has on the participatory, collaborative, social and reflective aspects (Morales, 2016). By d eveloping and iterating on the curriculum together with the Core Team leaders at the research site, we were able to create learning experiences that were flexible to the needs of the community through a combination of formal and informal data shared during weekly meetings colle cted from a variety of perspectives. Data collection inc luded artifacts from HCD activities (such as "sailb oat" which help teachers identify motivations, goals and hurdles), interviews with six volunteer teachers, reflective exercises, and anonymous surveys. Data was analyzed using thematic analysis to identify patterns related to values, hurdles, and expectations.

## Results

Teachers valued HCD for both student benefits and p rofessional benefits. Teachers emphasized HCD's role in developing essential mindsets: “Teaching kids how to think flexibly and use information in different ways to find solutions and collaborate are all skills we know by research is needed." Many noted that HCD develops “durable skills” that "translate to the professional environment.” In regard to professional benefits, teachers saw HCD as a "way to make boring content interesting” through empathy-based engagement. They appreciated opportunities to facilitate authentic, self-driven learning and “destroy old ways of teaching.” Three primary hurdles emerged from the themes durin g interviews regarding content integration, time constraints, and student buy in. Teachers struggled connecting subject-specific content with HCD frameworks: "I need to be in the target language 90% of the time... Figuring out how to integrate HCD, where my main goal is language acquisition, is challenging." Limited time  for designing and implementing new HCD lessons was frequently cited: "The only hurdle for me is time... just being given the time to develop the curricul um would be it for me." Teachers worried about engaging students accustomed to traditional learning models: "Students aren't motivated unless there's a specific grade tied to it or they know exactly what the outcome is." Some noted students "don't have the grit to pivot and iterate" when facing challenges. Surveys revealed that for the PLC, teachers expected to learn about HCD, collaborate with colleagues to design new lessons, and acquire new implementation tools for classroom integration. Survey results sho wed relatively high confidence in understanding HCD (3. 97/5, SD=0.69) and identifying HCD processes (3.69/ 5, SD=1.14), but lower confidence in designing multi-day projects (3.29/5, SD=1.40). We found from inter views that teachers were placing great emphasis on indivi dual HCD tools, rather than comprehensi ve projects. "What I've done is some of the interpersonal activities, the icebreakers... That's great for building energy and cohesion." ## Discussion and implications

While teachers recognized HCD's potential benefits for developing 21st century skills and creating engaging learning experiences, they faced significant implem entation challenges. The gap between teachers' comf ort with individual tools versus comprehensive projects sugg ests a need for more focused support in bridging co ntentspecific learning with HCD frameworks. Future HCD PLC’s should look to: provide  collaborative opportunities to identify content-HCD intersections, allocate sufficient time for des ign and implementation, address student buy-in through revised assessment approaches, and devel op scaffolded coaching sessions during PLCs to help teachers identify and address implementation challenges. Further studies should also document and evaluate t he long-term impact of HCD integration on both teacher practice and student skill development, especially regarding empathy, collaboration, communication, and creativity – the skills teachers in this study most valued.
