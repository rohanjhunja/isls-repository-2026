# More or Fewer? Effects of Multiple Representation Simulations in Secondary Physics Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examined how simulations with multiple representations affect secondary students’ physics learning. Simulations employed in the experiment group (n = 36) instruction with multiple representations, whereas the control group (n = 42) used single ones, taught by the same teacher. Results revealed that experimental group students outperformed their counterparts in academic achievement, with less positive emotional engagement. These results indicated that multiple representations enhanced learning outcomes, but single representations fostered greater emotional engagement.

## Introduction and literature review

Physics intertwines with everyday lives and  fosters understanding of the physical phenomenon, t hrough observation, imagination, modelling, and comput ation. Previous studies have shown that simulations integr ated with multiple representations can simplify model buildin g and enhance students’ physics understanding (Chang et al., 2008; Geyer & Pospiech, 2019; Sumardi et al., 2022), with reported positive influences on students. Compared with single representations involve pure simulations to present complex physical phenomena without additional visualization to represent data (Jaakkola & Veermans, 2020), multiple representations refer to utilizing concrete representations (e.g., tables, c harts) to elucidate the direct relationships betwee n abstract principles and everyday phenomena (Ainsworth, 1999). Engaging with diverse information and processes, students transition from one representation to anot her, ultimately constructing a more profound knowledge understanding (Geyer & Kuske -Janßen, 2019; Kusumawati et al., 2015). However, th e application based on multiple representations in secondary physics education  is unexplored. More studies are needed for some pedagogical implications.

In classroom settings, students’ emotions are assoc iated with their positivity, interest, and enjoymen t, thereby shaping their learning achievements (Chen e t al., 2020; Finch et al., 2015; Pekrun et al., 200 6). Besides, previous studies have highlighted that students’ em otional status encompasses both positive emotions (e.g., enjoyment) and negative emotions (e.g., boredom, an xiety), which may impact their learning process and academic performance (Chen et al., 2020; Do & Schal lert, 2004). However, limited studies have explored the relationship between students’ emotional engagement  and their sustained learning performance in classr oom settings supported by multiple representations.

In this study, we utilized simulations with multiple representations to support classroom instruction and examined their influence on students’ achievements and emotional engagement. Our study addresses the following two research questions:

(1) Does the experimental group outperform the control group in terms of physics achievements?

(2) What changes can be observed in students’ emotional engagement in the experimental and control groups?

## Methods

Participants were recruited from grade 8, a regular  junior high school in China. The teacher was suppo rted with simulations on six lessons during one-semester instruction. These classes were randomly assigned to either the experimental or control group, both taught by the s ame physics teacher in Mandarin. In the experimental group (n = 36, 20 girls,  16 boys), the teacher utilized simulations integrated with multiple representations to enhance students’ learning. In the control group (n = 42, 20 girls, 22 boys), the teacher used simulati ons with single representations (e.g., Figure 1). No statistically significant differences were found be tween these two groups for student gender ratio, age, and class size. Furthermore, the t-test revealed no statistically significant differen ce in students’ prior knowledge (t =.574, p =.471) between the two groups, indicating the comp arability of these groups.

Figure 1 Simulations with Multiple or Single Representations for Two Groups Student academic achievements were collected, including pre-tests, monthly tests, and post-tests. A ttest was conducted on paired pre-tests and post-tests using SPSS software for RQ 1; as for RQ 2, we measured the students’ emotional engagement with self-reported six-point Likert scale questionnaires and semi-structured interviews with a subset of students (Chen et al., 2020; Pekrun et al., 2011). The interview data were first transcribed verbatim and then coded using NVivo software and cross-checked.

## Results

We observed improvements in academic achievement scores across both groups, as shown in Figure 2. However, there was no significant difference in their post-test scores (p =.323, t = 1.590, df = 76); specifically, the average score of students in the experimental group (M = 70.694, SD = 15.462) was slightly higher than that of the control group (M = 64.476, SD = 18.591).

Figure 2 Simulations with Multiple or Single Representations for Two Groups Fifty-seven valid questionnaires were collected. No significant difference was observed in enjoyment (p =.61, t =.514) and anxiety emotional engagement between the two groups at the pre-test stage (p =.323, t =.997). However, there was a significant difference in thei r post-test scores (Enjoyment: p =.003, t = 3.155; Anxiety: p =.034, t = 2.174); specifically, the average score of studen ts in the experimental group (Enjoyment: M (SD) = 3.994 (.964), Anxiety: M (SD) = 3.286 (.983)) differed with the control group (Enjoyment: M (SD) = 4.853 (1.322), Anxiety: M (SD) = 2.664 (1.165)). Interview data (see Appendix) showed simulations ha d a significant impact on the emotional engagement of students in b oth groups. Despite finding the classes interesting, students in both groups experienced a certain degree of anxi ety due to the challenges posed by multiple and sin gle representations.

## Discussion and conclusion

This study aimed to enhance physics classroom practice by integrating simulations with multiple representations. The overall improvement in both groups was the novelty of using simulations in classroom learn ing (Cheung & Slavin, 2013), which fostered interest and motivation among students, driving their engagement and contributing to their academic growth. However, multiple represe ntations utilization did not result in a significant difference in academic performance between the two groups, whi ch might be due to the teacher’s pedagogical strategies, which could have hindered their ability to take the  initiative in their learning process. Our future research will focus on exploring strategies to better utilize mul tiple representations to foster active learning and  deeper understanding of physics concepts.

The control group students exhibited more positive emotions (enjoyment) and le ss negative emotions (anxiety). However, the experimental group students faced increased difficulty in understanding because multiple representations may require students of greater mental effort or cognitive loading (Moreno & Mayer, 2007), which in turn heightened their anxiety toward learning. In future research, we aim at more processing data and evidence from students to better capture their continuous emotional engagement.
