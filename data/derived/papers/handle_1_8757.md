# Enabling Self-Regulated Learning in MOOCs Using Community of Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
One of the main reasons for learners to drop out from Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) is the lack of self-regulated learning (SRL) skills necessary to learn effectively in online environments with low levels of support and guidance. Although there is an agreement on the importance of SRL for MOOC learners, little research has examined which SRL strategies learners need help the most, or how to help them practice those strategies to further develop their SRL skills. This study identifies key SRL strategies that are crucial for MOOC learners to develop in order to improve their learning outcomes and increase course completion. Based on results from interviews and a questionnaire, recommendations were presented on using a chat feature embedded on MOOC platforms to enable the community of practice and help learners practice SRL strategies effectively.

## Introduction

COVID-19 pandemic has enabled a surge in enrollment  across Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) on platforms including Udemy and Coursera (Impey & Formanek, 2021). But MOOCs suffer from extremely high dropout rates of 90% or more, and the lack of self-regulated learning (SRL) skills can be an important factor that leads to high dropout in MOOCs (Terras & Ramsay, 20 15). Nawrot and Doucet (2014) found that the most common reasons for dropping out from MOOCs can be related to time management and metacognitive strategies such as goal setting and strategic planning, both of which are part of SRL. Although there is an agreement on the importance of  SRL to improve learning outcomes for MOOC learners and reduce course dropout rates, little re search has examined which SRL strategies learners n eed to develop the most, and how to help them practice those strategies to further develop their SRL skills. The purpose of this study is to identify key SRL strategies tha t are crucial for MOOC learners to improve their le arning outcomes and increase course completion, and explor e new features MOOC platforms can implement to help learners hone SRL skills. The study aims to answer the following questions: What are key challenges for MOOC learners to self-regulate their learning? Which SRL  strategies can help MOOC learners improve learning outcomes the most? How can MOOCs be improved to help learners practice crucial SRL strategies?

## Methods and data analysis

In order to understand key challenges for MOOC lear ners to self-regulate their learning, 15-minute int erviews with 31 MOOCs learners were conducted. All were recruited from an email sent to a group of graduate students in a research university in the United States. They  all had taken multiple MOOCs before and were in th eir late 20s to late 30s. 16 (51%) were female and 15 (49%) were male. Questions in the interviews focus on the learners’ personal learning experiences, attitudes toward MOOCs, and key challenges learning in MOOCs. To identify SRL strategies that are most helpful for MOOC learners to practice, a questionnaire aiming to evaluate MOOC learners’ level of mastery of six different SRL strategies was sent to the same 31 participants. The questionnaire was adapted from the work of Kizilcec et al. (2017) with 18 statements (3 statements per SRL strategy) that participants can select on a 5-point scale (1=not at all true for me; 5=very true for me) how true each statement is describing them while learning on MOOC platforms. The questionnaire was implemented in Google Forms, allowing participants to make their selections easily on a likert scale.

## Findings

Four types of challenges from MOOC learners emerged  from the interviews: lack of time, boredom, lack o f pressure or urgency, and lack of peer interactions. 74% participants mentioned the lack of time as a key challenge, and 77% participants mentioned boredom as an important reason that they dropped out. The third type of challenge for MOOC learners is the lack of pressure or urgenc y mentioned by 84% participants. One participant sa id, “I would love to have more strict deadlines and clear objectives for learning on MOOCs.” Lack of peer interactions was named by 90% participants as another key challenge. According to one participant, “I want an accountability buddy to check in regularly on my progress and goals and to ask questions.” Most of these challenges the participants are facing as MOOC learners can be interpreted as needing to learn SRL strategies. For example, the lack of time  can be interpreted as needing time management and goal setting strategies, and the lack of pressure or urgency can be interpreted as needing strategic planning strategies. The qualitative dat a helped uncover key areas of SRL strategies that students s hould practice to overcome those challenges: goal s etting, strategic planning, and help seeking strategies.

According to the questionnaire, elaboration and tas k strategies have the highest means, with 4.76 and 4.57 respectively, whereas goal setting and help seeking have the lowest means, with 4.10 and 4.19 respectively. It shows that participants have a high level of mas tery of SRL strategies such as elaboration and task strategies, but need more support with learning about goal sett ing and help seeking. Elaboration has the lowest st andard deviation of 0.42, whereas help seeking has the hig hest standard deviation of 0.83, showing a high var iation of participants' evaluation of their mastery of help seeking. The results revealed that goal setting and help see king are SRL strategies that MOOC learners should practice the most, which also matched with interview data that goal setting and help seeking are two of the four challenges MOOC learners are facing. It is importan t to focus on learners with a low level of mastery in goal setting and help seeking, and design interventions to help learners build SRL skills more effectively.

## Recommendations and implications

How can MOOCs be improved to help learners practice these SRL strategies like goal setting and help seeking? In order to foster help seeking, a sociocultural pe rspective is chosen because each MOOC includes a le arning community. Because MOOCs attract learners with different backgrounds and levels of expertise, in the c ontext of legitimate peripheral participation, some learne rs who are new to the subject are at the periphery of the community, whereas others are considered full participants (Lave & Wenger, 1991). The recommendation proposed is an embedded chat fea ture based on the idea of connecting more advanced learners to learners whoneed help in a community of practice. When learners need help and open the chat, they can see a list of other learners who are available to help, as well as their learning score which has been calculated using the course surveys, their engageme nt on discussion boards, and their assessment resul ts. The learning score shows the level of expertise a learner has on the subject, and can be seen as the learner’s position in the community of practice. Learners are advised to ask for help from people with learning scores higher but not too high compared to their own scores. This simulat es the community of practice, where more advanced participants help less advanced ones move closer to the center. After a learner identifies a potential helper, they can connect in real-time and work on the problem together. One expected effect this solution will have on learners is that it can encourage them to ask for help, since the chat feature is part of the platform and is easy to access. By making it easier for learners to ask for them, this solution can help learners practice the help seeking SRL strategy. Another expected effect is that the learners can feel a greater sense of belonging to the MOOC community. They can realize that they are not simply individual learners, but learning community participants who can get help and offer help to others.
