# Mapping Interventions of Teacher Reflective Dialogue

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This structured poster session brings together a community of scholars who conduct research on support for teachers' reflective dialogue and its outcomes in diverse contexts. Conjecture mapping is positioned as an approach to develop a shared language around research where teachers' reflective dialogue plays a central role with the aim to advance this diverse field.

Symposium introduction Teacher reflection has been recognized as a crucial  (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017) and frequent practice of expert teachers (Anderson & Taner, 2022). Research indicates that reflective dialogue with colleagues or coaches and based on representations of teachers’ own practice, such as video, holds potential for improving practice (vander Linden et al., 2022; Lefstein et al., 2020). Both practitioners and researchers understand what reflection is and how it is performed in multiple ways. While there a ppears to be little debate about its value, questions rema in how to facilitate it and what specific outcomes can realistically be expected. For example, the role of the facilitator to support reflection is of key importance, but als o challenging (Gelfuso & Dennis, 2014). Within the learning sciences, scholarly discussions about teacher refle ction have often taken place in different communiti es, each foregrounding different aspects of the design arran gements, like collaborative discourse (Lefstein et al., 2020), video clubs (van Es & Sherin, 2010), visualization to ols for video-based PD (Chen et al., 2020), video coaching (vander Linden et al., 2022).  Bringing together different approaches from diverse  contexts can enrich understanding of teachers' reflective dialogue, in particular because the design of learning arrangements is shaped by the theoretical background, as well as the contextual conditions (McKenney & Reeves, 2019). Building on the work from the ISLS 2023 pre-conference workshop on this theme (vander Linden et al., 2023), this structured poster session aims to strengthen scholarly dialogue by exploring synergies and differences in related, but distinct lines of resea rch. We think this is timely and important, not onl y to exchange ideas, but also because it can contribute to a shared language that supports the creation of argumentative grammars for how learning ensues through teachers' reflective dialog ue across the varied arrangements and contexts. Thi s is not to say that approaches and underlying theories must be assimilated, but rather that the articulation of differences and similarities, can reveal new insights, as well as important next steps for future research. This symposium positions conjecture mapping as an approach to structure syst ematic inquiry and to begin to develop a shared lan guage around research where teachers' reflective dialogue  plays a central role. Conjecture mapping is the wo rk of "specifying theoretically salient features of a learning environment design and mapping out how they are predicted to work together to produce desired outcomes" (Sand oval, 2014, p.19). Conjecture maps can be seen as l ogic models, illustrating how design embodiments yield  enactment processes, and how these result in specif ic outcomes. Two kinds of conjectures are distinguished: design conjectures explain why inputs influence enactment processes, while theoretical conjectures explain why enactment processes (will) lead to the targeted outcomes. This session features four individual poster contributions which together build an initial conjecture map for teacher reflective dialogue as shown in Figure 1. The first two posters focus on articulating design conjectures between design and enactment. Kobiela, Connolly, and Wallace discuss four design principles for reflec tive practice, namely practice being adaptive to local c ontexts, attention to forms of seeing, teachers as active collaborators, and the consideration of tools and norms. Vander Linden, Chen, and McKenney show how design of technology can be leveraged in video-based reflection interventions to attend to aspects of scale (depth, spread, sustainability, and shift), as well as how these scale aspects could be captured during enactment. Two other posters focus on articulating theoretical conjectures betwe en enactment and outcomes. Carvalho and Slotta show  how reflective dialogue around the design of critical action pedagogy has been of great importance to the solidification of the PLC. Finally, Walsh and Schunn show how expa nsive and strategic framing during teachers' reflec tive dialogue supports the development of adaptive expertise. During the 60-minute structured poster session, authors will first provide a short pitch to introduce their posters, followed by a breakout session where authors will discuss their poster contributions with the audience, and the session will be concluded with a panel discussion. Figure 1 Symposium Overview (individual posters in triangles) Poster 1: Conceptualizing teacher reflection as a professional practice Marta Kobiela, Edel Connolly and Adia Wallace Building upon the work of Schön (1983), teacher reflective practice is often described as an active and intentional process that enhances professional practice and ide ntity (e.g., Farrell, 2016; Rossi & Thorsen, 2019).  This conceptual poster aims to interrogate this descript ion by drawing upon theories from the learning scie nces and scholarship about professional practice. By bringin g together our varied positionalities as researcher s, teacher educators, and teachers across three different coun tries, we hope to offer new insights that other, mo re siloed perspectives fail to address.  We ask: (1) What does it mean to conceptualize teac her reflection and reflective dialogue as a professional practice? (2) What are design implications for supporting and studying teacher learning of reflection as a professional practice?

Drawing upon theoretical perspectives such as the s ituative perspective, distributed cognition, and mediational means, we conceptualize professional practice  as situated within social, historical, and cultural contexts and guided by the interactions and norms w ithin those contexts (Greeno, 2006; Werstch, 1998).  From this perspective, reflection as a professional practice is more than just a set of processes: It entails forms of activity that are shaped by one’s social, historical, and cu ltural context (e.g., standards, institutional pressures). How teachers learn to engage in reflective practice is shaped by the communities of practice that constitu te their profession (Lave & Wenger, 1991). We consider reflective dialogue then to capture the interactions that teachers have as they engage in reflection. Reflective dialogue occurs not only amongst individuals, but also as individuals interact with artifacts that embody socially and culturally constructed norms and perspectives. Building from these theoretical perspectives, we propose a preliminary framework of four theoreticallygrounded design principles for supporting teachers in learning to engage in reflection as a profession al practice (Table 1).  First, because professional practice is socially, h istorically, and culturally situated, educators may consider how to adapt what counts as reflective practice and how teachers learn reflective practice to local contexts (Lefstein et al., 2020). This includes attention to the disciplinary contexts and communities within which teachers are situated. Secondly, because professional practice involves developing a professional vision (Goodwin, 1994), when supporting teachers to learn reflective practice, it may be helpful to consider the forms of “seeing” that are part of reflecting and the socially-constructed norms that shape those forms of “seeing.” Thirdly, because learning professional practice involves apprenticeship, teachers may be empowered as active and engaged collaborators in their learning  of reflective practice. Finally, because interactions with tools are crit ical to the learning  of and engaging in professional practice, reflective practice may involve flexible consideration of norms of interacting with tools.

In reflecting on the nature of reflective practice, we have come to see the above design principles as one step towards imagining reflective practice in a dyn amic way, i.e. as a multi-dimensional spectrum. In this poster we will address our struggles in balancing generic design conjectures and context-specific demands. For example, common issues that manifest in different ways include: tim e constraints and pressures, a lack of professional support to empower teachers to develop this practice, and a lack of a commitment from policy makers to include teachers in shaping future policies. We developed this initial framework by gathering literature within the learning sciences and about professional practices and discussing key tenants from these readings and our own experiences as teachers, teacher educators, and researchers. Fo r the ISLS poster, we will continue to develop the framework through a more systematic review of the literature following these steps: (1) keyword search of Google  Scholar for articles usi ng terms such as “reflective practice”/  “reflection”/ “reflective dialogue” AND “teachers”/ “workplace learning”/  “teacher learning” and “professional practice” AND “learning;” (2) hand search of key journals in the learning sciences (e.g., Journal of the Learning Sciences); (3) review of abstracts to select articles that relate to the learning of professional practice and/or reflective practice; and (4) drawing upon our theoretical perspectives, analysis of articles to identify themes related to supports for professional and reflective practice and dialogue.

Poster 2: Scaling technology-rich support for teacher reflective dialogue Sara vander Linden, Gaowei Chen  and Susan McKenney Video-based interventions with a focus on teacher reflect ion through coaching have much potential to support teacher learning and development (Gaudin & Chaliès,  2015; vander Linden et al., 2022). While these ty pes of interventions can be effective, they are often chal lenging to sustain at scale, for several reasons. F irst, coaching interventions are costly due to the personalized na ture of the  support provided (Kraft & Blazar, 2018). Second, while researchers often act as coaches in coaching studies, the availability of high-quality coaches who can provide prolonged support based on effective progra ms is limited (Kraft & Blazar, 2018). Third, video data can illustrate complex classroom situations and serve a s input for learning, but the analysis of videos is  highly laborintensive, which can pose challenges for coaches wh o are already pressed for time. This poster explore s how technology can help address the aforementioned chal lenges at scale while also providing rich, sustained learning opportunities that are tailored to local needs (Kraft & Blazar, 2018). Scale can be conceptualized according to the depth,  spread, sustainability, and shift in ownership (Coburn, 2003). Depth concerns a deep change in hum an activity. Sustainability concerns the extent to which participants in the project have (the intention) to use project materials or pedagogical ideas after  a project ends. Spread concerns the extent to which others, outside  the project context, are using project materials a nd pedagogical ideas. Finally, a shift in ownership ca n be observed from external to i nternal when local systems come into place that support the adoption of materi als, adaptation of materials to local needs, as wel l as maintenance and dissemination.

Technology has the potential to support (coaching of) video-rich reflective dialogue at scale. Technology can support the analysis of discourse and text, and  natural language processing (NLP) can be used to a utomate discourse analysis and is often used in combination with learning analytics (McNamara et al., 2017). Dashboards display this visual information on key processes and illustrate patterns, often visualized in one screen to help users achieve their goals (Teasley, 2017), and can be foc used on the learner or instructor/ coach. Visual le arning analytics (VLA) combines visual and automated analy sis to provide interactive, process-oriented visualizations for coaches, tutors, and instructors, facilitating them to locate and (re)examine the relevant portions of video data and enhancing their focus and efficacy of reflectiv e dialogue (Chen, 2020). Conversational pedagogical  agents are intelligent systems that can engage in a dialog ue with learners in real-time, and fulfill the roles of coaches, tutors, or instructors (Gulz et al., 2011), and sup port student learning through the provision of meta cognitive suggestions (e.g. Hayashi, 2020) or discussion prompts (e.g. de Araujo et al., 2023). Poster 3: Facilitating pedagogical transformation: Teachers' reflective dialogue on adopting critical action education in the classroom Renato Carvalho and Jim Slotta This study was conducted during an international pr ofessional development (PD) workshop for teachers i n the Summer of 2023 in the context of a project called the Critical Action Learning Exchange (CALE; Carvalho et al., 2022, 2023). CALE has grown as a professional learning community (PLC) that connects educators from around the world engaged in designing, enacting and sharing critical action curricula. CALE curriculum aims to empower students in response to complex socio-environmental issues, such as climate change, socia l justice, and pandemics, that affect themselves and their communi ties. CALE is grounded in the theoretical framework  of Critical Pedagogy, a philosophy of praxis that underscores the importance of the educational process to intertwine theory, action, and reflection as a means to enable societal advancement toward social change and justice (Freire, 1970; Kincheloe, 2004). One central practice of Cri tical Pedagogy is the negotiation of a meaningful c urriculum with students. This requires a fundamental change i n the social relations of discourse in the classroo m: the unidirectional relationship between the teacher who  detains and imparts the knowledge and the students  who receive it has to give way to a dialogical method o f teaching and learning that engages both student and teacher in the production of knowledge (Shor et al., 2017). Within CALE, educators take part in professional development activities, including the collaborative design of n ew critical action lessons as well as reflective  dialogue about their problems of practice. Since 2020, researchers  have designed activities, resources, and technolog y environments to support the CALE PLC, testing and improving designs through iterative cycles of implementation and redesign (Carvalho et al., 2021).

This poster reports on the most recent implementati on of the CALE professional development model, which occurred in the Summer of 2023. Our research objective was to understand how reflective dialogue within a PLC can facilitate the epistemic shift that Cr itical Pedagogy requires. Participants (n=34) from 15 countries were K-12 teachers from upper elementary through universit y level. Workshop sessions were held on Zoom for two consecutive weeks in four three-hour sessions (12 hours in total). Each session was designed to facilitate the community's advancement in three mutually reinforci ng aspects: 1) the theoretical understanding of Cri tical Action Learning, 2) the collective design work of C ritical Action curricula, and 3) engagement in refl ective dialogue and development of shared identity as a professiona l learning community. In each session, participants watched presentations from more experienced members  of the community who reported on their successful designs and implementations of critical action lessons. They then participated in reflective dialogue on problems of practice and worked in small teams to design new  lessons (n=30 lessons in total) that would engage their students in critical action on topics related to climate change, diversity and inclusion, environment, health, media and technology, economy, mental health, and the future of work. We collected data from pre-and post-surveys, video recordings of the PD sessions (inclu ding Zoom breakout rooms), discussions on the CALE online pla tform, and the participants' design documents. We u tilized a framework for assessing teachers' readiness fo r pedagogical transformation (Jones and Swanson, 20 22) to analyze the data. Participants confronted the chall enges entailed by change of practice to focus on cr itical action education. They report a low level of support and encouragement for such a change from their educational systems (ministries, boards, etc.) Nevertheless, they repor t that being a member of a community of practice and participating in structured PD opportunities can effectively facilitate this paradigmatic shift. In particular, the role of reflective dialogue has been of great importance  to the solidification of the PLC, and often takes form in communications between teachers and peer mentors. O ur presentation will focus on this aspect of the CA LE professional development model.

Poster 4: Reconceptualizing framing theory and research for coach-guided teacher reflection: The role of expansive and strategic framing for developing adaptive teaching expertise Marguerite E. Walsh and Christiand. Schunn Education researchers widely agree that adaptive expertise is central to facilitating student-centered instructional reforms (Munson et al, 2021). Expert-guided reflection on teaching artifacts is often cited as critical for supporting teachers to transform entrenched practices and skillfully enact new kinds of instruction (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017). However, our understanding of the specific m echanisms that facilitate teachers to develop adaptive teaching expertise in these contexts is still underdeveloped (Carbonell et al., 2014). In this concept ual poster, we describe a novel framework that draws from existing  research as well as our own empirical work to study and develop coaching routines that support robust teach er learning and change outcomes. We specifically dr ew from prior analyses of hundreds of pages of coach-teacher reflective dialogues across disciplines (ma th and literacy), conducted as a part of larger project, to identify interactional patterns that consistentl y characterize the work of highly effective coaches (i.e., coaches whose teacher partners showed the highest growth in instructional quality). Together with in-depth analysis of adaptive expertise theory and research, our framework specifically proposes a revised theory of frames and framing  as a central mechanism for supporting teachers’ ada ptive reasoning, problem-solving, and knowledge transfer in reflection-based coaching. Rooted in socio-linguistic research, frames are interpretive schemata individuals use to repres ent and make sense of a situation (Goffman, 1974). In our framework, we define a teaching frame as a kind of interpretive schemata comprised of 2 elements —teaching schema  and teaching principle —that jointly activate to define a particular classroom situation or problem in situ. The teaching schema refers to a basic categorical  distinction involving one or more components of the “instructio nal triangle” (i.e., interactions among students, t eacher, and task) that are emphasized (or not) within a particu lar instructional episode (e.g., ‘students completi ng work’ or ‘teacher eliciting student ideas’). The teaching principle shapes the significance of these details in relationship to an envisioned goal state, particularly with respect to epistemology (what knowledges are relevant and valued) and social positioning (how participants’ expected role s in these processes;  Greeno, 2009). Because a key goal for education reform is to promote students’ active lea rning (Engle & Conant, 2002), teachers will likely need help to shift towards less traditional, more student-centered ways of framing their teaching interactions. As is detailed below, we propose two dimensions of frames and framing—expansive and strategic—that we argue are especially needed to support this kind of shift.

Building on prior research (Engle et al., 2012), we define expansive framing in a teacher learning context as the scope of applications enabled by the teaching frame across time and setti ngs.
