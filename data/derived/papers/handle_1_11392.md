# $30 Million in Missing Funds: How One Data Scientist Represented Civic Values in an Investigation of a CO2 Emissions Policy

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Data science education scholars are looking for guidance about how to teach youth to practice making ethical considerations throughout the data science lifecycle (National Academies, 2018). Such guidance should draw from images of data science that are grounded in empirical studies of professionals. In this paper, I present a case study of a data scientist at a 501(c)(3) non-profit who investigated a city policy designed to reduce CO2 emissions. I examine how the data scientist sensed whether a set of values, that he shared with the public, were represented in the policy. I will discuss implications for data science education.

## Introduction

Data science is a rapidly emerging discipline, with  a dizzying increase of university and high school programs, courses, curricula, activities, and standards. The  National Academies of Science, Engineering, and Med icine convened a workshop on data science education (2018) where they discussed what a data scientist should  know. They emphasized that it is important for data scientists to have experience making “ethical considerations in each step of and throughout the data science life cycle (i.e. when posing a question; collecting, cleaning,  and storing data; … exploratory analysis and visualization; making infe rences; and communicating results)” (ibid, page 30). The workshop authors called for ethics to be taught in standalone courses and integrated into coursework. The National Academies workshop relied on images of how ethics are considered throughout the work of the data science. These images stand to be built on in two ways. One is that they drew from self-reports and surveys, which are limited for depicting the practice of data science; alsoneeded are ethnographic studies. Second, while images of case studies of how ethics take a back seat to p rofit in data science practiced in Big Tech can be useful (O’Neil, 2017), alsoneeded are images of data science that prioritize civic and democratic values above profit. I have been building such an image by studying a 50 1(c)(3) charitable non-profit in a large city. In this paper, I will focus on how data scientists consider ethics throughout the data science lifecycle.

In this paper, I take core values to mean principles that people use to organize their lives. People engage with their core values through their body and visual faculties; people sense when their values are not in accordance with an action, which they use to build their sense of right and wrong, their ethics. This paper is about how one data scientist sensed whether a set of values, that he personally held and were  shared by the public, were being represented in a  city policy initiative. I understand data science to be a process of simult aneously representing values and facts in written text, following actor network scholars  (Law, 2008). I understand the  data scientist’s sensing of values to be a form of disciplined perception, which refers to the learned ways that people in a discipline see and interpret their phenomena through their tools and representations (Stevens & Hall, 1998). In the current paper I will empirically demonstrate how one data scientist engaged three of his core values

— sustainability, service, and transparency — to investigate a city-level greenhouse gas reduction policy. He

found that city employees charged with administering the policy failed to carry those values forward, resulting in $30 million in missed public funds. He gathered evidence by using his senses, including seeing a visual discrepancy that indicated  a deleted file, a sense of time  (“suspicious timing”), and locutions that indicated  that city staff had deviated from proper  record keeping protocol. He then wrote  a blog post and a report to the State Inspector General, and proposed that the energy policy be revised to more closely represent those values. This paper speaks to conversations in the Learning Sciences ab out data science education. The paper provides an empirical case of how data scientists engage in textual practices where they consider ethics throughout the data science lifecycle, responding directly to the National Academies  (2018) and speaking to critical data literacy scholarship (Louie, 2022). The case supports designs of activities for data science education.

## Methods

This paper draws from a broader corpus of work on data sc ientists at a non-profit that I call Civic Technology Volunteers (CTV), where I have been conducting an ethnographic stud y since 2022. The CTV is in a large midwestern city that I call Gotham. CTV members meet weekly, after work, to get together into teams to use data to address public matters of concern. From 2022 to 2023, I followed one team project th at worked for transit accessibility (Nguyen & Stevens, 2024). Since December 2023, I have been following another project team at the CTV’s Climate Change Team, called Electrify Gotham.

For the current paper, I will use data I have colle cted about Electrify Gotham’s activities. I engaged in data collection oriented at two time periods. One time period was December 2023 to April 2024. I have been a participant observer of the team since December 2023 until April 2024, where I collected video-recordings of the team’s weekly meetings. During that time, I also collected the following documents: message threads from the team’s Slack communications platforms, computer programs and the HTML text for a blog post from the GitHub software development platform. These sources represent roughly 25 hours and 20 pag es of documents. I focused on one particular data scientist, who I refer to as  Vincenzo, who started Electrify Gotham. After April 2024, I went back and conducted two 90-minute semi-structured interview s, where I focused on retroactively understanding how the data scientist came to hold his expressed values, and to start Electrify Gotham. My analytic approach was to follow across time and space how people and materials come together into stable associations, both over extended time and at the level of tasks (Latour, 2007). In Latour’s terminology I am studying an actor network. By tracing associations, I identified how Vincenzo enacted three values — environmental sustainability, service, and transparency. I will lay out the case as follows: first I will draw on my two interviews with Vincenzo, to briefly describe his core values and the start of the Electrify Gotham project up through December 2023, which lead up to the current  case. Then, I will recount a series of events from January to April 2024, where Vincenzo engaged in a data science investigation. I separate  the events into vignettes.  In each vignette I present ethnographic data from video-recordings and field notes, then show how Vincenzo enacted the three values. Vincenzo is a data scientist at the CTV. In high school, Vincenzo’s family fell on hard times and relied on help from G otham City and volunteer service workers, an experience that he credits with his belief in the importance of service. From 2014 to 2018, Vincenzo earned a computer science degree. He began to volunteer for CTV and gained an appreciation for Open Data and government transparency. During COVID, Vincenzo joined multiple climate activism nonprofits  and developed stronger environmental commitments.

In early March 2023, Vincenzo began to look for another way to use his skills as a data scientist to support a Climate Change effort. He read Gotham’s Climate Action Plan and learned that the city’s largest source of CO2 emissions was buildings. He read about Gotha m’s Environmental Benchmarking Ordinance (EBO)  of 2013, the city’s largest policy for reducing building emissions by requiring that all buildings publish their data on the city’s Open Data portal. Vincenzo, after seeing that his own alma was a polluter and feeling compelled to act, started a project to rank the city’s top polluting building s and to mobilize citizens to act politically to el ectrify those buildings. From March to December 2023, Vincenzo built that ranking system. Vignette 1: Vincenzo filed a FOIA request for government records By late January 2024, Vincenzo noticed that  Gotham’s Energy Benchmarking Ordinance listed a number of buildings that had not submitted data. So Vincenzo went in search of answers by re-reading the legal text of the EBO that amended Gotham City’s municipal code. In the EBO legal text, he read a section titled “enforcement”, which stated that Gotham City would fine buildings for no t complying with the EBO. He also noticed that the department of Gotham City Government that was charg ed with enforcing the fines was  the Department of Business Affairs and Consumer Affairs (DBCA). Vincenzo filed a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request to DBCA requesting that they share their records of enforcement with Climate Defense. In vignette 1, Vincenzo acted in accordance with two values, environment and transparency, that he held personally, and expected of his elected government.  For Vincenzo, the EBO text carried forward Gotham City’s plan to reduce emissions. Vincenzo also exercised his right as a citizen to a transpar ent government. He worked to parse the bureaucratic legal terminology of the doc ument to identify the DBCA as the department respon sible for enforcement, and he also invoked the Freedom of Information Act (5 U.S.C. § 552) (FOIA), which enshrines citizens’ rights to request access to  records from any federal agency  which federal agencies are required by law to comply with. Throughout vignette 1, Vincenzo engaged with the DBCA around two values, environmen t and transparency, by drawing on the materials of legal text and FOIA law. Vignette 2: Vincenzo examined the DBCA’s response to his FOIA request A week later, on February 3 rd, employees from the DBCA answered the FOIA request. DBCA included the original legal text and a notification letter for b uilding owners,. But when Vincenzo looked through, he noticed that the DBCA employees wrote that “there were no m atching records”, which he registered as a discrepa ncy between what he had read in the legal ordinance tex t. Vincenzo noted that even though Gotham City typically “complies as weakly as possible”, by using all the time allotted for their response, request extensions, or contesting requests, they were still required to keep records of all financial transactions including fines or dates of collection. Through vignette 2, Vincenzo used his knowledge of proper protocols in Gotham City bureau cracy, to register gaps between those  protocols and the data recording and communication actions he observed of the DBCA, and that led him to question whether the public service employees had properly kept records, and therefore possibly eliding two values he expected them to uph old — service and transparency. Sensing a possibility that those values were not being upheld in the EBO system, Vincenzo kept investigating. Vignette 3: Vincenzo registered “suspicious timing” Two weeks later, on February 20 th, Vincenzo came to the CTV headquarters  to continue his investigation. He observed on the DBCA’s website that on February 5th, a mere two days after his FOIA request, they had changed a particular document —an official legal notice letter notifying building owners of their legal responsibilit y to comply with the EBO law— which he called “suspicious timing”. Vincenzo then proceeded to use an instrument called Wayback Machine, to visually check any document on the internet against its past versions. He found that the new legal notice did not mention financial penalties. He inferred “that tells me someone [at the DCBA office] updated the notice to now inform building owners th at there were fines… and left information hidden…[a nd worked to produce] a paper trail”.

In vignette 3, Vincenzo investigated a possible breakdown in the value of transparency from the DBCA. Vincenzo relied on his sense of time (“suspicious timing” between the deletion and replacement of a legal notice without explanation), which he confirmed visually with an archive instrument. By the end of Vignette 3, Vincenzo had evidence the DBCA had hid information thereby failing to hold to the value of transparency as expected. Vignette 4: Vincenzo calculated the extent of the DBCA’s failure to enforce the EBO ordinance Vincenzo and Osuke worked together to figure out exactly how much the DBCA employees had missed in potential fines for non-compliant buildings. Vincenzo interacted with the Data Portal to query and filter data after 2018 and reporting status, and concluded that 2,905 buildings did now submit. Vincenzo also read the policies for fine collection. Vincenzo and Osuke ran a calculation by writing a line of code in Visual S tudio, which showed that from 2019-2023, the city had missed $26,726,000 in  fines. Vincenzo and Osuke documented  their investigation on GitHub.

In vignette 4, Vincenzo built a fact — the number $26,726,000 in missing fines from the DB CA. Vincenzo drew on multiple media — a Python computer program, the City Data Portal, and the text of legal notice describing fine collection. Vincenzo’s fact constituted evidence of the extent to which  the DBCA had failed to uphold its service of carrying out the terms of the EBO ordinance as it had been voted on and passed. Vignette 5: Vincenzo wrote a report of the investigation The next day, on February 28th, Vincenzo wrote a blog post on the Electrify Gotham website. In the blog post he described his findings, which were that Gotham City had failed to collect $30,590,000 in fines, and that had “reduced accountability and transparency”. Vincenzo backed up his findings with evidence  — he included his calculations and the city portal data source, the Wayback Machine’s image capture of the old legal notice, a copy of his FOIA request and the DBCA’s response, and a URL link to EBO ordinance text. Vincenzo argued that Gotham City may not meet its expressed 2040 emissions reductions targets. Vincenzo juxtaposed the missing $30 million with equally costly and sought for programs like lead pipe replacement. Vincenzo suggested that the city redesign the EBO law and initiative to fine buildings based on their size and wealth. In vignette 6, Vincenzo’s blog post described how Gotham City had failed t o uphold three of its values

— environment, transparency, and service, and marshaled evidence in multiple media. Vincenzo addressed his

blog post to an audience of taxpayers, with the power to demand that their government work for them.

## Vignette 6: Vincenzo publicized the results of his investigation

From March to April  2024, Vincenzo worked to publicize his investigation across Gotham  City. Vincenzo sent his story to a tip line at the Gotham’s premier new spaper the Gazette and, when that did not receive a  response, he forwarded the request to the managing editor. Vincenzo also tweeted his blog post, and edited its metadata  to make it so that search engines would prioritize it in results shown in internet searches. Finally, Vincenzo with the Climate Defense nonprofit contacted the Office of the State Inspect or General (OSIG), which is charged with “investigating allegations of fraud, waste, abuse, mismanagement, misconduct, and violations of the Et hics Act (5 [State]CS 430) by State employees”. Currently Vincenzo is waiting to hear from the Inspector General’s office. In vignette 6, Vincenzo worked on publicity. He worked to circulate his report of his investigat ion through multiple channels and actors — citizens, a journalist, a newspaper managing editor, Twitter influencers, and the State Inspector General.

## Discussion

In the current paper I have demonstrated how one da ta scientist investigated government agency employees who were charged with administering an energy benchmarking policy. Vincenzo kept in view a set of a core values — sustainability, service, and transparency. Vincenzo sensed when these values were not being represented  (e.g. “suspicious timing”), his memory (of proper bureauc ratic protocol), his visual faculties (e.g.  FOIA, visual comparisons using the Wayback Machine). Vincenzo understood written text (t he EBO policy legislation, his blog post) to be a place for him to argue for these  values to be represented in the policy initiative.  Vincenzo engaged his three focal values  by engaging in multiple overlapping iterations of the data science lifecycle, of data collection (1,3,4), calculation (4), and communicat ion (1,5,6). This empirically based case of how ethics are considered throughout the data science lifecycle responds directly to a call from the National Academies (2018). This case study also suggests ways that the practices taught by the  emerging discipline of data science education can and should expand.  The case abounds with practices that can be turned into activities and lessons, e.g. how to make sense of legal ordinances, craft a FOIA response, use the Wayback Machine, pitch to a reporter. All of these involve reading and writing, and speak  to literature in critical data literacy (Louie, 20 22). This case study also suggests possible ideas for integrating youth’s values into data science education. While in this case Vincenzo expressed values of sustainability, service, and transparency, my broader fieldwork shows that Vincenzo focused on accessibility in his other data science projects. It  would make good sense to build on established approaches that start with  students’ values, connect them to values shared by publics, and re-engage them throughout the data science lifecycle. I am currently working to design an educational pro gram, based on this case study and others, to teach youth to use data to represent democratic values within policy in their cities.
