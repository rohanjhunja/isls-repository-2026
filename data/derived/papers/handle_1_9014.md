# Student Reflections on Data Investigations of Local and Extreme Weather: A Cultural Historical Activity Theory Perspective

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study analyzes middle-school students' reflections on working with large-scale scientific data to investigate local extreme weather using the digital tool CODAP. Drawing from student focus group interviews and Cultural Historical Activity Theory, we examine factors that appeared to impede or facilitate data science learning in a sample of rural middle schools.

## Introduction

There is increasing demand for data science skills in modern science (e.g., developing climate data models). Data science requires the use of digital tools to manage, visualize, analyze, and communicate findings from extensive amounts of data (Hey, Tansley, & Tolle, 2009). Many argue that learning data science can lead to enhanced scientific literacy among the public (Ceccucci, Tamarkin, & Jones, 2015). Middle school students currently have limited exposure, however, to working with authentic large-scale data (Wilkerson & Polman, 2020). Our project has been collaborating with rural science teachers in northern New England to design and implement two curriculum units in which students investigate local extreme weather phenomena using largescale weather data and the data analysis tool CODAP. Drawing on student focus group interview data, this study examines challenges and opportunities for integrating data science into rural middle-school science classrooms.

## Theoretical Framework

We use Engstrom’s (1987) Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) to analyze students’ science classroom contexts and factors that may impede or facilitate data science learning. CHAT suggests identifying the following activity components: Subjects, or the histories and characteristics of major participants; the object, or the activity’s goals; tools or mediated artifacts, or the socially shared cognitive or physical resources; rules and norms, which govern social interactions; the community, or the group

## context; the division of labor, how

responsibilities are shared; and the outcome, or the product of the activity. CHAT attends to the contradictions (i.e., conflicting ideas or experiences at the time of change or reform) and affordances of activity components to understand why activities may succeed or fail. Previous studies have used CHAT to understand how subjects’ backgrounds and interests may shape scientific norms, rules, and practices and affect classroom activities (e.g., Saka, Southerland, & Brooks, 2009). Others have looked at contradictions in science teaching activities to gain insight into the culture and outcomes of specific programs (Roth and Tobin, 2002). Using CHAT, this study addresses the following question: What contradictions and affordances for learning data science are present in rural middle-school science classrooms when investigating local extreme weather?

## Methods

Study participants were middle-school students (age s 12-14) from four U.S. rural schools in northern N ew England. Study data are from five student focus group interviews conducted in winter/spring 2021, each lasting 45-60 minutes with 2-4 students per group. All inte rviews were conducted through Zoom, recorded, and transcribed. Initial coding categories were based o n CHAT components. Coded excerpts were analyzed usi ng micro-level discourse analysis (Gee, 2010). For ins tance, microanalysis led to the following subcatego ries for Subjects: a) student academic background in data and b) student experiences with local weather phenome na. Themes were organized around contradictions and affordances.

## Findings

Preliminary findings suggest that contradictions and affordances fall under four major themes: 1) building a local classroom data source, 2) digital tools for conceptual and epistemic learning, 3) prior student experiences/interests, and 4) shifting scientific and pedagogical norms. ## Discussions and Conclusions

Integrating authentic data practices in science classrooms goes beyond "cognitive skills and statistical algorithms" (Radinsky, Tabak, & Adach, 2021, p. 1072). It requires attention to social-cultural dynamics within classroom cultures and the histories students bring to their learning activity. Because daily-life decisions and future careers are becoming more data dependent, learning scientists are exploring effective ways to integrate data in school curricula. Building innovative, digital tools and meaningfully designed educational activities are crucial steps. However, researchers need to understand how students engage with these tools and view the importance of data for their lives and communities. We contribute to these efforts by offering a CHAT perspective for analyzing students' reflections, which can help reveal unseen social, cultural, and historical factors when learning data science skills in a weather science context.
