# How Inquiry-Based Learning Approach Affect the Impact of Augmented Reality on Science Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite the potential advantages of using Augmented Reality (AR) in education, most of the existing AR studies have failed to analyse the impact of pedagogical approaches as well as the pedagogical strategies of implementing AR in teaching and learning. An inquirybased learning framework, coined QIMS, was proposed in this study by considering AR affordances. Through a quasi-experimental design, this study compared three conditions for science learning in a primary school. Results indicated that though students did not demonstrate statistically significant improvement in their academic performance when AR was used, students’ self-directed learning and creative thinking skills did significantly improve via experiencing the QIMS inquiry-based learning activities. The use of AR played a more significant role in enhancing students’ critical thinking and knowledge creation efficacy. Integrating QIMS and AR was more effective for students with lower abilities. The study provided suggestions on how to integrate AR with inquiry-based learning in science learning.

## Introduction

Augmented Reality (AR) technology, with its current advances, has become readily available in education. While technology can be a catalyst for transforming the n ature of teaching and learning, it needs to be util ised with appropriate pedagogies, as pedagogy is a crucial co mponent in the employment of AR technology (Chadeea  & Prinsloo, 2019). Inquiry-based learning (IBL) is one of the most common pedagogies in AR interventions (Wen & Looi, 2019; Garzón, et al., 2020). AR-enabled inquiry activities could engage learners in an immersive context that enhances scientific investigations –where stud ents can collect data outside the classroom, intera ct with an avatar, or communicate face-to-face with peers in a  more authentic setting (Dunleavy et al., 2009). De spite the potential advantages of using AR in education and i ts suitability for supporting science learning thro ugh simulation activities, AR’s concrete uses are relatively not as well understood as that of other technologies (Wu & Wen, 2020). Most existing studies have failed to analyse the impact of pedagogical approaches as well as the pedagogical strategies of implementing AR in teachi ng and learning (Garzón, et al., 2020). To respond to this need, this study works on investigating how the IBL approach can be integrated with AR-supported formal science learning and if and how it can help to improve students’ academic performances and 21st century skills. AR-based Inquiry-based Learning Activity Design QIMS inquiry framework for guiding AR activity design In this study, we articulated a framework for scien ce learning leveraging AR technologies: Questioning, Investigating, Making and Synthesising (QIMS). With reference to Kolb’s cycle (2014) in the comprehension and transformation dimensions, students learn in the AR environment to address our proposed QIMS framework. (1) Questioning: Students are guided to raise questions  in a topic; (2)Investigating: They will have a con crete experience of the phenomenon through  AR simulation s and reflect on that experience; (3) Making: They will apply the formation of abstract concepts (analysis) and generalisations (conclusions) which are then used to test hypotheses, resulting in new experiences and inquir ies; (4) Synthesising: They conclude their findings  to summarise knowledge to capture both comprehension and transformation dimensions. Learning package of plant reproduction The learning package on the topic of plant reproduction was designed based on the QIMS framework, meant for grade 5 students.  It is centred on a story where a group of aliens need help in growing plants, as their planet is in ICLS2022 Proceedings 1069 © ISLS trouble and their plants are unable to thrive there. The students were tasked to help the aliens, with their acquired knowledge on plants. The QIMS cycle includes (1) Questioning: Students ask questions about the alien’s planet and on the topic of plants; (2) Investigating: Students concretise their experience of the phenomena by using AR tools and conduct experiments related to the topic.  They also note down their reflections and complete  some worksheets on the subject; (3) Making: Students create a plant to save the aliens’ planet. They develop hypotheses on why their creations can save the planet by apply ing the concepts they had learnt; (4) Synthesising:  They conclude by presenting their plant creations with supporting explanations on why their plants are feasible. The Plant Lifecycle AR App was developed for the topic of plant reproduction, built using the Unity3D engine, with AR functionality provided by the Vufor ia plugin. ## Methodology

A quasi-experimental design method was used to set up an experiment that lasted for 2 weeks to determine whether AR-supported IBL significantly improved pupils’ science learning performance and learning competencies better than inquiry-based learning without AR or non-inquiry-based learning. Two research questions were proposed to investigate the students’ academic performance and 21st century skills in different learning design condit ions. They are: (1) Did AR-supported inquiry-based scienc e learning generate a more positive impact on pupil s’ learning outcome? (2) Did AR-supported AR-based inquiry-based science learning have a more positive impact on pupils’ 21st skills? Which aspects were these reflected in?

Upon setting the context, the teacher got  students to ask questions that they were curious a bout (Qquestioning). Students learnt about the structure of plants independently, through The Flower Anatomy activity (Fig. 1a) of the Plant Lifecycle AR App. There was little formal ins truction from the teacher. These tasks were designed to be open-ended and intended for students to explore and investigate independently before the teacher gave them the answers and explanations.

Similarly, the teacher in class B framed the lesson with the same story of the aliens. She did not use the AR app but led students in a cycle of questioning a nd investigating. She brought different types of se eds for students to touch and feel, guiding them to think a bout why and how the seeds could help the aliens le arn more about pollination and seed dispersal. The teacher in class C did not relate the story of the aliens, nor did she make use of AR or QIMS. As in traditional classes, she employed a more teacher-led and direct instructive approach. The students did conduct experiments but were given more explicit instructions.

Instruments The pre-test checks students’ existing conceptual k nowledge of the topic of plant reproduction before the intervention. It was a pen-and-paper test with a total score of 16 marks. The test had ten questions and the students were given about 20 mins to complete it. The survey  questionnaire, adapted from Chai et al. (2015) mea suring students’ perceptions of 21st century learning practices, was administered to students after the intervention. The post-survey, consisting of 5 scales, was administrated online in the week of the last intervention. A total of 112 students’ responses were collected and analysed. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficients for the survey was 0.966, and the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficients of each scale are above 0.8. We also conducted online interviews with teachers. The three teachers were interviewed individually via zoom. Each interview lasted about an hour. The teachers’ interviews were conducted in a semi-structured format including questions such as – how they perceived students’ attitudes towards the use of AR; their opinions on the use of the QIMS framework.

## Impact on learning results

We performed an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to examine the significance of the meandifferences among the three learning design conditions. The post-test score was used as the dependent variable and the pre-test score was used as covariance. The results indicated no si gnificant difference in learning outcomes among the  three conditions, F (2,111) = 0.303, p = 0.739. Though the estimated marginal mean of the class using AR with the QIMS framework was higher than the other classes, t he LSD post-hoc comparisons showed that there were no significant differences among 3 classes.

Impact on students’ 21st learning skills To interpret whether and in which aspects AR and QI MS had an impact on students’ 21 st learning skills, we conducted separate analyses of variance (ANOVA) for  each scale. As shown in Table 2, there are signifi cant differences among classes in self-directed learning [F (2,109) = 4.040, p = 0.020], critical thinking [F (2,109) = 3.142, p = 0.047] and creative thinking [F (2,109) = 4.079, p = 0.020], but no significant differences among classes could be found in other scales.

LSD Post-hoc tests further revealed that there were no significant differences between the experimental classes with or without using AR in all the scales.  However, the pairwise LSD comparisons revealed tha t there were significant differences in mean values between the class using AR with QIMS and the control class in selfdirected learning (p= 0.012), creative thinking (p = 0.007), critical thinking (p = 0.018), and knowledge creation efficacy (p= 0.036). There were also significant differences in mean values between the class using AR without QIMS and the control class in self-directed learnin g (p= 0.021), creative thinking (p= 0.048), and authentic problem-solving (p= 0.021).

The findings suggested that there was little differ ence between the class using AR with QIMS and the class without using AR but with QIMS. Yet integrating AR with QIMS had a more significant effect on students’ self-directed learning, creative thinking, and critical thinking skills, especially for the low progress learners. This is consistent with the findings drawn from the teachers’ interviews. According to the teacher B who used QIMS, the activities based on QIMS provided students the opportunity to be creative, as they were able to do their own questioning and generate their own ideas on how to solve a question or problem. Though IBL was emphasised in ICLS2022 Proceedings 1071 © ISLS the curriculum, the teachers pointed out that the QIMS was more student-led. Teacher A using AR also mentioned that “with the use of AR, we need to be able to give students autonomy for their own learning.” It was worth noting that the use of AR did not affect students’ perceptions on authentic problem-solving. On the one hand, this finding might indicate that t he immersion of paper-based AR was not sufficient. On the other hand, this might suggest that instead of replicating real-world experiments or being limited in the classroom, our subsequent AR activity design should further st rengthen the link with the living environment.  Bes ides, although the experimental Class A had a higher self-rating in the scale of collaborative learning, the re was no significant difference among the three classes. This might indicate that the collaboration script embedded in the AR and QIMS-based learning activities could be further improved.

## Discussion and Conclusion

The aim of the study was to investigate how to inte grate IBL with AR in science learning to improve students’ learning performance and 21 st century skills. This paper presents an inquiry-bas ed learning framework (QIMS) that consists of interactive processes of questioni ng, investigating, making and synthesizing. Instead  of approaching inquiry as purely discovery on one’s ow n, Mayer (2004) has stated that there should be gui dance, structure and objectives stated in the inquiry-based activities. The paper reports an empirical study to demonstrate how the QIMS framework was used to guide AR learnin g activity design, and the impact on grade 5 studen ts’ learning outcomes and development of soft skills. The findings indicate that the class using AR did not outperform the class following QIMS without AR. However, the two classes engaging in QIMS activities outperformed the class without QIMS. Though students did not demonstrate statistically significant improvements in their academic performance, their self-directed learning and creative thinking skills did significantly improve via e xperiencing the QIMS inquiry-based learning activities.

This study has several important limitations. The main drawback is the limited time of intervention. Secondly, as the AR app is self-developed, the user  interface and 3D animations can still be further p olished to better engage learners. Our initial hypothesis is that the class using both AR and QIMS could outperform the other two classes. However, this is not supported by the data of this study. It may be caused by the interve ntion time and/or the AR app design. The AR app will be further updated, and relevant work has been planned and will be conducted in more schools. The following studies wi ll also explore the impact of AR-based QIMS learnin g packages on different topics. Besides, future resea rch will pay attention to how students interact dur ing the learning processes to further explain how individual differences influence on the success of the AR activities and how to design AR activities to benefit diverse learners.
