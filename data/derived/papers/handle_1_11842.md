# Learning from Failure Through Game Testing Feedback in Virtual Collaborative Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how learning from failure can be supported through game design feedback activities in a virtual collaborative environment. Through analyzing video recordings of three checkpoint sessions in a two-week virtual game design camp with nine youth participants (ages 8-13), we identified six feedback styles and examined how designers responded to feedback. Our findings reveal that game design feedback in virtual environments transcends traditional linear relationships, forming a complex discursive relationship among testers, designers, and game artifacts. Screen sharing emerged as a crucial boundary object, facilitating communication and transforming traditional critique sessions into collaborative learning experiences. Facilitators played vital roles by modeling playfulness, vulnerability, and productive failure responses. The study demonstrates that successful virtual design feedback requires thoughtfully curated environments that balance structured facilitation with playful engagement. These findings have implications for design education in virtual settings and suggest new approaches for supporting learning from failure in collaborative contexts.

## Introduction and background literature

Learning from failure is a crucial process for fost ering creativity (Stretch & Roehrig, 2021) and prom oting longterm content learning and skill development (Simpso n & Maltese, 2017; Kapur, 2016). Design activities expose students to authentic emotional and technical failures—experiences rarely available in formal education se ttings (Blikstein, 2013). Even though scholars acknowledge the importance of engaging learners in collaborative design activities to support effective learning from failu re, our knowledge of how to support this type of le arning is insufficient. Most of the literature on learning from failure focuses on the individual failure factor. Organizational learning literature has an extensive representation  of failure management studies (Cannon & Edmonson, 2005), but few have a focus on children. Therefore, we n eed to understand the learning process from failure  and understand what instructional activities might support groups to make failure productive (Yan et al., 2022). In this study, we situate our focus on the process of learn ing from failure in a fully virtual collaborative d esign environment.

Observing the increasing digital divide and inequit y during the pandemic, we recognized the challenge, and opportunity of transforming the conventional in-person collaborative design club into a virtual lea rning environment over Zoom (Xia, Yan, & Jiang, 2024). In such a learning environment, there were no ta ngible artifacts to make and tinker with. Rather, learners  are working on digital design artifacts. Our goal was to offer such learning opportunities to students from across  the nation and, being fully virtual, participants attended our sessions from their homes with their own devices and Internet. By doing so, we could work with childre n from different time zones. However, being fully virtual brought challenges, such as the challenge of adapti ng commercial tools and platforms to allow verbal and non-verbal communication (Xia, Yan, & Jiang, 2024) and establishing effective communication and knowledge sharing (Swart, Bond-Barnard, Chugh, 2022). When adding the layer of learning from failure, which requires a psychologically safe space for participants to share fail safely (Cannon & Edmondson, 2005), is an even more challen ging task in online space, where building relations hips and trust tends to be more challenging for online participants (Swart, Bond-Barnard, Chugh, 2022). In this study, we explore how learning from failure can be support ed through game design feedback activities with the  aid of collaboration tools and technology in the virtual c ollaborative design context. To guide our study, we  ask the following research questions: 1) What were the emerging patterns of feedback given a nd received in a virtual collaborative game design environment ?  2) How does the feedback activity support learning fro m failure in a virtual collaborative game design environment?

Failure is interpretive, interactive, and experiential under the game design context In general, failure is defined as the gap between the learners' results and the expected outcome. Though this simple definition worked for some contexts, it was unsucce ssful in describing others with its method of viewi ng failure as absolute and objective.  More and more scholars, especially those who study failure in design and making activities, have started to recognize the interpret ive nature of failure (Maltese & Anderson, 2018). I nstead of treating learning from failure as a cognitive ability or inability, we viewed learning from failure as a phenomenon that values the learners’ agentic power of interpreting the failure and as a process influenced by their socio-cultural background (Vanrenne  & McDermott, 1988). Under the influence of the huma n-centered interaction (HCI) approach to strive for interactive creation, we tak e an affordance theory perspective to view game des ign and design failure. First proposed by Gibson (1979) fro m perceptual psychology and adapted by Norman (1988) to transform information system design. Under this vie w, design has been defined as more than a processin g mechanism; it is a relationship between the users a nd designers in manipulating the artifacts (Maier &  Fadel, 2009). Given that design failure is a main part of the design process, we also view it as a relationsh ip formed between the designer, the games, a nd the players through iterative interaction. Unlik e the traditional failure that is regarded to be avoided, f ailure as an important mechanic is actively designe d as part of the player experience in making games engaging and fun (Foch & Kirman, 20 22). On the contrary, failure in the gameplay exper ience is what made it fun and engaged the players (Juul, 2009). Taking advantage of how  failure is experienced and framed in the gaming environment, designers have su ccessfully turned failure into playful events in a literacy development context, which ultimately engages  learners in a deeper level of learning (Fornós & Be ier, 2024). Therefore, under this dimension, we view failure as an experiential phenomenon that is accepted and expected in the gaming context.

Establishing a feedback loop is an important learning process in collaborative design In the learning context, creating a feedback loop as the collaborative activity among peers has been argued to have a beneficial effect on learning (Indriasari, Luxton-Reilly, & Denny, 2020), especially when compared to  the use of explicit instruction or d irect instruction, especially if the process also i nvolved in engaging students into understanding their failure (Metcalfe et al., 2023). Despite the learning benefits of seeking feedback as designers or giving feedback as users, it was a challenging task due to its emotional and cognitive demands since the quality of learning depended on the engagement level on how feedback was given and received (Indriasari, Luxton-Reilly, & Denny, 2020). Given the critical role of seeking feedback from others to learn from failure in a design context, designers often share the collective artifact as pr ototypes with users to seek feedback, test design, and iterate for improvement based on the failure identified (Brown,  2008). The iterative design allows designers to refine, and revise based on the end-user’s feedback. The possibility to revise provides  designers with a safe nutshell to fail fast and often (Litts & Ramirez, 2014). Iteration is a powerful practice because it generates a safe space that allows failure, which is known as a key to innovation (Litts & Ramires, 2014; Ryoo et al., 2015). Boundary objects are important elements to support communication in virtual collaborative design In this paper, we apply the concept of boundary obj ect (Fominykh et al., 2016) to highlight one practi ce during the game testing activity. Boundary object is “an a nalytic concept” (Star & Griesemer, 1989, p. 393) t hat could be in the forms of artifacts, di scourses, and processes. Design artifacts can be bo undary objects that bridge a “distance” - spatial, temporal, and conceptual gaps between multiple stakeholders by Fischer and Reeves (1995), and between knowledge across groups (Bergman et al., 2007). Also, processes that allow coordination and sharing of design knowledge across groups can also be bound ary objects. In virtual collaborative design environment, such processes would potentially be unique and diff erent from processes that allow such coordinatio n in person. In fact, the distance in the virtual collaborative design environment, especially the spatial and temp oral gaps, could be exaggerated and deepened (Marheineke, Vela muri, & Möslein, 2016) due to the constraints of technology implemented in the system (Swart, Bond-Barnard, Chugh, 2022). Built on the existing concep t of boundary objects, Lee (2007) argued for a more nuan ced version of the concept-boundary negotiating artifacts, to account for the fluid and dynamic nature and the  negotiation as a central process in collaborative work. Given that boundary objects are more commonly known, we s till use this term for the rest of the article. At the same time, we consciously adopt Lee’s (2007) definition to capture the constant construction and negotiatio n process of establishing shared understanding through social interaction in the virtual collaborative design environment.

## Context

In the summer of 2023, the research team implemente d the first iteration of a fully virtual collaborat ive design camp. Given that the three facilitators resided in two different time zones, we strategically schedule d the club sessions at times most convenient for all facilitators and potential participants. We distributed recruitment flyers with registration form links through university lis tservs and WeChat (a Chinese instant messaging appl ication with social media features). The summer camp ran fo r 10 days over two weeks, with 1.5-hour daily sessions Monday through Friday. Nine youths aged 8-13 participated from various time zones across the US. All participants were Asian, with three females among  them. They were organized into three small learning  groups: Group 1 consisted of Mark (8 yrs), Aron (8 yrs), and Evelyn (10 yrs); Group 2 included Angel (9 yrs) a nd Alice (11 yrs); Group 3 comprised Ryan (8 yrs), Clayton (10 yrs), Ricky (11 yrs), and Nathan (12 yrs). To support learning from design failure, we strateg ically implemented three checkpoints during the 10-day program. During each checkpoint, team members and facilitators served dual roles as both designers of their own games and testers for other teams' games. This structure created a cyclical feedback process where  teams would revise their games based on tester feedback and their own observations, either within the checkpoint session or during the subsequent design session. The virtua l environment was supported through three main plat forms: Zoom for synchronous sessions, Google Suite for doc umenting and updating curriculum materials, and Blo xels as the game design platform. These platforms collec tively facilitated communication, design activities, and feedback processes essential to virtual collaborative learning. Data source and analysis We used Zoom to record all sessions, capturing both whole-group interactions and breakout room activities. These recordings documented not only verbal exchanges but also shared screens during gameplay, providing rich visual data of the design and testing processes. For this study, we analyzed recordings from three checkpoint  sessions (Lessons 5, 8, and 10). Our analysis followed a multi-stage process: First, we transcribed four videos from Lesson 5 (one whole-group session and three breakout groups), one 90-minute video from Lesson 8, and one 90-minute video from Lesson 10. Second, two authors independently coded the transcripts, dividing them into discrete event units with three primary codes: game context (the game being tested), player feedback (reactions and suggestions from testers), and designer responses (reactions and actions taken by game creators). Third, we conducted a more detailed analysis of each event, focusing on types of feedback provided by testers (evaluative comment s, design suggestions, t echnological issues, gameplay strategies), types of  responses from game designers (acceptance, resistance, implementation strategies), and the rol e of boundary objects in facilitating communication  between testers and designers. Finally, we examined patterns across multiple feedback sessions to identify recurring themes and developmental trajectories in how teams gave, r eceived, and implemented design feedback throughout  the workshop. This analytical approach allowed us to ex amine not only the explicit cont ent of feedback exchanges but also the underlying processes through which bou ndary objects mediated collaborative learning in th is virtual design environment.

## Findings

The study revealed six distinct ways through which youths provided game design feedback during testing sessions. Players 1) provided evaluative feedback (positive, negative, or neutral),  2) demonstrated ways to hack the game,

3) engaged in thinking aloud to figure out gameplay, 4) sought clarification and strategies directly from designers

or among each other, 5) reported technological problem(s), and 6) offered concrete design suggestions. Notably, they rarely occurred in isolation; instead, they we re often intertwined to create more complex feedback patt erns. The content of feedback predominantly centered arou nd three main areas: game mechanics, aesthetics, and technology. When discussing game mechanics, players addressed various aspects, including the overall length of games, difficulty levels, clarity of objectives and  goals, and the extent to which players could exerc ise agency within the game environment. Aesthetic feedback was  mostly associated with the affordances of the visu al cues to their functions. And technology feedback was mostly concerned wit h the problems of linking different levels of games together to form one complete game experience. Designers' responses to player feedback varied cons iderably along a spectrum. At one end, some designers fully embraced player feedback, translati ng game experiences into concrete design revisions.  At the opposite end, a few others simply enjoyed watching  players struggle with their games without implement ing changes. However, most designers adopted a middle-ground approach, balancing their enjoyment of watch ing others play their games with consideration and impl ementation of player feedback. A notable fin ding was the designers' enthusiasm for feedback, actively seekin g player input rather than avoiding it, especially towards the end of the workshop. Young designers faced particular challenges in balancing different game elements, with the alignment between story and mechanics proving especially difficult. From the data analysis, most games had more aligned stories, aesthetics, and mechanics. It is worth noting that not all feedback was received with equal clarity; some player suggestions failed to reach designers in an actionable form. ## Discussion

Transforming failure learning through virtual collaboration.

The study reveals that game design feedback in virt ual collaborative environments transcends tradition al linear relationships between designers and players. Instead, we observed a complex discursive relationship among three key elements: the testers/users, the designers, and the game artifact. Through an affordance theory lens (Maier & Fadel, 2009), this triangular relationship created unique opportunities for interaction and learning t hat would not have been possible in conventional feedback setting s. The dynamic relationships between designers and players are essential for making design failures both visib le and productive (Cannon & Edmondson, 2005). This was particularly evident in the first case study, where  design failures were collectively identified a nd analyzed, and solutions emerged through discourse between designe rs and players. More importantly, through the refra ming from the facilitator, the failure and solutions tha t occurred transcended from the dyad discourse to t he whole community (Borge & Mercier, 2019).

The case of Clayton's challenging game levels illus trates what began as individual struggle can be transformed into collective strategizing, with play ers sharing techniques and designers reconsidering their assumptions about game difficulty. Unlike Alice, Clayton resisted implementing changes despite recei ving clear feedback about their games' difficulty levels. Rath er than viewing this resistance purely as an obstac le, we interpret it as a complex negotiation between designer autonomy and user experience-a tension that exists at the heart of game design practice. This resonates with Maltese and Anderson's (2018) findings on how stude nts' personal investment in their work can sometimes imp ede their ability to learn from failure. However, o ur virtual environment allowed the public nature of gameplay through  screen sharing forced designers to witness, in rea ltime, the consequences of their design decisions. T he transformation we observed in Clayton and Ryan's behavior—from initially refusing to modify their game to eventually offering strategies to help players succeed— suggests that resistance itself can become a pathwa y to learning. This shift occurred not through dire ct confrontation about their resistance but through the accumulated experience of watching multiple players struggle with their design.

Screen sharing as a boundary object between all participants. We argue that the virtual environment, when thought fully curated, creates unique opportunities for mea ningful design dialogue and iterative improvement, transfor ming traditional critique sessions into collaborati ve learning experiences. Our analysis reveals that screen sharing served as more than just a technological tool—it functioned as a sophisticated boundary object (Star & Grieseme r, 1989) that transformed the nature of feedback it self. This technology enabled the establishment of feedback lo ops between designers and players, supported negoti ations about the attribution of failures, and created shared gaming experiences among players. It created a virtual bridge that made complex design feedback tangible and disc ussable for all (Fominykh et al., 2016; S tar & Griesemer, 1989; Wenger, 2000).

Previous research on boundary objects in design has primarily focused on physical artifacts (Bergman et al., 2007). Our findings extend this focus by demonstrating how virtual boundary objects can create shared spaces for experiential learning from failure. The real-time nature of screen sharing created environments where failure becomes a shared experience. Built on Lee's (2007) concept of boundary-negotiating artifacts, this study adds a temporal dimension unique to virtual environments. In these spaces, players and designers could collec tively diagnose issues through synchronous failure analysis. They engaged in real-time strategy development and testing, creating immediate feedback loops between design intentions and player experiences. Also, the virtual environment's screen-sharing feature played a crucial role in transformi ng traditional critique sessions into shared game streaming experi ences that everyone enjoyed. This approach aligns w ith emerging practices in gaming communities (Hil vert-Bruce et al., 2018), where game failures become a s hared experience and opportunities for community building  and learning rather than sources of frustration or embarrassment. This study extends existing research  on the gamification of peer feedback (I ndriasari, LuxtonReilly, & Denny, 2020) and suggests that successful  feedback integration goes beyond merely adding gam e elements to engage reviewers. The shared game exper ience and inherent playfulness reframed the testing  event itself. This reframing lo wered stakes for both reviewers and designers, tran sformed constant failure into communal entertainment, created a safe space for honest feedback (Cannon & Edmondson, 2005), and encouraged active feedback-seeking behaviors. The playful environment fostered  a community where iteration and experimentation were celebrated rather than criticized (Yan et al., 2022). Facilitators adopted multiple roles throughout the game-testing sessions. Facilitator support emerged as a key figure in the feedback process, particularly in relation to narrative and story elements. While players focused primarily on mechan ical and aesthetic aspects, facilitators were respo nsible for most narrative-related feedback. They consistently prompted designers to thin k critically about how their game mechanics and aesthetic choices aligned with their intended storylines. However, this proved to be one of the most challenging aspects for designers to address, espec ially among groups that prioritized creating engaging gam e mechanics over narrative coherence.

Facilitators demonstrated versatility in their role s during testing sessions. They actively participat ed in gameplay through screen sharing, making themselves vulnerable to game failures while modeling player behavior. Additionally, they served as interpreters, translating player experiences into actionable design considerations and providing technical expertise when needed. Their ab ility to switch between these different roles – from player to mentor to technical advisor – proved crucial in maintaining productive feedback sessions. Our data revealed that their role as players was particularly influential.  In this role, the facilitators modeled playfulness  through live streaming their gameplay (Yan et al., 2022; Ryoo & Kekelis, 2018) and openly displayed vulner ability when failing at games. Through this modeling, facilitato rs cultivated an environment where failure was not only acceptable but could be transformed into an engagin g and enjoyable experience. This turned out to have  a transformative impact on lea rning when we observed that, in later sessions, des igners actively sought feedback from peers and game reviewers. These playful environments foster positive learning behaviors in feedba ck exchanges that traditional critique sessions often fail to achieve.
