# Examining Learning Opportunities for Integrating AI Education in English Language Art Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We conducted a co-design study with high school ELA teachers to investigate their perspectives in the integration of a technology-enhanced AI curriculum. The teachers explored the AI and text mining activities and reflected on them from teaching and learning perspectives. The findings of this study suggest that building models with texts positioned ELA teachers as experts in AI teaching and they were empowered to identify existing learning resources and opportunities in ELA classrooms that could be leveraged in integrating AI learning in their classrooms.

## Introduction

Understanding of AI concepts, the ability to use AI knowledge in real-world settings, and analyzing the potential long-term benefits of AI are turning into essential 21st-century skills (Steinbauer et al., 2021). These skills are the basis of future career opportunities. Therefore, pr oviding high-quality AI training to our young gener ation, especially underrepresented K-12 students, is a cri tical way to provide equal learning opportunities f or these essential skills they need in an AI-empowered socie ty. Following this trend, scholarly attention has t urned to examining how to support K-12 AI education with resources, tools, and projects. Many attempts have been made to motivate K-12 students in understanding AI through developing machine learning (ML) models (Yoder et al., 2020). Yet, the field calls for more empirical studies investigating how to support students to learn modeling data in k-12 classrooms (Steinbauer et al.,2021). On the  other side, to integrate these technologies into t he K-12 curriculum, teachers’ perspectives play a critical role in successful implementations (Lindner et al., 2019). Thus, we developed the AI curriculum activities and the S toryQ application, a web-based text-mining and narr ative modeling platform, to bring AI education to high school non-computing classes, in this study, ELA classes. This study addresses the following research question: What are the ELA teachers’ perspectives to integrat e AI education in their classrooms as they reflected on their interaction with the AI and text mining activities?

## Methods

Two ELA teachers from two public high schools, Mart in and Emma (pseudonyms), participated in co-design workshops on the AI unit and technology. Martin has  20 years of teaching experience in ELA while Emma has 10 years of ELA teaching. We focused on ELA teacher s in this study as our prior co-design experiences with ELA, mathematics, and computer science teachers ind icated a critical need to learn more about the lear ning opportunities in the ELA classrooms (Tatar, 2021).

Martin attended approximately 10 hours of co-design sessions to test the initial designs of the curriculum from 2020 to 2021. Emma attended the 2021 February workshop for three hours with her colleagues to learn the basics of AI and test the curriculum. After the Feb ruary workshop, we invited Martin and Emma to atten d the 2021 August workshop to test the new design of the curriculum. The August workshop was held on Zoom for two days (seven hours per day). In the workshop, Martin and Emma were guided to complete five lessons related to extracting features from texts, building models, and classifying texts. We collected 27-hour video rec ordings, including the teachers’ interactions in the StoryQ while completing the activities, their responses to the learning tasks, and their discussions. This study draws on t he case study approach (Crowe, 2011) regarding them atic analysis methods (Clark et al., 2015). We analyzed the transcript of the recordings using the thematic  analysis method. We first closely examined the data to inves tigate the teachers’ experiences, expectancies, and  their understandings of how AI works. Then we discussed and finalized the themes and categories in weekly meetings.

## Findings and Conclusion

We found that after the teachers analyzed features and built AI models, they started to foresee the op portunities to integrate these lessons into their classrooms. T he teachers identified these opportunities as enric hing their ICLS2022 Proceedings 2106 © ISLS students’ ELA understanding and raising awareness of the impacts of AI on their future careers. In the workshop, Martin and Emma worked collaboratively to complete the learning tasks. They developed models and then created a confusion matrix (i.e., a visual representation t o make comparisons) to investigate where the model made mistakes. With the help of the confusion matrix, th ey identified specific sentences which the model mislabeled. While reviewing these reviews, they discussed how people use first-person pronouns when they complain about something. Also, they realized that people tend to use third-person pronouns when they like something. Based on their experience with the model, Martin mentioned that “…we tell students to speak from their own experience, speak in the first person, we set these goals and we never explained why”. He believed that this activity could be a part of their “norms of communication” unit and illustrate how different statements are used in different contexts. Emma also agreed with him and saw the value of show ing different examples to reinforce students’ ELA understandings.

Another opportunity that the teachers pointed out w as to show students how language arts play out to express feelings. As a part of the learning activity, Emma and Martin trained the model by using the word “love” as a feature. After training the model, they create d a visual representation to investigate in which r eviews this feature was used and how. They then examined differ ent instances to understand how the selected featur e was placed in both positive and negative labels. During  this activity, Martin discussed how people express ed their feelings while complaining. Based on the instances,  he claimed that when people complain about a resta urant, they use “certain language that’s more general appraisals that decrease the weight of the praise”. After that, Emma joined the discussion and brought another perspective by thinking of the classroom implementation. She believed that discussing the form and content of the words could help students in the language analysis like “they're trying to access someone's trying to express either a favorable opinion or an unfavorable opinion”. Moreover, she further explained that this lesson can be used in the poetr y class to analyze emotions. She stated that “…I fe el like this lesson would just be set up perfectly for poetry... that's valuable in and of itself, we see words of p ositive connotations, positive tone…”. From these examples, we can clearly see that while engaging with the activities, the teachers developed a basic understanding of how AI models work through exploring features in the text data and examining the visual representations to compare  human and AI-predicted labels. After they develope d an understanding, they discussed the connections betwe en ELA classrooms and the curriculum lessons. They considered the learning opportunities to enhance their students’ ELA understandings by integrating the AI lessons into their classes.

This study contributes to the K-12 AI education on  integrating an accessible text mining learning environment into ELA classrooms to engage high scho ol students in developing an understanding of machi ne learning concepts through building AI models regard ing to ELA teachers’ perspectives. Taking an ELA perspective to design AI learning activities could potentially broaden access to AI through leveraging students’ insights of texts, drawing from their own cultural backgrounds. Future studies could examine different culturally relevant pedagogical strategies to provide widely inclusive AI experiences to engage high school stude nts from underrepresented, diverse backgrounds.
