# Learning About and Against Generative AI Through Mapping Generative AI’s Ecologies and Developing a Luddite Praxis

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, I respond to recent conversations in the learning sciences about how the field might develop research with generative artificial intelligence (GenAI). Rather than a narrow focus on what we might do with GenAI, I argue that learning scientists should expand our approaches to consider ways to research and learn about and against GenAI. While AI literacy is a common framework for helping people learn with and about artificial intelligence, a literacy framework can struggle to contend with AI’s opaque systems and is vulnerable to hegemonic influences. I suggest an alternative: mapping ecologies of GenAI and its social, technical, and political-economic relations. Finally, I argue that learning scientists candraw inspiration from the Luddites, 19th-century English clothworkers who destroyed machines to defend their craft and dignity, to develop a Luddite praxis that combines mapping ecologies of GenAI with collective interventions against unjust AI systems.

Generative AI and the future of the learning sciences The lecture hall was so crowded that people sat on the floor. Some ISLS 2023 conference attendees had to stand, ringing the room. A few people, I noticed, craned t heir necks, trying to listen from their place in th e hallway. Other people joined the live stream from more comfortable locations. All of us had gathered physically or virtually into John Molson 2.210 for the special session, “Th e new generation of AI: Opportunities for research and the role of ISLS”. We listened to the panelists offer t heir perspectives on how our field might respond to the rapid growth of generative AI. Meanwhile, on a shared digital document, audience members replied to two prompts: 1) What affordances does generative AI create for lear ning and instruction? 2) How can we, as ISLS resear chers, develop an agenda on research with generative AI?

Months later, I am still reflecting on those questi ons and their underlying assumptions that the learn ing sciences should embrace generative AI. For instance, the first ques tion focuses strictly on the technology’s affordances. What about its limitations? The often dire consequences for people and planet? The second question lacks a similar skepticism. Instead of only asking  how the learning sciences might research with chatbots like ChatGPT, what possibilities are opened when we fram e the question as resea rching about and even against generative AI? Scholars outside the learning sciences have been vocal critics of Large Language Models (Bender et al., 2021), and many of these scholars have incr easingly warned the public about individuals and co mpanies selling AI hype that draws attention away from  the technology’s immediate impacts in favor of doom sday narratives that harden existing power structures (Bender & Hanna, 2023). The learning sciences has pivotal roles to play in how people understand and use (or refuse to use) GenAI. Perhaps an important initial step would be to ask a new question: What might be required for a more heterogeneous approach to generative AI, one that makes space for learning with, about, and against?

In this paper, I make a theoretically grounded conceptual argument for mapping platform ecologies when learning about and against generative AI. Platforms are the infrastructures on which appl ications are constructed as well as digital spaces where people socialize and conduct economic exchange (Nichols et al., 2022).  Platform ecologies are constituted of the intertwined relationships between three dimensions of a platform: social, technical, and political-economic (vandijck, 2013). GenAI includes Large Language Models (e.g. OpenAI’s ChatGPT and Google’s Gemini) and Large Image Models (e.g. OpenAI’s DALL-E and Stability AI’s Stable Diffusion) pre-trained on enormous amounts of data scraped from th e internet. However, conceiving of GenAI only as a set of tools leaves untroubled the often harmful systems u ndergirding the advanced technology. In the argumen t that follows, I will explain that while an AI literacy f ramework is a more common approach for helping peop le learn with and about generative AI, it may lack the necessary attributes for “expansive and insurgent ways of learning, being, and acting” (Warren et al., 2020, p. 278) th at attends to generative AI’s distributed, often ob scured social, technical, and political-economic relations. And in the spirit of insurgency, I will outline what learning scientists might gain from developing a Luddite praxis and its possibilities in the struggle against unjust AI systems. Tracing the emergence of AI literacy and its vulnerabilities AI literacy appears to be having a moment. The popular press offers explainers of the term (Klein, 2023), largely as a consequence of the rapid growth of chatbots and other GenAI. The push for increased AI literacy is also coming from powerful actors in education policy. Fo r example, the non-profit Digital Promise provided funding to the U.S. Department of Education’s Office of Educational Technology and its policy report, “Artific ial Intelligence and the Future of Teaching and Learnin g: Insights and Recommendations”. According to Digi tal Promise, “AI literacy is essential for traversing a n increasingly digital society” (Shah, 2023). Descr ibing the history of AI literacy and its different definitions helps to illustrate the underlying ideological assumptions about the term and to what ends developing AI literacy may be put. According to Southworth et al. (2023), the term AI literacy was first used by Konishi (2015)  in a series of posts about priorities for the Japanese economy.  In Konishi’s formulation, AI literacy is concerned  with identifying new situations for applying AI, focusing on monetizing often very personal artifacts. For example, AI literacy may involve “ record[ing] your mother’s recipe as digitized text. When a cooking robot be comes reality in the future, having the data for one recipe could  lead to a new business  of reproducing home cooked meals” (para. 5); people should also “constantly examine w hether the information we possess has the potential  of generating high value added,” and we should “be emo tionally prepared to let go of tasks that AI can pe rform in our place” (para. 5). Here AI literacy is intertwined w ith a broader narrative about AI’s inevitability and its promise of individual economic success (if only we can move beyond our doubt and trust the technology and its makers). Absent, however, is a consideration of the  ethical and relational implications involved in ha nding over to Big Tech a family recipe passed down from generation to generation. Another early articulation of AI literacy emphasize s its role in individual economic gain. According t o Laupichler et al. (2022), the first appearance of AI literacy in a peer-reviewed publication was Kandelhofer et al. (2016), who wrote, “AI literacy: It allows people t o understand the techniques and concepts behind AI products and services instead of just learning how to use ce rtain technologies or current applications” (p. 2).  Again, it’s important to ask to what ends the authors envision AI literacy and  its attending technical and conceptual knowledge are working towards. Like Konishi (2015), Kandelhofer et al. (2016) argue the need for AI literacy is predominantly driven by speculative economic trends; they state, “[J]obs will largely be related to AI as it will be the basis of the products where our future wealth will be built on (smart production, internet of things, autonomous driving, robotics...)” (p. 1). Such assured prognostication is troubling–and, as it turns out, wrong. Amazon lost $10 billion on Alexa, its AI voice assistant, in 20 22 (Amadeo, 2022); autonomous driving cars continue  to be a harmful fantasy of the Silicon Valley elite (Marx, 2022). And who, exactly, do the authors include and exclude in their use of “our” in “our wealth”? An AI literacy devoid of power analysis and civic action risks bec oming a talking point for Big Tech, and as I discuss shortl y, a means for corporations like OpenAI and Google to set the terms of how educators and students think about and use their chatbots. Notably, Kandelhofer et al. (2016) also explicitly root their version of AI literacy in what they refe r to as “classic literacy,” or the development of readin g and writing skills. Conceiving of AI literacy as analogous to “classic literacy” evokes early theorizations of li teracy, known as Great Divide theories (Higgs et al., forthcoming), that often used a binary of literate versus illi terate to strengthen assertions of white, Western dominance over oral cultures and their literacy pra ctices that did not co nform to “civilized” modes of communication. Formulations of AI literacy rarely c ontend with the multiple meanings of literacy/liter acies and how literacy/literacies are sociocultural practices (Street, 1995) enmeshed in struggles for power and legitimacy. Unlike the authors of the early definitions of AI l iteracy, another influential definition of AI liter acy acknowledges that literacy is a contentious term la den with power. In a scoping literature review on A I literacy in higher and adult education conducted by Laupichler et al. (2022), the most cited definition of AI literacy came from Long and Magerko (2020), who draw on Freire (1 972) to recognize that literacy is entwined with ef forts to repress and liberate. Building on this understandin g of literacy as  political, Long and Magerko (2020) go on to define AI literacy as “a set of competencies that e nables individuals to critically evaluate AI techno logies; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace” (p. 2). I want to briefly address two elements of the d efinition: a focus on AI literacy as an individual skill set; and AI literacy’s emphasis on ethical concerns.

The framing of AI literacy as a set of competencies  practiced by individuals emerges in part from this version of AI literacy’s roots in a particular form  of digital literacy, which Long and Magerko (2020)  argue is a requirement for AI literacy. Digital li teracy can be characterized as a more prescriptive set of cognitive and behavioral skills for using technology to “find, ev aluate, create, and communicate information” (Ameri can Library Association, n.d.). Literacy scholars have noted the limited usefulness of a skills-based approach to digital literacy, arguing that such an approach “strains to accommodate the political and economic flows that underwrite contemporary digital activities” (Nichols  & Stornaiuolo, 2019, p. 19). A parallel exists with AI literacy: t he competency-based model may not be sufficient for understanding, analyzing, and responding to the  social, technical, and political-economic systems that constitute contemporary GenAI. Even so, multiple formulations of AI literacy ackno wledge the need to address the ethical issues raise d by AI (e.g. Miao et al., 2021). Recall that in Long and Magerko’s (2020) influential definition, AI literacy includes the ability to “critically evaluate AI technologies”, a skill further specified through a competency devoted entirely to ethics. To effectively demonstrate this competen cy, students can analyze how AI deepens existing in equities through applications like predictive policing and j ob hiring software. Whether in a computer science classroom (Jones & melo, 2021; Vakil, 2018) or an English language arts classroom (Garcia & de Roock, 2021; LeBlanc et al., 2023), justice-oriented education needs to help young people and teachers engage with the ethical dimensions of technology. However, the inclusion of ethics as a core competency of AI literacy must contend with the ways Big Tech has co-opted and sanitized the term “ethics”.

AI ethics and safety have been captured by Big Tech  firms (Ganesh & Moss, 2022; Rességuier & Rodrigues, 2020; Whittaker, 2021). As a result, a more robust form of ethics co mmitted to centering the lived experiences and expertise of marginalized communities has atrophied into “a practical set of tools and processes, a to-do checklist rather than a moment for critical reflection on power itself” (Ganesh & Moss, 2022, p. 6). D oes this cynical, hollowed out version of ethics mean A I literacy should be abandoned ? Not necessarily. But proponents of responding to the rapid growth of GenAI with AI literacy curricula must attend to the wa ys Big Tech actively shapes the very definition of AI literacy by exercising control over one of its most crucial elements: ethics. Indeed, when we see Common Sense Education publish an AI literacy curriculum  for grades 6-12 (Common Sense Education, n.d.), we must consider how its parent company Common Sense Media’s partnership with OpenAI (Common Sense Media, 2024) may be shaping how GenAI is framed in the  lesson plans. An AI literacy that fails to examine how Big Tech influences students, educators, and administrators allows corporations to exert a form of soft power through embedding preferred versions of digital literacy that lack analyses of power (Pangrazio & Sefton-Green, 2023). For the learning sciences to continue our commitment to developing learning environments that embrace people’s inherent dignity and agency (Espinoza et al., 2020), we must be explicit about what we mean by AI literacy and be clear-eyed about AI literacy’s possibilities and liabilities. Mapping the ecologies of generative AI and its relations To make explicit the ways AI and issues of power are enmeshed, scholars have argued for a need to develop critical AI literacy (Bali, 2023; Goodlad & Stoerger, 2023). Like critical turns in other forms of literacy (e.g. critical literacy and critical digital literacy), critical AI literacy practices assist people in analyzing how power operates in ways tangible and less so, and in the case of generative AI, that includes issues related to bias, privacy, misinformation, and the environment.

Recently, scholars have questioned the suitability of a literacy framework for analyzing digital platforms (Nichols et al., 2022). Using a literacy lens may be appropriate for understanding different representational forms like an Instagram story  or a TikTok video; a literacy lens may also be help ful for analyzing the social practices, connections, and communities that grow as a result of engaging with and through platforms (Ito et al., 2013). But platforms are more than memes and affinity groups. Platforms are composed of other social dimensions, such as targeted ads made possible by surveillance; technic al dimensions such as algorithms trained on limited  data sets that reinforce inequities; and political-economic dimensions such as business models that pe rmit the platforms’ owners to sell users’ data to third parties (Nichol s & Garcia, 2022). Therefore, the traditional assemblage of critical digital literacy may not be sufficient for  addressing the often opaque social, technical, and  political-economic systems undergirding platforms (Nichols & LeBlanc, 2020). By extension, AI literacy, whether critical or not,  may also be an insufficient framework for analyzin g and responding to GenAI platforms like Google’s Gemini or OpenAI’s DALL-E. Instead, as Nichols et al. (2022) argue, the “familiar critical digital literacy tactics may need to be augmented or reimagined in a media ecosystem where algorithmic rationality is being deployed to foreclose horizons of political possibility” (p. 346). Mapping a platform’s ecologies in order to intervene in its s ocial, technical,  and political-economic relations is one such alternative. As Crawford (2021) argues, “A topograp hical approach [to AI] offers different perspective s and scales, beyond the abstract promises of artificial intelligence or the latest machine learning models”  (p. 11), making possible a deeper understanding of the interconnected landscapes of generative AI. A short example examining OpenAI’s ChatGPT allows a  contrast between a literacy framework and an ecological framework. Drawing on Long and Magerko’s  (2020) popular definition and competency model, learning scientists might design educational settin gs and/o r curricula to help educators and their students strengthen their AI literacy by analyzing the outpu ts of ChatGPT. OpenAI readily admits its chatbot pr oduces false information and contains bias (OpenAI, n.d.).  Long and Magerko (2020)’s AI literacy framework in cludes 16 competencies, and at least two are useful here: describing AI’s strengths and weaknesses; and inves tigating the human role in AI. Both direct a closer examination of how the chatbot’s social and technical relations combine to produce incorrect outputs. Working with an AI li teracy framework to help educators and youth analyz e the origins of ChatGPT’s biases is helpful. And yet it’ s worth asking: what does the literacy framework le ave uncharted?

An ecological framework that begins to map some of ChatGPT’s social, technical, and political-economic relations helps to illustrate other essent ial landscapes of the generative AI platform. For i nstance, ChatGPT’s social relations includes the company’s leadership. OpenAI’s CEO is Sam Altman, whose ideological commitments are influenced by longtermism (Olson, 2 023) and its legacy of eugenics (Torres, 2023). Map ping the ideologies of the powerful people releasing GenAI into (and onto) the world raises questions about how those ideologies shape the technologies and whether we should be institutionalizing GenAI platforms through lucrative, long-term contracts that further concentrate power. Attending to these efforts at embedding GenAI platforms and their often damaging ideologies –and related attempts to privatize public education through venture capitalism (Komljenovic et al., 2023) and the Googlization of education worldwide (Kerssens et al., 2023)–is essential work for the learning sciences if, as the conference theme urges, we are to be agents of positive change. Perhaps one of the most important affordances of an  ecological framework is its mapping of generative AI’s political-economic relations. AI literacy helps people “[r]ec ognize that humans play an important role in programming, choosing models, and fine-tuning AI systems” (Long & Magerko, 2020, p. 6); it’s a  necessary competency, even if it adopts a seemingly political ly neutral stance towards labor. An ecological fram ework situates that human labor in a global network of ghost work (Gray & Suri, 2019), exposing the ways Silicon Valley technology firms depend on often harmful, low-wage work on a planetary scale. This work is not on ly “important”; it’s damaging and exploitative. In the  case of ChatGPT, OpenAI contracted with a third-party company to hire Kenyan  workers to train the model used for the chatbot. Workers annotated violent, grotesque text for little pay; many workers were left traumatized and with few resources to heal (Perrigo, 2023). Such violent extraction and indifference to human suffering  is characteristic of the AI Empire and its “heterop atriarchal, colonial, racist, white supremacist, and capitalist oppression” (Tacheva & Ramasubramanian, 2023, p. 10). Big Tech’s business and ownership models are anothe r dimension made visible through mapping the ecologies of generative AI’s relations. Educational  technology companies are rolling out GenAI tools, many of which rely on OpenAI’s GPT-3 and its more recent updates. Khan Academy recently announced its own chatbot, Khanmigo. Schools are piloting the chatbot, including several in Newark, New Jersey. And while the pilot phase is free, the distric t will eventually need to pay $70 per student to li cense Khanmigo (Singer, 2023). Khan Academy’s business model relies on a rentier-rentee relation with schools, a relation that allow s Khan Academy and OpenAI to further consolidate power through var ious forms, from the monetary gains obtained throug h licensure fees to the extraction and monetization of teachers’ and studen ts’ data (Komljenovic, 2021). Notably, the Newark schools piloting Khanmigo–a chatbot without empirical research supporting its ability to aid teaching and learning–serve nondominant students (Singer, 2023). Khanmigo must therefore be understood as yet another teaching machine foisted upon nondominant communiti es by outsiders with mixed intentions. In the mid-20th century, B. F. Skinner attempted to enroll Black students in Harlem schools as test subjects to see whether or not his teaching machine could help them learn to read (Watters, 2021). While he failed, Sal Khan and his company’s problematic business and ownership model appears to be gaining traction. Generative AI’s extractions are not limited to human beings. Our planet is also a source of extraction for GenAI. OpenAI used coal and natural gas to power the compu ting used to train its GPT-3 LLM, which resulted in the release of 500 metric tons of carbon or driv ing the average gasoline-powered car one million miles (Luccioni, 2023). Big Tech companies like Microsoft  and Google acknowledge they are using more and mor e water to cool the servers needed to maintain their LLMs and other GenAI tools (O’Brien &  Fingerhut, 2023). Emerging research estimates that OpenAI’s ChatGPT r equires 16 ounces of water to respond to 5 to 50 pr ompts (O’Brien & Fingerhut, 2023). The water used to make  generative AI possible is making life increasingly impossible for humans and more than humans living c lose to the data centers that contain the whirring servers (Hao, 2024). As water is diverted to cool servers, less water is available for residents in places like West Des Moines, Iowa, taxing a water system already at risk  thanks to ongoing droughts. One organizer with the  Iowa Citizens for Community Improvement put the problem like this: “‘ChatGPT is not a necessity for human life, and yet we are literally taking water to feed a compute r’” (Adralo, 2023, para. 3). Hogan (2024) sums up the  crisis succinctly: “AI is a hot mess.” Climate change is one of the most urgent problems facing humanity. An ecological approach to mapping generative AI’s relations helps  people to understand the web of connections betwee n a dwindling water table in Carlisle, Iowa, and a blank ChatGPT interface on a laptop in Buffalo, New York. Mapping the ecologies of generative AI platforms and their relations does not guarantee such an approach will be beyond Big Tech’s influence. Creating maps and atlases has always been a subjective exercise, one that presents a particular view of the world (Crawford, 2021). The method demands reflexivity and an ongoin g commitment to using design practices that deconstru ct normative power hierarchies (Bang & Vossoughi, 2 016). One way learning scientists and the people we work alongside can build these just relations is through  using a mapping framework to identify points of intervention in order to take action. From maps to movements A map is not only a static image of interconnected environments; it’s also an invitation to action. I have focused on the harms of generative AI, yet mapping the ecologies of generative AI and its social, technical, and politicaleconomic relations is not meant to solely illustrate forms of extraction. As Nichols and Garcia (2022) argue, “The promise of platform studies in education, then, is not to map in detail the vast scope of exploitations encapsulated in networked media but instead to identify where and how these relations might be changed and what rol e education might play in such transformations” (p. 2 25). Maps in hand, learning scientists and the dive rse groups we work with can identify points of intervention to bring about change. We have historical and contemporary examples to help guide us. First, we can look towards the Luddites. The 19th century English clothworkers did not, contrary to popular belief, o ppose technological change. Rather, the clothworkers opposed wealthy factory owners installing machines that automated their craft and impoverished their communities, and when attempts to regulate th e machines failed in Parliament, the clothworkers embodie d the fictional heroned Ludd and banded together to smash gig mills and power looms to protect their in dividual and collective dignity (Merchant, 2023). Like the Luddites, contemporary screenwriters understood the social and political-economic relations between an advanced technology deployed by powerful bosses and the screenwriters’ livelihoods. They went on strike, demanded limited applications of generative AI, and they won. The screenwriters’ new contract dictates that the write rs have control over when and how GenAI platforms are used in their work (Anguiano & Beckett, 2023).  What emerges from these examples is a praxis that c ouples the need to map and understand the relations of generative AI with a commitment to acting on that knowledge to intervene in unjust systems.

Developing a Luddite praxis extends existing calls for learning scientists to join organizers in bringing about social change (Curnow & Jurow, 2021). To imagine what living a Luddite praxis might look like, consider the learning and coalition-building that could occur to combat the construction of a new data center (Pasek, 2023). In this scenario, students might begin much as I ha ve: by mapping some of the ecologies of generative AI and their relat ions. Then, after locating their nearest data cente r, students can begin their inquiry into the center, examining questions such as, “What potential land use, energy, or water conflicts might exist because of the data center?” and “Who benefits from the data center being here? Who loses?” (Pasek, 2023). From there, youth might explore what local groups may be affected by the da ta center’s construction, from Indigenous peoples o n whose lands and waters the data center threatens toneigh bors who will be force d to live with the data center’s noise pollution. Alongside and in conjunction with the di fferent coalitions, students can assert their civic  leadership through multiple, youth-led actions (Mirra & Garcia, 2017). Here, perhaps, is a role for literacy: as a nested set of practices within an ecological stance towards generative AI t hat can help, for example, youth construct and analyze a persuasive set of images to include in a fight against Microsoft and a proposed data center. Working in tandem, both approaches combine to make for powerful learning about and against generative AI. A Luddite praxis is counter-hegemonic; its pedagogy is one of “disobedience and  dismantling…of refusal and of care” (Watters, 2020, para. 16). In its open embrace of politics and action, a Luddite praxis aligns with the learning sciences and the field’s in creasing acknowledgement that learning is political  (McKinney de Royston & Sengupta-Irving, 2019; The Politics of Learning Writing Coll ective, 2017; Vea et al., 2023). If, as a field, we are to realize the conference theme and t he call to “strengthen our own practices leading to  human flourishing,” we might design learning experiences that help people to map the ecologies of GenAI and its relations as part of a broader Luddie praxis dedicated to collective action against inequities and towards more just futures.

## Conclusion

The learning sciences has a complicated relationship with artificial intelligence. While the field’s ori gins appear to have been a deliberate break from artificial int elligence (Hoadley, 2018), recently scholars have a rgued it’s time to revisit the relationship between the learning sciences and AI (Doroudi, 2023). Importantly, what we mean when we speak of AI has evolved since Roger Shank renamed what was supposed to be an Artificial Intelligence in Education conference into the first Internationa l Conference of the Learning Sciences. For some, AI is now a powerful marketing term (Raji, 2023; Whittaker, 2021). AI in education is a methodology for academic research; a potential revenue stream for industry; and politi cal rhetoric wielded by policymakers in the  pursuit of educational reforms (Williamson, 2023). The politic ized nature of AI and its multiple definitions dema nd approaches to learning with, about, and against GenAI that accounts for the visible and invisible rela tions that constitute AI systems.

As I’ve argued, mapping the ecologies of generative AI and its social, technical, and political-economic relations is a productive approach to understanding the opaque, distributed landscapes of GenAI. When combined with a Luddite praxis, an ecological approach can support collective intervention and possible transformations of unjust systems. AI literacy may have a more limited role to play because it’s enmeshed with a history that deepens hegemonic conceptions of literacy; struggles to counteract a literacy framework’s focus on representational forms; and suffers from a vulnerability to Big Tech’s pref erred versions of self-serving ethics. An ecological approach to generative AI platforms is not the only way for addressing the complex problems posed by GenAI. However, our maps can bring into focus the connections betwe en a planet warming from the carbon produced by tra ining chatbots and a school-issued laptop warming from entering prompts into an  increasingly expensive, proprietary chatbot. The connections laid out before us, we can join together with others to resist, refuse, and reimagine what and for whom generative AI might be put to use.
