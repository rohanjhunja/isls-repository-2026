# Expertise in Two Dimensions: Mathematical Problem Solving with ChatGPT

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Using tools to solve problems within a subject domain requires both knowledge of the domain and of the tool itself. Drawing from perspectives on building expertise, we analyzed task-based episodes where undergraduates solved a mathematics free response question with ChatGPT-4o. We present four contrasting cases of students’ problem solving and discuss how their knowledge of (a) the underlying mathematics and (b) ChatGPT-4o appeared to influence what they did and did not understand. We situate our findings in broader education debates on the role of intelligent agents in developing human expertise.

We situate our findings in broader education debates on the role of intelligent agents in developing human expertise. Intelligent agents such as ChatGPT are influencing how students use tools during mathematical problem solving (Bastani et al., 2025). We argue that by studying how students interact with intelligent agents during problem solving episodes, we can better understand how these agents are changing how students think, reason, and acquire subject-domain expertise. We contribute a video analysis of undergraduate students’ mathematical problem solving with ChatGPT-4o, a commonly used large language model-based chatb ot that is frequently marketed toward collegiate learners (Delaney et al., 2025). We propose that by examining students’ problem solving processes through a lens of acquiring expertise, we can learn how automation intersects with students’ abilities to think mathematically, and the extent to which it helps them demonstrate mathematical competence. The research question we will answer is: “How do students’ (a) mathematics knowledge and (b) knowledge of AI tools influence problem solving on a free response question?” Solving mathematics problems is a cognitively demanding endeavor. We draw from theoretical perspectives on problem representations, which evokes notions of how students understand what a mathematics task is asking (Chi et al., 1981), and schema, ways in which their knowledge is organized and accessed (Chi & Glaser, 1985). Having well-developed representations and schema can enable learners to move toward solving a problem (Schoenfeld, 1985) even when the problem is demanding or lacks structure. Hiebert and Carpenter propose that students who demonstrate strong understanding of mathematics have well-developed abilities to create problem representations and use their internal networks of interconnected mathematical ideas (schema) during problem solving processes (1992). An open question for the field is the extent to which students can and do show math problem solving expertise (as evidenced by highly developed representations and schema) when using consumer-oriented AI tools. Williams-Pierce (2025) identified differences in expert/novice identifications of AI-produced definitions, and emergent works detailed in Pepin et al.’s systematic review on chatbots and mathematics learning (2025) have begun to explore the intersection of math learning with AI tools. It is still unclear whether and how students have opportunities to think and reason like math experts when using AI tools that can likely solve problems independently. In this context, AI literacy for mathematics suggests knowledge of interacting with an AI to ol in ways that do not fundamentally undermine the student’s agentic thinking and reasoning opportunities (Wu et al., 2025).

We recruited 36 undergraduates across a variety of majors to participate in task-based interviews (Goldin, 2000). Potential candidates completed a brief screening survey to confirm that they had taken a math course within the last year. Students were audio, video, and screen-recorded while solving three 20-minute tasks with ChatGPT-4o, the tool licensed to them by our university. We instructed students to think aloud during all aspects of problem solving and to explain their purpose and rationale for using ChatGPT, if at all. We focus on the Roller Coaster Task  (Virginia Department of Education, n.d.) (Figure 1) because students would likely be familiar with algebra, because it displayed a non-routine, graphical, and situated representation of a function, and because we believed the roller coaster image might cause ChatGPT to hallucinate (which we observed during pilot tests). To analyze the data, the first two authors reviewed the data corpus and identified student cases who showed expertise in (a) mathematics, (b) interactions  with AI tools in mathematically-productive ways, (c) both (a) and (b), and (d) neither. We developed analytic cases (Yin, 2017) 2641 for our selected students by attending to how each formed problem representations, visibly coordinated their mathematical knowledge by linking two or more concepts (schema), and used ChatGPT-4o to advance their problem solving. Demographics and details for the selected students are displayed on the conference poster. Figure 1 The Roller Coaster Task Kamden: High mathematical expertise and knowledge of AI tools. Kamden was a junior civil engineering student who used ChatGPT seven times while solving Roller Coaster. He generally and quickly understood what each component of the task was asking (representations) and prompted ChatGPT in ways that reflected knowledge of mathematical processes (e.g., “plug in 20 for x in the equation provided for me above,”). He knew that ChatGPT often hallucinated in multi-step mathematics problems with precise values, so he asked it to display the Python code used to develop its solution. This showed Kamden’s coordinated schema across mathematics and AI tools (e.g., instrumental genesis for math, Drijvers et al. 2010). Addy: High knowledge of AI tools.

Addy was a first-year bioengineering student who used AI but struggled with math. She did not immediately know what the task was asking; for instance, she conflated domain and range with statistics (a schematic error). However, Addy knew that to understand mathematics with ChatGPT, she needed to break down her queries into multiple prompts (“That’s my next question. Like, given the domain and range…examples?”). This strategy helped break the task into sub-goals, although did not appear to support her overall problem solving processes. Willow: High mathematical expertise.

Willow was a first-year mechanical engineering student who distrusted ChatGPT because “it gave [them] wrong answers once on a problem.” Willow relied on their own mathematical representations and accurately proposed that the roller coaster could take on the function form of a dampened sine wave, a concept they had learned about in physics (coordination of schema). When Willow prompted ChatGPT to obtain the dampened sine wave equation, they remarked, “ This was not what I am looking for. ” Willow ultimately re verted back to their knowledge of parabolas to solve the task, although they could have prompted AI to gain more clarification. Laurel: Neither AI tool nor mathematical expertise Laurel was a senior aerospace engineering student who worked only on domain and range. While she prompted ChatGPT often, she coordinated its output with her schema in ways that led her to develop incorrect notions of the task representation. She found two separate intervals of domain and range for each hill, in part  because she misunderstood ChatGPT’s output. Laurel was aware of her confusions (“I don’t know how to approach this problem”) but did not appear to know how to interact with ChatGPT in ways that might lead toward resolution. Both mathematics and AI tool expertise influence students’ problem solving processes. Knowledge of mathematics was essential for evaluating AI output; in fact, we observed in Kamden that developed math schemas were associated with sophisticated problem sol ving strategies that took longer to construct and verify. Similarly, students’ knowledge of AI impacted the degree to which they pursued clarification and validation of their ideas. This includes knowledge of what and how to prompt AI tools to obtain mathe matical clarity. Our results, though exploratory and in a clinical setting, suggest that researchers and educators should attend to multiple knowledge modes when analyzing student-AI interactions.
