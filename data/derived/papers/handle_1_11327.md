# Datafying Spaces and Places: Synergies and Disconnects between Youth-Authored Maps and Data Visualizations

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research on informal learning often elevates the knowledge that youth develop in their daily interactions, relationships, and movement across their homes and communities. Youth-authored geographies-whether in drawings, maps, or photographs-offer one rich perspective into this knowledge. We extend analytic techniques developed for youth geographies to another kind of youth-authored representation: data visualizations. Specifically, we use Davis and Barsoum’s (2022) framework to identify ways that the spatial/temporal layers, hierarchies, relationships, and rhythms of phenomena that youth elevate within their communities are reflected or obscured within visualizations they create of sensor data collected in those spaces. We also emphasize annotations as a powerful tool for youth to surface these elements. We propose that awareness of these synergies and disconnects could inform data science education that leverages youth’s everyday knowledge.

## Introduction and background

Young people bring rich, nuanced understandings of the world around them to their data science endeavors, drawn from their “lived landscapes” co-constituted with and within the built, historic, and natural landscapes of daily life through social relations (Lanouette, 2022;  Seyer-Ochi, 2006). Citizen science-also referred to more inclusively as community science-often seeks to leverage young peoples’ proximity to  local issues as a means for supporting disciplinary learning and civic engagement (e.g., Ballard et al., 2024). Rarely do these participation structures support young people to formulate the an choring questions that drive such local inquiries, produce the data relevant to these questions, and author data v isualizations to serve their expressive goals. Inst ead, young people are often tasked with pursuing adults’ and b roader organizations’ a  priori commitments, with limited opportunities to carry their nuanced understandings  of their worlds into data science pursuits and pra ctices (Jadallah et al., 2024; Phillips et al., 2019). As such, how young people move between familiar g eographies and datafied (Mascheroni, 2020) versions of these places and spaces remains less un derstood, particularly related to what persists and what is lost as youth construct and reconstruct the world in data. In this study, we asked, how do young people’s rhythms, relationships, hierarchies and spatial/ temporal sense of local places carry through to their data representations? We explored potential synergies and disconnects between youth-authored geographies and data visualizations. We applied Davis and Barsoum’s (2022) framework for analyzing youth drawings of place to the drawin gs, maps, and photographs that 11-and 12-year-old youth created of people, places, and pets as part of a 5-day data science camp. We also applied that framewo rk to visualizations youth created of the sensor data they collected to explore those places and phenomena. In analyzing youth’s data visualizations, we attended not to ind icators of normative visual design (e.g., Tufte, 2001)  or mathematical practice (e.g., axis labels, units, scale; Common Core State Standards, 2010) but instead to indicators of the lived knowledge and experience that youth br ing to their sensemaking of data. By sketching patt erns in youth’s use of drawings and data visualizations and  presenting two, more detailed examples, we illustrate how hierarchies and spatial/temporal layers persist and  change across youth’s drawings and data visualizat ions. We also note how annotations were a critical tool for communicating these elements. Implications include a new perspective on data visualizations as a medium in which to elevate and connect to place-based knowledge as well as proposals to amplify youth voice within data visualizations.

## Methods

Activities We draw on data from 14 11-and 12-year-old youth who participated in a 5-day camp at a science museum in a major metropolitan area in the Northeast U nited States. Participants formulated research questions about meaningful spaces in their communities, used low-cost environmental sensors (e.g., water quality, illuminance, sound, button sets) to collect data about their topic, cre ated visualizations of their data, and presented to  their families in a poster session. When youth printed vi sualizations for their posters, facilitators encour aged them to add hand-written annotations to explain how the visualization addressed their guiding research question. Data sources To study how youth depicted familiar places in imag es and data visualizations, we draw on their drawin gs of places of interest (Day 1; prompt “What's a place you care about and want to learn more about?”) and the drawings, photographs, and data visualizations on their poste rs (Day 5). We complement this information with the ir work throughout the week, including at-home worksheets (Days 1 and 2), conversations while  preparing their projects (Day 4), and video of their presentations (Day 5).

## Data analysis

We used Davis and Barsoum’s (2022) framework for analyzing youth’s drawings of places and spaces. From their analysis of students’ drawings of their school comm unities, Davis and Barsoum identify four dimensions  of interest within youth’s place-based drawings: ● Relationships-representations and connections between people; ● Hierarchies-centrality or elevation of certain personal, material, or social elements; ● Rhythms-the flow of focus across elements within the drawings; and ● Spatial/temporal layers-the simultaneous depiction of multiple spaces or times. Davis and Barsoum also propose conventions for visu ally depicting these four elements (Figure 1), with relationships indicated by connected yellow and ora nge circles, hierarchies indicated by pink dashed arrows, rhythms indicated by blue curves, and spatial/temporal layers indicated by layered rectangles. We especially attended to youths’ written explanations  and annotations on their data visualizations. Desp ite inherent subjectivity in interpreting youth’s writing, we  interpreted their annotations as efforts to highlig ht particular dimensions of their visualizations. Elements of the  framework look different across drawings and data visualizations. For example, relationships might lo ok like human figures in drawings and  words like “people,” “my mom,” or “I” in data visualizations. Nonetheles s, we studied if and how the elements that youth em phasize in their drawings appear in their data visualizations and vice versa. We analyzed each youth’s drawings and data visualiz ations separately. We did not seek out dimensions from their drawings in their visualizations but ins tead aimed to identify which elements arose in each representation. Three researchers performed the dat a coding. The first author selected one youth’s work to establish coding norms. Each researcher coded this youth’s drawings and data visualizations independen tly and collaboratively resolved any discrepancies. The res earchers coded the remaining youths’ work independe ntly, reviewing each other’s work and revising coding decisions through conversation. We present overall trends within this analysis and showcase two youth’s work to illustrate how elements of the framework appear across their representation s. Focal youth were selected based on the clarity w ith which framework elements appeared across their drawings and data visualizations.

## Results

The framework elements appeared across both represe ntation types with roughly the same relative freque ncy: hierarchies were the most common (used by 80% of youth in their drawings and 81% in their data visualizations), followed by spatial/temporal layers (33% and 38%, respectively), with relationships and rhythms equally and third most common (27% and 13%, respectively). Hierarchies in youths’ data visualizations typically emphasized numeric values, whereas hierarchies in their drawin gs emphasized locations. We note that relationships and rhythms appeared in data visualizations at about half the rate as in drawings. Given the salience of hierarchies and spatial/temporal layers across both representations, we present tw o focal youths’ work to illustrate how they maintained and shifted hierarchical elements and emphasized spatial layers across representations. Reflecting spatial layers across drawings and visualizations Nathan explored “the different noise levels of hous e birds and wild birds” (Day 1 worksheet). He indic ated this spatial separation of inside vs. outside in his dra wing (Figure 2, left), which also included the rela tionships between three outside birds and elevated the sounds they made. ## Discussion and conclusions

Our analysis of youth-authored representations of community phenomena dur ing a 5-day data science camp surfaced differences across drawings and data visua lizations; whereas locations were of preeminent imp ortance in drawings, numerical values were-predictably-emphasized in data visualizations. Additionally, yo uth developed techniques to communicate spatial layers in their data visualizations. To the extent that th ey appeared in drawings, relationships and rhythms were notably  scarce in data visualizations, suggesting an area for further support. Yet youth can and do express local knowled ge of their “lived landscapes” (Seyer-Ochi, 2006) in data visualizations they create, a finding that can info rm researchers’, designers,’ and facilitators’ work  in elevat ing that knowledge and helping youth interweave it into their data science learning. We also note the critical role of annotations for youth to express-and for researchers to understand and document-their local knowledge. Indeed, our analysis of youth’s data visualizations relied on those annotations; we took them as signals of what youth valued in their daily lives and in their visualizations. Annotations provided evidence of all the framework elements: spatial lay ers and hierarchies, as the examples above illustra te, and also of relationships (often “each dot represents a person”) and rhythms (movement between places indicated by dots). We suggest that annotations serve as an important middle ground between young peoples’ “lived landscapes” and their drawings and data visualizations, a means to express personal and place-based knowledg e within the otherwise somewhat rigidly quantitative world of se nsor-based data visualizations. Better characterizing th e role of annotations, identifying additional, similarly e xpressive tools for data authorship, and including these tools in data science  curricula could further amplify youth voice and loc al knowledge in increasingly datafied (Mascheroni, 2020)  worlds and shift power back towards youth-authored (Jadallah et al, 2024) insights and futures.
