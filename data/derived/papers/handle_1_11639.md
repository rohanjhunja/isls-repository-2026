# Reasoning and Geometric Patterns in Visual Programming with Non-STEM majors

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We explored how non-STEM majors reason while programming geometric patterns in Scratch. Participants (a) used recursive reasoning to transform patterns into manipulable forms via visual cues and (b) applied nested loops to expand patterns across distant cases. Fraction-based reasoning emerged while adjusting proportions to refine pattern components.

## Introduction

Generalization involves identifying  commonalities, expanding thinking beyond given parts, and recognizing patterns (Kaput, 1999).  We asked: How do novice non-STEM programming learners reason while programming geometrical patterns? Visualization supports pattern generalization by re vealing structural relationships through visual scanning and animations  (Carraher et al., 2008; Stylianides et al., 2016; W ilkie, 2021). Geometric transformations help justify assertions and identif y relations without relying solely on numbers  (Lannin, 2005; Rivera & Becker, 2007), aiding in rule generation  (Rivera, 2010). Visual programming offer s a space  where virtual agents and animations support coding (Basawapatna, 2016) and tinkering as a reasoning process (Kim et al., 2021). In mathematics learning, explicit reasoning involves deriving a closed-form rule (Carraher et al., 2008), while recursive reasoning builds rules from relatio nships between consecutive elements  (Lannin et al., 2006; Stylianides et al., 2016). Students favor recursive reasoning (e.g., Zazkis & Liljedahl, 2002), using it as a starting point for generalization (Rowland et al., 2018). In contrast, many struggle with explicit rules (Obara, 2019). Recursive reasoning is essential for solving some problems and understanding relationships (Store, 2018).

## Method

The participants were from a large university in the US. Participants included undergraduate students majoring in kinesiology, psychology, and humandevelopment and family studies (HDFS) (see Table 1).  A sample pattern generalization problem and Scratch were introduced, and then participants engaged in programming activi ties (see Figure 1). In Zoom sessions, screen recordings were 347 minutes in total. Participants were prompt ed with thought-provoking questions such as:  What was the relationship you discovered to create the pattern? and How would you describe this relationship? Data analysis process, interpretation of the coded data, and deriving themes were guided by visual cues and mathematical reasoning. Rounds of discussions between the authors occurred. ## Participants reasoned recursively and manipulated patterns via visual cues

Participants visualized expanding the given case through Scratch cat, blocks’ colors, and the pattern drawn. Sarah interacted with the Scratch cat by changing its position and tracing its movements while expanding the base case (see Figure  2). Participants used recursive reasoning and nested loops to build complex patterns All participants used a base case to create a geometric pattern and nested loops and relations to expand the pattern, managing increasing complexity. For example, Lucy r easoned using nested loops to manage increasing pat tern complexity. While creating the five joint squares, she noticed and used a repeat block at a higher lev el: “Like there’s gotta be a way to use the repeat part becau se, well, could I just... put like a bigger repeat if that makes sense? Like put a repeat outside of that repeat, th en do that for a hundred.” Similarly, Ellie coded a square using “take 50 steps and turn 90 degrees,” which is the base case for expansion. She considered linking shapes through motion and positioning, showing recursive expressio n by building multiple squares from the single-square solution. Refining her approach, she decided to “ju st continue this path,” using recursive relations r ather than creating separate squares. As the pattern grew, she  aimed for eff iciency, describing the process of “adding on” and “expanding,” signaling a shift to loops for scaling the pattern. Two participants used recursive and fractional reasoning to program patterns Conner and Natalie demonstrated recursive and fract ion-based reasoning while debugging their code. Conner scaled patterns by repeating a base unit of four re d and three blue squares, noting the ratio as “almo st... like a fraction.” He scaled this to eight red and six blue  squares, showing an understanding of proportions a nd how to adjust code values to maintain consistent relationships. Natalie compared side lengths of red and blue patterns (in Task 2: Debugging Square Patterns), aiming for equa l sizing. Her calculations (e.g., 45×3=135; 4×30=12 0) reflected fractional reasoning. She adjusted the mo ve value to 33.75 to fine-tune proportions, demonstrating precision and understanding of how fractional changes affect the pattern. Iterative testing showed reasoning about parts and wholes in proportional relationships.
