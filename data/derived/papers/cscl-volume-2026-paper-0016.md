# Exploring Variations in Teacher Modelling and Scaffolding Through Chatbot Interactions in Middle School Classrooms

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, we develop new insights into how teachers can model, scaffold and coach students when using tailored chatbots in a school subject. Our interaction analysis, based on a cognitive apprenticeship framework, shows how t eachers affirm student statements regarding the chatbot's lack of knowledge structures. W e identify a few instances of teacher elaboration on the chatbots' subject-specific knowledge structures during classroom interactions. Teachers mainly provide concrete scaffolding support for the tasks and confirm students' questions and claims about inaccuracies in chatbot outputs. Our analysis also indicates that the balance between modelling and scaffolding depends on how specific sequences in teaching and learning activities are executed.

The emergence of generative artificial intelligence (genAI) presents significant opportunities and challenges for classroom research, particularly concerning contextualization, knowledge, and agency (Bastani et al., 2025; Klar, 2025; Warschauer et al., 2023). Researchers in AI and education highlight the importance of both students and teachers understanding the functions of large language models (LLMs) and their potential to enhance specific learning processes. Moreover, it is vital to establish new collaborative repert oire and roles among AI, students, and teachers (Tjønn et al., 2025).

Partnerships with schools to support development in the classroom is a key issue within the field of CSCL. In the research project “Learning in the Age of Algorithms ” (LAA), we have designed chatbots with subject-specific and generative capabilities for use in middle schools together with school developers and teachers in a Norwegian municipality. It  has been necessary to develop new designs for teaching in classrooms that incorporate chatbots, creating what we refer to as local cultural fit  (Ludvigsen & Mørch, 2003). In our research, we aim to describe and understand how chatbots are utilized in middle school classrooms with students aged 10 to 16, including the teacher’s instructions. To analyse teacher scaffolding and modelling of interactions with chatbots, we pose the following research question in this paper (RQ1): What characterizes teachers’ scaffolding and modelling of students’ chatbot use in two Norwegian middle school classrooms, as analyzed through the cognitive apprenticeship framework?

RQ1 guided our analysis of how teachers model their interactions with chatbots for 9th grade students to observe. We used a conceptual framework (analytic stance) to anchor the analysis. This framework incorporates key concepts in collaborative learning that capture the intersection of social and cognitive processes. Our analysis builds upon existing literature regarding the use of AI tools in K-12 classrooms.

## Literature review

In this brief review, we emphasize studies that provide insights into the early phases of AI in education, as well as recent studies utilizing various forms of genAI, including both quantitative and qualitative research. We argue that in this new phase, it is esse ntial to examine different research designs to understand how students interact with and learn from genAI.

Interaction with different AI tools in the classroom has been explored in educational research, particularly AI in education, computers and education, including Computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL). A classical CSCL study by Baker and Bielaczyc (1995) investigated how students aged 16 –17 engaged with the CHENE system, an AI platform designed for scientific mode lling in physics. It aimed to foster co-construction of explanations using scientific vocabulary between student partners in interaction. The study revealed missed opportunities; only one student engaged in higher-level reasoning while their partner did not, limiting their chances for deeper understanding. In contrast, Suthers et al. (1997) examined the networked software system, Belvedere, which used AI coaches to support groups of 2-6 students. Observations showed that students found the activities engaging and teachers were satisfied with their training. The authors asserted that the success of CSCL technology depends on its acceptance by teachers (Suthers et al., 1997). However, effective dialogues in computational learning design cannot be assumed, and c ognitive challenges persist even with 61 technological advancements (Baker et al., 2021). These limitations highlight the necessity for further exploration of teachers' roles with genAI tools in educational contexts, as genAI, in contrast to systems like CHENE/Belvedere, do not function as knowledge systems (Holmes & Tuomi, 2022). Borge et al. (2024) examined how interactions with ChatGPT 3.5 could foster new learning environments that support socio-metacognition. Their study revealed promising results from an experienced CSCL researcher interacting with ChatGPT, highlighting a pattern of  both domain-specific and generic expertise. The authors suggested that genAI is particularly effective when students are shown expert interactions with chatbots (modelling). However, they emphasized the need for further exploration in natural classroom settings and adapting models for student use across various levels and subjects. Chen et al. (2023) illustrated differences in student experiences with generative AI through their study on high school students (n=10). Interviews revealed that students found ChatGPT helpful for information retrieval and completing monotonous tasks but had mixed views on its potential to enhance more advanced knowledge building, as well as varying levels of critical thinking toward its use. While both studies imply the importance of modelling and support, neither study consists of explicit examples of teacher modelling/scaffolding.

We have identified relevant experimental studies of the use of genAI in K-12 classrooms as part of a general research exploration stage. Klar (2025) examined how 9th and 10th graders (n=106) interacted with a generic version of ChatGPT 3.5. The study compared pre-and post-tests of an experimental group receiving brief guidance on adaptation prompting with a control group that did not. Students found the chatbot easy to use, while the control group underutilized its adaptation capabilities. The experimental group used adaptation prompts twice as often (M=6.0) as the control group (M=2.5), indicating different interaction patterns. Both groups, however, showed similar knowledge gains and high satisfaction levels. The study is based on a 50-minute session (Klar, 2025). In contrast, Lademann et al. (2025) conducted a randomized control study with 6th grade students (n=214) using a domain-specific, custom chatbot based on ChatGPT 4.0. The results demonstrated that AI-generated material significantly impacted students’ situational interest and self-efficacy while reducing cognitive load. However, its impact on learning outcomes was not significant. Recent CSCL studies Research on genAI  interaction in K-12 classrooms is emerging within CSCL. Ohsaki and Matsukawa (2025) analyzed high school students’ (n=280) interactions with genAI, finding that the chatbot frequently provided advice to science project groups without explicit requests. Their analysis revealed significant differences in message lengths (436.3 characters for chatbots vs. 54.8 for user messages), suggesting limited engagement from students. Xin et al. (2025) introduced CocoNote, a genAI-driven platform that enhances students' socially shared regulation (SSRL) in collaborative environments. A quasi-experimental study with 6th graders (n=78) showed significant improvements in SSRL capabilities, supported by positive feedback from five interviewed students. Ojeda-Ramirez et al. (2025) explored how elementary and middle school students (n=30) aged 6-12 engaged with generative AI in storytelling (Story AI), revealing that students balance AI contributions with their own creativity and reflect on its role in their narratives.

Our literature review reveals a lack of studies on the  interaction between students and teachers in naturalistic settings (classrooms). Few genAI studies provide detailed descriptions of chatbot design, teacher and student perceptions, or analysis of the actual partnerships and teacher modelling occurring in K-12 classrooms during experiments. Additionally, limited research has explored instructional designs that integrate tailored genAI into learning activities. Consequently, current research does not adequately address how genAI technology can be combined with teacher instruction, ongoing (e.g. design-based) research, diverse resources, and interactions among teachers, students, and peers. This gap is what we aim to address by investigating RQ1. Analytic stance Modelling and scaffolding are key concepts in our analysis. Both are central components of the approach known as Cognitive Apprenticeship (CA) (Collins et al., 1991; Collins & Kapur, 2014), applied in classrooms. Collins et al. (1991) argue that insufficie nt attention is  often given to the reasoning strategies experts use to acquire and apply knowledge for complex real-world tasks. Consequently, the conceptual and problem-solving knowledge students gain in school often goes unused; they struggle with problems that deviate from established patterns and neglect available resources due to a lack of effective models. To improve students' skills, it is essential to understand expert practices and develop appropriate learning methods. In the CA approach, the actor s’ thinking must be made visible for both teachers and students, whether in reading, writing, or problem-solving. Additionally, CA seeks to contextualize abstract tasks in ways which are meaningful to students and introduces a range of tasks from systematic to varied, encouraging students to reflect on and articulate common characteristics. The goal is to help students generalize the skill, recognize its relevance, and transfer it tonew situations 62 independently. Collins et al. (1991) ha ve developed a framework outlining principles for designing cognitive apprenticeship environments. We primarily utilize the defined characteristics of the Content and Methods dimensions, as these serve as the central focus of the empirical analysis. In addition to the descriptions in Table 1, modelling involves students constructing conceptual models of the processes needed to complete a task. Coaching also entails providing hints and feedback related to specific issues that arise as students work to accomplish a task (Col lins & Kapur, 2014).  Scaffolding refers to the support a teacher provides to help students complete a task (Wood, Bruner & Ross, 1976). An important distinction between coaching and scaffolding is that scaffolding more specifically pertains to the support given to the learner and can take the form of suggestions or direct help. The timing of this support is critical: it can be offered initially and then gradually fade as students gain independence, or it can be provided after the learner encounters an impasse or fails to comple te a task (Collins & Kapur, 2014). Additionally, the CA framework consists of additional characteristics of the M ethod dimension: articulation (the teacher encourages students to articulate their knowledge, problem-solving processes or reasoning), reflection (the teacher enables students to compare their performance with that of their peers or an expert) and exploration (the teacher invites students to formulate and solve their own problems) (Collins et al., 1991, Collins & Kapur, 2014). Like the previously mentioned genAI studies, interventions based on CA in K-12 classrooms have also been investigated in recent studies. Wang et al. (2024) conducted a quasi-experimental study with high school students (n=150) that found cognitive apprenticeship models in math outperformed conventional teacher-centred instruction (Wang et al., 2024). Similarly, De La Paz et al. (2024) examined 8th grade students (n=110) argumentative writing in science. The results show that an intervention based on a cognitive apprenticeship approach helped students develop skills needed to improve their written sc ientific arguments (De La Paz et al., 2024).

To understand how classroom dialogue develops knowledge and understanding, we must examine the temporal relationship between the organization of teaching and learning as sequences of lessons and activities and how this is enacted through conversation (Merc er, 2019, pp. 190– 191). Our analytical approach draws on key Cognitive Apprenticeship (CA) concepts in Table 1, focusing on how teacher modelling, coaching and scaffolding of chatbot interaction are manifested in classroom dialogue (Bakhtin, 1986; Greeno & Vande Sande, 2007; Tabak & Kyza, 2018).

## Teacher 2: “Ted” – modelling activities in whole-class discussions

Ted teaches social science and English at another middle school in the municipality. His class was observed during the initial testing; thus his 9 th grade students have prior experience using chatbots. In the first observed lesson, one of Ted’s initial sequential actions is to display the subject plan and learning goals for the period on a large screen: Learn to be critical of sources. Consider advantages and disadvantages of different kinds of sources. Obtain a general overview of the conflict known as the First World War. The students will work with both analoge and digital sources during the period, including SchoolGPT. During the fifth lesson, Ted facilitates the students’ use of SchoolGPT as a source during his lesson introduction: [Ted]:  Today, we are going to do the same thing, except that... Today we will use SchoolGPT. Or AI. Artificial intelligence. So, before we get started, I want you to talk a little among yourselves about whether you remember... what is AI? What is SchoolGPT? If you recall this ? Also, if you remember any tips and tricks for how to get better answers when using it. In a subsequent whole-class dialogue, students suggest making the content easier to understand by giving SchoolGPT context and boundaries and by checking information with other sources (community of practice). The student task is to identify two significant events from World War I. Students are instructed to conduct a source 66 critical analysis of the chatbot's output by comparing the  SchoolGPT responses with those from other digital sources. We interpret the excerpts as showing that both Ted and Mike display several characteristics of different CA dimensions that support students’ interactions with chatbots. Consistent with studies of expert and novice chatbot use (e.g., Borge et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2023), notable differences appear in how teachers articulate their rationale for writing inputs and interpreting outputs. Both teachers make their thinking visible by identifying, demonstrating, and correcting issues in students' prompts during whole-class and teacher –student interactions. The CA framework’s dimensions (Collins et al., 1991) of coaching, modelling, scaffolding, and problem-solving strategies are relevant in the analysed interactions. In the literature review, some K-12 studies report promising results when students interact with genAI (Chen et al., 2023; Lademann et al., 2025; Xin et al., 2025; Ojeda-Ramirez et al., 2025). As both literature (e.g., Baker et al., 2021; Borge et al., 2024; Klar et al., 2025) and the excerpts demonstrate, novice students may require teacher support when interacting with genAI tools. Our contribution to the CSCL field  is the identification of distributed scaffolding prompts, namely how the teachers share their initial guiding prompts before students engage with the chatbots. In the CA framework, scaffolding denotes the support a teacher provides to help students perform a task (Collins et al., 1991). The scaffolding prompts used by the teachers do not necessarily require continuous teacher intervention during exchanges among students and chatbots, neither must they include demonstrations of chatbot interaction; rather, they could serve as an essential starting point for students’ subsequent interaction with the chatbots. We therefore suggest that teachers, as Ted and Mike do, ahead of time design and distribute subject-specific scaffolding prompts before engaging in modelling, coaching, or other kinds of scaffolding in their classrooms. The teachers’ enactment of the C ontent dimension in the CA framework is an interesting finding. As they model and scaffold chatbot interaction, we find instances where incorrect knowledge claims are neither dealt with nor elaborated upon, or where explanations for SchoolGPT’s inaccuracies remain implicit. For example, in line #1 of Table 4, S5 independently flags a partly incorrect claim by referencing other sources. Mike subsequently prompts them to disregard the false birth year, deeming it less central to the task. In Table 5, S7 observes that SchoolGPT cannot provide concrete sources, and Ted confirms but does  not explain the model’s underlying knowledge structures or limits. In  Table 6, Ted tends to confirm students’ statements instead of elaborating on how the system generates answers or why it should not be treated as an epistemic authority. Both teachers explicitly emphasize critical thinking about SchoolGPT’s output in their introductions, and coaching is an important CA characteristic in these lessons (Collins & Kapur, 2014). However, relying primarily on coaching by confirming student claims without concrete scaffolding or modelling support, could represent missed opportunities to develop students’ understanding of the technology and content’s epistemic status. Through their explicit learning goals, initial scaffolding prompts, and whole-class discussions, both teachers highlight and somewhat problematize potential inaccuracies in the domain-specific knowledge presented by SchoolGPT. By questioning and adjusting students’ chatbot inputs and by making their reasoning visible when addressing chatbot output, Ted and Mike both scaffold and model how to treat SchoolGPT as an interactive classroom resource. This kind of support could be vital when students interact with genAI tools as emphasized by previous research (e.g., Baker et al., 2021; Borge et al., 2024; Klar et al., 2025). Beyond providing distributed scaffolding prompts that can support students over a sequence of actions, the teachers demonstrate practical  preparation to provide various supports that may occur during lessons. This could include offering explicit explanations for  why SchoolGPT and other tools built on LLMs can produce inaccurate information, as these models cannot list the sources for the output. In collaboration with our project partners, we will continue to explore additional support for teachers integrating genAI into instructional design, including further refinement of SchoolGPT aimed at reducing the incidence of inaccurate outputs. Additionally, we will emphasize another important finding, namely  how the integration of chatbots into specific content-generating sequences will depend on the lesson design. Overall, our study shows that students need to be taught to always think critically  when interacting with chatbots, as  the information provided may be incorrect or imprecise.
