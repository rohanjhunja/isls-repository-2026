# Educator-Learner Interactions in Failure-Based Problem-Solving During STEM Activities: Patterns in Solo and Group Contexts

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The purpose of this study was to examine common failure problem-solving patterns within one learner-one educator interactions (i.e., solo) and many learners-one educator interactions (i.e., group) when experiencing failure moments during STEM activities in museum settings. The results of this study indicate that educators engage in the problem-solving process more cyclically than learners, regardless of context. In addition, educators and learner(s) engage in the failure-based problem-solving cycle together with no clear divide between roles.

Purpose Educators in informal learning environments play a vital role in young learners ’ reasoning and learning of concepts (Sager et al., 2024), engagement (Shaby et  al., 2018), and persistence through failure (Simpson et al., 2019), grounded in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM). We built upon this knowle dge base to consider educators' interactions  with young learners as part of the problem-solving process, particularly through moments of failure. Specifically, we addres s the following research question: What are the com mon failure problem-solving patterns within learner-educator interactions when experiencing failure moments during STEM activities in museum settings, particularly wi thin one learner-one educator interactions (i.e., solo) and many learners-one educator interactions (i.e., group)? As researc h highlights, problem-solving is a key skill for young children to develop as STEM learners (English, 2023), and serves as a skill to support learners’  cycle of failure analysis and design iteration (Erol, 2024) and promote innovation and creativity (Adeoye & Jimoh, 2023). Vossoughi and colleagues (2021) argued for pedagogi cal approaches which engage educators and learners in joint activity, where children work alo ngside adults to carry out activities” through a co llaborative process (p. 214). This approach  moves beyond rigid binaries, such as adult-or educator-centered versus child-centered frameworks (Vossoughi et al., 2021) or school knowledge versus everyday knowledge (Gutiérrez, 2008). However, this shift in approach is not without chal lenges as adults adapt their role from an expert to a facilitator and collaborator within the learning process (Ramey & Stevens, 2023). Moreover, in a group setting, di alogue is multidirectional as opposed to bidirectional (Gilli es, 2006), which implies that educators may facilit ate learning differently when interacting with groups than in a one-on-one situation.

## Methods

This study involved ten museums from various locati ons across the United States. Across these museums, 31 informal educators participated. We collected 38 self-recorded videos of educators interacting with a learner or a group of learners (e.g., class, family) during a ST EM-making activity. There were 20 videos (52.6%) where the educator interacted with a group of learners and 18  videos (47.4%) where the educator interacted with a solo learner. The length of the videos ranged from 00:02:40 (hh:mm:ss) to 01:35:28 (hh:mm:ss). The group videos were 19:25:59 in total, and the solo videos were 06:56:18 in total.  The first two authors  watched each video to identify episodes in which failure(s) by the learner or educator occurred. Failure within the activities was defined as when something did not go as expected, and was o bserved more often during the testing phase. Next, Author 2 and Author 3 coded the episodes line-by-line, providing codes for both learners and educato rs. Coding was informed by the failure-based problem-solving cycle by Tawfik et al. (2015), and included such elements as experienced failure, new solution generation, and a ssess viability of outcomes. Throughout the coding process, the two authors met to discuss disagreements in coding and come to a consensus. The interaction between the educators and the learn ers in each video was analyzed using  sequential analysis, a method used to study the sequence of interactions (Bakeman, 1995; Bakeman & Quera, 2011). In this study, we incorporated lag sequential analysis to determine how behavior A affects behavior B. In this study, we considered four relationships: (a) educators as the  given and the learners as the target (EL); (b) learners as the given and educators as the target (LE); (c) educators as the given and the target (EE); and (d) learners as the given and the target (LL). In order to analyze the impact of the context of the learning environment, we separated the data by pooling solo sessions and pooling group sessions. We calculated the corresponding z-scores and the subsequent p-values with a significance level of.05.

## Results

In comparing the results  (see Figure 1), we made four observations. One, educators engaged in the problem-solving failure cycle in a more cyclical and complete process  than learners, regardless of solo or group session. For learners, although they engaged in the differen t components, one component of the problem-solving failure cycle did not necessarily lead to another. For exam ple, identifying the reason or cause for the failur es did not significantly transition to lea rners generating new solutions. Two, in the solo se ssions, educators informed learners’ engagement in the problem-solving failure cycle more often than in the group sessions: E Inquiry L Inquiry (p<.05, z=2.35), E GenerateL Implement (p<.01, z=7.16), and E Mental Foreshadowing L Generate (p<.01, z=3.21). In the group session, this only was observed as E GenerateL Implement (p<.01, z=4.35). Three, in both solo and group sessions, educators ’ experience with failures became a moment for learne rs to engage in the problem-solving failure process through assessing the outco me (Solo: E Failure L Assess, p<.05, z=2.13; Group: E FailureL Assess, p<.01, z=2.94) and identifying possible causes and reasons for the educators’ failures (Solo: E Failure L Identify, p=.05, z=1.97). Four, in the group sessions, educators were  more likely to engage in dialogue with learners through their failure moments: L TestE Assess (p<.01, z=4.91); L FailureE Inquiry (p<.01, z=8.18); L AssessE Inquiry (p<.05, z=2.14); L Mental ForeshadowingE Test (p<.05, z=2.22). ## Discussion

The results of this study highlight how educators a nd learner(s) are engaged in the problem-solving process as a joint activity as they work alongside one another through moments of failure within a STEM activity (Vossoughi et al., 2021). There is not a clear divide in the r ole of the educators and the role of the learners; they are engaged as an intergenerational collaborative in both sessi ons. This points to the problem-solving process as knowledgecreating learning between learners and educators wi thin i nformal STEM learning environments (Baker et al.,

2022) that can inform the development of learners’ problem-solving skills as STEM learners (e.g., Erol et al.,

2024). In the solo sessions, we further observed ed ucators significantly shaping the learners’ engagem ent in the various components of the problem-solving failure cycle more than the group sessions. This may support the idea that dialogue is bidirectional (Gillies, 2006), but the difference lies in who initiates the interaction. The results of this study ha ve implications for informal STEM learning environment s and the learning sciences. First, research should look beyond binari es and consider how learning may occur collaboratively between learners and educators. Second, we need to invest time in supporting the pedagogical practices of educators as relational and an artistic interaction  as opposed to uni-directional (e.g., adult-centered). Third, fostering learner-initiated dialogue should be considered in the design of STEM learning to support joint activity.
