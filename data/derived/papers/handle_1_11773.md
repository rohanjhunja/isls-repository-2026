# Learners’ Attitudes, Wishes, and Concerns Toward Learning Analytics in Higher Education: Students’ Opinions and Teachers’ Views

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To facilitate the optimal use of Learning Analytics (LA) in educational settings, it is es-sential to ensure that learners’ needs and preferences for LA are aligned with teachers’ as-sumptions about these. This study examined students’ attitudes, wishes, and concerns to-ward LA in higher education and investigated the accuracy of teachers’ assumptions about their students’ opinions. Two surveys were conducted: one with Educational Science stu-dents (N = 19) and another with their lecturers (N = 12). The results revealed a general matching between students’ and lecturers’ assessments. For example, both groups indicat-ed that students favor LA that refer to learners’ individual learning histories against LA that promote social comparison with peers. But we also observed some notable discrepan-cies. Teachers considered their students to be most in need of support on the motivational dimension of self-regulation, while students indicated the least need for support on this dimension.

## Introduction and theoretical background

Over the past decade, the field of Learning Analytics (LA) has attracted considerable interest from both researchers and practitioners in the educational landscape.  The use of LA in higher education and beyond can as sist in monitoring learning processes, providing students with timely, personalized support, facilitating individual learning, and helping students achieve their academic go als (Jivet et al., 2018). Although the high potenti al of LA is widely recognized, several challenges remain regarding their implementation. One of these  challenges is the involvement of the key stakeholders – students and teachers – in the development and implementation of LA. This appears to be a fundamental prerequisite for the creation of thoughtful, user-centered LA and the development of targeted features that add ress the genuine needs of learners and educators (G uzmán-Valenzuela et al., 2021). Paying close attention to stakeholders’ attitudes, wishes, and concerns ensures that they accept the output presented by LA systems, consider it relevant, and take it in to account in their self-reflection and further decision-making (Márquez et al., 2024). Nevertheless, higher  education stakeholders are often not sufficiently engaged in the process of developing and implementing LA (Kaliisa et al., 2023). Clow’s LA Cycle (Clow, 2012) divides the process of LA into four st eps. As a foundational preliminary step, Clow defines the identification of learners. Indeed, this step can be critical in determining wh at data can or should be collected and in what particular manner. The identification of learners may encompass some characteristics which are relatively straightforward to ascertain, for example, whether a learner is a first-year graduate, an experienced Master’s student, or an informal learner taking part in a free online course. However, certain aspects, such as attitudes, wishes, and concerns regarding L A, can only be accurately assessed through direct e xchange with the learners themselves. Concurrently, when identifying learners, it is important to consider the teachers’ perspective as they can provide invaluable, experience-based commentary from a didactic standpoint. Considering lecturers’ views is not only essential for gaining a more comprehensive picture of the learners to be supported by LA, but it can also ensure that teachers are willin g to actively and consistently use LA in their cour ses as they consider them fitting for their students’ needs (Bennett et al., 2015). Thus, it is critical to ensure that lecturer s’ assumptions about their students’ needs and wishes toward LA are accurate in order to facilitate their willingness to use appropriate LA as instructional activities in their courses. The aim of this study was therefore twofold: First, to investigate students’ attitudes, wishes, and concerns toward LA; and second, to determine whether univers ity teachers have accurate assumptions about attitu des, wishes, and concerns of their students.

Students’ attitudes, wishes, and concerns toward LA Considering stakeholder expectations at the pre-implementation stage of any innovation development is crucial in order to ‘provide valuable insights into subsequent acceptability of the software product to be developed’ (Davis & Venkatesh, 2004, p. 44). It is important to adher e to value-sensitive design and implementation principles at the earliest stages of development, as this is instrumental in fostering stakeholder trust and the optimal use of LA (Drachsler & Greller, 2016). By identifying and incorporating their expectatio ns, developers can reduce the risk of users becoming dissatisfied with the system and abandoning it as a result (Kaliisa et al., 2023). As students can be considered key stakeholders of LA, their involve ment in LA development and implementation is crucia l. If students are not given the opportunity to participate in the decision-making process regarding the selection of the LA, there is a risk that the features provided by t he LA may not align with their actual needs and exp ectations (Whitelock-Wainwright et al., 2019). Consequently, the usefulness of these may be quickly questioned. In higher education, learners appear to have high e xpectations for the functionality and effectiveness  of LA, preferring advanced systems with diverse features (Schumacher & Ifenthaler, 2018), and have clear attitudes about the use of such systems, especially regarding  privacy and ethical considerations (Roberts et al., 2016). Empirical research has demonstrated that students have explicit preferences regarding the design of LA, aimed at facilitating their agency – for instance, the option to customize the design of learner dashboards and the ability to comprehend the provenance of data, thus enabling self-identification of actionable insights (e.g., Bennet t & Folley, 2019). In general, learners have positive atti tudes toward LA. They are willing to share their da ta for LA purposes, provided that the system is transparent, and their privacy and sensitive data are protected (Jones, 2019). However, apart from a few studies (e.g., Roberts et al., 2016), research on student engagement in the development and implementation of LA in practice remains limited.

Teachers’ assumptions abouts student attitudes, wishes, and concerns toward LA Despite the increasing interest in LA in educational research, there is a lack of empirical studies that have specifically analyzed teachers’ assumptions about student s’ attitudes, wishes, and concerns toward LA. In th is section, we present empirical evide nce on teachers’ assumptions about and judgments of  various learner characteristics, as we believe that teachers’ ability to assess these characteristics is closely linked to their ability to assess students’ attitudes, wishes, and concerns toward LA. In educational practice, the accuracy of lecturers’  assumptions about their student characteristics is considered essential as it allows teachers to tailor their course plans more appropriately and increase the likelihood of achieving the learning goals (B egeny et al., 2008). Empirical studies have demonstrated that teachers can often make relativel y accurate presumptions about students’ academic pe rformance (Südkamp et al., 2012), although their assess ments of students’ intellectual abilities are often  oversimplified and rely heavily on generalized measures of academic achieve ment. Further studies revealed a frequent discrepancy between teachers’ perceptions and students’ actual performance anxiety and other self-reported experiences (Boehnke et al., 1986). Finally, teachers’ as sessments of students’ self-concepts, self-regulation strategies, intrinsic motivation, and enjoyment also appear to be only moderately consistent with students’ self-assessments (e.g., Friedrich et al., 2013). However, a certain level of consistency is very imp ortant as teachers’ assumptions about their students’ characteristics may influence  their willingness to proactively adopt certain LA in their courses and even to activate certain LA features and assistance for their students or not. For instance, an optimal choice of the frame of reference used by LA (comparison with peers, distance to goals, or with the learner’s former self) is only possible if teachers can accurately a ssess their students’ baseline motivation and self-efficacy (Jivet et al., 2018). Furthermore, an accurate perception of learners’ existing deficits allows for the most suitable selection of the provided LA-based assistance, particularly with respect to the advancement of self-regulation competencies (e.g., cognitive, metacognitive, or motivational). This study therefore aimed to address two research questions: (Q1) What are students’ attitudes, wishes, and concerns toward LA in higher education? and (Q2) Do teachers’ assumptions about their students’ attitudes, wishes, and concerns toward LA match their students’ actual attitudes, wishes, and concerns?

## Methods

To address our research questions, we conducted two su rveys at a German university: one with Educational Science students (student survey) and one with their l ecturers (teacher survey). We specifically chose pa rticipants from the same institution as our research questions  target the comparability between the views of these specific target groups – students and their teachers. Thus, we needed to make sure that their responses were aligned to the same institutional context.

Samples and survey procedures A total N = 19 Educational Science students participated in the student survey (f = 18, m = 1; Mage = 22.79, SDage = 3.47; Msemester (Bachelor of Arts) = 4.79, SDsemester (Bachelor of Arts) = 1.99), which was administered in one-on-one interviews. Most participants were relatively unfamiliar with LA (M = 3.47, SD = 1.35; on a scale from 1 (strong disagreement) to 5 (strong agreement), which we used in all r atings). Nevertheless, they welcomed the use of digital teaching and learning concepts in higher education (M = 4.37, SD = 0.60). In the teacher survey, all lecturers from the Institute of Educational Research at a  German university were contacted by email and invi ted to participate in an online survey. In total, N = 12 teachers took part in the online survey, of wh om n = 8 (m = 4, f = 2, d = 1, n. a. = 1; Mage = 36.38, SDage = 10.45) filled out the survey completely. Their average number of years of teaching experience was M = 8.94 (SD = 8.69). Five of the teachers were already familiar  with the concept of LA, while four had no prior experience with LA and three were uncertain. 25% said that they used no LA-based features in their teaching and 50% said they used only one or two LA-based tools. Both groups – students and teachers – answered the same six survey questions. The questions addressed students’ (1) support needs, (2) preferred frames of reference, (3) concerns about data sharing for LA, (4) preferred feedback provided by LA, (5) preferred way of passing on information to the teac her, and (6) assessment of the usefulness of LA features. The questions and answer options were formulated based on the findings of previous studies in the field of LA (e.g., Schumacher & Ifen thaler, 2018). The items were adapted to the target groups: While students were asked about their personal attitudes, wishes, and concerns, teachers were asked to assume their students’ attitudes, wishes, and concerns. All questions, except for the third, required participants to assign a ranking from most to least preferred or similar. For the third question, each category was to be ranked on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 (absolutely not concerning) to 4 (absolutely concerning).

## Data analysis

Due to the characteristics of the data collected, s uch as sample sizes and ordinal-scaled responses, we used non-parametric statistical methods for the analyses. Fi rst, we analyzed the results of the student and tea cher surveys descriptively based on Friedman’s mean ranks (calculated subsequently by Friedman test). To determine the statistical significance of the differences between the assigned rankings, we first calculated the Friedman test within each group separately. A significant result (significance level of 5%) indicates that the ranks within a sample were significantly different. Additionally, Kendall’s W reveals the effect size and the extent of the agreement amo ng the raters. For more detailed analyses, we calculat ed a series of Dunn’s post-hoc tests applying Bonferroni correction to compare the rankings within each sample pairwise. For the third question, we calculated the Wilcoxon tests to compare the responses within the groups, a s the participants ranked the given options twice. To compare any given ranks between the student and teacher sur veys, we carried out Mann-Whitney U tests for each option. Here, a significant result indicates a significant difference between how students and t eachers ranked an option. For each pair, we calculated effect sizes using Ros enthal’s r to assess the magnitude of the differences between the options (Rosenthal, 1991).

## Results

The results must be interpreted as follows: A lower value indicates a higher position in the ranking (first place) and thus a positive evaluation by the respondents. In the teacher survey, some participants did not answer all questions. However, all available responses were included in the analysis. For this reason, the sample size is reported for each analysis, respectively. Students’ support needs (first question) Student survey: In the first question, students were asked to rank the type of support they need most in their studies, from most needed (1) to least needed (3). The options given were (a) support on the cognitive dimension (e.g., by establishing relationships between learning materials), (b) support on the metacognitive dimension (e.g., by setting learning goals), and (c) support on the motivational dimension (e.g., by promoting self-efficacy). The results indicated a tendency for students toneed cognitive support in their studies (mean rank = 1.68). This was followed by the need for support on the metacognitive dimension (mean rank = 2.05) and finally on the  motivational dimension (mean rank = 2.26). The Friedman test showed no sig nificant difference between the assigned rankings of the three given options (χ²(2) = 3.26, pasymp. =.196, n = 19). Kendall’s W =.086 indicated a small effect size and a slight agreement among the raters. According to Dunn’s post-hoc tests, the pairwise differences between the students’ rankings were not significant (padj. ≥.223 for each pair). Teacher survey: In the first question, teachers assessed what kind of supp ort students need in their university courses. In contrast to the surveyed studen ts, teachers ranked the provided options in the fol lowing order (from most to least needed): motivational dimension of self-regulation (mean rank = 1.75), metacognitive dimension of self-regulation (mean rank = 2.08), and cognitive dimension of self-regulation (mean rank = 2.17). The Friedman test indicated no significant difference b etween the rankings assigned to the types o f support teachers believed students need in their courses (χ²(2) = 1.17, pasymp. =.558, n = 12). The effect size was very small and there was also a slight agreement among the raters (Kendall’s W =.049). Dunn’s pairwise post-hoc tests revealed no significant differences between the teachers’ rankings (padj. ≥.922 for each pair). Comparison: There were different descriptive trends in the two groups. While students attributed the most value to support on a cognitive dimension of self-regulation, teachers rated this as comparatively less needed. In contrast, students reported the least need for support on a motivational dimension, while teachers rated this type of support as the most needed by their students. However, it is important to consider how diverse the assessments were, especially among teachers (Kendall’s W =.049). The results of Mann-Whitney U tests indicated no significant difference between students’ and teachers’ assigned rankings (pexact ≥.085 for each option). ## Discussion

The aims of this study were to examine students’ at titudes, wishes, and concerns toward LA in higher e ducation and to investigate whether university teachers exhi bit a matching assessment of their students’ attitu des, wishes, and concerns. In the first question, students indicated that they need the most support on the cognitive dimension of self-regulation and the least support on the motivational dimension of self-regulation. In contrast, teachers ranked these options in reverse order.  This finding highlights the importance of providing students with LA that are personalizable (Bennett & Folley, 2019) to ensu re that they can receive LA-based support that is tailored to their actual needs, even if teachers’ assumptions a bout these are not entirely accurate. Moreover, it shows that teachers may alsoneed support to get precise insig hts into their students’ needs and wishes, which ca n be done through personal exchanges with learners, but also by consulting the LA data to get initial impression s of the students, for example through teacher dashboards (Molenaar & Knoop-van Campen, 2017). In the next question, both groups indicated that learners prefer frames of reference that focus on students’ individual learning progress (e.g., individual learning history) over those that encourage social comparison with peers (e.g., best-performing fellow students). This finding is particularly relevant given that social frames of reference are the most used in LA in research and practice, while individual frames o f reference are used comparatively rarely (Jivet et  al., 2017). Interestingly, there was a moderate difference between students’ and teachers’ ranks for fellow students with similar goals and features. In both surveys, this option was ranked as averag e, yet students placed a higher value on this reference frame than teachers. We assume that learners value such information about their peers for building learning groups. Given the effectiveness of collaborative learning, it is essential that teachers recognize this wish and provide learners with the necessary resources to facilitate their participation in collaborative learning. Further findings revealed that the sharing of certain data,  such as physiological data, was identified as a major concern by both students and teachers. This emphasizes that privacy is a critical factor in the adoption of LA (Jones, 2019). Furthermore, students attached more importance to t he recipient of the shared information thandid the  teachers. Sharing LA-generated data with their peers – even in aggregated and anonymized form – was perceived as more concerning by students than the sharing of data wit h teachers in non-anonymized form. Finally, teachers and students showed quite matching assumptions about the usefulness of different LA features for the learners. Noteworthy, students ranked the feature overview of individual learning time  in the past  as the least useful, while teachers rated it as quite useful. One potential ex planation for this discrepancy is that such an overview may not be particularly useful to students who are novices to the course. They may not be aware of the extent to which tasks build upon each other and how analysis of past learning time can be indicative of future learning. In contrast, teachers have a de ep understanding of the course content, which makes  time-based data more valuable to them because they are better able to assess its predicti ve potential. This example illustrates that students and teachers may have access to different information, which can lead to disparate assessments of the usefulness of LA features. As per usual, the limitations of the present study should be considered. First, due to the focus on specific target groups (students and their lecturers), the sample size of this study was relatively modest. Given the limited sample size, we did not expect small or moderate ef fects to become significant, and our data analysis focused on effect sizes. Therefore, it would be beneficial to conduct a replication study with a larger sample size as we ll as in other disciplinary contexts in the future. Second, even if the participating students were willing to use technology-enhanced learning in their studies, the concept of LA was novel to many of them. Moreover, 25% of teachers surveyed indicated that they had not previously use d LA in their teaching. This may have resulted in t he participants’ assessments being based on presumptions that  did not fully align with the reality of using LA i n higher education. Finally, this study was also limited by the use of closed-ended questions with predefined response options. In future research, it would be beneficial  to expand the response options and to include more  individual responses from students and teachers.
