# Climate Education for Justice: Navigating Geographies, Data, and Agency

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium brings together diverse research exploring the intersections of climate change education, social justice, and community empowerment. As climate change increasingly impacts vulnerable populations, the importance of integrating justice-oriented approaches in K-12 education becomes urgent. Studies featured in this symposium investigate how climate education can leverage community strengths, foster empathy through quantitative reasoning, and critique the racialized nature of data to promote environmental justice. Presentations will examine innovative uses of data in teaching, highlighting its role in addressing inequities and engaging students in climate justice advocacy. Key themes include fostering climate agency through cross-disciplinary approaches, rethinking the "natural disaster" narrative to highlight systemic inequalities, and designing professional learning programs to equip educators with tools to navigate climate-related injustices. Together, these studies underscore the need for inclusive, justice-focused climate education that empowers students to engage meaningfully with the climate crisis and advocate for systemic change.

## Theoretical framework

Currently, in the United States, people’s views on climate change are largely determined by where they live (Pew Research Center, 2024). State-level governments and their policies reflect this g eographical bifurcation in accepting or denying the environmen tal consequences of climate change and preparing fo r climate careers; in 2024, the U.S. Climate Alliance, a group of governo rs representing 24 states, launched the Governors’ Climate Ready Workforce Initiative to train one million new  apprentices in climate and clean energy fields by 2035. For instance, Washington’s governor, Jay Inslee, states, “We’re aligning our ambitious climate policies wi th workforce development to have 1 million more worker s poised to take these good-paying, union jobs that serve our communities and strengthen our economies.” Emerging training and apprenticeships fall into three focus areas-clean energy fuels, and technologies; clean buildin gs and industry; and resilient communities and land s. In

contrast, Virginia’s governor, Glen Younkin, has wi thdrawn the state from an existing 12-state carbon cap and trade agreements, Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiativ e (RGGI), with workforce preparation decoupled from climate change. As such, climate futures and learni ng reciprocally co-develop w ithin dynamic socio-political landscapes (Lanouette & Taylor, 2024), the wider ne sted systems and structures that constitute and rec onstitute land, place and young people’s geographies. But how  this shapes local learning processes and pathways is less understood.

## Methods, data, and analysis

As the learning sciences seeks to envision and enac t sustainable solutions to global crises (ISLS 2025  call), focusing on how socio-political, spatial and educational geographies impa ct learning opportunities and climate career pathways will be key. While scholarship on climate change education has predomi nantly focused on the K-12 context, we seek to contribute to studying learn ing within community-led initiatives in relation to these multifaceted geographies. In this study, we ask “How do state-level politics and discourses influence climate careers and advocacy–and young people's opportunities for learning this kind of work? In what ways are spatial data central to these activities and endeavors?” Drawing on comparative case study methodologies (Yi n, 2014) in two coastal regions of the United States, we first engaged in participatory network m apping (Wijenberg et al, 2024) the socio-political and spatial terrain in which climate education/ climate j ustice education is happening, focusing on how poli tical (e.g., governors, state government, Dept of Energy messagi ng), spatial (e.g., coastal watersheds), and educat ional indicators (e.g., NGSS-aligned, climate letter grades, non-formal climate education) are shaping climate chang e education and careers. Data sources include websites, legislation, news articles, maps, geospatial data, and survey reports. In the second phase, we study the ways in which teaching and learning about climate change and climate justice learning opportunities are taking shape within these two divergent contexts, focusing on four organizations that reflect both urban and rural climate education al organizations. For example, in Virginia, we are learning alongside (Oyinloye, 2022) two reforesting organiza tions that seek to counter redlining and its ongoin g impacts in Richmond, Virginia, while also opening up career s in landscaping and urbandesign. Data sources inc lude interviews, participatory co-design work, and document analysis.

## Findings

As geographic sorting, based on values, political o rientation, and religious affiliation, continues in  the United States (Paris & Wallace, 2024), so too do the oppor tunities for young people to learn about climate ch ange. This study contributes a framework  from which to see and understand learning (and teac hing) within a politically complex and fraught national terrain; climate chang e is either part of the “official” discourse or den igrated as a hoax. From network analysis, we see sharp contrasts  in political messaging from governors (UW DOE and governor climate messages, VA governor absence of a ny climate change mention and VA DOE with vague environmental literacy), with career readiness focu sed around climate careers but VA has no such progr am. In our comparative analysis of how partner organizations teach  and learn within these landscapes, initial finding s point to the unequal barriers, and levels of suppor t, that organizations face depending on where they are doing their work. On one coast, this work flows with the currents of state-level initiatives. On another, the work resists, sometimes openly, the stance held by the state’s highest office.

As the learning sciences seeks to counter the poly-crises and possibilities inherent to climate change, it will be key to develop partnerships and conceptual frameworks that engage with the dynamic and divergent geographies shaping climate change education. Unpacking climate agency in science education: A cross-disciplinary approach Giulia Tasquier

## Theoretical framework

As the climate crisis intensifies, young people are demonstrating a strong willingness to act; however, false beliefs about climate change are prevalent, often leading to misdirected efforts or radical activism lacking clear purpose. This disorientation can be traced to significant educational gaps: despite six decades of UNESCO’s advocacy for scientific literacy, nearly half of global curricul a fail to explicitly address climate change (UNESCO, 2021). Recent frameworks, such as the GreenComp (Bianchi, Pisiotis, & Cabrera Giraldez, 2022), advocate for a n education system that equips students to face complex, real-world issues. In line with this, there is an urgent need to reconsider the role of science education in fost ering agency, particularly by integrating social and transformative dimensions into the curriculum (Laherto & Rasa, 2022; Tasquier, Knain, & Jornet, 2022). This study positions the agency as a critical compe tency necessary for young people to navigate 21st-century challenges. Drawing from philosophical, eco nomic, and educational theories, it proposes a mode l of agency that encompasses individual decision-making, moral reasoning, and collective action (Emirbay er & Mische, 1998). This approach extends the traditiona l concept of scientific literacy, incorporating ref lective and action-oriented competencies essential for engaging with t he multifaceted nature of cl imate change. Philosophy contributes insights into ethical responsibilities and the motivations driving climate action, while e conomics illuminates the practical aspects of decision-making under real-world constraints. Specifically, Philosophy’s ethical focus prompts students to reflect on the motives and responsibilities underpinning climate action, enabling them to consider moral imperatives in environmental  stewardship. Economics, on the other hand, introdu ces a practical perspective, illustrating decisi on-making under resource constraints and emphasizing t he systemic impact of collective actions. When applied to scien ce education, this model encourages students to vie w climate change through both personal and societal lenses, f ostering a balanced sense of agency that is informe d by individual actions and societal obligations. Together, these perspectives enable a deeper understanding of climate agency grounded in the interconnected roles of knowledge, values, and systems thinking (see Figure 1). Integrating these disciplinary perspectives within science education has the potential to bridge the g ap between knowledge and action. This interdisciplinar y model empowers students with skills to critically  evaluate and meaningfully respond to socio-environmental issues. By situating their individual acti ons within broader socio-political contexts, young people can better balance personal responsibilities with collective ones, leading to a more coherent and impactful approach to climate a ction. Such a holistic model prepares students not only to understand climate issues but also to act as responsible agents within an increasingly complex and interconnected world.

Justice-oriented science education in an age of unnatural climate disasters Wonyong Park, Noemi Waight, Christopher St. Vil, Jennifer Trip, Fatemeh Mozaffari

## Theoretical framework

As the impacts of the climate crisis intensify and diversify, there is growing discussion about how to  address climate disasters and extreme weather events in curriculum and instruction, particularly in justice-oriented ways. Justice-oriented climate change education in K-12 schools can be centered around community experie nces of climate change, inequitable distribution of vulnera bility, representation of community voice, and advo cacy for social justice. One core aspect of justice-oriented climate change education is how social, ec onomic, and racial inequalities exacerbate the effects of climate chan ge. As Sultana (2021) emphasizes, climate justice “fundamentally is about paying attention to how cli mate change impacts people differently, unevenly, a nd disproportionately, as well as redressing the resultant i njustices in fair and equitable ways.” Accordingly,  to achieve justice, “it becomes imperative first to identify injustices that exist and then address underlying causes … critical climate justice praxis d emands systemic changes to address structural inequ ities and destabilize power systems that produce various climate injustices” (Sultana, 2021, p. 119). Climate-related disasters such as floods, droughts, hurrica nes, blizzards, wildfires and heat waves are often framed as “natural disasters” by organization s, the media, and politicians in order to obscure t he impact of the human actions and decisions that led to these disasters and to deflect responsibilit y. However, the United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction reminds us that there is no such thing as a “natural” disaster, because “risk is ultimately the result of decisions that we  make. We make d ecisions about the hazards to which we are willing to expose ourselves, we make decisions about where to build schools, factories, dams and dykes and how much to invest in disease surveillance and we make decisions about how our societies organize and care  for vulnerable people and assets.” (UNDRR, n.d.) In his  seminal study of the devastating 1995 Chicago heat  wave that claimed over 700 lives, Klinenberg (2015) illu strated that the mortality rates could be attribute d to poverty, isolation, and lack of community infrastructure rat her than to temperatures alone, highlighting the ro le of social systems in mitigating, or exacerbating, the consequences of extreme weather events. We argue that teaching about these social determinants of climate impacts is key to empowering children and youth for climate action, and that this can be done by challenging the “natural disaster” narrativ e that often absolves people in positions of power  of responsibility by blaming nature. Our goal in th is paper is to articulate ways in which educators can intentionally de-naturalize these disasters in K-12 (science) education. We illustrate how climate disasters are often framed as “natural”  phenomena, and how such framing can obscure the role of societies and policymakers in reducing, or increasing, the risk of disasters and limit justice-oriented teaching about them. Drawing on critical disaster studies literatu re, we propose four core areas for climat e justice education: redistribution, representation, recognition, and re paration. We illustrate this approach through a com munityengaged project with residents and teachers who experienced the deadly blizzard that struck Buffalo, NY in 2022, disproportionately affecting historically marginalized groups within the community.

## Methods, data sources, and analysis

Our study participants included science educators who work in Buffalo Public Schools serving racially, ethnically, and linguistically diverse students. Community leaders from local organizations working in youth services, public health, climate justice, fo od (in)security and equitable mobility were also in vited, as well as families who have school-age children. Between October 2023 and June 2024, 2 6 interviews were conducted. In June 2024, two meetings were convened with 13 individuals from the community to discuss the social justice and education issues related to the winter weather in the region. Data w ere analyzed qualitatively using an iterative proce ss of coding and theme generation, focusing on the aspects of di saster justice in the context of Buffalo and how they relate to the goals of justice-oriented science education.

## Findings

The study findings revealed how climate injustices have been perpetuated for generations within the Bu ffaloNiagara community, highlighting a range of issues r elated to the four areas of climate justice educati on. At the same time, both residents and teachers mentioned th e critical importance of education as a means of re paration and recognition after the blizzard experience. Impo rtantly, we noticed that their memories and narrati ves of the 2022 blizzard are closely linked to mid-and long-term justice issues in the area, such as the 1977 Buffalo Blizzard and the 2022 Buffalo shooting, both of which were a cts of violence against people of color. This point s to the imperative of situating climate change education wi thin the local context of social justice and  drawing on community experiences as a starting point for resto rative justice and reparations. In this process, qu alitative data such as narratives and recollections could facilitate learners’ development of empathy. These personal accounts can be complemented by statistical data, including contemporary datasets such as FEMA’s National Risk Index (https://hazards.fema.gov/nri/map) as well as historical datasets about redlining (e.g., Mapping Inequality; https://dsl.richmond.edu/panorama/redlining) to understand climate hazards in tandem with social injustices and vulnerabilities holistic ally in K-12 settings. Students can start from residents’ per sonal accounts about the impact of climate disasters and inquire into the intersecting injustices that contr ibuted to the deaths through scientific, economic and social data about risk, vulnerability, and resilience.

## Scholarly significance

Overall, our study implies that challenging the “na turalness” of climate disasters is a first step to exposing and addressing the underlying social and systemic inequ ities that exacerbate their impacts, especially on historically marginalized communities. The Buffalo Blizzard of 2022 serves as a poignant case study, highlighting how racial and economic disparities increase the vulnerabilities faced during extreme weather events. The use of quantitative and qualitative data can be instrumental in denatur alizing climate disasters and facilitating critical  discussions relating to redistribution, representation, recognition, and reparation in K-12 classrooms. ## Theoretical framework

Numbers can be used to convey emotions.   For examp le, a youth climate organizer asks an auditorium fu ll of middle school students to raise their hand if they know someone who is incarcerated.  She continues to  impress that “one in three firefighters” in California is incarcerated, making less than “one dollar” an hour to fight wildfires that are fueled by climate change.  In another exam ple, Bill McKibben argues that if the earth warms b y more than “3oC” that “one in five” people will be climate refugees.  Using simple numbers that are connected to personal experience is a powerful way to communicate the magnitude of the climate crisis and its disproportionate impacts on marginalized communities.

It is important for STEM education to critically examine the intersection between quantitative reasoning and justice and morality.  Quantitative reasoning a nd telling stories with numbers can be a powerful w ay to advocate for change  (e.g., Zummo et al., 2023).  We build on this work to address the ways that quantitative reasoning can be used to develop empathy for others.  As many scholars have argued, empathy is needed to humanize STEM and STEM education, to consider the i mpacts of STEM work and foster cultural change with in STEM.  To this end, we propose and explore the cons truct of quantitative empathy, which we define as a way of using quantitative reasoning, such as sense of scal e or proportional reasoning, to understand the scop e or impact of a phenomenon and connect emotionally to the experiences of others.  Our study explores how teachers engage in quantitative empathy to examine issues of environmental injustice.

## Methods, data, and analysis

We report on a professional learning institute on a ir quality and environmental justice for K-12 teachers and informal educators in San Francisco’s East Bay. The  institute was co-designed by the West Oakland Environmental Indicators Project (WOEIP), a resi dent-led environmental justice organization.  West Oakla nd is a historically black neighborhood that has dispropo rtionately been impacted by pollution from ground transportation and the local port.  The grounding experience of the institute was a walking tour of West Oakland, led by an activist from the organization. The rest of the institute focused on the importance of centering community advocacy and bringing together stakeholders, including polluters, to generate solutions.  Additionally, we applied multiple justice lenses to the study of air quality:  addressing positionality, honoring multiple ways of  knowing, analyzing structures of oppression, and taking action.  Activities included storytelling, analyzing and creating data visualizations, hands-on science investigations, and group discussion. The present analysis focuses on selected activities, including creating a fact and a comment to be shared in a public forum, such as a school board or city c ouncil meeting, as a form of advocacy. Data sources  include written artifacts, field notes, and video and audio recordings of small group participant discussions collected from consenting participants (N = 16 consenting of 24 to tal). We identified instances of quantitative empathy  and determined the purposes for which teachers were engaging in this form of reasoning.

## Findings

During the public comment phase, all groups used qu antitative empathy to appeal to their audiences, wh ich was the goal of the design. For example, one group who called themselves the “Loco-motives” commented on the impact of freight trains on their students to the O akland City Council.  They played a YouTube clip of  the sound of a train and told the audience that it is “noise that my students have grown to know and love because it means I have to shut up for five minutes while the train goes by.” The other teachers laugh.  Although he is practicing a comment for the city council, he emphasizes the amo unt of time so that teachers can imagine what it mi ght feel like.  He continued “does anyone know what the maximum allowable particulate matter in the areas in the United States is?” telling his audience that their school surpasses that standard “on any given day in the year. Teachers also used quantitative empathy in a way th at we had not planned for or explicitly supported: taking the perspective of different communities who have experienced environmental harms.  The aforementioned group read a scientific paper on coal trains  that reported that they “add an average of 8.32 PM 2.5.” There were audible gasps around the table. The group referenced data they had collected on the university campus earlier that week, finding that the coal train was comparable to  the emissions at the hazardous waste processing pl ant and “worse than cigarette smoke in your face.”  In doin g so, they were able to understand the magnitude of environmental harms they had not experienced and build empathy with impacted communities.

Numbers are central to understanding the impacts of  climate change, but they can often feel abstract. Designing for quantitative empathy helps to develop our cognitive ability while building our emotional muscles for empathy for the experiences of other communities.  We see t hese skills as central to building solidarity as de scribed by youth climate activist Isha Clark: “Climate justice  is about completely reimagining the way that we live on the planet... It is about collective liberation and di smantling systems of oppression in every way that t hey exist.” Designing for quantitative empathy with this end in  mind represents an opportunity to put quantitative  STEM moves “in their place” (Morales-Doyle, 2023), in service of climate justice. Towards environmental justice by reading against quantitative data Emily V. Reigh, Jacob Barton, Helen L. Fitzmaurice,  Gita Dunhill

## Theoretical framework

Quantitative data play a critical role not only in understanding and making predictions about environm ental phenomena, but also documenting their disproportion ate effects on communities of color and low-income communities. Datasets are not “neutral”; rathe r, they are a form of text that is actively constru cted by its authors and re-interpreted by others on the basis of their persona l experiences, cultural practices, and the social, political and historical context (Lee, Wilkerson and Lanouett e, 2021). Datas ets reflect the racialized contexts of their creation; supporting students to develop data liter acy in STEM contexts, therefore, requires an interr ogation of the way that race and power shape how data are created and used (Philip et al., 2016).  Tensions between personal experiences and quantitative data can reveal the ra cialized context of data, particularly for minoriti zed communities who are often not centered in the development of scientific questions or data collection and analysis protocols.  Thus, preparing students to use data towards justice-focused ends requires not only using existing data for advocacy, but also reading against the data to understand the biases in its creation that may undermine justice projects.  We explore how DataBytes, a data discuss ion tool that centers the constructed nature of dat a, provides opportunities to engage in justice-focused reasoning about environmental phenomena.

## Methods, data sources, and analysis

We report on the professional learning institute on  air quality described in the previous proposal.  W e focus on one activity in which teachers analyzed data about air pollution in Oakland.  The activity was based o n the DataBytes tool (Wilkerson et al., i n press), which includes the following components: making sense of data, building personal connections to data, reflecting on the context and history of data, and future uses of data.  Data sources include video recordings and artifacts from  teachers’ enga gement in the focal data analysis activity. Transcripts were analyzed using an emergent coding scheme for the justice-focused moves.

## Findings

We found that the DataBytes tool supported teachers  to engage in multiple types of justice-focused sensemaking about air quality data, including 1) challenging assumptions behind the data; 2) questioning the usefulness of the data to impacted communities; 3) analyzing their own privilege and that of others; and 4) making bids for collective and justice-focused action.  For example, they noted that the s ensor used by regulatory agencies was far away from the port and highways of West Oakland, meaning that it  did not provide an accurate reading of pollution for nearby communities.  They argued that  the air quality board is failing to meet their mis sion, as the data they collect is not benefiting the community. They recognized their own privilege in having mean s of transportation that contribute to pollution, but pu shed against individualized solutions, which they a rgued are supported by the petroleum industry.  Instead, they  pressed for collective action, such as diverting t raffic and subsidizing public transportation.

## Scholarly significance

While quantitative data is critical to documenting environmental injustices, this study shows the value of engaging teachers in analyzing the limitations of data to un derstand the racialized context of data creation and support justice-focused reasoning.  Based on our empirical findings, we suggest adaptations to the DataBytes tool to explicitly encourage justice moves when analyzing q uantitative data and highlight elements of our larg er professional learning design that supported this form of reasoning. These design principles can be adapted across instructional contexts, including both teacher professional learning and K-12 education. Data, justice, and climate education: Exploring the role of data practices in addressing inequities in climate change impacts Asli Sezen-Barrie, Hosun Kang, Mary K. Stapleton

## Theoretical framework

Engaging students in data practices is essential fo r understanding and addressing climate change, prom oting just action and adaptation (Sezen-Barrie et al., 2025). Despite the importance of dev eloping students’ data fluency, navigating vast climate data sources is overwhelmin g for most teachers (Rosenberg et al., 2022; Sezen-Barrie et al., 2015). Few studies provide theoretical and prac tical insights into this critical issue. In this st udy, we explore how and under what conditions various forms of data  can support student learning about climate issues, particularly the disproportionately  negative impacts of climate change on underrepresen ted communities. Our research question is: What forms of data are used, and how can these data help students understand climate justice issues? Drawing upon the work of epistemic heteroge neity (e.g., Bang et al., 2022), we investigate a wide range of data practices, focusing on data types, sources,  and representations of the voices of communities a nd Western science, as well as how the use of data empowers learners and educators.

## Methods: Context, data sources, and analysis

This study employs a multiple-case study design (Yin, 2014) to explore data practices across different contexts. Data were collected from secondary school classroom s (ages 11-17) in California, Maryland, and Maine, in the United States. Each case focuses on different data forms (social science data, large-scale quantitative data, and proxy data) in exploring climate justice issues. We collected multiple data sources, including video recordings of lessons and professional development sessions, interviews, and student artifacts. Our analysis aim to characterize a wide range of data practices, including the repre sentation of multiple voices across contexts and th eir role in facilitating students’ understanding and actions to ward addressing climate injustice. We  used deductive and inductive coding to build rich narratives in each case iteratively over time. Cross-case analysis was used to theorize critical data practices across contexts (Glaser, 1965).

## Findings

The first case illustrates the use of social science data from high school students (e.g., interviews with community members to understand their concerns about food was te from school lunches and its impacts on climate) in a California school district. These data practices—collecting, analyzing, and communicating social sci ence data— drew students’ attention to social, cultural, and political factors beyond the scientific dimension, supporting their understanding of the issue’s complexity and its int ersection with multiple systems. Social science data also highlighted the voices of the impacted people, faci litating students’ critical reflection on privilege  and compassion. The second case illustrates the use of direct measurements of large-scale quantitative data (e.g., 30year-long temperature and wind speed patterns) to examin e how the frequency and severity of extreme weather events are increasing due to climate change in Main e. These data practices —analyzing large-scale data and constructing data stories —helped students connect global patterns represented i n the data to their daily experiences. In addition, these experiences provide d opportunities for youth to ask critical questions  about how scientific and statistical concepts can serve as tools to understand the climate impacts their communities face. The third case illustrates students’ inquiry into polle n fossils as proxy data to understand past climate conditions in Maryland. These data practices —analyzing longitudinal data to recognize patterns a cross t emporal scales — enabled students to critically examine various clai ms about climate change phenomena, revealing intergenerational injustice while predicting possib le futures. The analysis showed that each form of d ata has unique affordances and constraints in facilitating students’ understanding of climate justice issues. This suggests that using multiple forms of data is more likely to promote a complex understanding of these multifaceted issues. Furthermore, productive data practices help learner s connect with themselves, other people (including those impacted), and multiple factors within systems and across time.

## Scholarly significance

Climate change disproportionately impacts vulnerabl e and underrepresented communities, including indiv iduals living in poverty and Indigenous populations. These  communities often have limited resources and less capacity to adapt to climate-related hazards, such as extreme weather events and food shortages (McGinty & Bang, 2016). As such, youth must participate in decision-making to address these problems. This study highlights how engaging youth in multiple forms of data representations wit hin a justice-oriented framework supports students’ and educators’ understanding of the importance of data when thinking about climate change action.
