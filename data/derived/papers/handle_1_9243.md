# Understanding the Role of Negotiation Processes in Collaborative Engineering Estimation Problem-Solving

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We attempt to understand learners' negotiation processes while they collaboratively solve a complex engineering estimation problem in an open-ended learning environment MEttLE.  Learners are required to grapple with incomplete information, make assumptions and reach decisions. This open-ended and complex nature of tasks gives rise to multiple ideas, conflicting perspectives, and frequent negotiation. We analyze the discourse and interactions of seven dyads interacting with MEttLE. Our findings reveal how the negotiation processes influence estimation problem-solving practices.

## Introduction

Engineering estimation is an ill-structured problem widely used by and critical for practicing engineers (Jonassen et al., 2006). It is the process of determining approximate values for a physical quantity without access to complete information. The process of estimation involves using quantitative sense, general knowle dge, and intuition to identify realistic values and compare them against known values (Mahajan, 2014). Creating and evaluating a contextualized model is an underlying principle for estimation (Kothiyal & Murthy, 2020). Novice learners face significant challenges as estimation problem-solving requires “mastering the complexity” of a physical system by applying conceptual knowledge to a real-world system, identifying its dominant par ameters, understanding its behaviours and relationships, and making assumptions and judgments while solving the problem (Dunn-Rankin, 2001; Mahajan, 2014; Shakerin, 2006).

To support novice learners in solving engineering estimation problems, an open-ended le arning environment called MEttLE (Modelling-based Estimation Learning Environment) ha s been developed. MEttLE incorporates disciplinary model-based reasoning tasks and metacognitive scaffolds  (Kothiyal & Murthy, 2020). It provides opportunities for goal-setting and planning strategies, and scaffolds suc h as a simulator for building and evaluating models. When learners collaboratively work in MEttLE, the open-ende d and complex nature of tasks leads to frequent negotiation.  Negotiation is required in domains with multiple solutions and methods and where different viewpoints on knowledge are essential. However, negotiation processes and their role in learners' estimation problem-solving have not been well studied. This paper analyses learners’ negotiation proc esses to understand how collaboration and negotiation influence their estimation problem-solving practices in MEttLE. Negotiation Negotiation is essential for effective collaboration (Dillenbourg & Baker, 1994), particularly in complex problems that require critical examination and contributions from collaborators (Carell  & Herrmann, 2009) and where convergence between unconnected or incommensurable ideas is crucial. Negotiation involves reaching a common goal of the agreement through various strategies (Dillenbourg & Baker, 1994). Accor ding to Baker's model (Baker, 1994), a typical negotiation episode consists of four key features: i) negotia, i.e., what is being negotiated; ii) initial state, i.e., the common goal of interacting in a certain way to attain some learning, iii) final state, i.e., an agreement with respect to some or all of the Negotia and iv) negotiation processes, i.e., strategies like arguing, refining, and stand pat undertaken to achieve the agreement.

## Method

We conducted a study with seven dyads of undergraduate engineering students from diff erent colleges in India. Each dyad interacted with MEttLE in a lab setup for 90 minutes and collabora tively worked on the given estimation problem. We obtained video recor dings of learners’ interaction with MEttLE, audio, and front video, including facial expressions and gestures. We analyzed negotiation processes in the collaborative discourse using codes from Baker’s model, such as offer, conflict, defend, reject, and accept. In addition, we analyzed the episodes to understand the actions, stages, and processes of estimation problem solving, such as breaking down the system’s complexity, model creation and contextualization, estimate generation, model evaluation, a nd checking the reasonableness of the result. Metacognitive processes such as planning, monito ring, and evaluation were also considered. The primary author and a researcher independently coded four negotiation episodes and attained interrater reliability of 0.84 Kappa.

## Discussion

Learners engaged in various negotiation processes while solving the estimation problem in MEttLE. Findings from the above and other episodes (not shown here) suggest that negotiation facil itates breaking the problem's complexity into physical and conceptual parts, which is one of the principles in complex problem-solving (Foshay & Kirkley, 2003). Negotiation promotes metacognitive processes such as evaluating the problem model, which is an important estimation practice (Dunn-Rankin, 2001). In addition, negotiation promotes the effective use of simulators and fosters sense-making.

One limitation of this study is that our findings are based on a particular model of negotiation. Also, there could be other factors influencing the quality of negotiation, such as relationship between the collaborators and familiarity with domain knowledge, which need to be further studied. In future w ork, studying the role of negotiation in learners’ practices of estimation can be an orientation for inter vention strategies which foster collaborative knowledge construction.
