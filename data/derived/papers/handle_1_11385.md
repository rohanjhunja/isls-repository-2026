# Towards a Learning Sciences-Informed Framework for Investigating “Twisted” Knowledge Construction on Social Media

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper proposes a framework to investigate a phenomenon we term twisted knowledge construction on social media from a Learning Sciences perspective. We first analyze and then draw connections between literature from four fields of scholarship, with varying levels of connection to the Learning Sciences: social media from the perspective of Internet and Communication Studies; foundational theories of learning; social media as contexts for learning; and epistemic cognition with an emphasis on emotions. We articulate these connections as a loop-like system supported by the socio-technical affordances of social media. This system is fueled by ideologically motivated content and interactions that shape knowledge construction, whose affective and repertoire-based outcomes loop back into itself, leading to a cycle of distorted knowledge construction. Considerations for future research are proposed from a Learning Sciences perspective.

## Introduction

This conceptual paper examines the adherence to ext remist political ideologies as a phenomenon of know ledge construction mediated by social media. We argue tha t the strength of extremism associated with social media stems from the fact that people are, above all, learning, doing so in ways that are counterintuitive (and tragically) informed by modern pedagogies. In other words, well-supported theories that explain learning can also help clarify the radicalization fueled by social media. To begin, we provide  an overview of the current (and problematic) social media landscape, which, through technical affordances and human agency, enables the proliferation of hate speech and politically charged misinformation. Next, we refer back to foundational theories of learnin g and knowledge construction to situate our understanding  of these constructs as phenomena mediated by socia l interaction and technology, thereby positioning our  work within the realm of learning sciences scholar ship. We then proceed to review of research investigating learning in social media contexts, including learning that may be harmful to democracy. Next, we review scholarship o n epistemic cognition and epistemic emotions, which highlight the apparent paradox of proposing “powerful,” agentic learning as an explanation for the normalization of fringe ideologies. We connect these fields of sc holarship by proposing a working framework to study  this phenomenon through a learning sciences lens (Figure 1). Figure 1 A Working Conceptual Framework for the Investigation of Twisted Knowledge Construction Processes Supported by Social Media Social media’s multilayered, problematic setup Social media have become powerful tools in both ins titutional politics (e.g., Recuero et al., 2020) and citizens’ political expression (Literat & Kligler-Vilenchik, 2019), sometimes derailing into extremist ideologies (Ozawa et al., 2023). This entanglement of technologies and huma n agency often generates and spreads information th at, whether misleading or simply false, has deleterious  effects on the functioning of institutions. This i s evident in the case of the Alternative Influen ce Network (Lewis, 2018), which employs digital marketing strategies proven effective in commercial contexts to deliver misogynistic and racist ideas to large audiences. Considering the entanglements of human agency and s ocial media affordances, it is essential to nuance explanations of political polarization that overemp hasize the technical aspects of “algorithms.” For e xample, consider “echo chambers,” whose reach and ef fects vary significantly across contexts (Arguedas et al., 2022), akin to “filter bubbles,” both incomplete explanati ons for polarization (Fletcher et al., 2020). Other  explanations seek more profound connections between human actions and the sociotechnical affordances of platforms, such as data voids (Golebiewski and boyd, 2019), which aris e when there is a lack of information on a specific  topic, creating an opportunity for malicious actors to fil l that gap with content that serves specific agenda s—e.g., the proliferation of coup-plotting content in Brazil (Russo et al., 2024). Tr ipodi (2022) follows a similar path in discussing the “IKEA effect of misinformation,” ref erring to users actively piecing together content s trategically distributed online in a sense-making process driven by agency. This line of scholarship aims to balance technology and human agency as factors explaining the spread of highly ideological content within online networks. These explanations —because they link technical and  human factors —enable an examination of the phenomenon within the context of learning research. Rather than relying solely on abstract entities like algorithms, we should focus on how humans engage in sense-making through technologies and interactions with others. This perspective aligns with the concept of communities of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991), which has been revisited in Internet Studies to address distorted practices of knowledge construction (e.g., Marwick & Partin, 2022). These practices can oc casionally imbue extremist points of view with an a gential aura (Tripodi et al., 2023). They transform those channels into vehicles of “particip atory propaganda” (Asmolov, 2019), all under the gu ise of alleged rationality and elusiveness against the “tr aps” set by legacy media and other sources of authorit ative knowledge (Tripodi, 2022), such as networks that sp read hate and disinformation through knowledge-sharing dynamics (e.g., Rothermel, 2023; Ylä-Anttila, 2018).

Through this review, we aim to demonstrate how the sociotechnical affordances of social media and digital influence strategies are weaponized to diss eminate harmful ideologies. Malicious groups cultiv ate interactions that may resemble communities of practice while engaging with problematic information, oft en disguised as evidence. In light of this network of actors, technologies, and sense-making, we now turn to a review of theories that position these factors within learning processes. Learning and knowledge construction as mediated by social interaction and technology To discuss learning and knowledge construction in t hose contexts, we first disambiguate a few terms th at are central to the debate. In this section, we explore perspectives on learning that align our work with f oundational literature in the learning sciences by examining (1) a constructivist view of learni ng and knowledge “assembly,”

(2) technology-and socially-mediated knowledge “assembly,” and (3) learning as participation in communities.

In this work, we explore the intersections between classic constructivist and social constructivist tenets. We agree that learning happens through the active construction of mental schemas as individuals interact with the world (Nathan & Sawyer, 2022), through assimilation and accommodation that ultimately leads to updated, stable interpretations of reality (Ackermann, 2001). In pr evious work, we conjectured that political actors m ight be instrumentalizing knowledge construction from piece s of intuitive knowledge to promote harmful ideolog ies (Russo & Blikstein, 2022). Beyond processes “inside  the mind,” another critical assumption is that lea rning happens in systems that involve “the learner, other individuals, materials, and tools” (Nathan & Sawyer, 2022). It is crucial to explicitly connect these views of learning with knowledge construction, defined by van Aalst (2009) as “the processes by which students so lve problems and construct an understanding of conc epts, phenomena, and situations.” Other authors operation alize learning as gradual steps of knowledge constr uction, using that definition  to assess learning as knowledge construction in online communities (e.g., Nguyen & Diederich, 2023). The explicit connection between s ocial media and learning as knowle dge construction makes that research particularly relevant to our work, despite its focus on a different subject matter. Building on the work presented above, we conceptual ize learning as the process of constructing knowledge at both the individual and activity levels; that is, knowledge is constructed by individuals in interaction with the environment and its components. Individual  knowledge contributes to collective knowledge that, over time, becomes “canonical,” in the sense that it is shared within a community. Cultural artifacts gain centrality as they mediate internal processes and interactions between individuals and with the environment. Social media as a learning space The concept of social media as a learning environment is not new and has evolved to adapt to changes in how we utilize digital media. It considers the social, emotional, and relational aspects of social media, which are connected to social-constructivist perspectives of learning (Greenhow a nd Robelia, 2009), as well as elements of social constructivism and connectivism in social media-based learning, including the decentralized, co-constructed nature of knowledge (Dubovi & Tabak, 2020; Nguyen & Diederich, 2023). These studies exemplify how scholars employ a range of methodologies to evaluate learning through social media across various disciplines. Another crucial area of research links social media  and learning from socio-cultural and political participation perspectives; for instance, through a ugmented participation in social movements (Gleason, 2013), multimodal reading, and content production (Gleason, 2018), leading to the development of “reflective awareness” (Gleason et al., 2021). A further, contr oversial line of inquiry positions social media as a learning environment that subverts educational purposes in favor of creating a more democratic society: research highlights the existence of public pedagogies established to p ropagate hate speech, misinformation, and alt-right ideology, a manifestation of informal miseducation (Greenhalgh et al., 2021) that has its own curricula (Tebaldi, 2021) with multiple entry points. QAnon is cited as having “en acted a public pedagogy” more effectively than many  other social movements, utilizing epistemological tools a nd practices that res emble academic critique (Burdick & Sandlin, 2022). Their knowledge creation process de monstrates high epistemic agency and follows an epistemology akin to that advocated by critical ped agogy scholars, with the distinction being that QAn on’s practices distort those processes. All of this emer ges from online spaces to navigate the  mainstream of politics through discourse and “research” practices.

In this section, we aimed to illustrate that scholarship has explored the learning of a wide range of topics (e.g., climate change, civics) across different gen erations of social media (e.g., MySpace, TikTok) fr om various perspectives (e.g., cognitivist, socio-cultural). Tragically, research has also revealed t hat pedagogical practices have been hijacked to promote alt-right ideologies. A gap persists in aligning the two: investigating the detrimental forms of learning in online collaborative spaces from a learning-oriented perspective. Epistemic cognition and epistemic emotions Because the view of learning adopted in this paper assumes the learner’s agency, it is critical to und erstand the processes through which one evaluates one’s knowled ge construction processes, a line of research that is of interest to the field of epistemic cognition, which explores how people “enact practices to att ain knowledge and understanding” (Greene et al., 2021). Chinn and Sandoval (2018) outlined features of learning sciences approaches to epistemic cognition that benefit from our field’s synergies by incorporating diverse perspectives on learning (e.g., the social and situated nature of e pistemic cognition and the focus on practices. One of the ways epistemic cognition is operationalized is through the AIR framework (Chinn & Rinehart, 2016), which d escribes Epistemic Aims, Ideals, and Reliable processes for vetting knowledge and learning. In discussing these processes, the authors highlight the role of emotions in knowledge production. Emotions are also part of the curricular practices detailed by Ch inn et al. (2020) for a “post-truth world,” emphasizing the nurturing of producti ve epistemic emotions such as curiosity and enjoyment.

Certain emotions are linked to learning and knowled ge construction. Researchers who adopt sociocultural perspectives associate emotions with sense-making and participation, as illustrated by Vea’s (2020) emotional configuration framework, which Lanouette (2022) uses to explore the interplay of emotions in science instruction. Jaber and Hammer (2016) contend that epistemic affects are integral to the practice of science and should therefore be incorporated into science instr uction. Cognitivist research traditions have identi fied several impacts of emotions in learning environments, including epistemic curiosity and exploratory behavior (Litman et al., 2005), motivation (Trevors, 2023), and achievement (Frenzel et al., 2023). Our work hypothesizes that certain emotions have been “weaponized” to engage individuals in a “puzzle-solving” mode, maintaining the involvement of entire communities. For instance, in prior research, we identified an X/Twitter profile that engaged its followers through riddles related to a potential coup d'état in Brazil following allegations of fraud in the 202 2 presidential elections (Russo & Blikstein, 2024).

One line of scholarship has adopted the term “epistemic emotions” to describe affective states that have a causal influence on cognition and are directed to ward epistemic aims, with a strong connection to be lief acquisition (Vogl et al., 2021). A practical  implication of epistemic emotions in understanding knowledge construction is that research has pinpointed certai n epistemic emotions that serve as either precedent s (e.g., curiosity) or outcomes of inquiry and critical thin king (Muis et al., 2018; Thacker, 2024). We recognize that gaining a deeper understanding of emotions as motiv ators for and products of learning may shed light o n the learning strategies employed by extremist groups.

## A working conceptual framework for the investigatio n of twisted learning

processes involving social media Reviewing scholarship in these fields enables us to  devise a framework depicting the connections betwe en the socio-technical aspects of social media and the design of  those spaces as learning environments, agential knowledge construction, and epistemic cognition and emotions. First, a set of socio-technical affordances of social media creates an ecosystem that tolerates (and some times rewards) the spread of extremism and ideologi es that can be harmful to democracy (Phillips & Milner, 202 1). This is indicated by the number 1 in Figure 1. Notably, ill-intentioned actors exploit these affordances by emp loying strategically developed strategies and messa ging (number 2 in Figure 1). These strategies include appeals to emotions, linguistic devices, and elements of intuitive knowledge; content/messaging encompasses calls to “ do your own research” and “learn it yourself,” whic h have been weaponized to promote “evidence-based” misogyny (Rothermel, 2023) and racism (Ylä -Anttila, 2018), among other ideologies. Examples of “evidence” in these cases include cherry-picked data, exaggerated statistics, and testimonials from biased authorities. These str ategies and messaging form the foundation for the k nowledge construction processes that occur as individuals en gage within a ctivities at both individual and systemic levels (Engeström, 2022): the “learner” (in this case, cit izens using social media), in social interaction wi th other members of online communities and through the media tion of digital technologies (e.g., computers, mobile phones) and other human-made artifacts (e.g., charts, “scientific” reports), participates in individual and collective knowledge construction processes (#3). These processes of “learning” (although distorted) yield outcomes related to emotions and  repertoires that are significant for further learni ng (4 and 5). From the viewpoint of epistemic cognition and the study of emotions, some affects arise from learning (such as the feeling of competence; Frenzel et al., 2023) and motivate further exploration (e.g., Vog l et al., 2019), which feeds back into the knowledge construction process (#3). The learned “content” (a gain, distorted) also cycles back into the individu al and collective knowledge construction processes from a constructivist perspective: learning occurs based on what we already know—the twist here is that what the learner knows is th e result of distorted processes, influenced by ideological motivations. This working framework can  inspire research that examines its different components in isolation or explores their interactions to gradually provide a comprehensive understanding of the phenom enon from a learning sciences perspective.

Potential avenues for future research include deeper analyses of online “curricula” used by the alt-right, their “instructional strategies,” and materials (to  address #2 in Figure 1). Another possible lens foc uses on the learners and the cognitive and/or sociocultural pro cesses involved in knowledge construction: for inst ance, how do these processes compare to learning in conventio nal educational settings? What are the opportunitie s for potential interventions to disrupt the cycle of dis torted learning? Fro m a systems perspective, this framework presents a multidisciplinary challenge: The phenome non combines emotional gains for individuals (who f eel satisfied and socially valued because they “learned ” something) with economic benefits to platforms (a s that satisfaction keeps learners engaged and returning for more). While addressing the issue requires changes to K-12 education, the solution necessitates much broader c hange, as learning extends beyond formal education. Regulating social media is part of the solution, but the fast-changing technology ecosystem continues to challenge regulatory systems trying to keep pace.
