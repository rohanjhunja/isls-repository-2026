# Shared Epistemic Agency in A Collaborative Game for AI Literacy

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how middle school students enacted shared epistemic agency in Occultation, a collaborative Turing-test game for AI literacy. Analyzing 353 chat messages from 28 students, we find that socio-emotional behaviors — trolling, spamming, and banter — directly triggered epistemic reasoning and regulative coordination, extending Damşa et al.'s (2010) framework. Rather thandisruptions, these behaviors functioned as inquiry tools in a low-stakes, teacher-free environment. Findings suggest game-based contexts can productively leverage emotional engagement for collaborative AI literacy learning.

As Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) becomes more accessible and prevalent in  education, the core challenge is not simply a knowledge deficit but a potential epistemic challenge. If students believe GenAI to be an objective and reliable knowledge source, they will lack the disposition to question its outputs, even when instructed to be critical (Fan et al., 2025). The goal of AI literacy should address how to think about it as a non-human knowledge agent through epistemic agency (Barzilai & Chinn, 2024; Zhang et al., 2022). Well-designed educational games can function as high-agency, low-stakes environments that are uniquely suited to activating shared epistemic agency (Gee, 2003). U nlike teacher-directed settings where epistemic goals are externally defined, games position learners to define, test, and revise their own assumptions. We designed Occultation, a collaborative Turing-test game that positions AI as an object of collective inquiry, and ask: How do learners enact shared epistemic agency in a collaborative game for AI  literacy? Theoretical perspective To understand how learners collaboratively develop ways of thinking about AI, we ground our work in the concept of shared epistemic agency. We chose this framework over individual epistemic agency approaches (Zhang et al.,

2022) because our game context proposes collective collaboration that no single player can identify the hidden

AI alone. Unlike knowledge-building frameworks (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006) that focus on artifact production, Damşa et al. centers  the process of agentic inquiry – which fits a game where learning happens through live conversation.

This agency manifests through two dimensions: (a) the epistemic dimension, focused on the collaborative creation of knowledge, and (b) the regulative dimension, focused on managing the group’s process for producing that knowledge (Damşa et al., 2010). Yet many students’ behaviors, such as emotional reactions, banter, trolling, did not fit either dimension. The regulative dimension captures how the group organizes its process (e.g., turntaking, voting, misdirection), whereas the behaviors we observed genera ted relational signals — emotional evidence that peers used as epistemic data (e.g., "why would AI curse?"). This functional distinction, not merely categorical difference, justifies a separate dimension.  Following Yang & Markauskaite  (2023), who argue that emotions are central to epistemic agency, not just background noise, we identified a third socio-emotional dimension. We treat this as an exploratory extension that needs further study.

## The game and data collection

Occultation is a multiplayer conversational Turing-test-like game where players distinguish between human survivors and AI infiltrators. The game assigns two roles: Interrogators (identify the AI) and Traitors (protect the AI by misleading others). T wenty-eight students (aged 12-13) from a summer program in a suburban area of eastern China participated. Students were placed into six online chat rooms of four to five students. We focused on the conversational log that happened in one game session of a total of 353 messages.

## Data analysis

We conducted an inductive-deductive analytical approach (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006) to analyze 353 chat messages from six groups. Two coders independently open-coded the transcript on message level to capture 776 emergent behaviors and generated an initial codebook that included reasoning, questioning, and trolling. We then mapped codes to Damşa et al. ’s (2010) two dimensions-epistemic Dimension (knowledge-related), and Regulative Dimension (process-related). Two coders independently coded all 353 messages, achieving a percent agreement of 89.36%, with disagreements resolved through discussion. However, a substantial portion of behaviors did not align with either dimension. M any behaviors did not fit either dimension, including emotional reactions, banter, and trolling produced their own agentic actions that shaped both knowledge work and group process. Following Yang & Markauskaite (2023), we identified these as a third socio-emotional dimension. Students enacted shared epistemic agency through three dimensions — epistemic, regulative, and socioemotional — that triggered one another rather than running in parallel. Epistemic dimension actions showed students taking ownership of knowledge work rather than passively guessing (Damşa et al., 2010).  Students hypothesized that "quick and sloppy" replies indicated nonhuman authorship, then tested this by asking "Did you finish your homework?" — drawing on shared school knowledge to probe authenticity.

Regulative dimension actions showed students, not teachers, initiating productive struggle (Chen, 2022). One student declared "I am AI or Traitor" to sow deliberate confusion, testing whether peers could hold their reasoning under pressure, while others typed "1,2,3..." to set speaking order and proposed "Let's vote directly later." Socio-emotional dimension made up 31% of all messages and directly shaped inquiry. This pattern appeared across all six groups, albeit with varying intensity.  "What the heck?!" drew peers' attention to reexamine their criteria. Spamming ("Start the excavator," ×4 times) triggered "Fk your excavator," which sparked: "Wait, why would AI curse?" Trolling produced visible anger that students read as evidence of human identity — a form of applied AI literacy, as students used emotional provocation as a live Turing-test probe to distinguish human from AI behavior.  These behaviors emerged organically in a low-stakes game; we do not advocate their deliberate use without careful scaffolding.

The three dimensions triggered one another. One student declared "I am AI or Traitor" — peers responded not with a direct question but with banter ("No hard feelings, just kidding?") to probe the claim's authenticity. That exchange then prompted a call to vote, showing how regulative strategy enabled socioemotional probing, which in turn informed collective regulation. Frustration and playfulness directly triggered epistemic reasoning and regulative coordination, extending Damşa et al.'s (2010) framework and aligning with work on epistemic emotions in co-inquiry (Candiotto, 2022). Occultation made this possible by removing the teacher and the pressure for one correct answer — students need to experience epistemic uncertainty about AI, not just be told to think critically. For educators, socio-emotional disruption can be a resource, not just a problem to manage. Findings are exploratory — one session, 28 students, no interviews.
