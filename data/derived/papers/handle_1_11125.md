# Tracing Problem-Solving and Computational Thinking Processes in Elementary School Programming Activities

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In elementary schools, Computational Thinking is often taught within problem-based learning pedagogies, and learners leverage their generic problem-solving competencies to complete CT tasks. How grade 4 children solve a CT problem is explored in this paper. We conducted time-series analyses to examine which problem-solving steps and CT processes are used as the task progresses, and how problem-solving steps and CT processes interrelate. Findings indicate that while some steps are given appropriate emphasis, others, including problem formulation, are not given enough prominence, overall stymying successful task execution. We also find that abstractions are made only after testing, and that when solutions fail, students tend to fall back on trial-and-error strategies. The findings suggest that students should be taught more explicitly CT problem-solving to prevent use of ineffectual strategies. More broadly, our analyses provide a dynamic view of learning CT enabling the development of operational frameworks for CT teaching.

## Introduction

In elementary  schools, computational thinking (CT) is often taught withi n problem-based learning (PBL) pedagogies as they provide an engaging context where new ideas  and skills can be learned and directly applied (Ching, & Hsu, 2023; Jonasen, & Gram-Hansen, 2019). The motivation for teaching CT is to prepare students for the digital world, and PBL is associated with product development, thus it is thought that PBL contexts familiarize young students already with the world of work. The aim is to give students opportunities to gain new insights and competences in a learning environment that relates to the professional practice awaiting them after formal schooling (Kwan, 2009).

As young students work on CT problems, they leverage their problem-solving (PS) competencies which are often acquired from engaging in problems encoun tered in everyday life or have been taught in formal education. However, little is yet known about students’ problem-solving in CT tasks, or how their problem-solving relates to and supports the use of CT skills. Ultimately, the goal of CT learning designs is to teach students the CT way of problem-solving, which is typically thought to be quite uni que and specific (Wing, 20 06). But when young students do not have sufficient CT knowledge,  their problem-solving competencies provide a basis for successfully completing CT tasks.

In this paper, we trace which PS steps and CT processes are used as a function of time and explore their relationship. The analyses permit insights into the question s how problem-solving competencies can function as scaffolds for learning CT, and how the students may gradually learn the CT-specific way of problem-solving. The students in our study learned CT using educational robotics  that are engaging and powerful learning artifacts through which students are actively involved in their own learning and support acquiring knowledge in relatively authentic contexts (Sullivan & Heffernan, 2016). They facilitate building and hands-on activities thus stimulating young students’ interest while also directly engaging students in programming. Computational thinking and problem-solving The relation between problem-solving and CT has been explored in prior work, and different views have been espoused. Shute et al., (2017), for example, see problem-solving as a component of CT, while in Wing’s (2006) formulation, CT is a specific way of thinking that translates into problem-solving. This definition emphasizes formalization, i.e., the analysis and encoding problems so that they can be solved by an information-processing device.

But occasionally theoretically and often in practic e, the problem-solving aspect of CT is not given sufficient prominence. CT is frequently seen as being closely related to programming (Kite, Park & Wiebe, 2021) and this view has often percolated into learning de signs emphasizing programming rather than teaching students how to approach and view problems. In many current designs there is a notable lack of emphasis on encoding and problem formulation, which are also critical components for CT. Young children are often taught programming in the context of well-defined, structured problems, working on which they rarely are given the opportun ity to engage in selecting the appropriate formalisms associated with computer science. The relationship between CT and problem-solving has also been examined empirically. Studies found that proficiency in problem-solving correlates with proficiency in CT, although  CT is often assessed  using programming tasks (e.g., Román-González, Pérez -González, & Jiménez-Fernández, 2017). There is also increasing evidence that explicitly teaching young students problem-solving before CT tasks, they learn CT better than if they are not taught problem-solving (Calao, et al., 2016, Shin, et al. 2021). Thus, it appears that problemsolving competencies provide a structure and scaffo ld for students that are not yet sufficiently famil iar with CT, and that that structure may be critical for students to gradually become familiar with CT ways of thinking and be successful in CT tasks.

In this paper, we explore how grade 4 students engage in problem-solving and which CT processes they apply, to provide an informal evaluation and to exa mine the relationship between problem-solving steps and CT processes.

## Research questions

1. Which problem-solving steps and CT processes do the students engage in as the activity progresses

through time?

2. Are there identifiable phases in applying different PS steps and CT processes as the task is being

solved?

3. Are there notable relationships between problems-solving steps and CT processes?

## Participants

Participants for the study were students from four Grade 4 classes in an elementary  school located in a large, suburban school district in the Midwest. In total, 9 student pairs (18 students) participated. Task design The students were involved in a path-finding activity in a maze, using LEGO Spike. Stude nts worked in pairs. The activity encompassed building a LEGO robot able  to travel from a starting position to a target. To  reach the target, the robot had to continuously sense the map (maze) below it, on which colored lines were drawn, and turn in the right direction (Figure 1). Each classroom had two mazes; between 4 and 6 student pairs were working with their robot. Students were instructed to first deve lop and test the program on workstations (a space c lose to their desks), and then test the robot behavior on the maze itself. Figure 1 The Maze The robot had to be programmed  to move from the yellow area to the green area, turning when it sensed a colored stripe.

The activity took place over 3 days, about 30 minutes each day. Although not strictly structured that way, task execution followed an intuitive order, from plannin g the robot’s movements and identifying any constra ints and requirements related to programming, to implementat ion (programming), and then  placing the robot on the map to test the program.

## Procedure

The analyses are based on the assumption that learners’ talk and actions r eflect their thinking, although we recognize that many processes may not be externaliz ed. D ata collection and analysis focused on students’ utterances and physical actions while they were engaged in the educational robotic activity. The educational robotics activities were inserted i nto regular class time in a science class. Teachers allowed us to record students’ activities using video and audio. Recordings were transcribed and then segmented into 1-minute segments. A senior researcher and two research assistants coded the data using the codes described in Tables 1 and 2 below. CT and PS codes were applied simultaneously. Code rs were instructed to apply code s per segment, identifying the most prominent CT proc ess and PS step. Each research assistant coded abou t onehalf of the data. The senior researcher coded about  20% of each half. Interrater reliability was compu ted using Cohen’s Kappa (κ). Coding divergences of double-coded data were resolved through discussion until κ was greater than 0.8. Coding was based on categories of a problem-solving framework and a CT framework, described now. Problem-solving framework There is broad recognition that at a general level proficient problem-solvers engage in ordered steps. OECD (2013) distinguishes 5 distinct steps: recognizing that a problem exists, establishing an understanding of th e problem, planning a solution, implementing a solution, and, finally, evaluating the solution. Grounded in this work, Priemer et al. (2020) developed a more detailed framework encompassing the most important STEM PS steps of diverse disciplines such as mathematics, engineering, and c omputer science. This framework includes 13 steps, but not all steps are carried out by all disciplines – for example, mathematicians emphasize proofing, while engineers do typically not engage in this activity. We extracted from this framework the steps that most likely occur in a CT task context. Six steps were identified (table 1) and they were used as codes to classify students’ actions and talk. It is important to emphasize that the general probl em-solving frameworks assume that the steps are carried out in an ordered sequence. However, problem-solvers may also return to a previous step. For example, if a solution is rejected (step 6 in the table 1), they may sketch out a different solution (step 3 in table 1). (2017). The framework distinguishes between the following processes: decomposition, abstraction, algorithms, debugging, iteration, and generalization. Table 2 represent the processes, with definitions and examples of children’s talk and actions. We removed iteration and generalization as coding categories because they occurred infrequently but added Evaluation as a category. Ev aluation is often included in other notable framewo rks (e.g., Weintrop et al., 2016).

In contrast to the framework of problem-solving presented above, CT processes are not intended to describe a sequence of CT problem-solving steps. Nevertheless, we may cautiously forw ard a proposal of how CT problem-solving should occur or at least specify which comp onents should be more prominent as time progresses. We could expect decomposition to be particularly pr ominent during planning and problem specification, algorithmic thinking both during planning and imp lementation, and evaluation and debugging during testing. Abstraction may occur especially at the encoding stage, where students should gain an understanding of the fundamental nature of the task  and may develop a model which grounds further plan ning and implementation.

## Results

To answer Research Question 1, we traced the frequency of PS steps and CT processes over time and conducted chi-square analyses to identify correlations between time segments and PS steps and CT processes. Analysis of problem-solving A first analysis is to give a broad picture of which PS steps students used  at each day. Figure 2 traces the number of PS steps by day showing that students used several steps in parallel, but that some steps were more prominent than others. Of note is that already on the first d ay students implement solutions, corresponding to o ur in-class observation that they quite immediately work with the programming interface as it is new and exciting. Solutions on that day are often implemented without sketching them first out. Problem specification is less frequent on day 1 than it is on day 2. It is likely that the students recognized the importance of this step after early solution attempts failed. In line with an effective problem-solving sequence, problem formulation drops on day 3, but it must be noted that on that day many students tested their r obot on the maze and often their programs didn’t wo rk as intended. Their approach changed from the previous more systematic approach to using a trail-and-error strategy

– quick attempts to fix the program with very little planning or reflection.

Analysis of computational thinking processes Figure 3 shows that students engaged in several CT processes in parallel on each day, including those that should have been emphasized at a later stage. Day 1, for example, should emphasize decomposition and abstraction, but much work was focused on writing and debugging incomplete and non-workable programs. Algorithmic thinking was prominent on that day because children approach ed the task by thinking in terms of sequential inst ructions. Abstractions were rare on that day but increase d in day 2 and especially in day 3 when students spent their time primarily on testing their robot on the maze. It ap pears that they were prompted to abstract over and understand the problem once a concrete program was written and could be tested on the maze, and errors in the program were discovered. Once abstraction occurred, children rel ied much less on thinking in terms of sequential in structions and developed more modular programs. These modules represented short stand-alone robot behaviors that could be called from anywhere in the program.

To answer this question, and also to obtain a more precise picture of PS steps and CT processes over time, 1-minute segments were grouped into batches of 9-minute-long segments. Figure 4 Frequencies of PS Steps Codes by 9-Minute Segments Figure 5 Frequencies of CT Processes Codes by 9-Minute Segments The figures above reveal that the participants enga ged in the robotic activity in two major phases. Du ring the initial phase (up to mid-point of the activity), they were enacting a higher percentage of algorithmic thinking and decomposition but demonstrated fewer abstraction and evaluation activ ities. Similarly, during this phase, these CT skills were accompanied by high occurrences of specifying the problem and implement ing solutions. It is possible that the students were learning about the tool, and quickly tried it out.  In the next phase, the pattern reversed for both the enactments of CT and PS skills: solution sketching, accepting/rejecting solutions spiked, and these actions positively correlated with evaluation and abstraction activities. At the same time, the use of CT and PS processes demonstrated in Phase I dwindled. Phase II was a consequence of Phase I: after they became familiar with the tool, and formed a conjecture about the challenge, they went into an iterative process of forming, rejecting and revising the solution.

## Discussion

Overall, the picture emerging from the analysis is that the students engage in several problem-solving processes in parallel on the same day or the same 9-min periods, including in processes that should be emphasized at later stages. They rapidly develop partial solutions, engaging in short production-and-testing cycles, which often don’t produce viable programs. This in turn may prompted  a return to earlier steps, as in fact problem formu lation is quite prominent also periods in the middle of the activity (day 2, segments 4-6). When the robots are tested on the maze, a lack of systematic error detection and corr ection is clearly evident. We argue that this may b e the result of not sufficiently structure and formalize the problem from the beginning, nor to develop an appropriate abstract representation of the problem. The separation of tw o phases means that students’ learning of a subject area or topic is usually chaotic in the beginning and a more systematic approach isn’t evident until after new tools, ideas and subtopics have been explored. The analysis of CT processes shows that the students engaged in decomposition throughout all days albeit with a slight decrease i n day 3, and also  that they attempted to develop more abstract representations of the problem in day 3. This finding is not unexpected. Young learners require concrete material to think more abstractly, and the written program a s well as the observations of the robot’s behavior provided them with the information for abstraction.

## Conclusions

Our work is relevant for designing CT tasks within PBL contexts as well as for the question of what su pport should be provided. Broadly, our analysis shows that effectiveness of s olving CT tasks should be improved. Emphasizing appropriate encoding in the early stages of problem-solving is important as students tend to quickly write ineffective programs. This may prompt using trial-and-error strategies at later stages and may also lead to frustration and disengagement. Also, as young stude nts are prompted to think more abstractly about the problem once concrete material is available to them it is important to provide them with such material in the initial stages. More in general, students need to be provide d support to engage in well-structured problem solving. In many different learning contexts, it has been shown that  ineffective problem-solving inhibits learning (Antle, 2013). Teachers play a vital role  in this respect. Even if not specifically trained in CT, teachers are often skilled in detecting students’ challenges in problem-solving and are able to provide the meta-cognitive support children often need.

Our work contributes to making the transition from c omponent-based CT frameworks to more operational frameworks that describe how CT should be implemented in a classroom (Ioannou & Makridou, 2018). Shute et al. (2017) argue that commonly used framew orks describing CT do not lend themselves well to inform practice as they define CT as a set of components that specify key C T processes and skills. How the components link to problem-solving in classroom activities remains largely une xplored. By combining the analysis of problem-solving with CT processes, CT can be interpreted as  a problem-solving process, and its effectiveness evaluated. The development of a model  of CT problem-solving specifying which CT processes should be prominent at what problem-solving stage is helpful in developing effective CT activities. As for limitations, we recognize that problem-solving typically does not follow a strict linear sequen ce, but that also experienced problem-solvers may return to earlier steps when difficulti es arise. The students in our study may have followed the linear sequence of PS steps outlined in table 1 for a short period of tim e, but such processes are not captured in detail in the summary  representations. Nevertheless, our analysis broadl y captures the nature of problem-solving in CT tasks contexts and can be the basis for subsequent more detailed analyses.
