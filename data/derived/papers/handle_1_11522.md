# Bridging Schools and Industry: Lessons from Victorian Tech Schools in Entrepreneurship Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Entrepreneurship is increasingly viewed as a critical 21st-century capability, yet in secondary schools, programs are limited and fragmented. Design thinking and experiential learning are promising pedagogies for entrepreneurship education, but require time, expertise, and industry links. These are things traditional schools often lack. Tech Schools in Victoria, Australia address this gap through flexible, industry-connected programs.

## Background

Entrepreneurship is increasingly recognised as a key 21st-century capability, essential for preparing young people for a rapidly changing world of work (Burton, 2023). Entrepreneurship education at the secondary level fa ces several barriers, including limited teacher experti se, curriculum time, and access to authentic indust ry contexts (Williams, 2015). Design thinking and experiential learning have emerged as promising pedagogies, offe ring hands-on, team-based approaches that mirror entrepreneurial practice (Neck & Greene, 2011; Lynch et al., 2021). However, without real-world problems or industry mentoring, these approac hes are difficult to scale.  Despite growing interest, there remains limited research on how entrepreneurship education is implemented in secondary school settings (Shahin et al., 2021), particularly  in ways that are context-responsive and integrated with local industry. Much of the existing literature focuses o n post-secondary or business-specific programs, often overlooking the broader educational potential of en trepreneurship to support student agency, creativit y, and problem-solving across disciplines. There is a need to docu ment how secondary schools can adopt pedagogies such as design thinking and experiential learning i n practical and sustainable ways. This study examin es how Victorian Tech Schools in Australia have responded to this challenge, developing entrepreneurship prog rams in collaboration with industry that are both locally relevant and pedagogically rich. Victorian Tech Schools Established by the Victorian government in 2014, Te ch Schools provide high-tech STEM programs for local secondary school students and teachers. The primary  purpose of the Tech School Initiative is to increa se student engagement in STEM knowledge and skills, and link education with emerging industries to prepare young people for opportunities in Victoria’s fastest-growing, STEM-dominated sectors. F unded by the Department of Education, but hosted and operated by tertiary institutions, Tech Schools occupy a unique position, straddling the space between traditional schools and industry, whi ch grants them the freedom to innovate and experime nt with educational approaches that might be otherwise cons trained by conventional school structures or curric ulum demands. Tech Schools are also afforded additional resources, such as purpose-built technology centres, recurring budgets for emerging technologies and time allowanc es for educators to develop ties to industry. Media tion between schools and industry enables industry-generated problems are aligned with students’ needs, interests, and local contexts. Tech Schools then design and delive r STEM programs using design-thinking pedagogy which incorporate emerging technologies. As such, Tech Schools are developing innovative entrepreneurship programs that merit documentation and dissemination. For more information, see the Department of Educati on’s overview of the Tech School Initiative (https://www.vic.gov.au/tech-schools).

## Method

This study draws on interviews with 12 staff and pa rtners from six Tech Schools, using an abductive ca se study approach to explore how entrepreneurship education is developed in collaboration with industry. Interv iews focused on how Tech Schools design and implement programs, build industry connections, and navigate barriers to integrating entrepreneurship education in secondary schools.

## Findings and themes

Mediating industry partnerships Tech Schools act as intermediaries between local schools and industry. Each Tech School is expected to respond to the needs of its surrounding region by forging partnerships with sectors that represent local relevance and future job growth. Dedicated time and staffing for partnership development enables Tech Schools to address challenges that are often beyond the capacity of individual sc hool teachers, such as a lack of industry networks,  time and specialised expertise (Williams, 2015). As one Director put it, “The value of the Tech Schools is an understanding of industry and how to connect with experts. We can  help by getting that mentoring from industry and s howing them how this can be implemented inside of the classroom.” As an example, the Tech school, Eucalypt Springs, partnered with a local nursery and Parks Victoria t o co-design a four-day student program focused on environmental sustai nability. Eucalypt Springs facilitated site visits, structured the problem brief with industry partners, and supported students through the design  and pitch process. These place-based challenges were intended to give students “a more informed decision, not only about subject selections … but about what they might go and study at university” (Director). Similarly, at Banksia Bluff, students visited a local startup incubator for a one-day masterclass. Banksia Bluff coordinated the entire experience which allowed students to observe small businesses in action and build connections with local entrepreneurs. In addition to working directly with businesses, several Tech Schools collaborate with external providers such as startup incubators, where facilitators from these external providers bring firsthand experience in business development and entrepreneurship. These providers o ffer specialised knowledge and perspectives that complement the expertise of classroom teachers. Whi le many teachers bring deep pedagogical skill and curriculum knowledge, they may not always have the time or background to deliver programs grounded in current industry practice. As one external provider explain ed, “This is our value proposition — the fact that we’re not coming at this as teachers.” These partnerships between schools and external providers, brokered by Tech Schools, give students access to mentors actively working in the startup space and created opportunities to explore entrepreneurial thinking in a real-world context. As one Director noted, “What our schools are missing, and where the value of the Tech Schools is, is an understanding of industry and how to connect with experts.” Embedding design thinking as a pedagogical approach Several Tech Schools use design thinking as a core framework to guide students through the entrepreneu rial process. Tech Schools often use design thinking to support entrepreneurial learning by helping students identi fy user needs, generate ideas, prototype solutions and  gather feedback, which are all essential steps in launching a viable product or service. As one facilitator expla ined, “Students understanding how to do that backgr ound research, and that empathy especially, and the define stages of design thinking, to me, is key.” However, the way Tech Schools integrate design thinking within entrepreneurial programs varies. At Bluebell Hill, entrepreneurship is treated as an extension of the design thinking cycle, where entrepreneurial thinking bookends the design process by helping students identify viable ideas before they start, and guiding them through market consideratio ns once they have a prototype. In contrast, Jacaranda Vale places a stronger emphasis on market validation, and while students do engage in idea development, the focus is on ensuring that a product meets a real market need. As one facilitator put it, “We’re going to make this beautiful product … but we’ve done no customer interviews, we don’t even know if customers want that.” This emphasis on  market demand means that design thinking plays a supporting role rather than a central one. While so me Tech Schools embed design thinking deeply within  their entrepreneurship programs, others take a different approach. Banksia Bluff  and Wattle Grove, for example, prioritise business skills, networking or industry exposure over product development, and often omit d esign thinking altogether in favour of industry-led challenges and role-based business simulations.
