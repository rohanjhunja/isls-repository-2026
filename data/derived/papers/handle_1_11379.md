# Learning to “Fail Forwards” in a Game-based Environment: Interactional Insights on Failure from an Escape Room

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Failure – both conceptually and in practice – is a ripe site for theorizing learning. In the learning sciences, failure is heterogeneous, with many of its semantic offshoots (e.g., uncertainty, misconception) intimating how the experience of “failing” is dependent on broader questions of context (e.g., how and where failing occurred). Drawing on data from a larger qualitative study examining how five players learned to “escape” a series of increasingly difficulty escape rooms, this paper demonstrates that learning to escape-like all multi-part phenomena-requires the conceptual breakdown of a system’s constituent components and the lamination of semiotic substrates that contribute to and advance cooperative action among individuals.

## Introduction and research questions

“It wasn’t like we knew what we were doing,” Walter —a White, 21-year-old college undergraduate and participant—noted when discussing his team’s navigation across The Illusionist’s Library, an analog immersive escape room. “It was like a failing-forwards kind of thing,” he said, “a let’s do a thi ng wrong a bunch of times really fast so we know how not to do it, and then maybe figure out from that how to do it.” Failure —both conceptually and in practice, as seen in Walter’s r eflection—is a ripe site for theorizing learning. Failure is eponymous with many of its semantic offshoots (e.g., uncertainty, impasse, misconception), intimating how the experience of failing is dependent on broader quest ions of context (e.g., how and where failing occurr ed) and content.

Although the fields of game studies and the learnin g sciences have underscored collectively how failur e can be productive (see, e.g., DeLiema et al., 2023; Fis hman & Hayward, 2022; Gee, 2004; Juul, 2013; Kapur,  2008, 2015; Williams-Pierce, 2019), the need to further nuance the interactional grammars of uncertainty across analog gameful learning environments remains. Drawing on data from a larger qualitative study examining how p layers learned to escape a series of increasingly difficulty immersive escape rooms, we took up this invitation to explore two research questions:

1. How do failure and uncertainty shape the social interaction of five players navigating an immersive

analog escape room, The Illusionist’s Library?

2. Through co-operative action, how do these players navigate playful impasses when learning to escape?

## Theoretical framework

Theoretically, this paper takes as its starting poi nt that learning is an interactional accomplishment  distributed across a range of semiotic resources (Goodwin, 2013; Hutchins, 1995). For example, when a player navig ates a series of puzzles in an escape room, gestures, spee ch, and supplementary action supplied to problem-solve are “laminated” in interaction (Goodwin, 2013). Failure, from this perspective, is an interactional phenom enon. Drawing on interactionist perspectives, Goodwin’s (2018) “co-operative action”  in particular, we leverage the theoretical vocabulary of social interaction to hig hlight the cumulative and incremental nature of fai lure in play. We highlight the multiple ways lamination – the distribution of thought and action into coordin ated semiotic resources  –  advanced “escape,” individually and collectively, in unfolding interactions. Thinking with co-operative action Co-operative action (Goodwin, 2018) is a theory of interaction that traces how meaning is constructed, modified, and transformed across a breath of modal and semiot ic resources – such as talk, gesture, prosody, and design. Notably, co-operative action is not always cooperative nor mutually beneficial – instead, meaning is constructed across chains of interactions that “operate” on eac h other as participants manipulate and iterate upon  previous utterances. To demonstrate this, Goodwin (2018) cit es an argument between a neighbor and a child where  the neighbor says, “Why don’t you get out of my yard ?” to the child. The child responds, “Why don’t you make me get out of the yard” (p. 3). In this interaction, t he child reused the neighbor’s language while decom posing the arrangement of the neighbor’s utterance into two pa rts, adding “ make me ” to the middle of the utterance to transform the neighbor’s request into a confrontati onal retort. Similarly, our daily conversations and  interactions unfold across a series of co-operative actions in which utterances build upon and transform previously intelligible actions – or what Goodwin calls substrates. When analyzing escape room play, we attended to how participants offered puzzle solutions to The Illusionist’s Libra ry (the focal room under study), upon which their p eers generated subsequent, iterative problem-solving attempts. Although the example of the argument between the neighbor and child solely features talk (i.e., language and prosody), it is important to note that co-operative action constitutes a breadth of layered s emiotic materials. A linguistically identical utterance, for example, can be transformed into mocking antagonism with new gestures (e.g., an eye roll), prosodic inflections (i.e., sarcastic tone), or addressees (an opponent) in way s that go beyond word-based manipulations. Thus, co-operative action occurs within semiotic fields whereupon multiple, relevant signs and modal resources are layered – or laminated – in a single moment of interaction. To understand fa ilure and problem-solving during escape room play, our analysis examined how participants iterated upon and transformed each other’s thinking across a breadth of laminated semiotic materials – including material, spatial, aural, linguistic, gestural, and haptic interpretat ion. Indeed, the semiotic and modal density of The Illusionist’s Library made participants’ failure and iterative problem-solving interactionally rich phenomena for inquiry.

## Method

Focal room: The illusionist’s library The Illusionist's Library  – the focal room in this study – unfolds as participants discover that an illustriou s magician, in his thirst to master the arcane abilit ies of the dark arts, disappeared without a trace. Players act as paranormal investigators and delve into a concealed  library, surreptitiously situated within a deserte d vault, to unravel the broader mystery. While combing through the book titles in the library, players find their way out by uncovering cryptic messages embedded in adjacent artwork, deciphering anagrams found in newspaper clippings plastered on walls, discerning sequences within an array of wall-mounted light fixtures, and  harnessing their detective prowess to unlock a sequence of trunks le ading to a hidden chamber. In this smaller, secreti ve space, participants must match levitating rings to interpr et a cipher, precisely position an assortment of cr ystals, and piece together puzzles to find a hidden amulet. After successfu lly resolving the initial puzzles of the Illusionis t's Library, a concealed passage is revealed behind the bookc ase, prompting players to progress into the Magicia n's lair. This last puzzle, the room's larger meta-puzzle, is solved when players form a physical connection, hand in hand, creating a conduit of mystical energy that links the found objects to the Illusionist's enchanted altar. Data generation Video-recordings of play To generate data about how participants navigated e scape, Wargo and his team equipped participants with head-mounted Go-Pros to document play and activity across the envir onment. In conjunction with this-and with approval and consent from all involved parties-footage from surveillance cameras was also obtained. Th is provided a more comprehensive view of action within each room. These methods for generating data granted the research team extensive data on play, including par ticipants' speech and talk, movement, gesture, and gaze. The video recordings, which lasted between 43 and 68 minutes, further showcased aspects of the room, such as decor, theme, sound design, and other multimedia elements.  Aiming to capture both spoken and significant non-verbal communication accurately, we first transcribed the audio recording captured  by one Go-Pro. A research team member prepared a detailed initial transcript focus ing on the talk. This draft was subsequently cross-referenced and refined with footage from the other participant 's video. After, another colleague verified the tra nscript's precision by auditing each recording.

Individual and group interviews Players were interviewed both individually and coll ectively during the study. These interviews ranged in length from 17-48 minutes. Individual interviews were held prior t o playing the first room. They were used to generate insight into player’s fluency with escape rooms, th e dynamic of the team, and the rationale for pursui ng participation. Group interviews occurred immediatel y after play. These reflexive interviews interrogat ed collaborative sensemaking about escape, noting similarities and differences in design across the rooms. Although we do not draw on interview data for this inquiry, we share how and when they were completed, as the i nitial impetus to explore failure resulted from a 1:1 interview with a player.

## Data analysis

Data analysis was an iterative process in which we employed both qualitative and quantitative methods.  To understand the relations among modes, bodies, envir onments, and materials of escape room play, we empl oyed an adaptation of Erickson’s (2006) whole-to-part analytic procedure. First, we watched a single-player’s firstperson perspective video all the way through without audio and made field notes to mark time and note nonverbal phenomena. Then, we rewatched the recording, this t ime with audio, and noted  how, if at all, talk textured play and shaped social interactions. Next, we corroborat ed our notes by watching other players’ videos. Usi ng theoretical insights from Juul’s (2013) typology of  failure in games, we then identified significant e pisodes, or what Jordan and Henderson (1995) referred to as shapes of events, that matched our inquiry and interest in failure. In particular, we looked for individual puzzles tha t showcased how uncertainty (i.e., failure) resulte d in remedy and repair.

To augment and confirm that these episodes signaled  failure for the players, we created a timeline of play, highlighting the completion time for each of the 14 puzzles. Assuming that time spent on individual puzzles resulted in the greatest level of uncert ainty, the timeline confirmed that the three puzzle  sequences we observed as giving the most difficulty to participants—Puzzle 6, the Crystal Shard Puzzle; Puzzle 7, the Light Lock Puzzle; and Puzzle 14, the Human Chain Puzzle—indeed signaled the greatest level of player error and frustration. Taking these episodes forward, we then generated multimodal transcripts (Mondada, 2016) of the three puzzle encounters, including Jeffersonian transcriptions of participants’ talk (Jefferson, 2004). Next, using Lui et al.’s (2024) analytic technique, we coded the three episodes according to  three states: identifying a puzzle-based problem, flagging a fault in escape room play, and proposing new soluti ons. After coding, we calculated the elapsed time o f participants’ problem, fault, and solution talk, rounded to the nearest second. To better nuance and texture our quantitative analy sis, we engaged in qualitative microanalyses of the moments of co-operative actions (Goodwin, 2018), whereby particip ants employed discursive and interactional transformations from solution attempt to solu tion attempt. Specifically, we traced how interacti onal transformations across problem, fault, and solution  states were produced through modifications of inte rpretive substrates. Informed by our theoretical framework, we identified substrates as local know ledge (i.e., an interpretation of an escape room puzzle) and historical knowledge (i.e., prior experiences with puzzles and games) that provided the basis of meaning-making and subsequent iterations of escape room pla y. This microanalysis helped illuminate the accrual of players’ actions across space (i.e., rooms) and materials. As a result, our findings highlight how participants’ iterative transformatio ns across failure, uncertainty, and solution-oriented play co-operatively produced insights surrounding how they learned to escape the rooms.

## Findings

Findings are refracted here through one puzzle, the Human Chain. This puzzle took the greatest amount of time for players to navigate and prompted collaborative play from all five participants, highlight ing how elements of mechanistic failure and challenge both mediated gameplay a nd transformed problem-solving attempts. As a metapuzzle that combined a variety of material arti facts with insights and solutions generated from pr evious challenges. More specifically, participants were tasked to magically discharge multiple items throughout the room by holding hands (i.e., forming a human chain). Thi s chain of human touch connected a sequence of symb ols illustrated on a platform with their corresponding objects found throughout the room. The Human Chain lasted 5 minutes 55 seconds. Of this time, 1 minute 22 seconds (23%) were dedicated to problem identification, 2 minutes 12 seconds (37%) to addressing faults, and 2 minute s 9 seconds (36%) to solution implementation. In an alyzing the shapes of social interaction, we saw how this f ormation served as a perceptuomotor schematic subst rate (Flood, 2024)—a commonly understood configuration of bodies  and materials—on which participants enacted puzzle-solving attempts. After successfully discharging a few items in the room, players began to stumble and encounter failure. The final item the participants were supposed to discharge was a water jug. With the water jug crystalizing as the penultimate challenge to escape the larger room and puzzle, participants created a human chain that connected a water symbol on the platform to a water jug across the room. Unfortunately, their attempt failed to unlock the n ext component of the puzzle. Recognizing that their  attempt failed, the participants initiated a series of subs equent problem-solving attempts, building on and transforming previous interpretive substrates. Across each iterative problem-solving attempt, we noted that certain links in the human chain configuration were decomposed (i.e., br oken apart, omitted), reused (i.e., integrating pre vious materials from the escape room), and rearranged to generate new and transformed solution attempts. Each attempt systematically targeted and modified individual linkages of the gameplay configuration. As detailed below, these co-operative iterations of problem-solving attempts demonstrated an acute awareness of  the players’ broader schematic understanding of the escape room gameplay and the infrastructure of the room itself. Initial attempt and failure After failing to magically discharge the water jug,  Sam initiated a new solution sequence by rearrangi ng his interaction with the artifact and touching it from a variety of angles and orientations. When Sam’s ph ysical manipulation of the water jug proved ineffective, h e made a bid for another action by pointing to a la rge power switch in the room labeled “Magic.” With the goal o f resetting their attempt, he asked, “Do you want t o try it again with the magic off?” Walter and Casey decline d Sam’s bid, explaining how the power switch had to be on to discharge items in the room magically. Then, Wal ter recommended that the group retry their first at tempt sequence (i.e., creating a human chain between the water symbol and water jug) while ensuring that everyone was holding hands physically rather than grabbing onto clothing, or as he put it, “not, like, holding onto  someone’s shirt.” Frustration and further exploration of alternatives Despite their efforts, the group’s third attempt fa iled. Without feedback on how to successfully trans form and repair their solution attempt, Walter tapped the water symbol on the platform five times in frustration, seemingly trying to double-check that there was a secure connection to the platform. Nearly out of ideas, the group diversified their approach and solution. Sam suggested reconnec ting their human chain to other objects around the room, such as a globe and an owl statue. These subsequent attempts were unsuccessful. Sam iterated the need to use the water jug, bringing the focus back to the group’s o riginal solution configuration. This broadened expl oration demonstrated the participant’s adaptive thinking and willingness to experiment with different  solution configurations.

The scene ended with the participants disbanding the human chain, reevaluating aspects of the room, and proposing other potential transformations to their puzzle-solving (e.g., looking for a different water jug, t urning the magic off again, touching things randomly around the room). Each new attempt was a targeted transformation, reflecting the group’s deepening schematic understa nding of how their embodied and material configurat ions mapped onto the infrastructural requirements of the  escape room’s metapuz zle. Moments later, a technician entered the room and explained that a technical mal function prevented the players’ initial solution at tempt from unlocking the Human Chain puzzle. Indeed, the participants’ initial attempt was correct all along. This revelation validated players’ problem-solving process and underscored the impact of exter nal factors (i.e., the designed interface) on gameplay.

## Discussion and significance

Although limited, as our analyses only focused on five players navigating a single escape room and did not capture all variations surrounding troubleshooting particul ar puzzles within the setting, this work has theore tical and methodological significance in understanding failure as an interactional phenomenon. Reading across the broader video and activities of escape, the Human Chain Puz zle demonstrated how failure prompted participants to generate and transform a perceptuomotor scheme and grammar of playful interaction. Because the Human Chain was The Illusionist’s Library’s metapuzzle, the group was tasked to conceptualize how their bodies, substrates in their own right, formed a series of corporeal inter connections with materials in the room. The represe ntational infrastructure of the human chain became a central substrate for navigating escape. As Goodwin (2018) noted, The substrate constitutes a locus for co-operative action in which both those who constructe d the substrate and those who are using it build new action by working with it in different ways. The subsequent actor uses the resources provided by  the substrate to create something new, appropriate to his or her current task. The operati ons performed on a substrate to create a next action do not simply copy the substrate, but transform it into something different. (p. 267) Hence, each subsequent problem-solving attempt (i.e., failure) in composing the hu man chain featured a minor change in the schematic composition of the play con figuration. With surgical precision, players’ transformations targeted acute features and joints in their bodily and play-based configurations. Learning to escape The Illusionist’s Library was il lustrative of co-operative action. It was a trajectory and process of “building new action by performing systematic operations on something created by someone else” (Goodwin, 2018, p. 431). The configurations of dive rse resources (e.g., materials, language), as we sa w through players’ interactions, built locally relevant meaning and action. These were then subsequently laid over by others’ elaborations and revisions. Players, as our finding s highlighted, created new actions by building on t heir peers’ previous operations and the subsequent patterns of finding fault. Each puzzle led to states of copresence. These material artifacts —social and representational infrastructures that me diated escape —were unique substrates culminating in co-operative action and producing an interactional trajectory of escape.
