# “Where Do I Start?” A Tool for Interactive Literature Discovery in the Learning Sciences

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The rapid expansion of Learning Sciences scholarship makes it challenging for newcomers to navigate diverse theories, methods, and contexts. We present the Interactive Literature Review Database Template, an open-source tool that turns any corpus into a dynamic, filterable database. As demonstrated by Journal of the Learning Sciences articles and student-built instances, the tool enables sustainable, transparent, and easily updated literature exploration while lowering technical barriers for researchers.

The field of Learning Sciences has grown substantially over the past three decades, producing a rich but increasingly expansive body of scholarship. For newcomers to the learning sciences–many of whom are still developing familiarity with the field’s theoretical traditions, methodological approaches, and evolving areas of inquiry–navigating this landscape can feel overwhelming. Traditional keyword searches often presuppose that readers already “know what they don’t know,” yet, as one of our collaborators noted, “Sometimes you don’t know what you don’t know. And if I don’t know, how am I supposed to search for it?” Across our research groups, this sentiment reflected a broader challenge: newcomers to the learning sciences frequently lack accessible, structured tools for exploring disciplinary literature in ways that make underlying patterns, debates, and trajectories visible.

To address this challenge, we developed the Interactive Literature Review Database Template, an opensource, configurable web tool designed to support scholars’ discovery, exploration, and synthesis of research. The tool enables authors to transform any corpus—such as the full set of Journal of the Learning Sciences (JLS) articles used in our demonstration—into an interactive, filterable database organized around meaningful dimensions, including theoretical frameworks, methodologies, study contexts, pedagogical models, and constructs. Unlike traditional systematic reviews that culminate in static narrative summaries or meta-analytic alongside the literature it represents. We envision this tool as a living & crowdsource-able resource to support newcomers to the field and scaffold more transparent and sustainable review practices. In this demo, we will introduce the design and functionality of the Interactive Literature Review Database Template, showcase the JLS-specific implementation created through a systematic tagging and review process, and invite participants to explore the tool hands-on. We will also highlight how the template can be adapted to other corpora and research areas, offering attendees a practical pathway for integrating interactive literature databases into their own scholarly workflows.

Aims and rationale Despite the abundance of digital tools available for searching academic literature, two critical groups in the Learning Sciences community remain underserved: (1) scholars-in-training who do not yet know what to search for, and (2) researchers conducting systematic literature reviews whose work quickly becomes outdated, difficult to maintain, and non-interactive. The Interactive Literature Review Database Template was designed explicitly to support these needs.

Supporting LS newcomers’ discovery of literature For early-career scholars, entering the Learning Sciences means navigating a domain with multiple theoretical traditions, diverse methodological approaches, and highly contextualized phenomena. Traditional keyword searches—whether conducted through library databases, Google Scholar, or increasingly through large language models—assume that users already know the appropriate terminology for their area of inquiry. This epistemic challenge is not merely a matter of inexperience; it reflects the deeply interdisciplinary nature of the field, in which different research communities employ distinct vocabularies to describe similar constructs, contexts, or analytic traditions.

The interactive database provides a scaffold for this early-stage learning process. By enabling users to browse articles through structured filters—frameworks, methods, study contexts, pedagogical models, and constructs—it surfaces the field's conceptual architecture without requiring prior expertise. Users can move fluidly across categories, noticing patterns in theoretical commitments, methodological choices, or the evolution of research across decades. In this way, the tool supports both literature discovery and keyword discovery, helping scholars build the language necessary for deeper engagement.

## Addressing the limitations of traditional systematic reviews

The second underserved use case concerns researchers conducting systematic literature reviews. Traditional reviews require considerable time and coordination, often taking months or years to complete, only to become outdated shortly after publication. Moreover, once a systematic review is published, readers must rely on static narrative summaries or tables that flatten the complexity of the underlying research landscape. These formats limit readers' ability—especially for those unfamiliar with the content area—to explore the corpus interactively or identify specific articles aligned with their interests. The Interactive Literature Review Database Template offers an alternative: a maintainable, updateable infrastructure for systematic reviews that can grow alongside the literature. Because the template relies on lightweight configuration (a single JSON file and a CSV of items), research teams can easily update or extend their databases as new publications emerge. Rather than producing a one-time snapshot, reviewers can create a living systematic review—one that is not only transparent in its tagging categories and inclusion criteria but also navigable by readers with varying degrees of familiarity with the domain. Considerations of citational justice As we create tools to enable the discovery of literature, we must acknowledge the historical and current harms that such research has inflicted. As social sciences researchers, our work represents (or misrepresents) the lived experiences of those we study – and excludes those that we don’t. Learning and knowledge-production are nonneutral acts. We recognize that this software, the corpus of literature it references, and the unwritten manuscripts it will inform are not objective or politically agnostic. Rather, these products carry the same biases of the humans that designed them and reflect the agendas of government, military, and/or for-profit institutions that funded them. By including articles in our databases, we are enabling their discovery and dissemination to field newcomers as ‘canon’ knowledge to be cited. Citational justice scholarship argues that modern academic citation systems operate under the assumption of meritocracy, without attending to how such practices reproduce existing power structures and hierarchies (Kwon, 2022), thereby perpetuating a culture of epistemic injustice in which those with prestige are legitimized as knowledge makers (Gonzales et al., 2024). These scholars call for practices such as corrective citation, citation auditing, including under-cited groups in reference lists, valuing and crediting contributions, and the co-construction of citation criteria with marginalized communities, to name a few (Pereira, 2025; Sauvé, 2025). Indigenous scholars have pointed out that universities play a role in both perpetuating and producing knowledge that supports settler-colonialism (e.g., Grande, 2018). Within this, the call from Palestinian academics for the academic boycott of Israel, modeled after academic boycotts of Apartheid South Africa, highlights that research partnerships and funding models can serve the purposes of normalizing harmful institutions, such as settler-colonial states (PACBI, 2014). These efforts should push us beyond journals like JLS and possibly traditional academic publishing, towards prioritizing publicly accessible sources (Levendowski, 2025). Other actions can include supporting initiatives to disrupt systems of oppression, of which citational injustices are a part, both inside and outside the university. As such, inclusion in our databases is not meant to be a show of support for a particular work; rather, we hope that this tool increases the discoverability of research for scholars based on topical rather than algorithmically determined relevance. This tool offers an alternative to automated search engines and largelanguage models that demonstrably privilege Anglophone, cisgendered, racialized white perspectives (ArenasCastro et al., 2024; Hawkins, 2021), reify existing hierarchies based on citation counts (Kwon, 2022; Sauvé, 2025), and inaccurate/fabricated citation generation (Walters & Wilder, 2023). Software At its core, the template is a plug-and-play web repository that requires only two editable files: a single JSON configuration file and a CSV file containing the corpus. All functional logic–HTML, CSS, and JavaScript–is encapsulated in the template, allowing authors to customize, deploy, and update their databases without any programming expertise. This philosophy of lightweight, accessible configuration makes the tool suitable for a wide range of scholars, including those who are new to literature reviews or who wish to provide transparent, i nteractive supplements alongside formal publications. By lowering the technical bar, the template supports the creation of living literature reviews that remain relevant and maintainable long after publication. The beta release was used during deployment in parallel with a systematic review of all JLS publications (see Figure 1; publication in progress). Articles were manually tagged across five dimensions—frameworks, procedure (linked from the live JLS Database instance). This implementation sought to demonstrate scalability, stress-test the dynamic filtering, and assess the potential to explore longitudinal trends in LS. Considering our aims to develop a tool that was context-agnostic, the first author (an early-career learning sciences researcher and the lead software developer) facilitated a workshop for authors 2-5 (all learning sciences graduate students in their first 2 years of study), where each student was tasked with configuring their own instance of the database (using release version 1.0). Here, each student conducted their own exploratory literature review on a niche topic of their interest to populate a smaller (relative to the JLS article corpus) literature database. This workshop functioned to (1) test for usability and accessibility issues, (2) refine the onboarding materials for clarity, and (3) reveal the limitations of the software across varying levels of user expertise, familiarity with their literature, and disciplinary focus.

The template renders each literature item as an interactive tile that displays customizable metadata (e.g., title, authors, year) and provides 1-click access to the article. Users can filter, sort, or combine categories across five tagging dimensions—theoretical frameworks, methodological approaches, study contexts, pedagogical frameworks, and constructs—providing multiple avenues of discovery even for those without prior knowledge of relevant keywords.

F igure 1 JLS Instance Screenshots Screenshots of (left) Dynamic Filters from the JLS Database Instance & (right) Expanded/Collapsed Article Cards The tool’s architecture includes several features that enhance both usability and transparency: ● Dynamic, multi-filter navigation: Users can apply multiple filters simultaneously, generating live crosstabulations of the corpus that reveal patterns across methods, theories, contexts, and constructs. ● Browsable tag lists with live frequency counts: Rather than requiring users to guess keywords, the interface surfaces all available tags and their relative prevalence, allowing scholars to identify cooccurring concepts or underexplored topic areas. ● Automatic generation of filters based on corpus structure: The tool infers its filter categories directly from the CSV file, ensuring the interface adapts to the specific needs of any imported corpus. ● Flexible extensibility: Although designed with learning sciences corpora in mind, the architecture can be applied to any domain in which literature needs to be sorted, visualized, and explored interactively. Notably, keyword-search functionality was intentionally deprioritized. Existing scholarly databases already excel at keyword searches, but they do little to scaffold exploration for users who lack preexisting knowledge of the keywords associated with their area of interest. Instead, the template emphasizes structured discoverability— surfacing the conceptual organization of a field in ways that invite browsing, comparison, and curiosity-driven exploration.
