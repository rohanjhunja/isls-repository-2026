# Improving Co-Designed Asynchronous Graduate Learning Through Network Analysis

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This design-based research study investigated student experiences in a co-designed, asynchronous online graduate course. We applied learning experience network analysis to examine 43 student reflection papers. The analysis identified seven distinct learning ecologies, each characterized by different configurations of learner agency, theoretical principles, and challenges. While two ecologies represented successful engagement, five others revealed specific struggles. These struggles included translating individual work into group collaboration and managing anxiety during transformative learning. This study shows that co-design resulted in varied learning ecologies of experiences. We proposed specific design moves for each challenging configuration. Network analysis offered a valuable method for systematically improving co-designed learning environments through responsive, theory-based support.

The design of learning for online asynchronous collaborative learning contexts has been a focus of investigation since the early years of the learning sciences (Bruckman, 2006), as has the design and function of collective meaning-making (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 1993). The idea that students and teachers could take on roles of shared agency to engage in co-design of artifacts found a home in the community of practice around constructionist learning theory (Kafai & Resnick, 1996). The reframing of co-design to specifically situate learners as co-designers of their own learning experiences allowed the integration of critical perspectives which problematized the traditional power structures and dynamics of learning environments (Gutiérrez & Vossoughi, 2010). The current study investigated a co-designed learning experience in the form of an online asynchronous course in a master’s degree program in instructional design where the students and instructor co-designed the learning activities. Prior to the start of th e course, the program director spent months creating a plan, and pre-designed structures were constructed in the learning management system to scaffold and support the co-design process. The plan and structures were grounded in principles derived from many theories. The “core” foundation was a set of constructivist theories centered on constructionist learning theory (Kafai & Resnick, 1995; Papert & Harel, 1991) supported by cognitive constructivist learning theory (Piaget, 1952) and social constructivist learning theory (Vygotsky, 1978). To support particular constructionist principles such as learning by making and learner agency, we integrated principles of intrinsic motivation from self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2017) and humancentered design (Norman, 2013). To expand social constructivist learning theory beyond the impact of the social on individual learners to encompass learning at the level of communities of practice, we integrated principles from situated learning theory (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Finally, because situated learning theory goes beyond knowledge to frame learning as involving a community’s epistemology (ways of knowing), practices (ways of doing), and ontology (ways of being), we further strengthened development of ways of knowing through principles from transformative learning theory (Mezirow, 2000). Because learning involves development of new ways of being, we integrated principles derived from the dynamic systems model of role identity (Kaplan & Garner, 2017). The specific principles from these theories (described in the findings section) were used not only in the design of the learning, but also as the focus of analysis in this study. The research question was: What configurations of learner agency, theoretical enactment, and challenges characterize student experiences in a collaborative asynchronous digitally-mediated co-designed learning environment, and how do these configurations inform future design changes for supporting productive co-design?

This study investigated one iteration of a larger design-based research study (Barab, 2022) using learning experience network analysis methods (LENA; Donaldson et al, 2024) focusing on learner experiences in a codesigned graduate-level course in an asynchronous online instructional design program. This 7-week course came near the end of the program, with the students and instructor co-designing the learning activities. In the first week on a collaborative digital sticky note board, students brainstormed potential topics. The instructor used an AI tool to cluster all of the topics, name each cluster, and write an overview paragraph about each cluster. In the learning 1292 management system, groups were created for each of the clusters, and students self-selected into groups. In the second week, the students in each group (with help from the instructor as needed) designed the learning activities in which their group would engage in weeks 3, 4, 5, and 6. In the final week, each group shared what they had learned with the other groups. Students wrote individual reflection papers every week, and in the final week they conducted self-grading and submitted self-evaluations.

Data for this analysis was in the form of 43 reflection papers submitted by students each week in response to a prompt asking them to reflect on the aspects of the learning experience that worked particularly well that week, struggles they encountered that week, and strategies they would use to address similar struggles in the next week. To honor learner experiences, only self-reported data was used. The reflection papers were coded in qualitative data analysis software by the first author using a large codebook (over 300 codes) developed by the second author over a period of five years, but the research team met regularly to discuss the coding and make revisions to the codebook as needed. The codebook was organized into three categories. The first category included codes representing learner experiences indicating alignment with specific principles derived from theories. The second category included codes for distinct struggles. The third category included codes for aspects of the learning experiences that worked particularly well for students. Coding resulted in 736 coded segments. Correlation analysis (Pearson one-tailed) was conducted to determine the likelihood of any two pairs of codes appearing in the same reflection paper. A correlation matrix was constructed from all correlations at p <. 001, which was then transformed into a network database and a network map was created. Cluster analysis (Girvan & Newman, 2002) was conducted to identify distinct learning ecologies. Since this study is not qualitative data analysis, but rather prepares qualitative data for analysis, triangulation was not appropriate.

Figure 1 LENA Network Map of Learner Experiences of Co-Design 1293 In the red circles cluster, positive learning experiences stemmed from design features that allowed for creativity, flexibility, and agency. Students enjoyed the freedom to collaboratively plan their projects as they saw fit, all while learning new things,  and appreciated the iterative process of creating and then receiving feedback that prompted self-reflection and improved their creations. These experiences align with several learning theories. Principles from self-determination theory were clear in the s tudents’ enjoyment of autonomy as they worked on their project. Constructionist theory principles appeared in the students’ pride in their work, and in their appreciation for generativity. Students engaged in practices of a community of practice, aligning with situated learning theory. Students’ use of a variety of approaches to their work, including metacognition, aligned with the principles of cognitive constructivist theory, and their development of new practices in new roles highlighted principles from the dynamic systems model of role identity. In addition, transformative learning theory was represented through students’ exploration of different perspectives and self-reflection. There were no struggles in this cluster, and therefore no design moves were possible. The black down-triangles cluster represented an ecology of positive experiences which stemmed from relevance, development, and collaborative problem-solving.  Students enjoyed connecting learning to real-world situations, their future careers, and their o wn prior knowledge. They also appreciated looking at multiple perspectives and collaborating with their peers, and gained new insights and motivations through this. Students with experiences in this cluster also developed teamwork, leadership, and communication skills. These experiences aligned with several learning theories. Principles from cognitive constructivist theory were clear in the students' building of new schema and activation of prior knowledge. Students’ exploration of their self-perception and feelings highlight principles from the dynamic systems model of role identity. The transformative learning theory principles were also present through changing beliefs, and the social constructivist learning theory was present through scaffolding done by peers. This cluster contained no struggles, and therefore no design moves were constructed.

In the blue up-triangles cluster, students' positive experiences emerged from an improved understanding of the material, better time management, and developing confidence and self-efficacy.. However, students also had struggles in this cluster that were related to divergent thinking and team meetings. At times, they were learning cooperatively rather than collaboratively, and expressed a desire for more group discussion and peer work. The principles derived from learning theories that were aligned with thes e experiences were varied. Constructionist learning theory was dominant in this cluster, emphasizing tinkering, constructing, and exploring. Students’ use of mediating artifacts and tools aligned with principles from social constructivist learning theory, their construction of individual knowledge aligned with cognitive constructivist learning theory, and their development of intrinsic motivation through increased competence was aligned with self-determination theory. Students in this cluster struggled to translate individual work into group collaboration, especially when it came to divergent thinking and avoiding a simple division of labor. To address this issue, design moves could utilize the theoretical principle of using mediating artifacts in order to b uild students’ individual confidence. For example, the instructor could ask teams to begin weekly team collaborations (after the first week) with individual brainstorming through a collaborative technology, like a shared whiteboard. This process would allow each student to first build their individual understanding and confidence, and then collaborate with group members to synthesize these individual ideas into a shared concept map. Using a tangible artifact to bridge the individual and collaborative modes of work would make group work true collaboration and not just cooperation. Students with experiences in the dark green squares clusters experienced a mixture of positive and negative experiences centered around teamwork and self-regulation. They reported positive outcomes with teamwork and peer feedback, specifically with fulfilling team roles and enjoying the diversity of their teammates. They also experienced less stress and anxiety, and enjoyed well-managed team meetings and logistics. However, students struggled with time management, procrastination, and dealing with passive members of their team. This cluster’s experiences relate to social constructivist learning theory), with its emphasis work in the zone of proximal development and peer learning, along with self-determination theory principles related to developing confidenc e and comfort. The primary struggle of students with experiences in this cluster were team passivity and poor selfregulation. A potential design move that could leverage this cluster’s strength with peer review and feedback, which align with the theoretic al principle of learning within a zone of proximal development. For instance, the instructor could prompt more frequent progress check-ins within teams. Students could be asked to complete short, weekly, low-stakes progress reviews for their teammates. By creating a system of mutual accountability and positive social pressure within groups, this design move could provide students with the external structure they need to avoid passivity and procrastination.

The gray diamonds cluster centered around students experiencing the positive and significant negative sides of self development. Students had a positive experience developing self-awareness, but also struggled with 1294 feelings of anxiety, fear, vulnerability, and discomfort as they also struggled with understanding and comprehending the topics at hand. These experiences align with several learning theories. The process of engaging with the perspectives of others is a principle from social constructivist learning theory. Transformative learning theory was represented by students developing new viewpoints and changing their ways of knowing and thinking. Situated learning theory appeared in the development of identity within their discipline. Productive failure, where mistakes lead to positive outcomes, aligned with principles of constructionist learning theory. To address this cluster’s significant struggles with anxiety, fear, and vulnerability, the theoretical principles of productive failure and transformative learning could be leveraged. For instance, the instructor could engage students in co-designing a low-stakes activity early in the design process that is intended to result in failure. The activity could be followed by a guided reflection introducing the idea of productive failure and its necessity to the deep learning process (Kapur & Bielaczyc, 2012). By reframing the anxiety or discomfort felt by students after their failure as valuable, these emotions would be given a productive purpose as catalysts for students to improve their design and consider new perspectives.

Students with experiences in the pink circles-in-squares cluster faced challenges to the foundation of their learning process. They struggled with uncertainty, the ambiguity of unclear instructions, and framing problems related to the topic or scope of the issue. Students did however have a positive experience relating their work to future decision making. The theories that connected to these experiences included situated learning theory, represented in the way students navigated communication within their teams. Social constructivist learning theory was represented through the collaborative construction of knowledge and the scaffolding that was provided by the teacher and the pre-built design. In addition, design theory principles were evident in the students' development of a human-centered design mindset. The primary struggles for students in this cluster were feelings of uncertainty and unclear instructions, so a design move could leverage this group’s strength in finding relevance to future decision making and the theoretical principle of scaffolding. The instructor could provide more robust initial scaffolding when students in topic groups first encounter a complex or ambiguous topic like problem framing related to their team’s topic. This scaffolding could explicitly ask learners to link to the real-world impact of the work being produced. By connecting this scaffolding to how students can create a product relevant to their future, the design could reduce feelings of uncertainty and lead to instructions that feel more enabling. The light green hourglass cluster focused on logistical and organizational aspects of learning. Students with experiences in this cluster had positive experiences with planning creativity, organizing and outlining their project, and setting goals and expectations for the future. They also had a positive experience with collaborative and communication technologies. They did, however, struggle with teamwork logistics and scheduling meetings. This cluster contained no theory nodes, indicating that the experiences of these students were more related to practically executing tasks rather than having experiences that enact specific principles from learning theories. The struggles of these students centered around the logistics of teamwork, so a design move could be to leverage the strength in productive planning and effective use of collaborative technology. The instructor could encourage and work with teams to create and maintain a project management document through a shared collaborative tool. The document would include a team charter and a clear timeline outlining all members’ shared responsibilities. By applying their self-identified skills in organization and technology utilization, this design move provides a solution to their logistical challenges.

This study reveals seven distinct configurations of learner agency, theoretical enactment, and challenges within a collaborative asynchronous digitally-mediated co-designed learning environment. Two configurations (red circles and black down-triangles) demonstrated high learner agency and strong theoretical alignment without significant struggles. Five others revealed specific challenges, from translating individual work into collaboration (blue uptriangles) to managing anxiety during transformative learning (gray diamonds). Analysis of each of these five clusters resulted in targeted design support. These configurations illustrate that co-design produces heterogeneous experiences even within carefully theory-informed environments. For supporting productive co-design, these findings suggest that designers should anticipate diverse configurations of agency and struggle, and then construct complex scaffolding that leverages theoretical principles strategically. The design moves proposed for each configuration demonstrate how to address specific challenges while strengthening learner agency.
