# Evaluating Evidence in Epistemically Unfriendly Environments: Strategies and Exemplary Practices for Science Instruction

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The digital world is filled with an abundance of unfriendly and complex information. To prepare students to make sense of this information, they must learn how to engage with good and bad qualities of evidence. In this paper, we focus on the evidence evaluation dimension of the Grasp of Evidence framework to operationalize design guidelines with specific strategies that foster students’ evaluation of methodologies that generate evidence (Duncan et al., 2018). These strategies can shape learning environments to problematize good and bad evidence and the methodological processes involved in generating evidence.

## Introduction

The digital world presents copious amounts of information that can be difficul t to evaluate. This abundance of information can be overwhelming with its polarizing claims. Digital sources may provide evid ence to support their claims; however, the evidence often has poor methodologies.  For instance, anti-vaccine activists often cite methodologically poor and retracted studies when su pporting inaccurate claims about the effects of vac cination (Kata, 2012). Although the scientific community views these studies as poor in quality, the evidence nonetheless impacts societal views on vaccine safety. Making sense of claims req uires understanding the processes involved in generating evidence that supports claims. Scienc e education values reasoning with and about evidence. This reasoning should involve distinguishing between goo d and bad evidence, and what constitutes good and b ad methodological quality (Duncan et al., 2018). Interacting with a combination of evidence ranging from good quality to bad quality in terms of methodology requ ires exposure to epistemically complex information rather than the oversimplified evidence students may engage with (Chinn et al., 2021). Learning environments that foster engagement with evidence should include a blend of good and bad evidence, so that students can learn t o deal with epistemically unfriendly environments—environments that (like the real world) make it difficult to determine what is accurate due to rampant misinformation and disinformation (Chinn et al., 2021). This is contrary to typical, friendly environments in which information and evidence are straightforward, carefully curated, and accurate. Science instruction can prepare students to evaluat e evidence through processes that involve critiquing the methodologies used to generate evidence. In thi s paper, we use the Grasp of Evidence (GoE) framework developed by Duncan et al. (2018) to identify desig n practices specific for evaluating evidence. They identify general design guidelines that align with the overa ll GoE framework for reasoning with evidence. Our goal is to further specify the guidelines to enable their tailored application to  one dimension of the framework —evidence evaluation. We operationalize the relevant guidelines with specific strategies based on instructional practices in the field and provide exemplars of design that can help students learn to work with evaluating methodologically problematic evidence. Our intent is to identify practices that can prepare students to reason with evidence relating to scientific issues, like vaccine safety and climate change, and develop competence in identifying the strengths and weaknesses of methodologies used to produce evidence that supports claims.

## Evidence evaluation within the grasp of evidence theoretical framework

The GoE framework was designed to analyze what it means to reason with evidence based on five dimensions of evidential reasoning. The dimensions are evidence analysis, evidence evaluation, evidence in terpretation, evidence integration, and laypersons’ use of eviden ce. In this paper, we focus on the evidence evaluation dimension, which emphasizes evaluation of the methodologies that are used to produce evidence. Understanding the strengths and weaknesses of methods can help de termine if experimental outcomes  and claims  are appropriately supported.

Chinn et al. (2023) evaluated science curricula using the GoE fr amework as an evaluation tool. Initial findings revealed that students rarely engage with ranging qualities of evidence and that most evidenc e students encounter is methodologically good. Exposure to ranging qualities of evidence involves interacting with a variety of good and bad evidence. Mainly working with methodologically good evidence provides little to no opportunity for students to identify weak methodologies. Though some methodological processes are beyond t he scope of a student, like making sense of highly technical statistical procedures, there are a number of general processes that students can understand and evaluate (Chinn et al., 2021). These processes include but are not limited to evaluating comparisons that have been made, scrutinizing procedures including but not limited t o sampling, evaluating measures, and evaluating the generalizations that are made. Comparing the design and methods of studies can raise questions about the strengths and weaknesses of each study and how these may impact a study’s claims. Students may compare studies  and determine how conclusively the conclusions are supported by the data. Evaluating methodological measures involves appraising reliability and validity. Reliability of measures refers to the repeatability of getting the same results when the same measure is used across different occasions and settings. Validity of measures refers to the accuracy of a study’s measures. Students can also learn to make judgments about the sample size by considering what constitutes an appropriate sample or judging if the conditions in a study are reasonable. Generalization considers how applicable conclusio ns are to making broader claims based on the methodological processes of a study like sample representativeness and type of intervention. We operationalize three of the original cross-dimensional design guidelines proposed by Duncan et al.

(2018) to further specify their application for the  evidence evaluation dimension of the GoE framework. Their

first guideline states “include evidence that diffe rs in quality and strength.” We specify this guideline further to focus on the dimension of the quality of evidence that should be varied. Their second guideline states “discuss reasons for reliability of scientific processes.” This guideline is further specified to focus on the need for students to problematize the reliability of methods used to generate evidence. The third guideline from the GoE framework is “develop and regulate shared epistemic criteria and shared reliable processes.” We specify this guideline to propose shared and negotiated commitments towards reliable methodological principles. As we elaborate on how to operationalize these guidelines with specific instructional strategies, we also identify instructional practices that have been developed by researchers that exemplify these instructional strategies. Strategies for the evidence evaluation dimension Science instruction should foster complex reasoning in evaluating evidence. Educators should design instruction that allows students to grasp what good and bad evidence  is, and how good and bad evidence are produced. In this section, we discuss instructional strategies for promoting competence in evidence evaluation with exemplars from designs developed by researchers. Because designing instructional content that allows for mea ningful evidence evaluation can be challenging, we encourage the use of scaffolds (Quintana et al., 2004) to support instruction. Implementing scaffolds can provide pathways for s tudents to engage with complex instructional content to guide their thinking towards making reasonable judg ments (Rinehart et al., 2014).  We conclude with discourse approaches for fostering methodological principles. Include explicitly bad evidence for making judgments about methodological quality. Evaluating evidence of varying quality can help students become aware of how to distinguish between good and bad evidence. Instruction is typically designed for students to i dentify good evidence that supports claims. Science  instruction needs to be designed so it is intentional about including bad evidence and leading students to understand when to dismiss bad evidence. The Promoting Reasoning of Co nceptual Change in Science (PRACCIS) project has un its that include evidence with methodological problems (e.g., inadequate sample size, novice versus expert observations) that need to be considered when makin g judgments about claims (Chinn et al., 2021). One lesson has students examine four studies relating to aspartame  and its potential links to cancer. Each study is d esigned to encourage students to scrutinize the methodological strengths and weaknesses. Scaffolds can be designed that present a range of evidence, not only in terms of how much evidence is considered but in terms of varying qualities of evidence (Samarapungavan, 2018). Rinehart et al. (2014) developed the Model-Evidence-Link (MEL) matrix for organizing student understanding of evidence in terms of quality and evaluating models. The evidence presented needs to range in quality in order to help students recognize that any set of evidence contains a range of evidence qualities that should be taken into account. Problematize methods for making judgments about met hodological quality.  Evaluating good and bad quality evidence requires students to make sense of  which pieces of evidence support a conclusion, and  which pieces may be problematic based on methodological p rocesses (Duncan et al., 2018). Metz (2011) demonst rated that first-grade students can engage in productive scientific inquiry about confounding variables and uncertainty. In the intervention, students develop ed research questions and design studies relating to a nimal behavior (e.g., What affects cricket behavior? Do some crickets jump further than others?). Students consider the validity of their claims and the shortcomings of their designed studies. This interv ention guides them to make judgments about possible confounding variables and the purpose of conducting additional studies to enhance evidence quality and generate more compelling claims. Opportunities for students to evaluate claims, whether they are claims presented to them or claims developed from conducting experim ents, can shape students’ perceptions of how to des ign methods that lead to higher-quality evidence. Conversely, if students are not presented with enough details of a study’s methods to appraise the methods, they can identify how lack  of details can be problematic for making accurate conclusions.

Ford (2005) and Manz and Suárez (2018) emphasized the importance of iterative processes in scientific investigations for addressing methodological qualit y. In both studies, students problematized their experimental methods, including data collection processes, which allowed them to question and discuss different elements that may strengthen or weaken their methods. Student discussions include questioning what the term “correct” means based on their respective methods, or how to effect ively compare outcomes while considering the method s of another group’s experimental setup (Ford, 2005). Bo th studies require d students to revise their methodological approaches and to try to minimize uncertainty to yiel d higher-quality data. When weighing the strengths and drawbacks of methods, students should be able to defend why and when it may be reasonable to overlook potential weaknesses in methodology. For example, if only one  student is responsible for taking multiple measure ments, should this be considered a weakness in methodology ? A similar approach was observed by Tang et al. (2023), who described elementary school students grappling with the reliability of data based on a lack of class consensus for determining how to take measurements. Students’ perceptions of uncertainty varied between aiming for errorfree measurements and viewing error as unavoidable.  Students should be afforded the opportunity to enga ge in practices that help them recognize what aspects of a study are questionable or if additional data is required to make reasonable judgments about claims. Judgments about claims may include if and how generalizable the claims are. Problematizing methods and their complexities can  yield a deeper understanding of which follow-up questions to ask about evidence generated from a study and considerations for improving the study’s methods. Scaffolds can support the problematization of evidence. The MEL matrix scaffo ld has students problematize evidence and methods by rating varying qualities of evidence on a 0-3 scale where 0 represents poor quality and 3 represents high quality to determine which pieces of evidence are more valuable than oth ers when making conclusions. Providing the opportunity to assess the quality of evidence should extend to making informed judgments about the methodologies producing that evidence and why it may be of poor quality. Guide students towards creating shared commitments to methodological principles.  The social component of scientific practices can help  students develop a greater sense of how experts interact and  evaluate lines of evidence based on community ideals (Duncan et al., 2018). Constructing shared ideals and commitments centering on the evaluation of methodological principles warrants students a path towards gaining more inclusive perspectives of methodological processes. In some projects, students collaborate to develop a class list of reliable processes for generating evidence (e.g., controlled comparisons, adequate sample size, good measures) (Chinn et al., 2021, 2023). Through developing shared commitments to methods that they will count as reliable, students develop tools that they can use collaboratively to evaluate methodological quality. The Developing Teachers’ Epistemic Cognition and Teaching (DeTECT) project f osters an appreciation for these shared commitments by designing lessons centered on using them to resolve disagreements between different studies (Chinn et al., 2023). The Gradient Tool, a feature of The Argumentation T oolkit, is a scaffold used to rate evidence ranging from high to low quality in a collaborative setting (The Argumentation Toolkit, n.d.). Students are presented with multiple pieces of evidence which they place on the quality of evidence gradien t tool after they justify their rationale for their evidence rating and achieve group consensus. As part of their justification, students may discuss the appropriateness of features like sample  size, sample representativeness, and confounding va riables. The aim is to understand that different approaches and disagreements do not equate to science being untrustworthy. These activities can help students understand how to hold each other accountable while following community guidelines. Fostering discourse around methodological principles.

Fostering a learning environment for evidence evaluation calls for teachers to present students with opportunities to explore and question the evidence they are engaging with. It also means that teachers should become skilled at discourse that supports students’ growth in evidence evaluation. There is accordingly a need for research on how teachers can best support these discussions —how such discussions can be structured, what d iscourse moves are effective, and so on. Students can be encouraged through skillful orchestration of discussion to con sider methodological features that may not be obvious, such as whether a measure used in a class experim ent is really reliable and valid. Surfacing such issues highlights uncertainties in  methodological processes that students can work through by debating alternative solutions and reconciling differences in ideas.  Students designing and conducting experiments in groups can lead to class negotiations about the quality of methods (Ford, 2005; Tang et al., 2023). Teachers can ask problematizing ques tions and provide feedback as students are generating ideas about methodologies which can prompt students to reason at a deeper level for evaluating evidence. Using strategies like directed question ing can facilitate students towards noticing and thinking about specific features related to evidence and its complexities  (Manz & Renga, 2017).  In Manz and Suárez (2018), this occurs when students consider if insects are hiding when making estimates about insect populatio ns. The teacher guides the students to define the term “hiding”  which then leads students to further refine their methods for collecting data. The focus is not on a correct answer as the outcomes can vary depending on what the students ag ree upon rather than the teacher deciding for them. Teachers must recognize when and how to guide students as they bu ild connections between methodological processes and the quality of evidence generated. In general, an important topic for future research is how teachers can best encourage and enable these discussions.

## Discussion

In this short paper, we have elaborated upon the evidence evaluation dimension of the Grasp  of Evidence (GoE) framework by operationalizing guidelines with specific strategies that can tailor instruction to help students learn to evaluate methodological quality. We encourage science instruction to be designed so that students learn to deal with bad as well as good evidence as they learn to navigate the digital world. Learning to evaluate evidence well can be consequential in how students interact with information and make dec isions that impact our society. Whether it is to evaluate how generalizable a claim  is, or how a particular method may yield greater r eliability, discussions and disagreements about methods should be a central component of scientific inquiry. Stude nts candevelop the skills needed to flag claims and be bet ter equipped to ask appropriate questions relating to the methodological processes of a study. The examples f rom the literature that we have shared range from working with students in elementary school to high school. Future research can investigate how evidence evalua tion can be designed for different developmental stages. Given that we focused only on evidence evaluation in this paper, there is also a need to specify guidelines for each of the other dimensions of the GoE framework.
