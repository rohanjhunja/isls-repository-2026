# Feeling Irregularly White: The Role of Emotions in Activist Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Building on learning sciences literature that centers emotions, this paper explores the interplay between learning emotions and learning cross-racial allyship. Research in the learning sciences has often set aside the role of emotions in learning. In recognition that learning to counter White supremacy typically involves a wide range of emotions—from compassion and righteous anger to fear and denial—we explore the affordances of analyzing emotions around activist learning. For this purpose, we analyze a memoir written by Mab Segrest, a White anti-racist activist who learned to become a cross-racial ally with Black activists in the Southern United States. We incorporate affect theory with situated and sociocultural learning theories to conceptualize emotions as both social practices and cultural artifacts. The findings describe how cross-racial allyship learning involves the negotiation of one’s own emotions alongside the emotions in one’s community.

## Introduction

I had begun to feel pretty irregularly White. Klan folks had a term for it: race traitor. Driving in and out of counties with heavy Klan activity, I kept my eye on the rearview mirror, and any time a truck with a Confederate flag license plate passed me, the hair on the back of my neck would rise. (Segrest, 1994, p. 80) Emotions are core to human experience (Ahmed, 2013). As learning is a fundamental human activity, this means that the relationship between learning and emotions requires attention. This is particularly true in learning how to counter social injustice, as learning scientists have begun  to consider the emotional dimensions of learning t o be an activist (see Vea, 2020). Specifically in the context of racism, learning to counter White supremacy (i.e., anti-racism) typically involves a wide range of emotions: from compassion and righteous anger to fear and denial. Understandi ng the complex ways people negotiate this constellation of emotions can shed light on how people learn anti-racism. This paper focuses on the life of Mab Segrest, a W hite anti-racist activist who became a “traitor” to  White supremacy through grassroots organizing in the 1980 s. This work derives from a larger study of how peo ple learn anti-racism. Elsewhere, we have begun developing a methodology for characterizing anti-racism learning, particularly along long timescales (Shah & Hou, in press). Here we focus on the emotional entailments of anti-racism learning— in this case, as Mab learned to become a cross-raci al ally with Black activists in the Southern United  States. Specifically, we draw on situated and sociocultural perspectives on learning to analyze Mab’s shifts from sympathy for the Other, to sympathy with the Other, as well as the myriad of racialized emo tions that followed and expanded beyond empathy (Bonilla-Silva, 2019). The following research question oriented the study: What emotions mediated Mab’s understanding of herself as a White person, and how did they facilitate her learning of cross-racial allyship?

## Discussion & Implications

Our findings contribute to learning sciences research by illustrating the affordances of conceptualizing emotions both as social practices (see Vea, 2020) and as sociocul tural artifacts. We conclude by discussing how a pe rspective that accounts for multiple emotions, as opposed to one-dimensional approaches to racial healing, can lead to better designs for learning racial justice activism.

## Conceptual Framework

Here we explore the interplay of learning emotions and learning cross-racial allyship, with an emphasis on emotions specific to racialized societies (Bonilla-Silva, 2019). We use the term “Whiteness” to describe a form of discourse and ideology, rather than individuals with white skin. In other words, Whiteness denotes “the structural v aluation of [lighter] skin color, which invests it with meaning regarding the overall organization of society” (Leonardo, 2009, p. 92). We examine Mab’s personal and activist journey as she questions the validity of Whiteness and engages in antiracism. Our claims around Mab’s learning attempt to describ e emotions through the perspective of situated and sociocultural learning theories, particularly the c oncepts of social practices  (Lave & Wenger, 1991) and cultural artifacts (Vygotsky, 1978; Wertsch, 1998).  The field of learning sciences has often set aside the role of emotions “through outright exclusion or through ways of thinking that separate emotion as an external force on learning” (Vea, 2020, p. 314). However, addressing emotions is imperative if we want to comprehensively understand learning. For this purpose, we incorporate aspects of affect theory with situated and sociocultural learning theories. Through affect theory, emotions become a lens through which to understand human behaviors (Ahmed, 2013). Drawing on situated learning theory, we conceptualize emotions as “[producing] the effect of surfaces and boundaries of bodies” (Ahmed, 2013, p. 194), thereby acting as social practices t hat bound specific communities together (i.e., comm unities experiencing joy or grief or other emotions). Throu gh Vygotskian sociocultural learning theories, we i ncorporate Ahmed’s (2013) concept of “stickiness”, describing how “the work of emotion involves the ‘sticking’ of  signs to bodies: for example, when others become ‘hateful’, then actions of ‘hate’ are directed against them” (p. 13). By extension, emotions convey how different cultural artifacts “stick” to different racialized bodies. We add here to Vea’s work on viewing emotions as a form of social practice, “of expression/emoting and emotional management” (2020, p. 315), and explore the parallel affordances of viewing emotions as artifacts.

## Method

Our qualitative approach attempts to understand, in terrogate, and deconstruct the emotional dimensions  of lived experiences (Bhattacharya, 2017). We specifically employed methods designed to analyze emotions from written text. By treating a memoir as data, we examined emotions on an individual scale but also connected to the pe ople and historical events shaping a person’s life. We chose Mab’s memoir, Memoir of a Race Traitor (1994), from a collection of 20th-century U.S.-based anti-racist activist memoirs, which was generated from a larger study of how people learn anti-racism (Shah & Hou, in press). Given the necessary role of White people in anti-racist struggle, we were especially curious about Mab’s identification as a “race trait or,” and we analyzed a specific excerpt of the memo ir where the emotions around being a race traitor were prominent (e.g., Mab’s emotions around White individuals and in relation to her mother) (Segrest, 1994, p. 80-82). Our racial positionalities as Han Taiwanese (Hou) and as Brown (Shah) also informed our perspectives around cross-racial allyship. Set in the 1980s when Mab joined North Carolinians Against Racist and Religious Violence (NCARRV) and participated in anti-Klan activity, our selected data describe Mab’s shifting emotions, perspectives, and behaviors as she gradually understood herself as a race traitor. In other words, Mab began to recognize herself (and observe how others recognized her) as a White person who acts against White racial interests. In our analysis, we identified Mab’s emotions by focusing on explicit emotion words used  by Mab (e.g., fear, sorrow, self-hatred, suspicion). While we recognize that memoirists might not fully disclose all that they are feeling, we believe that using in vivo text provided valuable insight into Mab’s emotional landscape.

## Findings

First, we analyze events from Mab’s life through a lens of emotions as social practices, and then we a nalyze other events through a lens of emotions as cultural artifacts. Together, the findings show that while emotions are racialized ICLS2022 Proceedings 1054 © ISLS and situated within racialized societies, emotions evolve alongside a fluidity of racial subjectivitie s (Bonilla-Silva, 2019, p. 5). That is, in Mab’s case, although her emotions were situated in her position as a White lesbian woman, her emotions diverged from White folks around her as she continued her anti-Klan work. Emotions about Whiteness as Social Practices I often found myself hating all white people, including myself. As I took on racism, I also found its effects could be turned on me. The possibility of o vert violence or the reality of subtle ostracism gave me a sense of shared risk, not the same as the dangers faced by my friends of color, but close enough. I began to feel more uneasy around other whites and more at ease around people of color.... Could I find ways to share and appreciate other cultures without mimicking or appropriating them, without denying my continuing white privilege? (Segrest, 1994, p. 80) As Mab continued to participate in anti-Klan activi ty with NCARRV, she began to interpret Whiteness di fferently. Emotionally, she described feeling a growing sense of suspicion towards White strangers, seeing them as threatening and keeping her guard up around them. For example, in restaurants with predominantly White customers, she would sit with her back against the wall and eye White strangers with suspicion (p. 80). As a social practice, her emotion of suspicion described a sense of shared risk with her friends of color and established boundaries between her and White strangers, resonating with the ways that affect “...produces the effect of surfaces and boundaries of bodies” (Ahmed, 2013, p. 194). Mab’s participation in suspicion created distance between herself and White strangers and, by extension, Mab noted feeling more comfortable around Black, Indigenous, and other people of color (BIPOC) rather than White individuals. Overall, we view Mab’s suspicion towards other White people as a social practice central to the NCARRV community, which in turn bound her away from White people in other communities. Emotions about Whiteness as Circulating Artifacts Mab also began to interpret her own Whiteness as a target for self-hatred. She described how her hate for White people began to extend towards herself. Resonating with literature on the “stickiness” of affect (Ahmed, 2013), this newfound self-hatred can be understood in terms of Mab’s app ropriation of cultural artifacts as she grappled wi th viewing Whiteness as something to reconstruct or abolish (Leonardo, 2009). That is, she began wondering if it was possible to acknowledge White privilege while reclaiming her Whiteness. For Mab, Whiteness was no longer something that is benign and ignorable, but rather something that necessitated profound change. As Mab moved about the world as a race traitor, she experienced difficulty finding belonging in either White or BIPOC communities, which led to fears around her  safety and belonging (p. 80). We argue that her fe ar can be viewed as a cultural artifact, made available by Ma b’s position as a race traitor. Further, appropriat ing fear allowed Mab to forge an important connection: with her moth er and her mother’s emotions. Before Mab was born, Mab’s mother had been in a similar situation: she had rec eived a job offer as a registrar to oppose racist voter suppression but decided to turn it down and maintain her family  ties. In reflecting on this life event, Mab’s moth er said, “When people have to choose, they will go with their own race,” (p. 81). This particular experience generated considerable sorrow for Mab’s mother, which she later confided to Mab: “My mother was a womandeeply torn by the racism into which she was born. She shared her sorrow with me, often telling me stories of how she and a friend ha d stopped a Klan rally on the courthouse steps by protesting to the mayor” (p. 81). This sorrow, as a cultural artifact, was available across time and between bodies (see Figure 1). Specifically, when Mab began to feel the fear of not belonging to either White or BIPOC communities, she recalled her mother’s similar experiences, which took place years ago. She said, “I had inherited from my mother an awareness of the  deep schisms in our culture, and I suspected that it was her attempts tonegotiate them that eventually destroyed her. When push came to shove, could I make a different choice?” (p. 82). As Mab negotiated with her fears and her mother’s sorrows, she wondered if she could make a different choice ICLS2022 Proceedings 1055 © ISLS from her mother. In a certain sense, Mab’s appropri ating the artifact of her mother’s sorrow eventuall y led to a participation shift in social practices, as she fur thered her commitment towards anti-racism in compar ison to her mother.

## Discussion

This study has explored the affordances of analyzin g emotions around activist learning as both social practices and cultural artifacts. We concur with Vea’s (2020) pri or work highlighting the value of analyzing emotion s as social practices. We build on that work by bringing into focus the possibility of examining emotions as artifacts. In particular, we have described how emotions (as cultural artifacts) pass between bodies, traverse time, and shift one's participation in social practices. In relation to cross-racial allyship and anti-racism, this work also shows how such learning involves the negotiation of emotions beyond empathy and the negotiation of one’s own and one’s community’s emotions. In recognition of the role of emotions, we raise several questions for the learning sciences community. With respect to theory, what might an integrated theory that incorp orates emotions into learning theories look like? W ith respect to methodology, when should we choose to examine emotions as artifacts or as social practices? With respect to practice, how do we respond to these shifting emotions in order to create a more supportive environment for anti-racism learning? We believe further research into the role of emotio ns would be critical to promote better designs for supporting the learning of anti-racism.
