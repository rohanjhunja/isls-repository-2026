# Breaking Silos: Promoting Systems Thinking through Multi-Sector Environmental Education Partnerships

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This conceptual paper explores the integration of Systems Thinking (ST) into Environmental Education (EE) to address interconnected socio-environmental challenges, drawing on theories of critical pedagogy, Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT), and Situated Learning Theory (SLT). It argues that promoting environmental stewardship necessitates a transdisciplinary and systemic approach beyond fragmented and siloed practices. Using Engeström’s (2009) Activity Theory, the research frames EE programs across public, private, and civil society sectors as activity systems and investigates how their interactions can create new collective activity systems through multi-sector partnerships (MSPs). These partnerships are analyzed through the lens of boundary objects emphasizing collaboration, shared goals, and expansive learning. The paper proposes viewing different sectors that implement EE programs as collective activity systems to facilitate the co-construction of local EE programs that are more systems-thinking oriented approaches.

## Background

Systems thinking: The basis of critical consciousness Environmental issues and climate crises require sys tems-level changes. While individual actions are necessa ry, they cannot keep pace with the rapidly escalating e cological problems. Beyond individual consciousness  and actions, interconnected socio-environmental problems need more profound and broader colle ctive diverse understandings and community actions. Critical peda gogies, particularly ecopedagogy, reinvented by Gre g Misiaszek and Carlos Torres (2019) in their publication on the missing chapter of Paulo Freire’s (1990) Pedagogy of the Oppressed  book, introduce a new lens of critical Environmenta l Education (EE) that highlights the interconnectedness of social and ecological violenc e/injustices (Misiaszek, 2023). The ecopedagogical goals integrate objectives from already fragmented EE that mainly work in silos and often criticize each other. With time, EE is integrating and requiring other in terconnected and complex literacies. These literaci es are directly and indirectly discussed and emphasize d in ecopedagogical goals. Referring to Misiaszek (2023), Chaparian (2024) unpacked these literacies  to include– but not limited to – engaging with socio-environmental and global justice issues through ‘sociohistorical analysis’ and ‘aimed at ending histories of oppress ion and unsustainability’ (p. 42) (critical sociohistorical literacy),‘overcoming the fear of liberation and challenging  the dogma of ideological frames’ (p. 90) (critical literacy), engaging in ‘authentic freedom, participatory citizenship (from local to global to planetary), and participat ory democracy’ (civic literacy) (p. 115), critically analyzing media and postdigitalism (technology, media, and information literacy) (p. 113), and developing a nuanced understanding of local to global political dynamics  to comprehend power structures, globalization, opp ression, and colonization (political literacy) (p. 23), ‘challenging Development ideologies’ (p.  40), understanding the impacts of global economics on local communities and natural environments (global economic literacy) (p. 26), countering epistemicide (epistemic literacy), ‘widening the “ethics of human solidarity,”’ also implying planetary solidarity (p. 121), and acknowledging the denial o f the colonized histories and cultures (ethical and cultural literacy). The ecopedagogical community values the interconnected nature of its objectives and acknowledges the necessity of diverse literacies to accomplish them. As relationships, technology, and employment, institutions and communities grow more intricate and interrelated, a ddressing societal and environmental challenges bec omes more intertwined in this complex network of interdependencies (Richmond, 1991). A closer analysis of ecopedagogical goals reveals that critical consciousness requires Systems Thinking (ST). “Systems Thinking is a discipline for seeing who les. It is a framework for seeing interrelationship s rather than things, for seeing ‘patterns of change’ rather than static ‘snapshots’ (Senge, 2006). However, there is no clear understanding of how ST  is described and understoo d, particularly in EE. Some models from systems the ories, including the compass model (AtKisson, 1999), icebe rg model (Gurdur  and Torngren (2018), and social-ecological systems (Fischer et al. (2015), have bee n adopted for use in EE settings. Also, reductionis t and linear approaches are often used to describe ST in EE (Cha ndler & Boutilier, 1992; Richmond, 1991; Squires et al., 2011; Stave & Hopper, 2007; Sweeney & Sterman, 2000). That includes listing some skills and practices a nd usually representing ecological cycles as systems.

Considering the various literacies addressed throug h ecopedagogical goals, this paper  defines ST as enhancing the understanding of environmental issues  by exploring their connections to broader regional  and global concerns and emphasizing how environmental issues intertwine with social, political, historical, economic, scientific, health, technological, and media dimensions at both local a nd global levels. ST is viewed as dynamic, complex, and interconnected: "Systems thinking is a  system of thinking about systems" (Arnold & Wade, 2015, p. 670).

The researcher is developing a framework (see Figur e 1) that clarifies what ST in EE involves. This framework combines various literacies emphasized by  ecopedagogical efforts and is based on my experien ces piloting workshop activities with practitioners in  EE. This ST framework in EE highlights eight interc onnected dimensions: Society & Community, Politics & History, Environmental, Media & Communications, Health, Science, Technology & Energy, and Economy. Each dim ension corresponds to specific literacies re flecting ecopedagogical goals, including cultural, social, and ethical literacies; political and legal literacies; environmental and climate literacies; media literacy; health and nutritional literacy; scientific literacy; digital and energy literacies; and financial literacy. Together, these dimensions en hance learners’ understanding of the local-toglobal systems that shape environmental issues and promote informed and ethical engagement with the world. For example, it is common for scho ols to focus on nature-based and science education, for organizations and civil society groups to focus on civic engagement and activism for policy changes, a nd for governmental agencies to aim to promote individual responsibilities like waste management and energy consumption reduction.

Developing various types of literacy and promoting ST in environmental issues is challenging for organizations with specific objectives and  limited tools. This often leads to duplication of efforts among stakeholders. Effective sustainability in EE requires an interdisciplinary and collaborative approach with a clear division of labor. Each stakeholder’s focus should be viewed not as conflict but as an opportunity for partnership, allowing for differentiated actions among participa nts (Engeström, 2009). This paper discusses how MSP s enhance the understanding of ST in EE and establish principles for more systems-oriented approaches. Arguments and purpose ST provides a framework to teach ecological systems while explicitly highlighting the entangled nature of human systems, such as Bourdieu’s (1977) social structures and environmental systems, fostering critical consciousness. Furthermore, socio-environmental transformation becomes more achievable when repr esentatives from environmental literacy programs, including program designers, managers, and coordinators, across various sectors of the community share a collective and systemic vi sion and mission. As noted by Misiasek (2015), “transdisciplinary educational methods allow for mo re holistic understandings of the world, breaking away from linear and singular approaches that delve into valu es, ethics, and philosophical perspectives” (p. 289). Without transdisciplinary and systemic approaches, ‘harms t o society and nature [will continue] because of our  partially fragmented and limited visions and assumptions’ (Max-Neef, 2005, p. 16). This conceptual paper argues to gather insights fro m representatives from across sectors, understandin g their motivations, risks, benefits, barriers, and roles in a multi-stakeholder partnership (MSP) (Porter et al., 2011). First, it is essential to study the perspectives on the purpose, location, and implementation of an environmental literacy program to promote ST about local, regional, and gl obal environmental issues.  Analyzing how stakehold ers' roles, tools, labor, objects, and actors emerge to successfully implement the environmental literacy program is crucial. Furthermore, the paper argues the importance of engaging partner representatives in collaborative spaces to construct knowledge, integrate diverse perspecti ves, and develop a robust, systems-oriented environmental literacy framework, ensuring a comprehensive approa ch to designing ST and addressing local to global environmental challenges.

## Theoretical framework

This conceptual paper emerges theories from sociocu ltural theories focusing on Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) (Engeström, 2009), and Situated Learn ing Theory (Lave & Wegner, 1991). Moreover, it argu es that CHAT provides a powerful lens for analyzing and designing MSPs by focusing on the interconnectedness of activity systems and their potential for transformative collaboration. This perspective helps identify the historical and structural dynamics that shape how organization s develop their goals  and programs. Therefore, a CHAT enables a deeper understanding of how diverse organ izations can align their efforts to create shared g oals. Negotiating meaning, redesigning and reinventing pr actices, and co-constructing knowledge in CoPs provide opportunities for expansive learning experiences and foster the initiation and development of interconnected activity systems capable of addressing complex socio-environmental challenges. Partners as activity systems The researcher proposes using the CHAT model to und erstand and “profile” each EE organization and its programs. Beyond learning about the subjects, media ting tools, artifacts, rules, division of labor, ob jects, and outcomes of these organizations and programs, the r esearch uses the activity theory model to explore h ow these activity systems evolved historically, including th eir material and institutional structures. Through analyzing tensions and contradictions at the nodes of the act ivity system, can gain valuable insight into how organizations develop their institutional logic and programs.

Considering non-homogenous aspects of activity systems, the current  EE programs “contain sediments of earlier historical nodes and buds of shoots for its possible future” (Engeström,1993, p. 68). Study ing the activities within the activity systems supports  understanding “the relevance of intention and human  will.” Therefore, treating each partner as an activity system has the potential to illuminate the evolution of its objects and intentions and to study what dimensions of ST and l iteracies are associated  with that partner organization’s programs.

Activity systems are open systems, and their latera l interplay with other activity systems makes possible surprising empowerments and surges of agency. This indicates that it is helpful to extend units of ana lysis to encompass interactions between neighboring activity  systems that seem to offer the potential for expan sion and new forms of agency” (Engeström, 2009, p. 31). Leavin g sectors and activity systems behind in the analys is will hinder understanding and developing ST.

Engeström emphasizes examining activity systems ins tead of just implementation, highlighting the importance of expansion learning (2009, p.31). Whil e implementing climate literacy programs is crucial, this research focuses on the learning that occurs as pa rtners discuss and reflect on their activity system s and propose alternative methods. In our interconnected world, e xamining two interdependent activity systems can be  a useful unit of analysis (p. 24, Engeström 2009, citing Eng eström, 2001). To grasp ST in EE, this paper encour ages viewing EE programs from public, private, and civil society as interdependent activity systems. The emerging collective activity system as a multi-sector partnership The object of the collective activity system is to bring the separate activity systems or organizations from different sectors together to co-develop outlines or principles for more ST-oriented approaches in EE. While engaging in that practice, we understand what ST is or constitutes. This is similar to En geström's idea that the object directs the activity, but the activity also changes objects (Arnseth, 2008, p. 292). Furthermore, we create the opportunity for the representatives of the activity systems to  reflect on their actions and practices and transfor m them. Conflicts and contradictions in this new emerging a ctivity system are potential catalysts for social l earning and transformation. The participants are invited to que stion, analyze, and redesign pr actices, resulting in expansive learning in which representatives not only adapt bu t also change the structure of the activity itself.  This third space encourages representatives to innovate and reconsider their existing practices. The initiation of a new multi-sector partnership coincides with the development o f a collective activity system. Both evolve to strengthen relationships, al ign missions, and integrate diverse value framework s, emphasizing alignment across different cultural sys tems (Austin, 2000). Through boundary crossing and expansive learning, partners generate collaborative  tools and values, fostering long-term transformation in environmental education systems.

A collaborative activity system that engages partners in designing environmental literacy programs aims for expansive transformation, allowing the communit y to redefine its objectives and methodologies with  new tools (Engeström, 2009). Activities operate as open  systems interconnected through networks around sha red goals. The ambitious nature of the environmental li teracy program necessitates collaboration across va rious organizations, recognizing that while they may addr ess similar programs, the meanings and motivations behind them candiffer significantly (Engeström, 2009).

The foundational assumption in the expansive cycle is that learners' activity systems can confront and resolve contradictions to clarify their objectives and possible actions (Engeström, 2009, p. 26). Simi larly, partnerships and networks must navigate an initiation phase where participants align their interests and values to create a shared goal. This paper suggests using col lective activity systems to initiate partnerships a nd networks in EE. By applying the activity model as a conceptual tool, we can analyze each partner’s activities and design a literacy program that promotes scientific thinking regarding local environmental issues, fostering col laboration among various networks. As Engeström (2009, p. 24) notes, constructing a shared object and common mo tive among differing perspectives is a difficult but essential task. This approach could reduce fragmentation in EE and encourage cooperation among organizations with similar objectives. The emerging collective activity system Different activity systems can merge into collective activity systems when their distinct elements, such as goals, tools, rules, and community dynamics (boundary obje cts that can be flexible enough to adapt to the nee ds of the activity system and serve as connection points and facilitate collaboration), in teract and align around a shared object. This process often begins with recognizing contradictions and tensions within or between systems, which create the need for collaboration, networking, or p artnership. Through boundary crossing, where shared  tools, concepts, and artifacts facilitate communication, the researcher's involvement (in this case, acting like a boundary spanner) mediates between systems by creating colla boration, and the systems start to int egrate their practices. As they work towards a shared object, they may reconceptualize or develop new tools and rules and redefine their division of labor to align with the merged activity. Over time, this collaboration fosters the emergen ce of a new community/network/partnership with shared norms, practices, and collective identity, resulting in a fully formed collective activity system that integrates and tran scends the original systems. This dynamic transform ation is driven by resolving contradictions, c reating shared frameworks (in this case, a framewor k for ST in EE), and aligning efforts around common goals.

In this emerging collective activity system, multi-sector partnerships among EE organizations collaborate to design programs that promote ST for adult and public audiences. The subjects, including  program managers, designers, and coordinators from public, private, and civil society sectors, bring diverse expertise, and labor is divided accordingly. Mediating tools inclu de an ST framework for EE, communication platforms,  and shared educational materials. The object shifts toward ST and systemic change, guided by newly established rules and collaborative norms similar to initiating multi-sector partnerships. Outcomes include strengthened local partnerships, increased knowledge-sharing, and deeper critical consciousness. Artifac ts like past and current EE programs are anchors for understanding each organization’s contributions and approaches.

## Conclusion

This paper emphasizes the need for an ST framework in EE to address interconnected socio-environmental challenges. Grounded in critical pedagogies and CHAT, it advocates for integrating diverse literacies to promote consciousness and collective action. Lea rning occurs through collaborative activity systems  involving multiple partners, which helps tackle complex issues. The ro le of multi-sector partnerships and third spaces for transformative collaboration, alongside effective f rameworks for ST in EE, is crucial for sharing expe rtise. By fostering collaborative learning and transdisciplin ary curricula and building the capacity of educator s and stakeholders, we can effectively reduce the knowledge-action gap and advance sustainability initiatives.
