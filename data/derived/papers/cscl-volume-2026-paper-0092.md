# Rise Above State-of-the-Art: Designing New Possibilities of GenAI for Knowledge Building

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium presents cutting-edge research that explores how Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) can be leveraged to enrich human-AI collaboration in CSCL for Knowledge Building (KB). It challenges prevailing educational paradigms of ‘learning from AI’ and instead it foregrounds collaborative creation, emphasizing how GenAI can be harnessed to enrich epistemic agency empowering learners in knowledge building. Building on four decades of KB research, this symposium aims to enrich both the conceptualization and design of human-AI collaboration for knowledge advancement. Seven interrelated contributions demonstrate emergent KB-AI design approaches, including dialogic approaches that engage learners in collaborative dialogue with AI, and integrated approaches that embed AI affordances into knowledge building environments. These designs spandiverse contexts from doctoral interdisciplinary research to K-12 classrooms. Grounded in a design-based approach, this symposium positions these contributions as conceptual artifacts for design advancement and envisions new KB-AI synergies that enable emerging forms of CSCL and knowledge creation.

Since the public release of ChatGPT in late 2022, GenAI has been increasingly debated and adopted across educational contexts. Definitions typically emphasize GenAI’s capacity to produce synthetic content via generative modeling (Peñalvo & Ingelmo, 2023), but the educational question is not merely what it generates; it is how we conceptualize GenAI in relation to learning and the advancement of knowledge. Tan (2024) conceptualizes GenAI in education into three areas: learning about AI (GenAI literacy), lear ning from GenAI (GenAI as tutor), and learning with GenAI (use of GenAI as partner).  While current educational research and practice has focused on learning from GenAI, such as a personal tutor and students’ personalized learning (e.g., 577 Baillifard et al., 2025), there are relatively less studies which explore on learning with GenAI focusing on collaboration (Zheng et al., 2024), and even fewer studies on knowledge building with GenAI (Chen et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023). This symposium focuses on CSCL, examining the role of GenAI in idea improvement and knowledge creation within knowledge building environments. Early work by Scardamalia and Bereiter (1987) showed that expert writers engage in knowledge transforming—an iterative process of refining and restructuring ideas to achieve coherence and generate new understanding. Writing, in this sense, becomes a means of knowledge creation rather than simple communication, a view upon which CSCL and KB research on collective inquiry and idea improvement is built. Extending this to the collective level, KB conceptualizes knowledge building as idea improvement through shared cognitive responsibility occurring through idea-centered knowledge building discourse (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). Recent scholarship on essays in a time of GenAI echoes these foundations, positioning writing as an epistemic act through which students build, test, and refine ideas (Corbin et al., 2025). Emerging CSCL research investigates GenAI as a collaborative agent that supports co-construction of knowledge, reflection, and shared inquiry. Cress and Kimmerle (2023) argue that meaningful learning can occur through dialogue between humans and AI systems, where information is mutually exchanged and challenged to drive knowledge advancement. Empirical studies suggest that the integration of GenAI into collaborative digital environments can enhance real-time communication, task regulation, and collective me an-making (Wei et al., 2025). Conceptual work further advances this discussion by proposing frameworks which outline escalating levels of AI agency—from  adaptive instrument to peer collaborator—grounded  in CSCL work (Yan et al., 2024). Primarily, the dominant narrative of ‘learning from AI’ is problematic as it reinforces the ‘learning as acquisition’ and ‘mind as a container’ paradigms in education (Bereiter, 2002). Researchers have expressed concerns that a narrow focus on AI applications will result in “a type of digital duct tape to hold together an obsolete industrial-era educational s ystem, automating outdated instruction and assessment” (Dede, 2023, p.35). Re cent work in CSCL on GenAI has pointed to the importance of designing generative AI for collaborative learning (Anderson et al., 2025; Hernández -Leo et al., 2025). This symposium seeks to build on current work on Generative AI for collaboration to examine how AI can contribute to the cognitive and social dynamics of collaborative knowledge building and knowledge creation.

Theoretical perspective Knowledge Building (KB) transforms education from a model of knowledge transmission to engaging students directly in how knowledge in the world advances, with students taking collective responsibility for creating and improving ideas of value to their community (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). Knowledge Building moves beyond constructivist approaches by engaging all students as legitimate members of a knowledge-creating community, in which ideas are treated as real, improvable, conceptual artifacts that, through collective work, can be tested, refined and built upon to advance the state of knowledge in the community and in the world (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2010). A set of KB principles including epistemic agency, improvable ideas, idea diversity, community knowledge is fundamental for guiding KB research and design (Scardamalia, 2002). This principle-based approach is inherently dynamic and demands continuous adaptation to the evolving landscape of knowledge frontiers. Knowledge Builders attempt to work at the cutting edge of knowledge, driving innovation and fostering a culture of collective cognitive responsibility (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). There is a limited but growing body of literature exploring the intersection of KB and GenAI. For example, Lee et al. (2023) explored the use of GenAI for sustaining student discourse. The authors proposed that GenAI can support Knowledge Building by identifying and explaining authentic problems, summarizing ideas, synthesizing new perspectives, and prompting diverse viewpoints, suggesting improvements, connecting ideas to broader contexts, guiding students in using authoritative sources, and facilitating reflection. A study by Chen et al.

(2023) involved interviewing high school students who integrate GenAI into Knowledge Building in a “World of

Religions” course. Student interviews showed that GenAI was valued for its efficiency in information search, inspiring new ideas, promoting collaboration, and providing diverse perspectives. However, opinions on its support for creativity were mixed, with some students finding it limited to fact-based information while others appreciated its ability to spark creativity. This suggests the need to examine and develop KB norms and ethics for AI use in these open environments, where deeper  inquiry remains important. While these initial studies show the promise of GenAI in Knowledge Building contexts, there are also significant challenges. One such challenge is the potential compromise of student epistemic agency, defined as “students being positioned with, perceiving, and acting on, opportunities to shape the knowledge building work in their classroom community” (Miller et al., 2017, p. 1058). Since GenAI can generate seemingly convincing learning artifacts, there is a growin g concern that students may forge their epistemic agency and use GenAI as a shortcut instead of engaging in the intellectual heavy lifting themselves.  Tan (personal communication, 2025) 578 illustrated this with a “worst case scenario”, where the entire knowledge building process becomes artificial, with one student presenting an AI-generated idea, another student using AI to build on that idea, and a third student evaluating the idea using AI. While this scenario highlights potential risks, it also points toward the emergence of new forms of epistemic agency in the age of  AI. Chen  (2025, p. 2) proposes viewing “AI as epistemic infrastructure” that fundamentally changes “how knowledge is  created, validated, and shared in educational contexts.” In this conception, AI is not merely an add-on to human action but reshapes what we do and redefines how knowledge is produced. Chen describes AI systems as “active participants in knowledge co-creation” and highlights the need for “how new forms of epistemic agency emerge within human-AI ecologies” (p. 9). Key questions This symposium aims to address the key challenge of designing and examining GenAI for collaborative knowledge building. The following three key questions will guide our collective inquiry and idea advancement:

1. How can GenAI tools, systems, infrastructure  be designed to foreground epistemic agency and KB

principles?

2. What new knowledge building practices become possible through thoughtful design and integration of

3. What are the emerging design approaches to leveraging GenAI for Knowledge Building and what new

## Significance of the symposium

This symposium comes at a critical juncture of rapid advancement of GenAI and growing demands for knowledge creation, addressing a gap in current educational research  that has largely focused  on learning from Generative AI for knowledge acquisition and individual cognition. Situated in current CSCL work on GenAI, this symposium is premised on well-established KB foundational theories and design-based research for enriching ongoing CSCL academic discourse. In an age of information overload and rapid advances in GenAI, education urgently needs to move beyond  knowledge transmission toward collaboration and empowerment of  learners as creators of knowledge. These contributions build on current work on GenAI for collaborative inquiry (Cress et al., 2023), advancing AI as a partner in  creative knowledge work. Contributions are theoretically focused and align with a variety of methodologies and prototypes of AI tools, with practical classroom examples. We showcase the cuttingedge AI-enhanced design prototypes that foreground KB theories and principles across contexts, including higher education, K-12 classrooms, and professional development. These diverse but coherent designs have significant implications for CSCL theory, pedagogy and technology, suggesting ways to move beyond simply using AI as “digital duct tape” for existing educational approaches. They show how AI can support collaborative inquiry and genuine transformation of classrooms into knowledge creating communities (Dede, 2023). The diversity of perspectives and contexts represented in the symposium reflects the global effort in advancing AI for KB. Together, contributions of this s ymposium collectively shed light on how AI can be designed and integrated to enhance knowledge building practices and envision new forms of knowledge creation through dynamic and evolving human-AI partnerships. CraftPad: A Workspace for Teachers to Sketch and Refine Knowledge Building Designs Jiayu Cheng, Vivian Leung, Yue Zhao, Chen Wang, Bodong Chen Theoretical background: Designing effective environments for knowledge building represents a sophisticated craft that demands deep understanding of KB principles, careful balancing of structure and emergence,  and a design-oriented mindset. While generative AI has gained popularity in supporting teacher lesson planning, current systems—even the most advanced ones —operate within rigid templates that constrain teacher agency (Cheng et al., 2025). These limitations prevent educators from engaging in the skilled, iterative actions essential to the craft of teaching (Chen, 2025). Our work adopts an alternative approach that positions teachers as designers (Ma & Scardamalia, 2022) rather than mere end-users of AI tools. This perspective necessitates “meta-design”— designing for design itself —which empowers educators to configure and adapt tools in service of their professional craft and educational aspirations. By centering teachers’ expertise and creative agency, we aim to develop AI-enhanced environments that support the complex, nuanced work of knowledge building design. Context, methods and design of GenAI: In response to the limitations of current AI systems for teachers, we designed CraftPad as a flexible workspace that centers teachers’ expertise and creative agency. CraftPad features: (1) an infinite canvas (similar to Knowledge Forum) providing space for open-ended ideation and flexible organization; (2) an embedded prompt library informed by KB principles to guide various AI generation tasks in support of teacher design; and (3) a flexible workflow enabling teachers to quickly initiate designs, iteratively refine specific components with their professional input, and compile design artifacts (e.g., lesson designs) that incorporate AI-generated content while preserving the primacy of teacher judgment. This approach embodies our meta-design philosophy by s upporting teachers as designers rather than mere consumers of AI outputs. effort. Early design iterations have shown emerging insights into how CraftPad can support teachers’ epistemic agency in AI-assisted lesson design. Teachers are beginning to design lessons using KB principles also taking up 581 more agency not just focusing on the output --  These observations are guiding the next phase of refinement and empirical testing. Education is fundamentally an epistemic endeavor that requires preserving human judgment when introducing AI for epistemic operations such as lesson plan generation (Chen, 2025). Building on our metadesign approach, CraftPad represents a preliminary attempt to support teachers in designing and iteratively refining their ideas. Its core mission aligns with our perspective by re-centering teacher professional expertise while positioning AI in a supportive role that augments rather than replaces teacher design capabilities. We are planning user studies to evaluate CraftPad’s viability in practical settings and to further refine our approach to teacher-AI collaboration in knowledge building design.

A separate initiative explored a wider range of GenAI applications in a knowledge-building environment within a graduate-level education course. A study of practices across the disciplines Adiel Benjamin, Yotam Hod Theoretical background: This research explores how expert knowledge builders use Generative AI (GenAI) as a way to inform and refine established knowledge building design principles (Chen & Hong, 2016; Chan & van Aalst, 2018; Cohen & Hod, 2021; Scardamalia, 2002; Zhang et al., 2011). As GenAI becomes increasingly integral across fields, understanding its role in knowledge creation is crucial for educational researchers aiming to design human-centered, authentic learning environments that address contemporary challenges. By examining expert GenAI  practices, this  study seeks  to  align  educational  strategies  with  real-world knowledge building approaches, equipping educational systems to meet the demands of an AI-integrated future while emphasizing human elements of learning.

Context and methods: This study addresses two key questions: (1) How do expert knowledge  builders intentionally use GenAI to advance collective knowledge in their field? (2) How do these practices align with established knowledge building design principles? Using a grounded theoretical approach (Charmaz, 2008), we conducted semi-structured interviews that were video recorded and screen captured with 7 experts from diverse disciplines, including the humanities, social and natural sciences, and industry. Part icipants demonstrated their GenAI use, detailing their rationale, tools, and methods employed in knowledge building tasks. For this symposium, we focus on the second research question to advance discussion about the knowledge building principles. From this analysis, six design principles emerged as particularly salient: Improvable Ideas, Epistemic Agency, Collective Responsibility, Democratizing Knowledge, Idea Diversity, and Pervasive Knowledge Building. improvable ideas. Participants iteratively refined their work using GenAI rather than seeking a single, final answer-this iterative process was deeply integrated with epistemic agency, as experts were not passive users; they actively decided when and how to use GenAI, critically evaluated its outputs, and asserted human responsibility. By probing the system’s limits and cross-checking information, they affirmed the importance of discernment in AI-augmented work and demonstrated how users must own their inquiry trajectories. Principles related to community knowledge were also evident, though with some nuance. Collective responsibility emerged in collaborative settings where GenAI acted as a social mediator to stimulate group democratizing knowledge was powerfully embodied by a participant with dysgraphia who described GenAI as an enabling technology that allowed him to communicate ideas more fluently, thereby reducing barriers to academic participation. GenAI also supported idea diversity, acting as a “consultant” that could offer alternative perspectives. However, experts noted this required careful prompting, as GenAI could otherwise reinforce dominant narratives, highlighting the need for critical awareness from the user. Finally, the findings aligned with the principle of pervasive knowledge building, which holds that knowledge creation permeates life across all contexts. Participants frequently drew connections between personal and professional GenAI experimentation. One noted, “when I use it in an everyday way I see that as an opportunity to think about how I can use that in a professional way.” This cross-pollination of inquiry demonstrates how GenAI supports knowledge building as a continuous, lived activity, making the tool a shared epistemic resource that enables more equitable and distributed pathways for innovation and discourse. Implications: This study shows th at KB design prin ciples are enacted through an expanded range of practices (Zhang et al., 2011). For example, expert knowledge builders used GenAI not just to improve ideas, but to plan and streamline workflows, automate tasks, and test knowledge through code and simulation. These activities are not auxiliary, but essential in optimizing KB principles to a GenAI-enhanced context. To remain relevant, KB must evolve to acknowledge AI not only as a tool but as a co-actor in the community. Expanding KB pr inciples in light of experts’ GenAI practices responds to the call for designing AI practices that serve meaningful human go als  (OECD, 2025). As one of the participants explained, “These tools actually make a good researcher even better… but the usage needs to be educated, smart, and appropriate”. Generative Ideas (G-Ideas): Configuring Human-AI Partnerships for Knowledge Building Jianwei Zhang, Yanqing Sun, Sudarshan Kammu, Thomas Underwood, Mei-Hwa Chen Theoretical background:  While GenAI can be  used to enhance student performance in various cognitive  tasks such as problem  solving and writing,  student  reliance on external  AI  support risks shortcutting  th e effortful 583 cognitive processes required for high-quality thinking, causing “cognitive debt” over time (Kosmyna et al., 2025). The Generative Ideas (G-Ideas) project aims to design productive human-AI interaction that fosters generative thinking processes for knowledge building. Students take on high-level agency for advancing ideas through progressive inquiry with GenAI. Our design builds on insights gained from how professional researchers interact with AI agents while working with their team members to solve problems and create knowledge (Anantrasirichai & Bull, 2022; Crimaldi & Leonelli, 2023; Dell’Acqua et al., 2023; Gupta et al., 2023; O’Neill et al.,  2022). Knowledge builders work as intellectual stewards to orchestrate human and AI-based processes for continual idea improvement in a dynamically changing context (Zhang, 2026). Design innovation:  Generative Ideas (G-Ideas) was designed to enhance student-driven knowledge building featuring idea-centered inquiry and progressive discourse (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006; van Aalst, 2009; Zhang et al., 2022). G-Ideas integrates peer collaboration with AI interaction. It includes (a) peer collaboration spaces for students’ collaborative discourse and (b) AI interaction spaces (threads) where students— individually or as a group --interact with GenAI agents. The spaces of student discussion and student-AI interaction are interconnected, with options for selecting/copying information from one space to the other. Information from student discussions is used as context data to inform GenAI processing while the output from GenAI can be used to enrich students’ discussions. Students’ interactions with GenAI are scaffolded to achieve various epistemic needs, such as to assist problem finding, idea generation and improvement, research writing, and team reflection. Teachers can create and customize scaffolds —organize d as workflows and think types —to guide student interaction with AI, prioritizing students’ ideas as the focus of the interaction. For example, when taking the choice of idea generation and improvement, G-Ideas first prompts students to generate initial wonderings and tentative ideas and then connects students with the AI agent to improve the ideas while uncovering deeper issues. For transparency and accountability, the histories of student-AI interactions are shared with their teacher. Students can also choose to share the records with peers as a resource to enrich their knowledge  work. processes through design-based research among high school and college classes. In each context, students work as collaborative teams to conduct research and design to address authentic challenges. Data collection traces student engagement in the research processes supported by their interaction with GenAI. Student learning outcomes are evaluated using teacher-created assessments. without using AI, 2) using AI without reflective guidance, and 3) using AI with the reflective guidance provided by G-Ideas. Students under condition 2 using AI outperformed those of condition 1 in their project work during the class, but they scored lower in the final exam (conducted without AI help). Implications: G-Idea design and research showcase configurations of student-AI partnerships for addressing different epistemic needs in knowledge building. Depending on the roles for GenAI to play in knowledge building, human-AI partnerships may be configured in different ways, ranging from outsourcing welldefined work components (e.g., notetaking, artefact generation) to GenAI, to consulting GenAI about the knowledge and needs of a field, and collaborating with GenAI as a co-inquirer to seek continual idea advancement. As a guiding principle, it is essential to keep student-generated ideas at the center and support students’ reflective judgment for co-structuring creative knowledge practices performed by human and AI systems for continual knowledge advancement, personal growth, and community flourishing (Zhang, 2026). Leveraging large language models as a teacher learning companion for knowledge building Seng Chee Tan, Yee Yin Tan, Chew Lee Teo, Guangji Yuan Theoretical background: The design of AI in this study is aligned to Gao et al.’s (2025) concept of human-centred human-AI collaboration (HCHAC) in which AI systems are designed to augment human capabilities  while respecting human autonomy and values. Fostering Scientific Storytelling through Student–AI Knowledge Building Collaboration in Primary Science Xueqi Feng, Carol Chan Theoretical background: Scientific storytelling has been conceptualized as an epistemic practice that supports learners in organizing knowledge, constructing causal explanations, and narrating inquiry through disciplinary reasoning (Avraamidou & Osborne, 2009). Grounded in models  of how scientific communities advance knowledge, KB emphasizes progressive discourse and shared epistemic agency, where learners collaboratively pose questions, improve ideas, and construct explanations (Bereiter et al., 1997). As an emerging epistemic partner, GenAI can engage students in dialogic interactions that prompt elaboration, raise epistemic questions, and encourage reflection (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023). In this case, GenAI amplifies learners’  epistemic agency, contributing as a co-participant in KB discourse.

Design: This study implemented a KB environment in which a GenAI scaffolds were embedded as an epistemic collaborator to support scientific storytelling in primary science. Students co-constructed an epistemic trajectory—identifying  phenomena, formulating problems, building explanations, seeking evidence, and engaging in theoretical discourse—and engaged in sustained inquiry on Knowledge Forum. GenAI played a dynamic role across phases: supporting conceptual exploration during collaborative discourse, assisting experimental design in hands-on investigations, and facilitating narrative refinement through reflection. In the final phase, students co-developed criteria for high-quality scientific stories and used GenAI to iteratively improve their work. investigating natural phenomena including earthquakes, tsunamis, and volcanic eruptions. Data sources included 585 pre-and post-scientific stories, domain understanding tests, student Knowledge Forum notes and AI interactions, and classroom artifacts. domain understanding, and the depth of their KB discourse. Analyses indicated that the quality of students’ contributions in KB and AI dialogues significantly predicted storytelling performance, which in turn predicted gains in domain understanding. These findings suggest a mediating role of storytelling in linking collaborative discourse with domain learning. Qualitative analysis identified four themes that  supported the emergence of scientific storytelling including epistemic co-design, dialogic augmentation, evidence grounding, and discursive refinement.

Implications: This study introduces a generative model of human– AI collaboration that positions AI as an epistemic collaborator in student-driven inquiry. The findings contribute to the design of AI-mediated, inquirybased learning environments that promote deeper scientific understanding and epistemic agency through scientific storytelling.

Symposium structure This symposium uses a structured poster session format designed to foster collective knowledge advancement and interactive engagement with seven innovative KB-AI designs. Following introduction on problem framing (5 minutes) and short presentation of studies (3 minutes each), participants will move freely among posters (supported with artefacts and tools on notebooks) engaging with research teams in conversations about their design innovations and they can post their views on poster boards / online platforms (40 minutes). The symposium will conclude with the commentary from discussants and dialogue among participants (20 minutes). Rather than treating these as individual research projects, we invite participants to approach them as interconnected improvable conceptual artifacts – they are encouraged to interact and engage in meta-design discourse to rise above existing design approaches and envision new KB-AI synergies that not only enhance existing KB practices but also enable new forms of knowledge creation. Participants are encouraged to consider these questions:

1) What insights emerge when you consider these artifacts as a whole?

2) What is missing when you look at the collection? What new design spaces open up?

3) What new KB-AI possibilities become visible when you consider any single approach or rise-above

combining different approaches?

4) What new forms of epistemic agency within human-AI ecologies emerge from the AI-KB design space?

The session concludes with commentary from Dr. Marlene Scardamalia and Dr. Carl Bereiter, who pioneered Knowledge Forum for CSCL and conceptualized Knowledge Building as knowledge creation advancing the idea of students as knowledge creators, which now bec omes more important in this GenAI age. Their reflections will illuminate how these emerging AI designs relate to core KB visions enriching CSCL while opening possibilities for new conceptualization and innovative designs transforming human-AI collaboration. We envision the dialogue will inspire collective advances advancing new KB-AI synergies for enabling new forms of CSCL and knowledge creation.
