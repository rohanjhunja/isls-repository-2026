# Raising Teachers’ Awareness to Embrace Diversity in Knowledge Building

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Participating in Knowledge Building discourses for learners with visual impairments means that they can move from the current meritocratic and individual performance-based learning to ‘Currere,’ where they realize their existential meaning as members of society through others. This study compares the perspectives of special education teachers with those of general education teachers to promote inclusive Knowledge Building support through a mixed-methods case study, and analyzes the contradictions and tensions they currently face through Activity Theory.

Knowledge Building (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 1991; Scardamalia et al., 1994) can be powerful when students interpret a topic through their own experiential knowledge and solidify their position as epistemic agents and social members in a collaborative environment (as cited in Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2014; Dewey, 1897). However, learners with visual impairments have inevitably experienced differences in learning outcomes due to their physical disabilities, have experienced passive curricula to keep up with the pace of learning, and have become accustomed to deficit frame thinking in which they are constantly perceived as needing assistance (McDermott, 2001; Miyauchi, 2020; Ontario Human Rights Commission, 2012). Integrating learners with visual impairments into Knowledge Building classrooms can be viewed as a process that addresses three premises (see figure 1): first, designing an online platform (e.g., Knowledge Forum) interface and accessibility that supports the cognitive schemas of visually impaired learners (Han 2020; Han & Coppin, 2024); second, a learning environment that is pedagogically well-designed to promote students’ higher-order mental development (Vygotsky, 1978); and lastly, the belief and support of teachers that inclusive Knowledge Building, including these visually impaired learners, is worth pursuing (DeCoito & Estaiteyeh, 2022). Figure 1 Framework for Cognitive, Pedagogical and Social Support in Knowledge Building This mixed methods case study (Johnson & Onwuegbuzie, 2004; Johnson et al., 2007; Yin, 2018) identifies systemic barriers that general educators face to including students with visual impairments in Knowledge Building using Knowledge Forum software and compares their perspectives with those of special educators who teach learners with visual impairments. Teachers’ empathy and contextual meaning-making for a non-oppressive classroom culture can facilitate reflection and understanding of the unique autobiographical experiences of marginalized groups, such as people with visual impairments, and extend them to the group context (see, Currere, Pinar, 1975/2019), thereby contributing to creating a foundation for equitable Knowledge Building that benefits the community (Dewey, 1897; Makoelle, 2019).

## Literature review

Knowledge Building considers each individual’s different perspectives as diverse knowledge contributions and seeks to have the ‘whole class’ work collaboratively in a design mode (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2014). Involving learners with visual impairments in general classes conveys a sustainable message of equality from a democratic justice perspective (Miyauchi, 2020). Additionally, the diverse perspectives of learners with visual impairments can contribute to forming balanced perspectives without falling into bias, which can be a positive way for all learners (Makoelle, 2019).

However, teaching learners with visual impairments is often perceived as the domain of special education teachers, and it is difficult for general teachers to know about the cognitive challenges and teaching strategies for the specific needs of learners with visual impairments (DeCoito & Estaiteyeh, 2022; Han, 2020). Conversely, there is little awareness of Knowledge Building among special education teachers in blind educational settings where collaboration itself is not a priority (Han, 2020; Morris, 2021). The five variables that influence students’ participation in inclusive learning are ‘decision makers, strong supporters, student passion, student learning, and teacher passion’ (Brown, 1992). Except for ‘student passion and learning,’ the variables are not affected by students’ will and effort, suggesting that the role of teachers who are expected to act as strong decision makers, supporters, or facilitators may be key. Consequently, teacher training on Knowledge Building concepts, values, and effective teaching strategies can serve as a bridge to expand accessibility for visually impaired learners engaging in online mediated collaboration and move toward an equitable infrastructure (Kashi et al, 2023).

## Research questions

When sighted and non-sighted learners engage in Knowledge Building together, what pedagogical strategies do general and special educators implement to facilitate learners’ discourse, and what challenges do they face? This study will be a mixed method, multiple case study (Johnson & Onwuegbuzie, 2004; Johnson et al., 2007; Yin, 2018, p. 63). Two classes of secondary-level students, 15-17 sighted and 3-4 visually impaired, will participate in a knowledge building discourse over a one-semester period, led by a general education teacher and a special education teacher, respectively. Participants will first be informed and sign a consent form approved by the research ethics committee. Both classes will then address a range of issues under the same theme of ‘sustainability’. Engaging visually impaired learners in sustainable education-related classes aligns with the deoppression goals of this study as it implies that they share in their responsibilities as global citizens who can contribute to the social good (Israel, 2015; Kincheloe, 2005; Scardamalia, 2002) through a critical reflection process as epistemic agents (Freire, 1970).

In-depth qualitative data will be collected through observations, surveys, semi-structured interviews, and teachers’ reflective journals. The awareness, participation methods, and learning strategies of learners and teachers in Knowledge Building will be investigated through surveys (Yin, 2018). Knowledge Building discourse through the Knowledge Forum software will be conducted asynchronously, and the students’ discourse notes, written weekly, will be monitored by teachers, who will provide feedback based on their learning strategies, and the entire process will be observed by researchers. Semi-structured interviews will provide deeper reflections on the challenges participants face (Yin, 2018, p. 118). Teachers are asked to write reflective journals describing teaching strategies and decisions that help learners in both sighted and non-sighted groups to uncover the past, reconstruct the present, and create collective meaning for the future through Currere’s four stages of cognitive process (Pinar, 1975/2019): ‘regressive, progressive, analytical, and synthetical’. Student-generated idea notes collected through the knowledge forum platform will generate quantitative visuals and analytics of student contributions and interactions through built-in analytics tools (Scardamalia, 2002; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2021). When learners' complex cognitive processing is mediated in an online environment, it is necessary to understand the activity in its 'historical, social, and cultural context' to understand its connections with various factors such as teacher strategies and community relationships. Using Activity Theory (Kaptelinin & Nardi, 2012; Nardi, 1996) as an analytical framework, the contradictions and conflicts in knowledge building learning patterns, strategies, and student engagement between different groups led by two teachers can be analyzed (Kaptelinin & Nardi, 2012; Mwanza, 2001). Engeström’s triangle (1987/2015), which is an extension of Leontev’s Activity Theory (Nardi, 1996), is suitable for understanding the interaction of various factors affecting group discourse work from various angles by adding the division of ‘instruments-subjects-objects’ to ‘rules-communitiesdivisions of labor’ (Engeström, 2015; Kaptelinin & Nardi, 2012).

## Significance of the study

Ultimately, this study aims to expand the Knowledge Building online collaboration of visually impaired learners, which has been neglected in the field of education, into the general classroom to build a sustainable educational environment. Teachers valuing their assigned 'dignified role in performing moral duties in the community' and acquiring specific strategies to support learners from an inclusive perspective can be a proactive way to ensure that all learners, including marginalized groups, can participate in quality learning (Dewey, 1897). Children and teenagers have been included as an important user group for decades (Druin, 2002). This study explores the role of play in the computational empowerment of teenagers, examining how play emerges in participatory design and how it can be effectively utilized. While computational empowerment emphasizes critical engagement with technology and active participation in its development (Dindler et al., 2020; Iivari et al., 2023), the role of play in this process has remained underexplored, particularly among teenagers. Exploring from sociological and psychological perspectives, we investigate how play fosters creativity, self-expression, and social interaction in design contexts. Prior research highlights the developmental significance of play (Huizinga, 1955; Vygotsky, 1967). Theorists like Froebel, Piaget, and Vygotsky offer key insights seeing play as central to development,linking it to cognitive processes, and highlighting its role in learning within the Zone of Proximal Development (Froebel, 1887; Piaget, 1962; Vygotsky, 1967). While often applied to young children, these ideas also relate to teenagers. Yet teenage play differs from both childhood and adult play in its performative, exploratory, and identity-forming aspects (Johnston et al., 2023; Tieben, 2015). Johnston (2023) found that playful learning fosters independence and imagination in teenagers., although imaginative play is considered central to young children’s learning. In childhood play is an active pursuit that stems from children’s natural curiosity and imagination (Erikson, 1977; Froebel, 1887). In the study of Tieben (2015), teenagers used words like 'creativity' and 'imagination' to describe the benefits they experienced when their teachers used a playful approach to learning, suggesting that their experience of playful learning promotes their creativity. Play can be adult-directed or free, supporting creativity across ages (Brooker, 2011; Yarnal & Qian, 2011). Bruce (2012) and Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkoff (2008) argue that adult-led play may hinder spontaneity and imagination. Play is partly an individual, personal experience, and it has an individual dimension (Barnett, 1991, 1991; Lieberman, 1967; Staempfi, 2007). According to Johnston et al. (2023) play is a context for learning” that promotes well-being and relationships, where children express individuality and make connections with their existing knowledge (Proyer, 2011). When talking about social play in the context of teenagers, it is at least clear that teenagers are social beings forming cliques and crowds and having a strong peer conformity (Tieben, 2015). Children enjoy themselves while being socially active (Goldstein, 2012; Tieben, 2015). Social play, involving peer interactions, enhances well-being, creativity, and skill development (Piaget, 1962; vygotsky, 1980). It also includes performative activities, fostering engagement and motivation (Kort & IJsselsteijn, 2008; Márquez Segura et al., 2019). By integrating playful methods into participatory design, we aim to understand how play supports teenagers’ agencies in shaping technology and developing computational thinking. As our research question we ask: “How play can be utilized in in computational empowerment of teenagers?” So far, play has not been scrutinized in computational empowerment endeavors with teenagers during design, while its value for computational empowerment of young children has already been pointed out (Tikkanen et al., 2023).

## Research design and empirical findings

This study is part of a multidisciplinary game design project which aims to design and implement a virtual learning environment for teenagers. We organized participatory design and evaluation workshops in standard classroom environments at three different school and analysed data from eight design activities in the game design process to gain insights from teenagers from three different classes (7, 8 and 9 graders) aged 13 to 16. When analyzing our data (pre and post-interviews, video recordings, field notes, research diaries, photographs, children’s artworks, crafts and writings) collected from these sessions, we utilized abductive theme analysis (), and combined activities to four main tasks. The first three were carried out without the use of technology (unplugged). During task four, students needed their mobile or used school tablets. Based on the literature, we formed a framework concerning play and computational empowerment among teenagers which we combined to act as a sensitizing device in our empirical analysis (see Table 1).
