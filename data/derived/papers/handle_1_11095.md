# "I'm not the Math Parent": Exploring Gender Differences in Math Parenting Identity

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates the concept of Math Parenting Identity (MPI) and its influence on the Home Math Environment (HME), particularly examining the differing identities of mothers and fathers using the RESET Framework. With 847 parents surveyed, findings suggest a need for tailored family engagement programs that challenge traditional gender-based parenting paradigms and promote equitable math learning opportunities for young children.

## Introduction

The far-ranging impacts of early success in mathematics for both individuals and for society are well documented (Ritchie & Bates, 2013). Unfortunately, many students arrive at school with low prior knowledge and insufficient exposure to early math concepts, likely stemming from lack of sufficient enrichment in the home environment prior to the onset of kindergarten (Blevins-Knabe, 2016). Parent-child shared math engagement is known to have a significant positive impact on children’s early mathematics development; nevertheless, few studies have focused on parent decision-making around “math parenting” (Betts & Son, 2022; Blevins-Knabe 2016).

## Background

Math Parenting Identity  (MPI)—a new construct for understanding math parenting —lives at the intersection of parents’ identities as both parents and as doers and learners of math. For example, differences in parenting roles and perceptions between mothers and fathers, especi ally in the context of young children  (Minkin & Horowitz, 2023). To elaborate, mothers in the United States often express greater concerns regarding their children's mental health, safety, and face increased stress and judgm ent in their parenting roles. They are more likely to adopt overprotective behaviors and give in quickly to the ir children's demands. Fathers, conversely, tend to  give more freedom and adhere to their decisions, shaping a unique parenting identity. This divergence in parenting styles is pivotal in understanding how parents construct their MPI, influencing children’s early mathematical development. Gender differences in also play a crucial role  (Breda et al., 2023). Despite females currently outnumbering males in higher education, they remain underrepresented in their intent to study math-related fields. This gender gap in math-related fields is close to zero among the lowest performers in math and increases steadily with math performance. This disparity in math learner identity and the underrepresentation of women among the most able students in math have significant implications for policy and intervention strategies. The present study investigates parent perceptions around key factors influencing t heir math parenting identity, emphasizing the crucial role of cultural beliefs, and personal moti vations in shaping children's math learning experiences.  The primary question guiding this study is: How do the Math Parenting Identities of mothers and  fathers of young children differ, as examined using the RESET Framework?

## Theoretical framework

The RESET framework (Role, Expectations, Skills, Efficacy, and Time) offers a comprehensive approach to examining these varying parental identities, especially in the context of the Home Math Environment (Betts, 2021; Betts & Son, 2022). This study explores these identities, emphasizing  the crucial role of parental perceptions, cultural beliefs, and personal motivations in shaping children's math learning experiences.

## Methods

This study operationalized the RESET framework to examine the math parenting identities of 847 parents (634 mothers / 213 fathers) from a broad range of ethnic, economic, and educational backgrounds. The RESET survey instrument captured parents' perceptions of their math parenting Roles, Expectations, Skills (math), self-Efficacy, and Time available (Betts & Son, 2022). The comprehensive survey inclu ded quantitative items on these five domains, as well as measures of parent knowledge of early childhood mat h concepts and frequency of engaging in math activities with their children. The data we re collected through a commercial panel provider, e nsuring a representative sample. Statistical analysis focused on gender differences, employing t-tests for comparing means and chi-square tests for categorical data, to discern distinct math parenting profiles between mothers and fathers.

## Findings

Findings shown in Table 1 below reveal that parents of both genders demonstrate consistently high expectations for their children.  However, while Mothers parents see themselves as having an importan t role to play in their children’s early math development, they have signif icantly lower perceptions of their math Skills and selfEfficacy.  Conversely, Fathers express significantly higher perceptions of their math Skills and Efficacy, and express feeling like they have more time to engage mathematically with their children (though this was not significant), though they have a limited view of their Role in doing so.  A multiple linear regression was conducted to ascertain the effects of Gender  when controlling for Education, and Income on the p erceptions of math-parenting Role, Skills, and Efficacy. For the Role domain, Gender remained a significant predictor (β = −.206, t(845)= −3.015, p <.01), indicating that Fathers  (coded as the second group),  reported significantly lower perceptions of their math-parenting role compared to Mothers (coded as the first group), even after controlling for Education and Income. Income was significantly and negatively associated (β = −.095, t(845) = −2.081, p <.05). This suggests that higher Income levels were correl ated with lower perceptions of math-parenting Role, perhaps due to greater access to outside resources (e.g., p reschool or enrichment services) that reduced paren ts’ perceptions of their personal role. The regression analysis underscores the importance of considering socioeconomic factors when examining parental perce ptions of math-related responsibilities, with the influence of Income meriting further examination.

## Conclusions

These preliminary findings underscore distinct math parenting identit ies, where societal norms bolster Mothers’ nurturing roles and often leave Fathers with less support. These roles shape parents ’ engagement in children’s early math development. Addressing these divergent identities, stakeholders in family engagement progr ams might consider developing interventions that cater to the unique needs of bot h mothers and fathers, of varying income and education levels, challenging established parenting paradigms to pr omote equitable math learning opportunities.
