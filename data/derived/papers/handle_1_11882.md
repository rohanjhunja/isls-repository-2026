# Towards Supporting Group Learning through Design-based Research and Multimodal Data-based Tools

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents an iterative design-based research study that generated a multimodal analytics dashboard to support feedback and reflection in student groups enrolled in an undergraduate legal education course. Across two iterations, involving eight groups with 28 students and 7 teachers, we explored students’ collaborative project work and teachers’ pedagogical needs for guiding groups. We co-designed a multimodal analytics-based dashboard prototype that visualized meeting activities, project documents and survey reflections, and which was used to support reflection on group work. The findings indicate that students value analytics related to the collaboration progress and quality of collaborative input, while teachers are more interested in accessing data about students’ development of subject matter knowledge and the collaborative process.

Challenges in group work Collaborative learning  in educational settings has long been recognized as  a valuable approach to fostering learning and the development of diverse skill sets. Collaborative forms of activity, such as collaborative problemsolving or group project work, are often employed in (higher) education programmes, both as a method to support learning (e.g., through teamwork) and as ways of enculturing students into practices of solving problems, creative and innovative thinking, or of engaging in teamwork. However, the intricate nature of group work and particularly extended group projects presents challenges that can impact how collaborative processes unfold and the value of collaboration as a form of activity that is intended to support learning. Student groups undertaking projects that span an ex tended period (typically between 4 to 12 weeks, or an entire term) are typical learning activities in undergraduate curric ula. Such activities entail tasks that often mirror real-world complexities  and require a sustained engagement with subject matter, problem-solving, and decision-making. The gradual evolution of tasks over time ca n lead to challenges related to maintaining a consistent focus, tracking progress, and managing evolving goals. Collabora tion within student groups implies a multidimensional interaction, entailing, am ong others,  effective communication, coordination, and a shared understanding of goals  and actions (Damşa, 2014). Furthermore, especially in higher education, the assessment of collaborative learning is usually based on performance assessments (Chen et al., 2020; Muukkonen et al., 2020). Such assessments are labor-intensive and lack the potential to provide a holistic view of the complex collaboration processes taking place during collaborative learning situations. It is challenging for teachers and the collaborating students to assess and improve performance in authentic situations, a s they commonly have sparse evidence to promptly identify issues arising as effects of team’s various ac tions or interactions (Graesser et al., 2020). The challenges often arise when teachers lack visibility into students' progress between guidan ce sessions, hindering their ability to intervene and provide timely assistance, especia lly when groups are veering off track.  Teachers also face difficulties in determining what aspects of group work to monitor closely and when t o intervene. The evolving nature of projects makes it challenging fo r teachers to pinpoint when feedback is most benefi cial. Delayed assessment until the final presentation and  report stages hampers the po tential for identifying and rectifying issues early in the process. Thus, in the context of CSCL, the teachers need specific tools for monitoring and supporting students’ group work processes and interactions. Research has identified prompted reflection (throug h targeted feedback) as a mechanism that leads students to greater awareness, by training them to self-regulate or co-regulate activities (Järvelä et al., 2016), and monitor progress in knowledge or skills, and finally, to understanding themselves as learners and collabora tive partners (Nicol & Macfarlane‐Dick, 2006). Feedback that succeeds in targeting both the development of subjectmatter contents and the collaborative process has also potential to support adaptive activities that may encourage learners to stay motivated, i.e., to feed their autonomous desire  to learn (Martin et al., 2017). In addition, t he continuous development of new technologies and meth ods that allow capturing and analyzing of data abou t students’ learning collaborative processes in physical and digital learning environments, opens new opportunities to capture multidimensional and fin e-grained information during collaboration (Schneider et al., 2021); and  to generate basis for a more systematic support for co llaborative groups through tools for feedback and r eflection. Advancing technology has made it possible to develop approaches to analyze and support students’ collaborative learning in digital and collocated settings by mean s of multimodal collaborative analytics (MMCA). The  nature of MMCA range from low-level logs, such as clickstreams which are easily captured at a scale without observers influencing the activity, verbal and non-verbal indicators ranging from tangible interaction (e.g., gesture, posture, and head or hand orientation), speech-based cues (e.g., non-lexical speech features, speech-activity features, and speaker-based features), to gaze and eye interaction (Blikstein & Worsley, 2016; Martinez-Maldonado et al., 2019). These multimodal data sources can facilitate scalable feedback, assessment, and reflection (Marlow et al., 2018), which is typically complex, laborious, and unattainable in the time-bound of collaborative learning processes. Howeve r, few studies have taken a data-driven and co-design approach to establish the nature of indicators and analytics visualizations needed by students and teacher s. Moreover, a limited number of studies have examined in-depth the way users (students and teachers) engage with data/analytics-based feedback and how they value this type of feedback.

Aims of the study The main aims of th is study were a) to identify elements of collaborative  group work that require support/ guidance, based on needs expressed by teachers and students, b) to de sign and test a prototype to support the automatic collection and display of data about the identified collaboration aspects and c) to assess the value of the designed prototype. We sought to answer the following questions:

1. What aspects of group work are considered important  by students and teachers to be visualized through a

multimodal analytics interface for supporting students’ reflection during group projects?

2. How do students and teachers value multimodal visualizations for supporting reflection on and learning from

understanding of group dynamics?

## Empirical context and participants

This research was conducted in an undergraduate elective course on Lega l Technology, involving 28 students, 7 teaching staff and 1 teaching assistant,  in two DBR iterations. The students worked in groups for 12 weeks on real-world projects, using Microsoft Teams for communication and collaboration. Data collection included video recordings of meetings, student and teacher interviews, activity logs, and reflection surveys. Participants were recruited voluntarily with informed consent. We followed a DBR framework (Reeves, 2006) across three phases:

(1) needs analysis, (2) prototype development, and (3) empirical tri al and refinement. Co-design involved

researchers, teachers, developers, and students throughout the process. Phase 1: Analysis of practical problems/Needs elicitation The problem analysis was conducted through: (a) observation and analysis of student’s group work meetings, (b) interviews with students and teachers, and (c) a literature review. On one hand, students identified key challenges in managing collaboration. These included imbalanced contributions, unclea r roles, and difficulties maintaining oversight of the project. Many students expressed u ncertainty about how much they were expected to con tribute and lacked a shared understanding of who was respon sible for what. Some emphasized the absence of clea r planning tools and progress tracking mechanisms, noting that this led to ine fficiencies and last-minute work. Others highlighted the benefits of having a structured timeline, stating it helped maintain momentum and fostered smoother collaboration. Students also reflected on the potential value of visual representations of pr ogress and contributions to help orient themselves and others during the project. Teachers, on the other hand, were concerned with deeper pedagogical and assessment-related challenges. They noted difficulties in trac king student engagement between supervision sessions and raised questions about whether students were effectively applying subject-matter knowledge. Some teachers expressed a desire for tools that could surface behavioral indicators of participation, such as body language, while others stressed the importance of supporting students' social-relational development, including skills like empathy and communication. Notably, teachers differed in their willingness to directly intervene  with some preferr ing tools that allowed students to reflect independently, while others saw potential in analytics-supported mentoring.

Phase 2: Defining requirements and development of interface prototype The challenges and opportunities identified in Phase 1 informed the deve lopment of prototype visualizations in Phase 2, as illustrated in Figure 1. Drawing on students' and teachers' expressed needs, three types of visualizations were designed to support reflection on group processes: Visualization 1: Contribution to group discussion during meetings; Visualization 2: Levels of (digital) communication in groups, based on the frequency and distribution over time of number of posts and chats aggregated at a group and individual student's level; and Visualization 3: Reflections on group work,  based on individual students’ survey responses with  reflections on collaboration elements.

Top left a pie chart and bar chart with total speaking time per student during group meetings; right hand, line graph showing responses to a reflection survey; bottom left and right, bar graph and table with number of posts and chats in MsTeams per student during the project period. Phase 3: Empirical trial, testing of interface prototype, refining requirements The final phase of the study focused on empirically trialing the visualizations developed in Phase 2 to assess their perceived value and usability. Like the exploratory phase, we worked with four student groups comprising 3 –4 members who had expressed interest and consented to  participate. The testing process consisted of two steps: a group reflection session using the visualizations, followed by a focus group interview with a card-sorting activity. During the reflection, students shared their interpretations, highlighted helpful or missing elements, and discussed the usefulness of the analytics. In the subsequent focus group, students gave more detailed input on how the visualizations supported their understanding of group dynamics and perform ance, which an alytics were most insightful, and what additional data could enhance reflection. As part  of this, they completed a card-sorting task using Optimal Workshop (Martin & Macias, 2023) to rank different anal ytics types drawn from literature and earlier reflections as "very useful," "less useful," or "not useful" for collaborative reflection.

## Findings

Overall, students responded positively to the meeting analytics, especially in raising awareness about participation patterns. One student remarked, “Wow, I didn't feel like someone talked that much more with respect to the others,” highlighting the value of visualizing speaking time. However, students al so pointed out limitations, noting that speaking time alone did not account for individual roles or contributions, and some visualizations were not representative of all meetings. Digital activity graphs we re considered more balanced and reflective of group participation but were critiqued for excluding inte ractions on other platforms like WhatsApp. Survey r eflections were seen as useful for expressing unspoken group t ensions and improving awareness of colla boration quality, although some students found the overlapping lines in the graphs hard to interpret. In the card-sorting exercise, students ranked task progression, group document analytics, speaker turn-taking, and self-reported survey data as most useful, while sentiment and affect analytics were generally seen as less relevant for reflection.

## Discussion and conclusion

The results from the three phases of the co-design process have implications for the design and  implementation of collaboration analytics tools intended to suppor t reflection and monitoring of group projects. Stud ents and teachers expressed, as per our first research question, a preference fo r analytics about the task and collaboration progress and the quality of the collaborative input. Aligned with literature on social regulation (Järvelä et al., 2019), the students appear to prefer receiving support during the planning and reflection stages, rather than evaluating the products of their collaborative work. Students were di vided, with some showing preference for group-level analytics as a useful prompt for reflection, while others,  individual focused analyti cs citing the potential of within-group conflicts when data is presented to the entir e group. Some suggested having data on their own behavior in the context of the group without necessarily identifying specific individuals. This approach would enable them to understand their contributions relative to others and gauge their level of involvement in the group's activities (Divjak et al., 2023). Also, task progress anal ytics was identified as a useful prompt for reflection, self-regulation and reflection (see also Martin & Macias, 2023). From the teachers’ perspective, the focus was on analytics that showed students’ progression in subject matter knowledge. The contrast between what students and teachers need from multimodal analytics has design implications, such as the need for a customizable double-facing dashboard that allows the different users to  choose the kind of feedback they wish to receive f rom the tool.

Data representations were received differently depe nding on their form and context. Meeting analytics prompted useful reflection on individual and group participation but were sometimes seen as lacking nuance (e.g., accounting for role differences or meeting coverage). Survey-based visualizations helped raise awareness about unspoken tensions and facilitated self-expression, echoing Nicol and Macfarlane‐Dick’s (2006) argument that feedback should promote learner agency.  However, visualization complexity occasionally limited interpretation. Likewise, digital activity analytics revealed participation trends but lacked completeness due to platform constraints, a challenge noted in multimodal learning analytics lit erature (Blikstein & Worsley, 2016; Schneider et al., 2021).

Moving forward, we aim to develop an automated or semi-automated dashboard based on these insights. This next stage of the project will involve embedding tailored reflec tion prompts, supporting students’ self and shared regulation in real time. Further analysis wi ll include in-depth content analysis of group meetings, reports, surveys, and chats, applying techniques such as epistemic and temporal network analysis, sequential mining, and social network analysis. By combining user-informed design with advanced multimodal analysis, our goal is to refine analytics tools that not only support user p references but also enhance our understanding of co llaborative learning dynamics in authentic settings.
