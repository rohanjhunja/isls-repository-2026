# Problematic Problems: Navigating the Design Process Amidst Challenges

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In education, design frameworks are often used to cultivate creativity, innovation, and problem-solving skills but often overlook the complexities of design problems. As students identify personally relevant problems, their perspectives are shaped by their experiences with the challenges of the complex world. There is a need for learning activities that connect design methods and students’, empowering them to tackle challenges creatively and confidently.

Educators often adopt design frameworks to foster creativity, innovation, and problem-solving skills (Brown, 2008). While these frameworks guide students through structured design processes, they often overlook the inherent complexities of design problems.  Moreover, when students work to identify personally relevant problems (as many design frameworks and pedagogical practices suggest), they do so through the filter of their own experiences of multifaceted world challenges, which can, at times,  constrain their creativity and frustrate their efforts. As the vignettes suggest, how we as educators approach problems and position them within learning activities might be inherently problematic. Therefore, we suggest redefining the concept of problems in the context of learning and paving the way for a deeper comprehension of how life challenges intersect with design problems in educational settings. To inform this redefinition, we  draw on literature around design, learning, and problems in students’ lives, briefly reviewed below.

Within design methodologies, problems have traditionally been overshadowed by processes (Beckman & Barry, 2007). Nevertheless, design methodologies and problem framing are interdependent; just as design methods shape problems, problems catalyze and steer d esign. Although shaped by unalterable needs, design problems are inherently subjective and lack clear boundaries, often to purposefully offer room for interpretation and creativity (Rittel & Webber, 1973). In this way, ill-structured problems (Buchanan, 20 01) are similar to problems within education, a similarly ill-structured field, though for different reasons. Design problems are also iterative and dynamic, co-evolving with solutions (Dorst & Cross, 2001) in a way that needs constant refinement and adaptation, requiring flexibility and adaptability from designers, educators, and students. Lastly, information gathering in design is marked by unpredictability and the need for a deep understanding of user needs and behaviors, which generally characterize ill-defined problems. This process is often described as “inspired guesswork” (Jones, 1992) highlighting its subjective and exploratory nature. Closely aligning with learning theories, design encompasses understanding objective constraints and subjective meaning. Active engagement and mediation shape knowledge and the world (Ackermann, 2004), mirroring the principles of design, and e ffective learning occurs through hands-on experiences and collaborative problem-solving (Papert, 1980). Engaging in design-based learning activities not only fosters creativity, problem2403 solv ing skills, and self-expression (Doppelt, 2009), it allows students to actively explore solutions, complex issues, and innovative thinking. These activities serve as representation of goals, aspirations, and challenges. Educators often intertwine real-world challenges within educational activities to integrate students’ personal experiences and interests, encouraging them to draw on their values, beliefs, and motivations. Many design-based learning activities aim for students to tackle personally meanin gful problems, with support structures to guide them through complex situations and facilitate their involvement in authentic real-world projects. Today’s students face a myriad of challenges that go far beyond the classroom, from difficulties in managing stress  to bullying and fears of school violence (Pew Research Center, 20 19). These issues can exacerbate students’ feelings of despair and hopelessness (Brooker, 2020), hindering their ability to engage effectively in learning, and complicating their efforts to address these problems. Although personal experiences inspire the identification of design problems, providing personally meaningful and relevant insights serves  as motivation. Personal life issues affect attention, memory, and cognitive resources (Baddeley, 2013), influencing ideation, motivation, and decision-making. Emotions such as optimism and enthusiasm are crucial in design (Manzini, 2009), while cynicism can stifle curiosity and suffocate ideas  (Brown, 2008). Yet, fostering optimism can be challenging when faced with relentless uncertainty and a constant need to adapt. In this atmosphere, educators play a vital role in unveiling opportunities for hope (Freire, 1997) and the potential for positive change. Contributions In reflecting upon the earlier vignettes, it becomes evident that the challenges students face are intertwined with broader issues in learning and their personal lives. Alex’s  struggle to identify a meaningful problem amidst daunting societal issues, Maya’ s difficulty in distancing herself from personal experiences to analyze broader systemic problems, and Javier’s immersion in real-world issues at the expense of exploring diverse perspectives underscore the complexities inherent in problem-framing. These challenges not only impact students ’ ability to engage effectively in learning experiences but also highlight the need for an approach that bridges design methodologies with students’ life experiences. By recognizing and addressing the influence of personal struggles on problem framing, we must empower students beyond their feelings of frustration, anxiety, and anger (Holbert,

2020) and equip them to tackle personally meaningful proble ms while instilling hope. Moving forward, it is

crucial to interrogate how problems are defined and explore alternative methods of framing design problems. This may involve a shift away from pain-centered approaches and towards a future and happiness-focused perspective or delving into culturally situated practices that may have alternative starting points. In essence, our next steps involve exploring alternative approaches, to empower students to tackle challenges with creativity and confidence. Ackermann, E. (2004).
