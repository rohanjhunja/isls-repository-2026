# A Latent Growth Modeling for Individual Differences in Dispositions Toward Uncertainty Navigation in Computing Problem-solving

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In authentic problem-solving, students may exhibit various patterns of uncertainty navigation, reflecting their dispositions toward uncertainty navigation in computing problem-solving (DUNC). This study investigates how DUNC evolves—advancing, maintaining, or retreating— over time across multiple problem-solving learning sessions. A latent growth model was tested to examine inter-and intra-individual differences in DUNC and the impact of achievement goals (mastery and performance) and gender. Data collected from 431 Fall 2023 cloud-computing students was used. Results revealed variability in DUNC trajectories: some students showed increases, decreases, or stability over time. Higher mastery goals were associated with higher initial DUNC and faster changes, while higher performance goals correlated with lower initial DUNC and slower changes. Gender did not significantly predict DUNC changes. The findings highlight that authentic problem-solving sessions can enhance DUNC and that fostering mastery goal orientations may accelerate its development, reinforcing the value of DUNC in preparing future computer scientists.

## Introduction

Computer science education at college and graduate levels has put emphasis on authentic problem-solving experiences (Bruce-Davis et al., 2014; Falkner et al., 2009; Kay et al., 2000). The focus has been on providing opportunities for students to apply their conceptual and procedural understanding to solve complex problems they are likely to encounter in real-world tasks (Schefer-Wenzl & Miladinovic, 2019). However, practicing the application of conceptual and procedural understand ing may not be sufficient to develop the capability  for authentic complex problem-solving. Students often become uncertain, feeling stifled or frustrated, unable to make progress during computing problem-solving. They may not fully recognize or understand the reasons behind their struggles or how to address these uncertainties (Chen  et al., 2024; Park et al., 2022). These challenges are not solely attributable to conceptual or procedural understanding but relate to their capability to navigate uncertainties inherent in computing problems.

Productively navigating uncertainties is an essenti al capability critical to computer scientists' work, yet it remains largely overlooked in computer science e ducation. To enhance students' ability to tackle authentic computing problems, as computer scientists do in th eir tasks, problem-solving learning should not only focus on acquiring domain knowledge but also on developing students’ attitudes and strategies for navigating uncertainties. This research refers to the varying attitudes and s trategies individual students exhibit when encounte ring, wrestling with, and resolving uncertainties during computing problem-solving as Dispositions toward Navigating Uncertainty in Computing problem-solving (DUNC).

Student DUNC can vary not only inter-individually but also intra-individually. While different students exhibit different DUNC, an individual’s disposition  can shift based on past experiences (Thornton, 200 6). For example, in cloud-computing problem-solving, students may exper ience success or frustration as they address uncertainties related to understanding conditions, requirements, parameters, and contexts of the probl em (e.g., applying their knowledge of IaaS to develop a cloud computing app). Depending on their engagement with these uncertainties, the enjoyment or frustration they en counter, and the number of problem-solving sessions they participate in, student DUNC may advance, remain stable, or retreat. While student DUNC can be a pivotal asset for compu ting problem-solving learning, the factors influencing its development and individual differences are not well-explored. This study represents an initial effort to examine both inter-individual and intra-individual differences in DUNC, as well as the factors influencing these differences. This study proposes that a motivational factor—specifically, the type of achievement goal in science learning—and a demographic factor, gender, may affect DUNC.

## Conceptual framework

Achievement goal and student DUNC Achievement goals in learning, defined as students’ specific reasons for motivating their learning behaviors (Ames, 1992), revolve around desires to: a) develop competence (mastery) and b) demonstrate competence or outperform others (performance) (Senko et al., 2011). Students  focused on mastery goals may feel a sense of achie vement when they meet task-based or self-defined criteria, while those focused on performanc e goals may feel fulfilled when they demonstrate competence or outperform othe rs (Senko et al., 2011). While this study acknowled ges ongoing debates about the distinction between two t ypes of performance goals —demonstrating competence versus outperforming others (Hulleman & Senko, 2010)—it focuses on comparing mastery and performance goals in a broader sense, as they are distinct in being intrinsically or extrinsically oriented (e.g., Lee et al., 2010). Given the scarcity of research on student DUNC and its relationship to achievement goals, assumptions based on related literature can be made about how DUNC may change over time, depending on a student’s primary achievement goal type. Students with a stronger focus on performance goa ls may retreat in their DUNC, as they are less likely to engage with challenging or uncertain tasks where they cannot demonstrate competence, leading to lower self-efficacy or higher anxiety in navigating uncertainties (e.g., Brophy, 2005; Moller & Elliot, 2006). In contrast, students with a higher focus on mastery g oals are more likely to navigate uncertainties to d evelop competence, enhancing their DUNC through problem-solving (Darnon et al., 2008). Gender and student DUNC The dearth of research on DUNC and its relationship to gender necessitates a caref ul review of indirectly related literature before forming assumptions about how DUNC may change across genders over time.  Regarding the construct of strategies for uncertainty navigation,  Jordan (2015) found that female students were more active in using social strategies to navigate uncertainties i n engineering problem-solving, while male students were more likely to deny or avoid these uncertainties. Regarding epistemic orientation toward uncertainty, studies on personal epistemology indicate that male college students generally hold more sophisticated beliefs ab out the uncertainty of knowledge than female students (e.g., Chai et al., 2006). Regarding self-efficacy in uncertainty navigation, Demirören et al. (2016) suggest that fe male students show greater self-efficacy in navigating their learning processes and those of their peers compared to male students. The inconsistent results across broad and diverse research fields regarding gender differences, along with the scarcity of direct literature, create challenges in formulat ing hypotheses about how the overall DUNC— comprising all three constructs—changes across different genders over time. It is reasonable to propose that there may be no significant gender differences in how the  overall DUNC evolves. The limitations of this study concerning the investigation of overall DUNC, as opposed to each individual construct, are disc ussed in a later section of this study.

## Research questions and hypotheses

1.  Is there evidence of intraindividual changes in student DUNC during Cloud Computing problem-based

learning sessions?

Hypothesis 1: Students' dispositions toward uncertainty navigation are expected to increase linearly across the sessions.

2.  Is there evidence of interindividual variability in the initial level of student DUNC and rates of change

during Cloud Computing problem-based learning sessions? Hypothesis 2: Students are expected to vary in their initial DUNC levels and their rates of change across the sessions.

3.  How do mastery and performance goals in science learning predict the initial level of student DUNC

and rates of change during Cloud Computing problem-based learning sessions? Hypothesis 3a: Mastery goals are expected to positively predict initial DUNC level and rate of change. Hypothesis 3b: Performance goals are expected tonegatively predict initial DUNC level and rate of change.

4.  How does gender predict the initial level of DUNC and rates of change during Cloud Computing

problem-based learning sessions?

Hypothesis 4: Male and female students are expected to show no significant differences in initial DUNC levels and rates of change.

## Participants and procedure

Data were collected from a large university in the southwestern U.S. A total of 431 students enrolled in Fall 2023 Cloud Computing  courses taught by the same instructor participated in the study. With approval from the university’s IRB, the research team collected parti cipants’ names and gender information. Of the parti cipants, 19.5% (n = 84) were female (see Table 1).

Students engaged in two 1-month problem-based learning sessions, each requiring collaborati ve solutions to real-life Cloud Computing scenarios. Surveys were administered online three t imes: before the first session (Time 1, September 2023), after the first s ession (Time 2, October 2023), and after the second  session (Time 3, November 2023). ## Measures

Dispositions toward scientific uncertainty navigation.

Student DUNC was measured using a scale developed and validated in a previous study (Park et al., 2023). The 14-item DUNC in problem-based learning  scale consists of three subconstructs: Epistemic or ientation toward uncertainty (6 items), self-efficacy in uncertainty navigation (4 items) and strategies for uncertainty navigation (4 items). A 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) was used. The average score of the 14 items was calculated for analysis. The scale's reli ability was acceptable across measures: Cronbach’s α was.834 at Time 1,.856 at Time 2, and.880 at Time 3.

Mastery and performance goal in science learning.

Tuan et al. (2005) developed and validated a multidimensional science learning motivation scale, called Students’ Motivation towards Science Learning. This study ado pted the mastery (5 items) and performance goal (4 items) subscales from this scale. A 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = stro ngly agree) was used. The average score for each subscale was calculated, with acceptable reliabilities: Cronbach’s α was.776 for performance goals and.738 for mastery goals.

## Results

To address the research questions, two latent growt h curve models were specified and estimated (see Fi gure 1). The first model, a linear growth model, assessed ch anges in uncertainty navigation disposition across three time points, answering the first two questions. The second model included science learni ng motivation and gender as covariates to predict the intercept and slope from the first model, addressing the third and fourth questions. Mplus 8.0 was used for model specification and estimation, with full information maximum likelihood (FIML) employed to handle missing data under the assumption of missing at random. The estimation of the Model I (n = 412) was terminated normally. It fits the data well, χ2(1) =.162, p =.687, RMSEA =.00 [.000 -.097], corrected CFI = 1.00 (Little, 2013). There w ere no suggested modification indices at the critical value of 3.84. The R-squares for each measure of DUNC were.602 at Time 1,.559 at Time 2, and.728 at Time 3. Overall, the result indicate d that the hypothesized linear growth model was a g ood description of the data. The parameter estimates indicated that there was no significant average change in DUNC over time (mean slope =.008, p =.592) (Hypothesis 1 not supported). Students’ average initial DUNC at Time 1 was 4.07, significantly different from zero (p <.001), and there was significant individual variability in this initial level (variance =.143, p <.001). The rrates of change varied among individuals (variance of slope =.035, p =.003) (Hypothesis 2 supported).

Model II (n = 364) fit indices were marginally acce ptable: χ²(4) = 14.8, p =.005, RMSEA =.086 [.042 -.135], CFI =.894. There were no modification indic es above the critical value of 3.84. R-squares were.519 at Time 1,.597 at Time 2, and.617 at Time 3. Overall, the model was a marginally acceptable fit. Performance and achievement goals were significant predictors  (Hypotheses 3-a and 3-b supported). For the intercept factor, mastery goal had a β = 0.224 (p <.001) and performance goal had a β = -0.112 (p <.001). Thus, a 1-unit increase in mastery goal increased the predicted DUNC at Time 1 by 0.224 units, controlling for other variables,  while a 1-unit increase in performance goal decreased it at Time 1 by 0.112 units, controlling for other variables. For the slope factor, mastery goal had a β = 0.076 (p =.002) and performance goal had a β = -0.042 (p =.019). Therefore, a 1-unit increase in achievement goal increased the rat e of change in DUNC by 0.076 units, controlling for other variables, whereas a 1-unit increase in performance goal decreased it by 0.042 units, controlling for other variables. On the other hand, gender was not a significant predictor of the intercept or slope factors of the model: β = -0.075 (p =.213) for the intercept and β = 0.006 (p =.861) for the slope (Hypothesis 4 supported). The analysis of the two models revealed the followi ng findings. First, o n average, students’ DUNC remained stable during problem-based learning sessions, but individual variability was significant. This indicates some students showed increases, decreases, or stable  levels in their dispositions.  Second, v ariability in initial levels and changes in DUNC was influenced by mastery and performance goals, but not by gender. Students with higher performance goal s tended to start with lower DUNC and showed slower changes over time. In contrast, students with higher mastery goals tended to start with higher initial DUNC and demonstrated faster changes. Figure 1 Two Latent Growth Curve Models Tested in this Study

## Conclusion: Implications on theory and practice

This study is among the few, to the authors' knowle dge, that examines both inter-individual and intra-individual differences in DUNC, with mastery and performance goals and gender as covariates predicting these differences. The findings suggest that more efforts should be ma de to create learning environments that enhance DUN C by encouraging students to focus on developing their c ompetence rather thandemonstrating it or outperfor ming others.

Problem-solving in computer science education should help s tudents see the value of uncertainty navigation in competence development and thus foste r a mastery goal orientation. Simply offering oppor tunities to navigate uncertainties is insufficient; students  must recognize how uncertainty navigation benefits  their competence development. To support this, embedded u ncertainties should be relevant, timely, and manage able (Chen et al., 2024). In other words, uncertainties should be desirable for competence development. While performance goal-oriented students can benefit from uncertainty navi gation, they may show a decline in their DUNC if uncertainties impede their  ability to demonstrate competence. Therefore, it i s equally crucial to create learning environments where perfo rmance goal-oriented students either shift their focus to competence development or have positive experiences  demonstrating their abilities through uncertainty navigation.
