# “I Didn’t Know I was Into Birds”: Understanding Students' Perceptions of Nature-Based Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This qualitative study ventures into the wilds of nature-based learning (NBL). Despite documented health and academic outcomes of NBL, students’ perspectives on its challenges and benefits are underexplored. To bridge this gap, we examined students’ views through qualitative surveys and focus group interviews during a weeklong NBL program. Guided by experiential and situated learning theories, we analyzed survey responses and focus group interviews from 140 fifth and sixth grade students. We identified three major themes for benefits: connectedness with nature, exploration and freedom, and interest and skill development. For challenges, themes included: physical demands, preparation and logistics, and discomfort. The study concludes with recommendations for implementation strategies to support positive student experiences.

## Introduction and grounding

Nature-based learning (NBL) has been tied to several positive outcomes, such as lower levels of stress in children (Bell & Dyment,  2008; Chawla,  2015), academic achievement (Wells et.al, 20 15), self-confidence, critical thinking, and problem-solving (Kochanowski & Carr,  2014; Troung et al.,  2016). However, evidence of these positive outcomes  primarily stems from quantitative assessments and teachers’ viewpoints. There is limited research directly investigating students’ perspecti ves of NBL in their own words (Marchant et al., 201 9). This reflects a larger gap in the literature on learning, where there are relatively few studies that aim to understand how children perceive and engage with adult curated activities, from their own perspectives (Stevens, 2020). To truly honor students as active transformative agents, we must genuinely listen to their voices, value their perspectives, and integrate their insights into instructional practices, design, and experiences. Therefore, in this paper, we argue that it is important to listen to students’ perspectives  for at least four reasons. First,  their perspectives highlight their lived experiences (Stevens & Ramey, 2020). Second, if what we care about is shifting their attitudes, then it is important to understand their feelings and perspect ives in their own words. Third, children notice things that adults do not, and viewing these experiences through their eyes draws attention to important constructs that might otherwise be overlooked. Fourth, they can offer strikingly insightful perspective s on areas where educational design efforts are most needed for the main beneficiaries of these experiences (Stevens, 2020). Thus, this study aimed to address the following research question: How do students participating in nature-based learning perceive it, particularly its’ challenges and benefits, from their own perspectives? Our work is grounded in experiential (Dewey, 1938; Kolb, 1984), place-based (Sobel, 2003) and situated (Lave & Wenger, 1991)  learning theories. Place-based and situated learning theories emphasize the critical role of context in learning, advocating for the integration of experiential learning within relevant, real-world contexts. Kolb’s experiential learning model involves a four-stage learning cycle, including: concrete experience; reflective observation; abstract conceptualization; and active experimentation (Kolb, 1984). It suggests that learning occurs best when learners experience, reflect, think, and act in a cyclic process. This theory foregrounds the importance of understanding both learners’ experiences and their reflections on those experiences to understand their learning. By integrating these theories, our study delves into students' firsthand, situated learning experiences in nature, capturing their reflections on these experiences and how they are c onnected to the places and situated activities in which they occurred.

## Method

Participants included 140 elementary school students in grades five and six (ages 10-12) who participated in the School of the Wild (SOTW) in fall 2023. We developed qualitative pre-and post-surveys to capture students’ experiences in their own words, including specific questions on what they did or did not enjoy. We also conducted focus group interviews with students on the last da y of the program. These interviews were designed to explore students’ experiences in NBL and understand their perceptions of challenges and benefits inherent in NBL. SOTW is a statewide program, taking place in the Midwestern United States. It runs during the traditional academic year in the fall or spring. In a typical implementation, students spend 4-5 days visiting one or two state or county parks near their school. While students’ experiences differ somewhat depending on their local natural environment, programmatic goals and professional learning provided to teachers, including suggested activities and themes, are consistent across sites. In small groups of  eight or fewer, students explore different habitats and activities  each day, with local naturalists, conservationists, and their  classroom teachers. Among our participants, h abitats explored included the prairie, woodlands, and wetla nds. Specific areas of study included ornithology (study of birds), wildlife, ecosystems, natural history, archeology, and outdoor skills. Specific activities included kayaking, fishing, shelter building, tagging and releasing birds,  birdwatching, creating art projects using natural materials (temporal art), throwing an atlatl, prairie bingo, and photographing nature. Kolb’s experiential learning model guided the data analysis. The data was analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006; 2019).

## Findings

Students cited three main benefits of NBL. First, when asked in focus groups, “What did you enjoy about the SOTW experience?”, many students reflected on their active engagement in nature. For example, one student stated, “I really enjoyed going kayaking and hiking to find birds.” Also, calmer and more passive engagement with nature  allowed them to reflect on the  subtler aspects of the environment  promoting connectedness with nature. For example, one student said, “The water I think water calms me”. Second, in response to the survey question, “What (if anything) did you enjoy about your experience?” students noted opportunities for exploration and freedom, including "Getting to be free and be able to go wherever with out a teacher most of the time”, "Running off and having free time to explore”. Finally, students reflected on interest and skill development, saying “I didn't know I was into birds. Now I am into birds, I mean, I kind of already knew I was into this, but it got, like, I got more interested” and “Building shelters and forts in the woods was something I'd never done, but it was so fun”.

Students also cited three challenges they faced during NBL. The first was physical demands when asked in the survey and focus groups  “What did you not enjoy about your SOTW experience? ”. One student noted, “There was a lot of hiking. We had to hike a lot.” Another wrote, “I did not enjoy walking with a backpack.” This sentiment was echoed by others who said, “for two d ays straight, we walked like five miles”, and “my l egs hurt from all the walking”. However, while many students cited hiking as challen ging, a few students mentioned finding hiking fun despite the challenge.  The second challenge was discomfort. Insect encounters were the most common source of discomfort; one student said, "I did not enjoy the bugs. There w ere a lot, and one got stuck in my eye!!" Weather conditions, especially heat, were also cited; one student said, "One thing I did not enjoy was the HEAT.” A few students also experienced social and psychological discomfort, due to feelings of isolation or not being adequately integrated into activities; one student mentioned, "I wasn't really with any of my friends, so I felt pretty left out." The final challenge was preparation and logistics. When asked what advice students would give future program participants  during focus groups, one student ’s reflective observation was,  “Make sure to bring a lunch that is healthy and filling... there aren’t any bathrooms to refill water or anything li ke that”. Other students mentioned bringing adequate water and healthy, filling lunches to sustain their energy levels throughout the day. Still others emphasized bringing appropriate clothing and protective gear to ensure comfort and safety.

## Discussion

These findings provide new insights into elementary  students’ perspectives on NBL experiences. The findings illuminate the multifaceted nature of students’ experiences wi th NBL through the lens of Kolb’s experiential learning cycle. Students engaged deeply with nature  through concrete experiences, reflected on these encounters to derive meaning (reflective observation), concept ualized their insights  through the discovery of passions and interests (abstract conceptualization), and applied them in ways that extended their learning, e.g., development of self-sufficiency skills (active experimentation). Our research highlights themes similar to those identified by other learning sciences studies that have attended to youth perspectives (e.g., Stevens & Ramey, 2020; Stevens, 2020). By listening to student voices and incorporating in sights from their experiences into the design of fu ture NBL experiences, it is our hope that these activities can be further improved to inform and empower the next generation of world makers to address environmental and other global challenges that lie ahead.
