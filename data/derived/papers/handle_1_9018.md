# “I Would Rather Argue a Point”

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how the forms of writing that young people utilize to discuss civic issues with each other mediate their understandings of the nature and purpose of civic life and open (or close) potential communicative pathways to creative and empathetic forms of civic worldbuilding. Drawing upon a theoretical framework of speculative civic literacies and scholarship in civic cognition, democratic education, and academic argumentation, this study analyzes the discourse practices of 238 high school students from six communities across the United States who participated in a social design-based experiment aimed at fostering democratic dialogue across lines of geographic, social, and cultural difference. Findings from a content analysis of students’ digital posts and a qualitative case study of one student’s civic literacy repertoires demonstrate that student leveraging of normative academic argument writing structures largely stifles understandings of divergent perspectives and vulnerable civic communication by flattening considerations of identity, affect, and ideology.

## Introduction

This study represents one strand of inquiry from the Digital Democratic Dialogue (3D) Project, a social designbased experiment comprised of six high school literacy teachers and their students (n=238) from demographically distinct U.S. communities who leveraged digital media to communicate across time and space in order to excavate their civic histories, analyze their current civic experiences, and dream of collective civic futures. We sought to understand how the forms of writing that young people utilized to discuss (or “argue”) civic issues with each other reflected their understandings of the civic and opened (or closed) potential pathways to expansive forms of civic cognition. Specifically, we asked: 1) How do writin g structures mediate the nature, tone, and purpose of youth civic communication? Drawing from a theoretical fra mework of speculative civic literacies, initial fin dings indicate that the use of normative forms of argumen t writing largely precluded understandings of diver gent perspectives and empathetic civic connections becau se they invited competitive debate, encouraged the use of civic heuristics, and minimized the role of affect.

Civic Cognition, Democratic Education, and Academic Literacy Instruction The model of academic argument writing rewarded on standardized literacy exams (and thus taught in an overwhelming plurality of public schools) follows a formula developed by rhetoricians in the 1980s involving the statement of a thesis supported by a collection of claims, evidence, and warrants (Toulmin et al., 1984). When the Common Core policymakers situate this model as effe ctive preparation for deliberation in civic life, t hey presuppose a utilitarian public sphere in which claims are judged logically on their merits and in whi ch citizens take actions aimed at furthering the collective public good. The underlying assumption is that teaching students how to craft arguments in language approximating objectivity using credible evidence will increase the likelihood that they can successfully advocate for their prior ities within U.S. civic institutions (Gutiérrez, 20 09). This assumption minimizes what integrative theories tell us about the social and cultural aspects of civic learning that must be attended to in instructional design to disr upt societal inequality (Nasir et al., 2021). Civic  cognition is deeply tied to expressions of identity and communit y membership and decisions often rely upon heuristi cs and appeal to emotion, which suggests that conventions of academic argumentation do not map well onto the project of successfully advocating for priorities in the world as it actually is (Bougher, 2012). Speculative Civic Literacies The speculative civic literacies framework argues that normative practices of literacy and civic engag ement are insufficient building blocks for the creation of li beratory social futures; instead, it calls for the cultivation of “expansive, creative forms of meaning-making and communication aimed at radically reorienting the nature and purpose of shared democratic life toward equity, em pathy, and justice” (Mirra & Garcia, 2020, pg. 297). The framework takes up Thomas & Stornaiuolo’s (2016) ap proach of “restorying” existing narrative forms by supporting young people to interrogate and innovate the civic narratives that are too often foisted upon them. By supporting the development of new literacy practices that speak tonew social potentialities, re-story the existing ICLS2022 Proceedings 2074 © ISLS world, and are grounded in the cultural and historical truths that young people possess, this framework evaluates writing practices based on the extent to which they push on the normative and reach toward the speculative.

## Methods

We organized the 3D Project as s social design-base d experiment that sought to collaboratively reimagi ne syncretic civic literacy learning experiences for the purposes of social transformation (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Six high school teachers representing the states of  Alaska, California, Colorado, Michigan, Pennsylvan ia, and Texas were recruited from the National Writing Proj ect network to participate in the study with one or  more of their classes. Our team developed and enacted three design cycles during the 2018-2019 school year that teachers implemented in their ELA classes as part of their core curriculum. For the purposes of the specific inquiry detailed here, we focused on the discursive practices in whi ch students engaged during Design Cycle 2 when they organized themselves into 30 online discussion groups drawing from 14 topics ranging from animal rights and the environment to healthcare and homelessness. We anal yzed 372 exchanges across the 30 discussion groups, defining an exchange as a discussion consisting of at least two posts in which students took turns res ponding to each other about an initial posting (median of 12 exchanges across each of the discussion groups.

## Preliminary Findings

When analyzing Design Cycle 2’s cross-school civic discussion groups, the 3D Project teacher team foun d that the great majority of students gravitated initially  toward writing that drew upon the academic argumen tation paradigm – and that the utilization of this paradigm tended to end exchanges quickly. We found that 83% of the discussion exchanges began with an academic argumentation move. When other students in the discussion group responded with further academic argumentation (which occurred in 74% of cases), the exchange typically ended within three further moves (87% of cases). By contrast, exchanges that began with or transitioned to civic dialogue moves tended to foster lengthier and more complex exchanges. In one example, the writing practices that Cory, a white, male 11 th grader living in a small Alaskan town, leveraged d uring his participation in the 3D Project demonstrated how academic argumentation can serve t o both mask the social, cultural, historical, and p olitical nature of civic cognition and forestall vulnerable engagement with different perspectives. During the first design cycle of the project, Cory stated, “I do not enjoy talking about myself [and] would rather argue a poi nt.” He attempted to create a dichotomy between himself and  the points that he espoused throughout the project  by appealing to principles of academic argumentation, yet his claims continually remained subjective and served to reify the very identities he sought to downplay.

## Implications and Next Steps

This study’s analysis of student civic writing and communication practices raise important questions for the fields of literacy and civic education about the cognitive processes involved in perspective-taking and the effectiveness of current paradigms of academic argumentation to support the development of productive – let alone speculative

– civic dialogue. In further analyses of the 3D Pro ject, we seek to explicate the conditions under whi ch the

practices of questioning and storytelling led to mo re generative and imaginative forms of dialogue bet ween students. In future studies, we seek to conceptuali ze and test alternative writing models that could s upport new paradigms of stance-taking and civic value-sharing.
