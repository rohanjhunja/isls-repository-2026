# From Intuition to Mechanism: Tracing Resource Activation in a Middle-School Electromagnetism Unit

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how middle school students’ intuitive ideas about electrostatic and magnetic phenomenon can serve as productive resources for learning. Grounded in knowledge in pieces (KiP) and responsive teaching (RT) perspectives, the paper focuses on the thinking of one seventh-grade student, Reuben (pseudonym), engaged in a six-day unit on static electricity, magnetism, and electromagnetism. Data sources included classroom video, worksheets, and student-generated artifacts such as models and explanations. Through iterative coding, four productive intuitions were identified and traced across activities: force as mover, attraction/repulsion, closer means stronger, and proximity. These intuitive resources, also evident in peers’ written work, supported Reuben’s development of causal and spatial reasoning about invisible forces. Findings demonstrate how instruction can build on intuitive resources to help students develop increasingly mechanistic explanations, thereby contributing to efforts that integrate responsive teaching and resource-based perspectives in science learning.

It is widely agreed that science instruction should help students build new understanding on the foundation of the knowledge and skills they bring to their classroom learning (Smith et al., 1994). Students' diverse knowledge and skills are recognized as valuable assets that can be utilized in their development of scientific understanding (diSessa & Minstrell, 1998; Warren et al., 2001). Education researchers have encouraged teachers to embrace an asset-based approach, acknowledging that students bring valuable intuitive understanding and culturally acquired knowledge to their learning experiences, both in and beyond the classroom (Adiredja, 2019; Moll et al., 2006). Research has identified student resources  for understanding science topics ranging from elementary to undergraduate levels (Hammer, 2000; Rosebery et al., 2010) and across domains such as biology (Ly, 2011), chemistry (Taber & Franco-Garcia, 2010), physics (Robertson et al., 2021), and mathematics (Levin & Walkoe, 2022). Given the power of students’ prior knowledge in their learning, it is es sential for teachers to elicit and respond to students’ ideas.

Responsive teaching describes a family of pedagogical approaches that foreground, accommodate, and build on students’ emerging ideas (Levin et al., 2013; Robertson et al., 2016; Russ et al., 2009). Studies have shown that responsiveness can support conceptual understanding (Carpenter et al., 1989), foster engagement in disciplinary practices (Hammer et al., 2012), and promote equitable participation (Warren et al., 2001). Responsiveness typically involves three interrelated processes: identifying and interpreting students’ ideas, connecting those ideas to disciplinary knowledge, and adapting instruction to foster these connections (Robertson et al., 2016; Dyer & Sherin, 2016; Lam & Chan, 2020).

The present work examines the productive ideas middle school students br ought to their engagement in a unit on electromagnetism. Pr ior research has documented p ersistent conceptual difficulties in electrostatics (Başer, M., & Geban, Ö. 2007). Students commonly enter instruction with alternative conceptions about electric charge, field interactions, and non-contact forces. This includes interpreting neutral objects as having no charge, treating electric field lines as physical entities, and misunderstanding charge transfer processes.  Research  has further show that conventional teaching approaches do not effectively address these conceptions because they do not explicitly engage students’ prior reasoning.

Constructivist perspectives  (such as knowledge in pieces  and responsive teaching perspectives) emphasize that learning builds on students’ existing ideas, making it necessary to elicit and work with their ideas, rather than replace them (Swanson et al., 2025a)  Accordingly, our learning activities were designed to elicit students’ intuitive ideas about invisible forces and support their refinement through structured opportunities for comparison, explanation, and revision within a responsive teaching framework. In the context of these activities, the present study investigates how middle school students’ intuitive ideas about electro static and magnetic  phenomena can serve as productive entry points  to learning about noncontact forces. While resource-based analyses have been extensively applied in undergraduate physics (e.g., 793 Hammer, 1996; Gupta, Hammer, & Redish, 2010), far less research has examined how younger learners draw on and refine intuitive knowledge when reasoning about invisible forces. This study asks:

1. What productive intuitions do students bring to their understanding of electrostatic and magnetic

phenomena?

2. How do students refine their thinking over the course of the unit?

## Theoretical framework

This study draws on the knowledge in pieces  (KiP) perspective (diSessa, 1993) and principles of responsive teaching (Hammer, 2000; Russ & Sherin, 2013) to frame students’ reasoning as rich with resources for scientific thinking. On the KiP view, learners make sense of phenomena by activating and reorganizing fine-grained intuitive resources. In the case of physics sense-making, these resources are often grounded in everyday experiences of motion, contact, and resistance. Intuitions drawn from physical experience are referred to as phenomenological primitives (p-prims). They shape how students interpret physical interactions, often providing self-evident causality. Learning involves reorganizing relationships between these resources and their context of use, rather than replacing them. A responsive teaching approach translates this view into the classroom by treating students’ ideas as seeds of scientific thinking. Unpacking learners’ reasoning enables them to build on their intuitive understandings of invisible forces to produce more coherent, mechanistic models of electro static and magnetic interactions.

This study draws on data from a larger, multi-year design-based research project focused on engaging middle school students in modeling and inquiry-based science learning (Swanson et al., 2025b). We focus on a seventhgrade science class taught by Ms. M, a 7th-grade teacher with over twenty-five years of experience who has partnered with our research team since 2020. This paper draws on data collected over six consecutive days during Ms. M’s implementation of a co-designed unit on electromagnetism. The unit was designed to help students investigate the relationships between electric charge, magnetic forces, and motion through a series of hands-on activities involving static electricity, magnets, e lectromagnets, and simulations. Below is the description of the sequence of learning activities over the seven days.

Description of activities Exploring static electricity (Day 1-2) Students were introduced to the phenomenon of static electricity through hands-on and digital explorations designed to elicit their intuitive ideas about invisible forces. On Day 1, they used balloons and small objects (e.g., ping-pong balls, PVC pipe) to observe how rubbing a balloon on a sweater changed its behavior, sometimes causing it to attract the small objects, sometimes to repel them. They recorded their predictions, observations, and explanations in the worksheet columns, using phrases such as “the balloon pulled the ball” or “the wall attracts it because of charge.” A short class discussion followed in which students compared how distance and rubbing intensity seemed to affect the results.

On Day 2, the class explored the PhET  simulation "John Travoltage" (PhET, n.d.), manipulating the number of times the animated figure’s foot rubbed against the carpet and observing how the charge built up and discharged to the door handle. Students noted what they saw (“more sparks when rubbed more”) and what they thought was happening (“negative charge builds up and jumps”). They documented their observations and explanations and reflected on how the simulation related to their balloon experiences, recognizing that both involved invisible forces transferring through contact and distance. The activity concluded with a brief conversation about everyday examples (static shocks, clothes sticking, balloon on wall), setting the foundation for connecting observable effects to underlying charge interactions in later lessons. Investigating magnets (Day 3-4) Building on their experiences with invisible forces in static electricity, students transitioned to exploring magnetic interactions. They engaged in several hands-on stations, including “Magnetic or Not?” (testing objects such as paper clips, coins, and plastics for attraction to a magnet), “Lining Up” (observing how iron filings align around a bar magnet), and “Floating Magnets” (stacking ring magnets to observe attraction and repulsion). Working in pairs, students filled in data tables and sketches on their worksheets, noting patterns such as which materials were magnetic and how iron filings consistently pointed in one direction when the magnet was placed beneath them. 794 On Day 4, the class moved toward conceptual explanations. Students were prompted to compare magnetism and static electricity, discussing similarities in how both involved forces acting through space without contact. Several students described these effects  as “fields” or “spaces” between objects where forces could “push” or “pull.” Using examples from their investigations, they reasoned that “the magnet has something around it like the charge does with the balloon.” The teacher guided them to articulate how this invisible region could be represented and how direction mattered, laying the groundwork for understanding magnetic field lines and force interactions in subsequent lessons.

Exploring electromagnetism and design applications (Days 5–6) On Day 5, students examined a shake flashlight to trace cause-and-effect relationships between its parts and how light was produced. They observed that shaking the flashlight caused internal movement of a magnet through a coil and recorded their explanations, describing how “the moving magnet makes electricity” or “energy from shaking turns into light.” Through discussion, they identified each component, magnet, wire coil, and bulb, and reasoned about how these interacted to complete the circuit. The investigation then shifted to constructing and testing electromagnets. Students wrapped copper wire around iron nails, connected the ends to a battery, and observed how many paper clips each setup could pick up. They varied the number of coils and recorded observations and hypotheses such as “adding more loops makes it stronger.” On Day 6, students drew pictorial models of their electromagnets, labeling the battery, wire, and nail, and using arrows to indicate current direction and areas of magnetic attraction. Building on this, they assembled a homopolar motor, watching a small wire spin rapidly above a battery and magnet. Students discussed what caused the motion and compared it to their earlier electromagnet observations, recognizing that electric current and magnetic fields together can produce movement. The lesson concluded with students connecting these experiences to the broader question of how magnetism and electricity work together in engineered systems. Data sources included classroom video recordings, and learner-generated models and explanations. A structured coding protocol was used and codes were entered into a shared coding sheet. Data were first organized by instructional sequence (day, activity/cen ter, and worksheet prompt), allowing us to situate student reasoning within specific task contexts in a temporal order. Student responses were then segmented at the level of meaningful idea units, typically corresponding to explanatory statements or causal claims in written or verbal form. Each idea unit was coded using previously documented p-prims (diSessa, 1993), with codes applied alongside brief descriptions to capture how the reasoning was instantiated in context (e.g., attraction/ repulsion; closer means stronger). In addition to identifying p-prims published in the KiP literature, we documented accompanying elements of everyday thinking to track how intuitive reasoning aligned with or diverged from canonical explanations. The coding sheet also supported temporal comparison, as some prompts asked students to revisit and refine earlier explanations. This enables analysis of the stability and transformation of reasoning across the course of instruction. In this way, the  analysis adopted a primarily deductive, top-down approach, guided by established p-prims from the KiP framework. This approach was appropriate given the study’s aim to trace the activation and coordination of well-theorized intuitive resources within a de signed learning environment. At the same time, coding remained attentive to instances where student responses did not map cleanly onto existing pprims. Two researchers engaged in a process of social moderation (Erickson, 1986) to reach consensus on how student responses reflected particular p-prims. Cases where student reasoning did not map cleanly to previously documented p-prims were flagged within the coding sheet and discussed during social moderation to determine whether they reflected ambiguity in expression, context-specific extensions of exist ing p-prims, or entirely new resources. After coding classroom video excerpts, student worksheets, and lear ner-generated artifacts, four p-prims, force as mover, attraction/repulsion, proximity, and closer means stronger, emerged as the most frequent across students and activities.

To illustrate these reasoning patterns in depth, one focal student, referred to by the pseudonym Reuben, was selected as a representative case (Stake, 1995) because his written and verbal responses were detailed and appeared to represent class-wide reasoning.

RQ 1: Productive resources for reasoning about electricity and magnetism 795 Our analysis of Reuben’s work from the six-day activity sequence revealed four previously documented p-prims that he and his classmates  productively drew on to make sense of non-contact interactions and to build toward more sophisticated reasoning about electrostatic and magnetic forces: force as mover, attraction/repulsion, closer means stronger, and proximity. Each emerged across multiple lessons and supported students in reasoning about invisible forces, spatial relationships, and causal mechanisms that underlie magnetic and electric phenomena. Force as mover Reuben reasoned that motion occurs because one object actively makes another move: treating the cause of motion as an external push or pull.

Day 1 (Center 4 – Charged PVC pipe near water stream): Prompt:  Bring the charged PVC pipe near the water stream and predict what will happen. Make a hypothesis to explain why.

Reuben: “Water bends around or toward the PVC because it has been attracted to the static.” Reuben treats the water as being invisibly pushed or pulled by the pipe. The phrase “attracted to the static” implies an unseen action transmitted across distance. Day 3 (Center 2 – Lining up with iron filings): Prompt:  Slide the magnet around the surface of the case. What happens to the filings? Reuben:  “They follow the magnet.” The magnet is viewed as a continuous mover; the filings move because the magnet moves, preserving a causal link between agent and effect. Reuben’s statements from these two activities reveal a consistent causal framing in which force is conceived as an agent that sets objects into motion. Productive entry point This resource can be used to anchor abstract field concepts in students’ embodied experiences of action and motion. By perceiving the PVC pipe or magnet as active movers, students recognize that objects can influence one another without contact: an essenti al foundation for understanding forces as field-mediated interactions. Through successive activities, this intuition can be refined from “the magnet makes it move” to a mechanistic model in which fields exert forces through space.

Attraction/repulsion Students consistently applied a binary schema that things either pull together or push apart, depending on their “kind.” This intuitive reasoning framed interactions in terms of opposition or similarity, shaping how students explained visible motion between charged or magnetic objects. Day 1 (Balloon on sweater): Prompt:  Rub a balloon on the sweater. What happens?

Reuben: “Balloon is attracted and sticks to sweater” Student reinforces the push/pull and attraction intuition: rubbing generates a condition that produces sticking. The statement demonstrates growing causal reasoning, recognizing a prior action (rubbing) as the source of the effect (attraction).

Day 1, (Floating plates): Prompt:  Rub each balloon with cloth, place one plate face down on the table, the other on top of it. Try to get the top plate to float.

Reuben:  “Plate floated above the other plate […] because of the magnetic electricity that made it float." Prompt:  Explain your understanding of how this works.

Reuben:  “They were the same charge, so they were repelled and one plate floated." Reuben transitions from describing “floating” as mysterious (“magnetic electricity”) to recognizing charge-based repulsion as the cause, showing conceptual refinement from perceptual to mechanistic explanation. Day 3, (Post lab question): 796 Prompt:  What did you observe when you brought two magnets close? Describe how orientation of magnets N to N and N to S affected their interaction. Reuben:  “If both were negative, they would push but if one has negative and one is positive, they would go together.” Reuben recognizes like poles repel and opposite poles attract. He uses charge-like terminology (“positive,” “negative”), connecting his reasoning about magnetism with what he has learned about electrostatics. Productive entry point The attraction/repulsion p-prim provided a framework for sensemaking across contexts, from electrostatic sticking to magnetic floating. It enabled students to predict and explain outcomes before formal instruction on electrical charge or magnetic poles. As discussions and experiments progressed, this intuitive schema was refined from a descriptive binary rule (“together/apart”) to a mechanistic understanding that connects interaction type to charge or pole configuration. This productive activation illustrates how students’ intuitive binaries can evolve into formal models of mutual force and polarity-based interaction in electromagnetism. Closer means stronger Students intuitively linked the strength of an effect to the distance between objects, recognizing a continuous rather than all-or-none relationship. This p-prim reflects an early sense of proportionality: the closer two interacting objects are, the stronger the effect they exert on each other. Day 2, (John Travoltage simulation): Prompt:  Reset Balloons and charge both with an equal amount of charge. Stick one to the wall and other to the middle of the sleeve: How did distance affect the electric force? Reuben:  “ Closer the object is the stronger the force is, further the object, lesser the force. ” Here, Reuben articulates an explicit inverse dependence on distance, revealing emerging quantitative reasoning about field strength and a shift from perceptual noticing to relational thinking. Day 5, (Electromagnet test): Prompt:  What do you think will make the electromagnet stronger? Reuben:  “More coils and tighter coils and maybe a thicker wire.” Reuben extends the same strength-by-increase schema to current-carrying wire, treating greater physical closeness and quantity (tighter, more coils) as directly amplifying magnetic power. Productive entry point This intuition was one of the most generative activations in the sequence. By attending to how effects vary with distance and configuration, students began constructing gradient-based reasoning central to electromagnetic fields. The “closer → stronger” resource naturally evolves into quantitative models of field intensity and inversedistance relationships, providing a bridge from everyday observation to mathematical representation of force Proximity Students reasoned that effects only occur when two objects are sufficiently near each other, beyond a certain threshold distance, the effect disappears. This p-prim frames interaction as spatially bounded, implying that invisible forces have a limited range of influence.

Day 2, (John Travoltage simulation): Prompt:  Reset Balloons and charge both with an equal amount of charge. Stick one to the wall and the other to the middle of the sleeve, bringing the balloon closer or further away from the balloon on sweater. How did distance affect the electric force? Reuben:  “Closer the object is the stronger the force is, further the object, lesser the force.” Reuben articulates a clear threshold-like reasoning: interaction strength increases only when objects are close enough, reflecting the perception that invisible forces act within a limited range. Day 2, (John Travoltage simulation): Prompt: How does distance affect the strength of the electric charge? 797 Reuben: “The further away the less charge acting on it, and the closer the more force acting on it.” Reuben demonstrates a strong activation of the proximity p-prim, applying it productively across contexts. The statement shows movement from local perceptual reasoning (“pushed away”) toward a generalized causal pattern that can serve as a bridge to formal understanding of field strength and inverse-square relationships. Productive entry point Although initially categorical (“works only when close”), proximity sensitized students to the spatial limits of invisible forces. By focusing attention on when effects appear or vanishing, this resource helped them conceptualize fields as regions, areas in which interaction occurs and gradually diminishes with distance. In this way, proximity served as a natural foundation for understanding field boundaries and range of influence  in electromagnetism.

RQ 2: Temporal refinement of productive resources Across the six-day unit, Reuben’s reasoning revealed a temporal progression in terms of how intuitive knowledge resources were activated, refined, and recombined to support increasingly mechanistic understandings of invisible forces. This section represents the second layer of analysis, tracing when particular p-prims appeared, and how their coordination across lessons led to more sophisticated reasoning about electrostatic and magnetic phenomena, as shown in Fig 1. Activities with charged balloons and static electricity activated force as mover  and attraction/repulsion, allowing Reuben to interpret non-contact motion through familiar push/pull causality. These embodied intuitions established a foundation for treating invisible forces as real and directional. As investigations shifted to magnetism, closer means stronger and proximity extended this reasoning to spatial variation, helping Reuben notice how distance changes the strength or visibility of effects. By the electromagnetism activities, these resources recombined: causal (mover) and spatial (closer/threshold) reasoning were coordinated to explain how increased coils or current strengthen  magnetic action. Together, these activations illustrate a developmental trajectory from perceptual noticing to more mechanistic understanding, showing that intuitive p-prims can serve as productive cognitive resources for building toward scientific models  of electromagnetic fields and forces. The sequence highlights that productive learning was not a linear replacement of everyday ideas but a gradual reorganization of those ideas across contexts. Early lessons grounded students’ thinking in observable motion (e.g., objects moving when a magnet is nearby), which later supported reasoning about distance-based effects and interactions without contact. Subsequent activities helped students extend these ideas to spatial variation in field strength and, eventually, to a coordinated understanding of electromagnetic interactions as mediated through fields. This progression shows how revisiting and refining the same underlying ideas across contexts can support increasingly coherent reasoning about invisible forces. 798 Our analysis shows how students’ intuitive knowledge can function as a productive foundation for reasoning electrostatic and magneti c phenomena  when instruction is designed to elicit, connect, and refine rather than replace. Force as mover, attraction/repulsion, closer means stronger, and proximity represent locally productive knowledge elements that Reuben activated and reorganized across contexts. These resources were identified through an iterative coding process of written and verbal responses, focusing on instances where language reflected causal reasoning about invisible forces (Hammer, 2000). Across the six-day sequence, Reuben’s reasoning revealed that intuitive knowledge can be a seed for developing scientific knowledge. Force as a m over and attraction/repulsion grounded causal sensemaking in embodied experience, while closer means stronger and proximity extended this reasoning toward spatial variation and gradient strength. Together, these activations created conceptual continuity between everyday intuition and formal models of field-mediated interaction.

Throughout the sequence, instructional prompts such as “How did distance affect the force?” positioned the learner’s own explanations as objects of reflection. This responsive approach drew on the substance of the student’s thinking to guide subsequent exploration. Rather than correcting intuitive claims, teacher questions leverage them as opportunities for refinement, allowing causal and spatial reasoning to become increasingly coordinated across phenomena.

Viewed through this lens, the sequence illustrates how inquiry and reflection enable intuitive p-prims to be reorganized into more mechanistic explanations. Each investigation, from manipulating static charge, to exploring iron filings and testing electromagnets, invited comparison and reinterpretation of familiar experiences, fostering continuity across static, magnetic, and electromagnetic contexts. Viewing learning through a KiP perspective highlight  learning as a process of reorganizing existing resources through focused feedback. When instruction utilizes responsive teaching, it builds on students’ intuitive patterns of reasoning, enabling the progressive refinement of embodied ideas into coherent scientific models of fields and forces.

## Implications

This study underscores the value of designing instruction that elicits and builds on students’ intuitive resources, rather than replacing them. By tracing how p-prims such as  force as m over, attraction/repulsion, closer means stronger, and proximity were activated and refined across a six-day electromagnetism unit, we demonstrate that conceptual growth can emerge through reorganization of prior ideas. For practitioners, this suggests that effective teaching of abstract phenomena like non-contact forces depends on providing repeated opportunities for students to revisit intuitive ideas in varied contexts, with guidance that helps them make causal and spatial connections explicit. For teacher education, the analysis highlights the importance of cultivating teachers’ professional vision for recognizing productive moments in student reasoning and leveraging them through responsive questioning. For researchers, the temporal decomposition of resource activation offers a methodological lens for examining learning as a piecemeal process of knowledge refinement rather than a single conceptual shift. Designing learning environments that foreground the continuity of everyday and scientific thinking through cycles of eliciting, connecting, and refining can foster more productive and equitable participation in science, where students’ everyday reasoning is treated as an essential foundation for disciplinary understanding.
