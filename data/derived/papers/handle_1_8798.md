# Solidarity in Shifting Lines of Practice: Learning in the Weave of Social Movements

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While learning sciences researchers have explored forms of solidarity underpinning learning and relations across powered forms of social difference, they have largely not integrated insights from social movements literature. Building with lines of practice theory (Azevedo, 2011, 2013), we analyze ethnographic interviews with animal rights activists to understand how power relations and the felt obligations of privilege shape how individual lines of practice develop within the collective pursuit of justice. We find that individual learners feel the implications of their place in the “weave,” as one thread among many, shaping their practice in ways that are neither internal nor external, but fundamentally relational.

As the learning sciences have embraced questions of power, politics, and ethics in recent years, a the me around relationships of solidarity has organically emerged. In analysis of classrooms, of social movements, o f extracurricular activities, and other spaces, we have seen the significance of relationships of solidarity across diverse contexts. This is a promising field of analysis, wi th much to offer our understandings of who is learn ing, when, and how, and the emergent work actively addresses racialization and gender as significant relations that must be accounted for. However, across the different analys es, scholars have theorized the relations different ly and inconsistently, generating descriptive terminologie s steeped in specific contexts. As social movementattentive/immersed researchers, we read all of thes e examples as residing under the umbrella of solida rity, yet without attending to the social movement usage or the academic treatments of solidarity that underpin their use. This additional literature productively supplements  learning sciences theorization of relational enactments of solidarity. The learning sciences have long been sensitive to accounts of learning as an interpersonal phenomenon (Vygotsky, 1978), but have often left power relations under-examined (Esmonde, 2017; The Politics of Learning Writing Collective, 2017). Learning research in movements helps correct this, enabling new analyses of collective learning in the form of larger transformation in social structures that movements effect. There is also value in examining how learning transforms individuals within movements and how power inflects that process. Theories of learning in out-of-school spaces, such as “lines of practice” (Azevedo, 2011, 2013) and “cultural learning pathways” (Bell et al., 2012) can help us bridge these theoretical conversations. We revisit these ideas in the context of more recent work on relatio nships in politicized learning contexts to build on  what they offer for our accounts of individual learning withi n solidary relations. We seek to orient learning sc iences researchers to theories of solidarity in order to a nchor the emergent work on relationality in the bro ader theory and praxis of social movements. We then use the conceptual tools from solidarity studies to analyze the relations of solidarity that show up in our own research in social movements to articulate the messiness of learning in and from relations of solidarity and the contradictions that are inherent to those relationships. We argue that when we examine the learning of individuals as they act on the basis of solidarity in social movements, we can  see how their learning is constrained and opened up not only in relation to their own constraints and scopes of possibility, but in the deeply affective ways in which they understand the constraints and scopes of possibility of others. In the sections that follow, we briefly highlight work in the learning sciences that fits under the umbrella of solidarity, and then review social movement theories of solidarity to ground learning sciences work in larger conversations around political organizing across di fference. We also bring to bear insights from out-o f-school learning and how people develop increasingly sophisticated practice. We then provide an overview of the animal rights activist group Direct Action Everywhere, our data collection, and our methods for analyzing these shifting pathways and possibilities as activists mobilized solidarity frames. We analyze the data from activists reflecting on the solidarity tactics they attempted, tying them to both the theories of solidarity we review, and reading them through the lens of lines of practice. We conclude by arguing that learning in solidarity reveals important insights about the relational and collective nature of individual development. We see evidence of solidarity across many examples of recent work. Using humor, teasing, self-deprecation, and other interactional practices, these young people focused on building relationships of care wh ich both expressed and enabled their political work. In their work with promotor as in a mostly low-income and Mexican immigrant neighborhoo d, they examined how relations of confianza enabled expansive forms of learning by laying a relational foundation on which community change could be built. Vea (2020a) examined how friendship forged in shared ex periences of community enjoyment in an animal right s collective helped make learning possible through emotional configurations that enabled and sustained new forms of politics. Curnow et al. (2020) argued that relationships based in shared experiences of gendered marginalization within a social movement organization created space s where snarky critiques of masculinity and mainstr eam environmentalism led to stronger relationships and stronger political analysis through dialectical and  iterative processes. In each of these cases, shared experience was a foundation for political learning and change. The work of McKinney de Royston, Vakil, and colleagues (McKinney de Royston et al., 2017; Vakil & McKinney de Royston, 2019) both demonstrates the im portance of explicitly race-conscious solidarity and the consequences of its absence. McKinney de Royston et al. (2017) found that in a program for Black middle school boys taught by Black men, educators developed an ex plicitly politicized notion of caring in which acad emic success and addressing systemic racial inequities was seen as preconditioned on the social-emotional wellbeing of the Black youth and the intentional cultivation of strong instructor-student relationships. Vakil and McKinney de Royston (2019) developed the idea of “politicized trust” as encompassing mutual understanding, respect, and solidarity. Focusing on solidarity as an interactional accomplishment, they found that a lack of polit icized trust between a Black/Eritrean student and a white studen t ultimately limited the possibilities for equity-o riented learning in a multiracial classroom. While in one c ase, relationships based in shared racialized exper ience supported an explicitly politicized form of learnin g, in the other case, a lack of racial and politica l solidarity interrupted opportunities for learning.

These contributions demonstrate the significance of  relationality for shaping learning, and they put politics at the center of those relationships. In attending to marginalized communities, they highlight learning that happens in and through struggle, along with the joy, intimacy, and trust that emerge from those spaces. But these analyses do not draw on the larger body of scholars hip around solidarity and the complicated, and ofte n contradictory, relations of power that emerge when people work and learn across difference, especially in cases where people in positions of relative power and pri vilege are working for and alongside those with les s power, while contesting those very power relations they seek to leverage. These messy politics and relationships across difference push learning scientists to bring stronger theories of solidarity into focus alongside conceptual tools for understanding learning, finding new points of synthesis that can strengthen our understandings of both solidarity and learning. To do this, we now turn to the social movement and scholar activist literatures of solidarity to expand the discussion and anchor it in social movement praxis.

## Social movement perspectives on solidarity

We focus on theories of solidarity rooted in social  movement contexts, many of which are written by sc holaractivists embedded in solidarity campaigns, and we note that these applications that stem from the gro und are significantly different than the political theories that circulate (Curnow & Helferty 2017). Solidarity is defined as activism in one location—geographical, socio-econom ic, political—that works to “defend the interests, rights, and identities” of people in other locations (Olese n, 2005; Sundberg, 2007). The central premise of so lidarity, from activist perspectives, is that solidarity is defined by its attention to work on behalf of others (Finley-Brook & Hoyt, 2009), though many stress that solidarity i s not done on behalf of others—it is not charity. K oopman

(2012) argues that the difference between charity and solidarity is foundational: where charity makes no effort to

disrupt systems or imbalances of power (it is orien ted to helping people navigate existing systems) so lidarity is constructed as a process of amplifying voices of ma rginalized people (Koopman, 2012; Mahrouse, 2014) o r standing alongside the oppressed (Brown & Yaffe, 20 14; Shohat, 2001). These campaigns are based in sha red vision of collective liberation (Finley-Brook & Hoyt, 2009; Hussey & Curnow, 2016; Mahrouse, 2014). Across the strategies for solidarity, the distingui shing features are a recognition of inequitable pow er relations and an attempt to prefigure different kinds of relationships. This means that solidarity strategies explicitly attempt to use disparate access to power and privil ege as part of their strategic interventions (Koopm an 2012; ICLS2022 Proceedings 267 © ISLS Mahrouse, 2014). Solidarity campaigns often rely on  the participation of people who benefit from struc tural privilege in current systems working within their own communities to spread understanding of privilege and grow the base of people working to unmake systems of opp ression (Finley-Brook & Hoyt, 2009; Hussey & Curnow, 2016; Koopman, 2012), or to strategically focus on advocacy work withi n their own countries in support of campaigns elsewhere in the world (Finley-Brook & Ho yt, 2009). The research and campaigns framed around solidarity stress that mutual solidarity is possible and is built on resisting systemic power imbalances (Sundberg, 2007). Reviewing this work on various theorizations  of solidarity, Curnow and Helferty (2018) argue th at the contradictions of solidarity are significant, and t hat the ideas about using racialized power and priv ilege to counteract it ultimately rely on and reinforce thos e very positions of power. They argue that ideas of  solidarity are often incommensurable, and that the tensions em bedded within solidarity relations, while not resol ving anything in a tidy or satisfying way, do work to “remain unreconciled” (Whetung, 2017) and create opportunities for new political formations, both structurally and in practice and consciousness. For a learning sciences perspective, this body of w ork demands attention to how individual learners within movements make sense of their own locations relative to power and relative to others alongside whom they are learning. It also suggests the importance of lo oking closely at how these personalized and other-o riented analyses of power are felt and translated into poli tical and moral obligations to act in specific ways  within the overall collective. In order to achieve group goals, individuals acting in solidarity must learn to interpret what the power arrangement means for how their own trajectories of learning ought to take shape. Building on the navigation of “lines” and “pathways” Learning scientists have demonstrated how power arr angements shape individual learning in out-of-schoo l pursuits. For example, Azevedo’s (2011, 2013) concept of “lines of practice” suggests how patterns of interestdriven activity cluster at intersections between in dividuals’ “preferences” and “conditions of practic e” in their environment. “Preferences” refer to the “variety of  long-term goals, values, and beliefs” that shape a  learner’s pursuits (Azevedo, 2011, p. 162). They are conceived in individualized and interiorized ways, as “elements in a person’s psychology” (p. 163). “Conditions of practice” involve constraints and affordances that shape practice: the material infrastructure available to the practi tioner (e.g., rocket kits of varied assembly complexity), the immediate circumstances of the hob byist’s life (e.g., the person’s socioeconomic status and whether or not he/she has easy access to practice sites), the larger social, cultural and institutional spaces in which the hobby exists (e.g., the availability of hobby stores), and so forth. (p. 163) Azevedo’s research revealed that preferences and co nditions of practice were in dynamic interrelations hip with one another. They seemed to “cluster” into particular expressions of activity, or “lines of practice” along which the constraints and affordances external to the learner could be attuned in ways that satisfied and extended their preferences. In this way, lines of practice represent “the deep patterns of engagement” of an individual learner’s activity over time (p. 163). A line of practice is a particularized and developing form of life that a n individual navigates in value-driven and pragmatic ways.

A second navigational metaphor from the literature is “cultural learning pathways,” defined as “connected chains of personally consequential activ ity and sense-making” (Bell et al., 2012, p. 270). These pathways develop over the life course, spandiverse  settings, and reflect the values that guide the ac tivity of individuals and social groups. Like lines of practice theory, Bell et al. specify that cultural learning pathways are a way to account for the “opportunities and impediments faced by learners” (p. 271). They draw attention to the ways that “individuals and groups arrange or transf orm the conditions of their own learning in relatio n to their expectations, interests, concerns, and available resources” (p. 271). Bell et al. focus on acts of coo rdination that people make between their concerns and the arrangements available to them. As acts of coordination accrete over time, analysis reveals how “people come to particip ate in specific forms of social practices in increa singly sophisticated and socially coordinated ways” (p. 274). The authors present the notion of “scopes of po ssibility” to describe the dynamic interplay between learner’s actions, the social and material dimensions of place, and the social positions available within settings (p. 277).

In this paper, we build on these ideas by putting t hem into conversation with social movement theorizations of solidarity and learning sciences l iterature examining the politicized contexts in whi ch relationships across difference shape the possibili ties for learning and social transformation. Learni ng to be in solidarity entails undertaking personal and other-o riented analyses of power. Learners must conceptual ly understand how differential access to power, along racialized, class-based, or even species-based lines condition the practices they and others can engage in on the way to social transformation. This conceptual under standing ICLS2022 Proceedings 268 © ISLS must be combined with an axiological commitment, involving an emotionally rooted regard for the other, to act in ways that others may not be able. In this way, an individual’s line of practice or learning pathway in the context of solidarity, while individually articulated, is always perforated by social others—by what we know and feel of their positions in relations of power, and what we know and feel about how we ought to practice as a r esult. A solidarity relationship marks the connection between at least two lines of practice, and the ways in which ethical and political commitments shape their trajectories in tandem. Working together in solidarity may counterintuitively require lines of practice to diverge.

## Context: Direct Action Everywhere

This research was conducted with Direct Action Ever ywhere’s (DxE) San Francisco Bay Area chapter, the original chapter of an international animal rights network founded in 2012. Organizers reported that by 2017, the chapter had approximately 150 active members. Activists in DxE SF Bay Area were mostly White, though many Asian, Latinx, and Black activists also participated. The group aimed to shift social norms around the use of morethan-human animals through direct action, ultimately working to achieve “total animal liberation.” For example, the activists held protests in restaurants and grocery stores. They also conducted “open rescues,” using video to document conditions in farm facilities and leaving with sick and injured animals. For DxE, shifting social norms also required politicizing existing vegans within the organization so that they were prepared to take direct action. While many social movement organizations use solidarity as a coordinating frame, animal rights activism attend to these ideas in a distinctive way. For man y of the tactics, motivational frames, and emotiona l configurations (Vea, 2020b) deployed in animal rights, solidarity is mobilized unconventionally, in that it relies on ideas of cross-species help, rather than a colle ctive liberation approach. However, there are place s where mainstream solidarity discourses are visible, as we will explore in the data below. In these places we see solidarity emerge as a strategy and tactic between participants within the DxE community.

## Methods

The larger study on which this analysis is based us ed participant observation and ethnographic intervi ews to understand how people learned to become animal righ ts activists and participate in the valued practice s of the group. Vea engaged in participant observation with DxE SF Bay Area between December 2014 and May 2015 and between October 2016 and July 2017. Settings in cluded protests, social events, trainings, outreach  events, working meetings, and more. Vea wrote fieldnotes (Emerson et al., 2011) which became a primary data source. While analysis of fieldnotes inform the analytical claims, here we focus primarily on ethnographic int erviews with current and former DxE activists. Interview participants included 10 women and 10 men, ranging from earlyadulthood to middle-age and were majority White, with two identified as Latinx, one as Asian, and one as biracial. Vea asked about personal stories of changing involvement with respect to vegetarianism, veganism, animal rights, and activism activities. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed. To analyze the data, Vea coded the transcripts for privilege, risk, danger, and other concepts that relate to the idea of speakers using their relative power and position to enact change. A parallel track of coding focused on emotion, and how emotion was articulated, made s ense of, and cultivated (see Vea, 2020b). These par allel processes of coding help illuminate the ways in which individuals’ developing lines of practice were shaped both by a political analysis of where vulnerability resi des—and their own position relative to it—and by co nstructed emotional meanings that attached to their analysis. The coding was conducted iteratively, and the instances from the transcripts shared below are selected for their  ability to illustrate the multiple ways solidarity  showed up in the data, along with learning around power, emotion, and risk that supported those axiological and tactical choices.

## Findings: Lines of practice as threads in the weave

Unlike some other forms of interest-driven learning in out-of-school settings, participation in the practices of DxE carried some embodied and legal risks. In preparation for protests, DxE organizers always reviewed the principles of nonviolence with activists. In addition to being  sound strategy, they believed, nonviolence could a lso help prevent potential confrontations with bystanders from escalating to violence. DxE also assigned police liaisons at most actions. These people were trained to interface with law enforcement, if they were to show up, and all other activists were instructed not to speak with police under any circumstances. Other tactics, such as open rescue—a civil disobedience tactic that included breaking in to farms to collect information, bear witness, and remove animals for placement at sanctuaries—involved even more risk. To be trained to take part in open rescue, activists needed to sign a non-disclosure agreement, a measure DxE used to prevent infiltrators from industry groups. Risks were also mitigated through workshops on digital se curity and how to handle potential police interroga tion, for ICLS2022 Proceedings 269 © ISLS example, and DxE had a Legal Working Group who exam ined legal issues behind upcoming actions and who could be called up in case of arrest.

Taking on these risky strategies was often framed t hrough a lens of solidarity. Activists explained th at the open rescues and confrontational tactics with p eople they assumed would not be supportive were a c ritical way for them to build awareness of the conditions f acing more-than-human animals. As with the literatu re on solidarity, they believed that they had a responsib ility to use their relative positions of power in s ervice to the political struggle they engaged. Even as they conte sted the injustice of these systems (whether it be white supremacy or human supremacy), they intended to lev erage those power imbalances strategically. This te nsion created opportunities to learn and shift their scop es of possibility that are not easily explained in terms of “preferences” and “conditions of practice.” In this section, we introduce excerpts from intervi ews with three White DxE activists, each of whom were grappling with what their privilege meant for how they should pursue their activist practice. Yet  each of them articulated their understanding of privilege a nd solidarity in different ways. In our analysis, w e attend to how each activist developed a line of activist prac tice in relation to other lines of existence—both h uman and more-than-human. If a movement was made up of many individuals’ developing lines of practice, the movement itself can be seen as a woven fabric that uses all threads within it to reach toward justice. Daniel: Practicing community protection In some cases, DxE activists foregrounded human-human power relations in explaining why they were developing their activist practice in particular directions. For example, a young White woman named Daniel explained why she was training to be a police liaison: One of the reasons that I want to be a police liaison is to contribute to my community but also because I AM White. I have more privilege, and I feel like, okay, this is a way that I can really exercise my privilege in a way that is going to protect my community. I can physically put my body between police and demonstrators.

In her discussion, Daniel drew on the social-movement-rooted theories of solidarity that demand that people use relative racial privilege to provide safety to other people and to advance political goals through consciously using racial privilege to undermine white supremacy. She centers her racial position as the rationale for he r tactical choice and her specific role as a police liaison—the rationale being that police are less likely to brutalize a White person than someone racialized as a person of color.

It’s very stressful, [laugh] and it’s scary. It’s not something that I ENJOY doing. I don’t WANT to talk to police, but it’s a role that’s needed, and it’s one that I can use my privilege to do. Daniel’s line of practice in the role of police liaison is shaped by a sense of duty that emerges from her concern for the conditions of precarity affecting others in her community. She was good friends with many other activists, some of whom were members of communities she understood to be marginalized, and those relationships meant that her understanding of privilege was not just intellectual but deeply felt in her day-to-day work with the group. Daniel acknowledged that emotion could have pushed her in ways that constrained her participation as a  police liaison. She found it “stressful” and “scary,” but it was nonetheless an obligation for her to take up  the role and its practices. In this sense, Daniel’s felt freedom, a recognition of her privilege as allowing her range of motion in her activist practice that was not afforded to others, also entailed its own feeling of constraint. In this example, we see Daniel’s “preferences” (Aze vedo, 2011) come into direct conflict with other considerations that guide her line of practice as a developing activist. In particular, we see Daniel grappling with how her position within racialized power relations requires her morally and politically to step up to meet the overall needs of DxE’s cause. Central to this reasoning is the fact that others in the community cannot practice in the ways she can, due to her privilege, so she feels an obligation to “protect [her] community.” Daniel intentionally leveraged whiteness to insulate others from police violence—a strategy rooted in activist solidarity. In performing solidarity in this way, her ethical and tactical choices reflect an understanding of whiteness and white supremacy that is imbued with emotional meaning from her frie ndships and community ties. Her talk in the intervi ew also demonstrates some of her mixed feelings about this e thical obligation. Her preference would clearly be not to develop a line of practice as police liaison. First, we see Tyler adopting the mainstream-in-move ments ideas of solidarity, where power and privileg e are considered a rationale for taking up specific actions in service to less-powerful others, and in the process implicitly troubling the power relationship even as he relies on it. Tyler was aware of how the thread of his practice stood out in relation to the threads of those others, and it allowed him to feel he could make a particularly important contribution to the movement.

## Conclusions and significance

For members of DxE, practicing solidarity by using their privilege in service to the protection of less structurallyempowered others was an animating tactic and ethic. The idea of taking on risk to serve both human and  morethan-human partners drove the tactical decisions, the motivational frames, the emotional configurations, and the ethical foundation of these activists. We see the s ocial movement idea of solidarity extended across f orms of humandifference such as race and ability, and we c an also see the activists grappling with what those  forms of difference mean in relation to the species line as a powered form of social difference understudied in the solidarity literature. DxE members wrestled in multiple complex ways with the personal costs of solidarity and their theories of change as they related to power, privilege, and risk. For the learning sciences, this analysis shifts the focus of solidarity work. Where others in the field have productively considered how relationships of trust,  intimacy, and shared politics enable particular le arning ecologies, this work elucidates learning processes that at once draw members’ individual cognition toward power relations, while also shifting their collective lines of practice. Expanding the analytical power of lines of practice theory through solidarity highlights the ways that individual development of practice is shaped by com plex awareness of collective goals for social change (the more just future toward which the collective weave of lines is reaching) and of what other lines of practice in the collective are best positioned to do, according to their place in powered relations such as race, gender, or ability. We see this theoretical intervention as an expansion of lines of practice theory, toward a more rigorous accounting of how individuals’ lines of learning and development are always being navigated among the thickly woven line s of others. In our analysis, the understanding act ivists expressed about their place in the weave was more than a form of declarative knowledge. It was deeply felt as an emotional and axiological obligation to do right, according to the privilege one has. If “preferences” are seen as internal factors and “conditions of practice” are understood as external factors shaping an individual’s developing line of practice, the notion of the weave draws our  attention to the ultimate impossibility of separat ing what is internal and external in learning in solidarity. In fact, it is the powered relation between self and others that defined how these activists understood how and why to develop their practice in the ways they did. There’s a clear move from learning scientists to study the learning that is made possible in spaces shaped by solidarity. This move entails an interest in stu dying the solidarity made possible through learning  as well as how people learn about and practice solidarity. Act ualizing just social futures is not just about spec ulation or design work. It requires the right relations between people to carry out the work of acting in solidarity, even when it is difficult, long, and may conflict with some individuals’ supposed self-interest. For learning settings beyond social movements, educators and designers should co nsider whether learning settings make space for rel ational accountability and solidarity to emerge, or whether they foreclose those possibilities. Researchers should consider whether and how the felt freedom of privilege or the moral impingement of obligation shape individuals’ learning and participation. Though the embodied risks may be  less severe, all learning is done with others. It matters whether our theorizations are sensitized to what it  means to learn amid the weave, or whether they rei fy the supposedly stable boundaries between individuals. Combining social movement theorizations of solidarity with learning sciences theorizations of interest-driven practice in out-of-school settings helps reveal wha t people understand about how they are in relation with others in their learning activity, and how those understandings bear on what they cando together in the future. If the learning sciences want to concern themselves with the making ICLS2022 Proceedings 272 © ISLS of more just social futures, then we need to understand how people know and feel the obligations of their practice according to their place in the weave of the collective.
