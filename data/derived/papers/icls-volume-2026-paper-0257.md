# Transformative Disruptions: Using Call-In Strategies To Enact Solidarity In Community Partnerships

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As the Learning Sciences have continued discussing subtle nuances related to community partnerships, there has been emphasis on how to enact solidarity, healing, and care. In this piece, we reflect on our enactments of solidarity and care by sharing about moments of calling-in, which we refer to as moments of curiously challenging. We use examples from our co-design sessions to showcase how call-ins function as transformative disruptions of harmful knowledges and how these moments are situated within other relationship building practices. We call on other scholars within the Learning Sciences community to consider the complexities surrounding systemized mindsets and how to attend to harmful productions and reproductions of knowledge within community-based partnerships.

Community-engaged research methodologies have been extensively discussed within the Learning Sciences. These discussions have organically resulted in methodological contributions that focus both on the subtle nuances between partnership approaches (e.g., research-practice partnerships, participatory action research, community-based design). In this piece, we build on community-engaged research methods foregrounding solidarity and relationality (Vea & Curnow, 2022; Vossoughi et al., 2023), together with disability justice frameworks (Berne, 2015; Piepzna-Samarasinha, 2018) to explore how call-ins act as an enactment of care in partnership work. We define call-ins as an ontological shift that candisrupt harmful narratives while simultaneously inviting transformative thinking. This pivots us towards viewing people in their wholeness and holding space for their complexities (Brown, 2017). The purpose of our work is not to indicate which types of knowledge are correct, but rather to attend to the nuances of what it means to (a) view everyone as capable of holding harmful views and

(b) recognize and embrace the fluidity and complexity of what it means to be human. Just as Vea and colleagues

(2023) share about the harms of both sides argumentation, we too propose this work with the caveat that all parties

in partnerships (both researchers and non-researchers) must be committed to the shared principle of knowing and being better for one another. Through this work, we seek to contribute to ongoing conversations about how to hold complexity in community-engaged research, how to disrupt harmful narratives, and how to make care a core component of partnership.

To better understand call-ins as a mechanism for transformative disruption, where we are both disrupting harmful reproductions of knowledge and transforming our previous knowledges, we reflect on our multi-year research practice partnership with a school district that sought to tackle issues of collaborative transition service delivery. Transition services are the services that students with disabilities receive to help them prepare for adult life, specifically across three postschool outcome areas: independent living, education and training, and meaningful employment (Individuals with Disabilities Education Improvement Act [IDEIA], 2004). We r eflect on examples from our co-design sessions held with general education teachers, special education teachers, counselors, and administrators, where harmful views about disability and special education were shared. Specifically, we look at how a facilita tor (Author 3) called in community partners  to both disrupt harmful narratives about disability and transform them by offering alternatives. We use the term curiously challenging to refer to call-ins and to capture how our facilitator simultaneously sought out closeness and understanding through curiosity, whilst also challenging harmful reproductions of knowledge about disability and special education. Thus, we ask: How do call-ins function as a transformative disruption in relationship building practices in community-engaged research settings?

## Literature review

Contributions to community-engaged research methodologies have extensively discussed and explicitly named aspects of partnered research, such as recognizing power dynamics (Vakil et al., 2019)  and enacting and inviting intimacy and care (Uttamchandani, 2021). Rather than viewing partnership as a rigid checklist, partnerships have shifted toward anchoring the work in values (Bang et al., 2016), solidarities and shared commitments (Vea & Curnow, 2022), and relationality (Vossoughi et al., 2023). We join these  scholars in conversation, specifically 1767 building on the nuances related to disrupting harmful knowledges. To our understanding, this is a specific aspect of partnership that has only been lightly explored. Within the learning sciences, Vea and colleagues (2023) have approached this topic from th e perspective of both sides argumentation. Here, Vea and his colleagues share that both sides argumentation can “be a harmful strategy that creates conditions for advancing bad faith arguments, dehumanizing already minoritized communities, burdening people with the work of refutation, and compromising with immoral positions.” The Learning Towards Justice Team (2025) continues this discussion, sharing that this style of argumentation can offer up and reproduce bad-faith narratives while exposing historically  marginalized folx to violent rhetoric. We align with their anecdotes of care and posit that these nuances are important to include in our discussions of partnership work because of the inherently politicized nature of partnerships and the learning that takes place within them  (The Politics of Learning Writing Collective, 2017; Vakil et al., 2019). Jurnow and colleagues (2025) also examined the design of healing within the learning sciences, mentioning both aspects of self-care and the integration of the physical, spiritual, and emotional. We use these aspects of care, intimacy, healing, and solidarity to discuss the nuances related to holding systematic analyses, while also recognizing that the systems we are critical of are partly constructed by the same “flawed and complex individuals that we love.” (Brown, 2017, p. 82).

Outside of the learning sciences, issues related to navigating harm in transformative ways have been thoroughly discussed. Political organizers specifically have discussed strategies of how to enact solidarities and have shared the strategy of calling in (i.e., Legion, 2022). Calling in happens when “a supportive member of the group reminds their fellow member that the group has shared values and guidelines, supports them in reflecting on the ways their behaviors may have led to unintended consequences or harms, and continues to work with that person to make amends and change their behavior” (Interrupting Criminalization & Dragonfly Partners, 2021, p. 11). Call-in strategies recognize the cultural elements of carcerality and how we are taught to view others as disposable (Brown, 2020). Call-ins have been used by organizers as invitations to learn and grow together. They do not exert the expectation that someone needs to change their ideas immediately but rather invites them into change. In this way, call-ins act as a transformative disruption to the formation and reproduction of harmful knowledge. We view call-ins as an enactment of care in partnership, as call-ins and similar strategies shift us away from enactments of carcerality and toward enactments of solidarity and intimacy (Uttamchandani, 2021; Vea & Curnow, 2022). We also recognize that the burden of transformation has too often been placed on historically marginalized folx and pose this work as an antecedent to that burden by making collective solidarity and care a foundational element of partnership.

## Theoretical framework

Our theoretical framing brings together tendrils of disability justice and co-design as a third space. Disability justice is the tendril that names the why behind this work, while co-design as a third space frames our conceptualization of learning in community partnership settings. Disability justice The disability justice framework sprouted from the movement for disability rights and sought specifically to recognize and uplift disabled folx that were invisibilized by the mainstream disability rights movement. Disability justice recognizes how systems of oppression intersect to label people’s bodies and minds as unproductive, disposable, and invalid (Berne, 2015). The disability justice movement was led by the disability justice collective, founded in 2005 by Patty Berne, Mia Mingus, Leroy Moore, Eli Cl are, and Sebastian Margaret (PiepznaSamarasinha, 2018). The disability justice framework is practiced on an individual, societal and cultural level, and asks us to be responsible for both how we make change and what we do to make it (Disability Activist Collective, 2010). Enacting disability justice means finding ourselves in the margins or working to find commonalities and solidarities with those who the dominant society has deemed as the most disposable. Disability justice draws on the theoretical underpinnings of transformative justice, an abolitionist movement and political framework that crafts and theorizes about responses to violence, harm, and abuse without the use of policing and carceral systems (Brazzell, 2023). These carceral logics are connected to our understandings of disability justice, in the sense that both transformative and disability justice recognize systems of oppression that view individuals as “disposable.” In this way, disability justice informs how we build relationality in partnerships, as we move towards greater intimacy and care for others (Uttamchandani, 2021). We posit that our moments of calling in community partners are enactments of these justices, where we shift away from viewing others as “disposable” and shift towards committing to solidarity even amidst tensions. Co-design as a liminal space 1768 We extend the essence of transformation into our conceptualization of co-design as a liminal space. We recognize that being in between the deconstruction of ideals and the reconstruction of others is an often difficult place to navigate. As such, co-design as a liminal space refers to how within co-design, we candirectly practice new ways of thinking and being (Lam et al., 2018). The liminal space emphasizes the mutual nature of learning within codesign, where both researchers and non-researchers can engage in transformation. In our co-design, new ways of being and thinking were directly invited by our facilitator (Author 3), where we all could step intonew ways of conceptualizing both disability and collaboration in transition service delivery. We also recognize co-design’s inherent political nature. This politicization applies not only to the dynamics of the co-design space but  also recognizes that everyone entering the  space comes with their own politicized understandings (Brown, 2017). Audre Lorde shares a captivating summary of Paulo Freire’s  (1970) work that captures what we hope to address, which is that “the true focus of revolutionary change is never merely the oppressive situations that we seek to escape, but that piece of the oppressor which is implanted within each of us” (Lorde, 1984, p. 123). In other words, we recognize that everyone enters co-design spaces with systemized mindsets, or pieces of the oppressor, that can both produce and reproduce harmful narratives.

## Project context

The larger context of this work takes place within a research-practice partnership (Coburn & Penuel, 2016) between a local school district and a mountain-west university. Our partnership uses Design Based Implementation Research (DBIR) (Fishman et al., 2013) to co-design approaches to improving transition service delivery within the district. Our goal is to make sustainable, systematic change to improve postschool outcomes for students with disabilities.

As part of our project, we work with general education teachers, special education teachers, counselors, and administrators and hold monthly co-design sessions discussing barriers, pitfalls, and successes related to current district collaboration practices. The co-design sessions were 90 minutes in length and included both whole-group and small-group activities. Each session was audio and video recorded. Within the first year of partnership, our co-design sessions were primarily focused on understanding current district practices that would inform initial design directions. There were four total co-design sessions within the first year.  One established norm in our partnership was that after each  session, our research team had debriefs to share our biggest takeaways and inspirations with one another.

Our December 2023 session, the second session we held, was focused on developing common definitions for three postschool outcomes (independent living, education and training, and employment; IDEIA, 2004) and holistically understanding our partners’ previous conceptions of these outcomes. In the debrief that followed our session, Author 3 shared his ruminations on the difficulties encountered in his conversations while attending to harmful knowledge a partner had shared. Our team discussed our noticings around wanting to validate partners’ lived experience and expertise whilst simultaneously recognizing and disrupting harmful narratives of disability, some of which included conceptualizations of disability itself, purposes of special education, and responsibi lities in transition service delivery. After this, Author 1 felt deeply captivated by and inspired to further analyze how to enact care in partnered work while navigating tensions related to harmful narratives.

## Data analysis

Our team reviewed the original audio and video recordings from our December 2023 co-design session to look for instances of calling in community partners. Within this session, we specifically analyzed Author 3’s interactions and how he engaged in call-ins. Our original conceptualizations of call-ins were moments where our facilitator was navigating tension in conversation related to how partners’ referenced disability and their responsibilities in transition service delivery. Author 1 conducted memoing on the December 2023 session to identify and define instances of calling in (Birks et al., 2008). Through memoing, we identified that call-ins emerged as moments of curiously challenging, which were moments where the facilitator sought out closeness and understanding through curiosity, whilst also disrupting and challenging harmful reproductions of knowledge. After defining curiously challenging,  two authors looked across the video and audio recording from December 2023 and held weekly meetings to discuss and build consensus of our coding of moments of curiously challenging. 1769 Call-ins Call-ins manifested as moments where the facilitator curiously challenged an idea by questioning or challenging harmful reproductions of knowledge in a way that welcomed closeness and understanding through curiosity. In our co-design sessions, instances of curiously challenging were followed up by moments of voluntary self-disclosure or rephrasing and reiterating.  Voluntary self-disclosure was when the facilitator shared something about himself that related to the stories and experiences that our partners were sharing. This often presents as Author 3 sharing his background as a special education teacher. Rephrasing and reiterating occurred when Author 3 rephrased what partners shared in his own words to ensure understanding and followed up with questions to dive deeper into partner’s perspectives. Thus, moments of curiously challenging were situated within enactments of care and understanding through our facilitator rephrasing and reiterating or offering voluntary self-disclosures. Call-ins as a transformative disruption To exhibit the relational nature of call-ins and how they function as transformative disruptions, we recount two examples. Our first example comes from a whole-group discussion at the beginning of our co-design session, where common language on the postschool outcomes were being established before our small groups were formed. Everyone was first asked to discuss their definitions of independent living with people around them and then asked to share out to the larger group. In the share out, some of our part ners expressed that they believed independent living meant not living with family. This is where our facilitator first curiously challenged the preconception that independent living means being completely independent, “Is there anyone here who is completely independent? Like you don’t rely on anyone else for anything?” Many in the group chuckled and said “no.” To follow up, the facilitator then voluntarily self-disclosed and shared “Okay, so same,” and proceeded to tell the group that the homemade Brioche loaf he had brought for everyone to enjoy had been made following a YouTube tutorial. In this case, the facilitator began the call-in by curiously challenging the harmful notion that students with disabilities should not live without support from family or friends to be considered independent. Acknowledging the harm in this way did not shut down conversation, but sparked humor to transform the bias about what independence means and provided a concrete example from his own life. Our second example is situated within the context of discussing employment as a postschool outcome. Here, general education teachers were discussing their conceptualizations of employment in a small group as it related to transition and how they can meaningfully support special education students in their pursuit of employment. One partner expressed that he believed students with jobs tend to have a better understanding of boundaries and shared an example of how his kids with jobs do not invade his desk space. This statement has the underlying assumption that social norms are (a) known by everyone and (b) that the social norms we follow are not adaptable to the contexts we inhabit. Our facilitator first curiously challenged these underlying assumptions by asking, “Well, but so many of those are unspoken right?" After our partners reconsidered and agreed, our facilitator then voluntarily self-disclosed, where he shared an example of his teaching experience and how he taught students about unspoken norms. In contrast to the previous use of humor, this instance of curiously challenging posed a more pointed question to prompt self-reflection about what was said. By following up with an example from his own teaching practice, he allowed space for transformation from the initial assumption by prompting new ways of thinking about boundaries and social norms. In this piece, we addressed questions related to challenging and disrupting harmful knowledges in partnership. Our mechanism of curiously challenging is not intended to merely be a form of discourse, but rather an ontological shift that pivots us toward solidarity and care (Uttamchandani, 2021; Vea & Curnow, 2022). These enactments of solidarity are visible through how Author 3  invites partners to share their thoughts  and his invitations into transformative ways of thinking. We also find it imperative to consider and discuss the nuances related to the disposability of people (Berne, 2015; Brown, 2020) and how we can pivot toward partnerships that center repair, intimacy, and care (Jurow et al., 2025; Uttamchandani, 2021). We seek to inspire meaningful dialogue and reflections amongst the Learning Sciences that regard how to handle harmful knowledge reproductions in partnership. We pose this work as an antecedent to the burden often placed on marginalized folx to change b y making collective solidarity and care a communal effort. We also believe that this work furthers discussions of care and intimacy within the Learning Sciences by viewing partners as those who we can engage in difficulties with and transforms tensions faced within partnerships as potentials instead. 1770 Braun, V., & Clarke, V.
