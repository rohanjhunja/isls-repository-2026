# Intergenerational Conversations Through Redesigning Tabletop Games: Playing, Designing, and Learning With Canadian Families

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this poster, we present our study of the activities for families in Canada to redesign tabletop games, reflective of their interest and culture on their own time during the global pandemic. We collected data through online communications (e.g., video recordings, games). We describe how they expressed what matters to them individually and collectively.

Research has shown that game play and design positi on learners as individuals who engage creatively and critically with disciplinary ideas. Many tabletop games are played across generations (e.g., Mancala in Africa), and are reflective of cultures and ways of knowing,  learning, and being. Using movements and gestures with physical objects also makes tabletop games suitable  for supporting communication among players of diff erent ages and linguistic backgrounds (Kim et al., 2021). Our research explores how tabletop game redesign activities can help participants invent ways to draw on and connect with intergenerational differences and cultural practices, by seeing individual differences as resources for social learning (Ladson-Billings, 1995). Cultural Practices and Tabletop Game Redesign Our work is supported by the cultural-historical significance of tabletop games. Crist et al. (2016) described how board games were used to facilitate interactions “across kinship, ethnic and socio-economic boundaries” (p.181) and were adopted into varying societies by being decorated and reimagined appropriately to their cultures. While designing games engages learners in inventing alternative ways of knowing (Civil, 2002), redesigning activities could enrich linguistic and cultural diversity. In a study of students’ redesigning a game for mathematics learning (Kim et al., 2021), students demonstrated their rei magined play by adopting rules from the games from their cultures and showed that culturally-relevant games have the potential to bring historical and cultural contexts to disciplinary thinking (e.g., mathematics; Bayeck, 2 018). Historical and school game redesign work rese mbles how culturally sustaining pedagogy seeks cultural and linguistic pluralism in schools (Paris, 2012, p. 95), and may help educators capitalize on learners’ diverse resources. Learning should be seen as critically enriching strengths, moving away from replacing deficits and aligning wi th linguistic and cultural hegemony (Lee & Walsh, 2 017). Here, we see cultures as meanings and practices of the families and other social groups they may belon g to, inherited or (being) formed individually and collectively.

## Research Design

A pilot study was conducted through distant video c ommunications with Canadian families during the glo bal pandemic. The research team created a general guideline for their game redesign process. We explored the families’ approach to the project, the game they selected, their ideas for designing their own game, and their experience of game redesign as a family. They provided written descriptions and visual data from their in-progress designs and final artifacts. In our analysis, we reviewed our n otes and recordings of the online meetings and iden tified how they interacted as families and how redesign ideas formed. We examined their artifacts (i.e., games, p layrecordings, photos, blog) to understand the connections made with their different interests and practices. Tabletop Game Redesign by Families Many adult participants intuitively identified the shortcomings of existing games, compared cultural g aming practices from their youth, and reflected on interg enerational relationships and games they play in di fferent occasions. On the other hand, the game design proce ss and the redesigned games did not exhibit overt c ultural markers. Instead, we observed that games and game d esign practice could allow cultures to exist and in fluence action and decision-making in the process. Through this process, the families may engage in understanding their own cultures and what matters to their family members more deeply. Understanding and expanding a child’s cultural practices Some families focused on understanding and expanding a child’s practices through the process. For example, one family (Caucasian) redesigned the card game Love Letter, led by Ellie (the 5th grade daughter), “to correspond to ICLS2022 Proceedings 2186 © ISLS [her] current favourite book series, Warriors by Erin Hunter” (blog post). In our conversation, they discussed how there are parallels between the life represented in the renaissance courting theme of Love Letter and the hierarchal nature of the Warriors series’ characters: “((In)) Warriors there's different ranks. There's a leader, a deputy and medicine cat or you’re, um, kind of an apprentice” (Ellie). They crafted cards to play-test the game as family to see how the game works and to adjust rules. Supporting the redesign of the card game and playing the redesigned game provided an opportunity to learn about the literature that Ellie was invested for this family. Ellie was also motivated for the possibility of contributing to the Warriors online community. After the redesign was complete, the family created a blog page to share with the Warriors community to download the cards and the rules. Exploring the different interest of all family members and engaging in intergenerational conversations Exploring the different interest of family members was another pattern. A family with Chinese backgrou nd redesigned The Game of Life to make it more relevant to their current and future experiences. This game was of interest to the two daughters in grade 5 and 7, Ali a and Eira. While brainstorming, they pointed out h ow the choices and life paths in this game do not resonate  with their perspectives. For example, they conside red the careers in The Game of Life “outdated” and discussed a collection of careers to add to the game. The process of choosing the careers sparked conversations through which the parents got to know the careers the kids knew about and introduced other careers that they did not know. They also decided to create a board that reflect each family member’s interest and put all four boards together to create the game. “Originally, all of us had diff erent ideas and now we could pare it down into more of a team e ffort.... I think it was interesting because each o f us had a role to guide one another... how we want to start this ((the game)) and how to end this” (Hailey, mother). Across the boards, they explored how action and chance car ds could be similar but also different. Through red esigning the game, they had the opportunity to learn and appreciate each other’s interest and find ways to make them as a coherent whole. They underscored how each tried to attend to others’ viewpoints and what they valued.

## Discussion and Conclusions

We explored how families invent ways to draw on and  connect with intergenerational differences and cul tural practices while redesigning tabletop games. We obse rved that the families focused mainly on expressing  their interest through the design while noticing invisible boundaries between them. Without explicitly exploring their cultural practices, families expressed what mattere d to their members individually and collectively. T hey had opportunities to value and support the members’ int erest, while the cultures outside of their family b oundaries (e.g., fan-based practices, other cultures) contributing to their practices. They did not see their current game design as the end but as having the potential to evolve and expand. They demonstrated the value of redesigning an existing game by questioning what is depicted as dominant, taken-for-granted practices in the original game, which shows the glimpse of family’s developing critical conscio usness. In our pursuit of redesign activities, we s ee the significance in: (1) identifying traditional and evolving ways of acting in diverse social and cultural systems; (2) creating activities to develop critical consciousness; and (3) positioning diversity as resources for interdependence (Joyce, 2017; Ladson-Billings, 1995; Paris, 2012). This pilot study showed the tabletop game redesign as an initial step to deepening intergenerational conversations and develop critical consciousness as families.
