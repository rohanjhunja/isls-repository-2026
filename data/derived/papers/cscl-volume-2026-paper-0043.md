# Ways of Knowing in Global Informal Learning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
T his paper examines how adolescents develop boundary-crossing STEM competencies through participation in an informal, globally networked virtual learning program. Using post-assessment interviews and recorded sessions of participating youth in Brazil, Kenya, Mexico, and the United States, we focus on two key competencies that emerged: Embracing Other Ways of Knowing and Building Community. These two competences reflect students’ growing ability to engage across cultural and geographic differences and to esta blish inclusive, collaborative practices of learning and working together. The findings underscore the promise of boundary-crossing, project-based environments for developing STEM identities and fostering the human-centered skills necessary for globally connected STEM learning and future collaboration.

In a constantly shifting technological and global landscape, the skills necessary for success will involve more thandisciplinary knowledge. Students must develop interpersonal skills that help them communicate across cultures, work collaboratively, and navigate complexity. The idea of boundary-crossing competences is introduced by Walker and Nocon (2007) as the ability to function competently in multiple contexts, building off the idea of networked expertise brought forward by Moll et al (Moll, Tapia, & Whitmore, 1993). The need to support and foster such efforts become important in preparing youth for meaningful participation in future careers not only in STEM, but the broader global community.

Frameworks for education centered on 21st-century learning emphasize skills such as innovation, collaboration, and critical thinking (Starr et al., 2022; Salas Pico, 2013). While these skills are emphasized, many despite widespread endorsement of these ski lls, many youth learners —particularly those from marginalized communities—enter the postsecondary landscape with limited preparation of understanding or exposure from their educational experience (Allen et al., 2025). Supplemental learning experiences from  informal and international programs may offer complementary or alternative pathways that more effectively foster such skills through culturally diverse, real-world opportunities often overlooked in formal settings (Jaiswal et al., 2024). In STEM learning, another key component for adequate preparation is identity formation. Research studies have shown that youth who view themselves as capable and belonging in STEM are more likely to persist in these fields (Carlone & Johnson, 2007; Herrera  & Kovats, 2022). In their paper, Carlone and Johnson (2007) identify three interrelated components of STEM identity that guided their initial analysis based on prior work: competence, or knowledge and understanding content; performance, or demonstrating one’s scientific skills; and recognition, or being acknowledged as capable by self and others as a science person. These components are shaped in part by students’ access to supportive social contexts and meaningful engagement. Informal STEM learning environments have been found to foster settings that encourage identity development through hands-on learning, collaborative problem solving, and greater freedom to pursue student interests (Reid-Griffin et al., 2023; Nalipay et al.,  2024). These kinds of learning environments reduce pressure for performance which allow learners to take intellectual risks, work with peers outside traditional classroom structures, and engage in culturally responsive ways. When programs also involve col leagues from other places, especially internationally, students have the opportunity to develop intercultural competence, language flexibility, and appreciation for diverse knowledge systems.

This paper builds on the idea of boundary-crossing competences to aspects of STEM-focused boundarycrossing competencies, informed from an informal, STEM project-based online learning environment for adolescent youth. S ix competences emerged  from the data, with two specific boundary-crossing competencies that are the focus of this paper: (1) Embracing Other Ways of Knowing, which describes students' openness to and appreciation of diverse epistemologies and local knowledge systems and (2) Building Community, which encompasses the development of social trust, shared purpose, and collaborative resilience among international 309 peers. These competencies not only reflect what students learned, but how they approached collaboration, valued one another’s contributions, and developed as STEM learners in relational and cross-cultural ways.

## Context and methods

This study examines a project environment called the International Community for Collaborative Content Creation (IC4), a virtual STEM learning initiative that virtually connected adolescents from both high-income and low-income countries. Participants prim arily came from Brazil, Kenya, Mexico, and the United States, with additional representation from countries including Cameroon, Finland, India, Iran, Namibia, Nigeria, Singapore, and Uzbekistan. Adolescent youth participated through afterschool programs or nonprofit organizations, meeting regularly in person locally within their site and joining global video conference sessions —called "global meet-ups"—to meet and collaborate with their international counterparts. These sessions were recorded, and students were interviewed for post-assessments at the close of the U.S. academic year. Of the approximately 100 learners who participated in IC4 between 2020 and 2022, this study focused on data that involved six high school students selected for their consistent and active engagement throughout the entire period. Table 1 provides an overview of these students' demographics, reflecting students based in Brazil, Kenya, Mexico, and the United States. Our dataset includes nine recorded global meet-ups and a set of post-program interviews conducted in 2021. A team of four researchers transcribed and open coded the data, refining shared codes through an iterative process. These codes were then grouped into themes representing competencies fostered through the learning environment. While six competences were identified, two competencies were identified for the focus of this initial paper: Embracing Other Ways of Knowing and Building Community. Embracing other ways of knowing One of the most significant competencies identified was the ability to recognize and integrate diverse epistemologies. In global collaborations, students often encountered approaches to STEM that differed from their own. This kind of engagement parallels the concept of funds of knowledge (Moll et al., 2006), which asserts that culturally rooted knowledge systems hold value within learning environments. Similarly, Nasir and Hand (2008) highlight the importance of learners accessing and negotiating multiple knowledge traditions as part of identity development. In IC4, students did not simply tolerate difference—they came to appreciate it as central to learning. Dominic, a student from Mexico, reflected on the mutual learning that occurred through collaboration. In a post-assessment interview, he emphasized the exchange of both cultural and academic knowledge: “We could help each other by using our own knowledge… I can share my culture with him.” Dominic also noted how others’ 310 expertise in mathematics helped expand his own understanding. His experience demonstrates how informal peerto-peer learning across borders contributed to his developing STEM identity. This sentiment was also reflected in Dominic’s discourse during global meet-ups over time, as seen in his ENA models in Figure 1 from February (left) to October (right). In February, there is not much acknowledgement of Openness  or cultural Competence, but by October, Dominic is frequently relating content Description  to both cultural Competence and a desire to be vulnerable to things he’s not familiar with (Openness). Amani, another student from Kenya, shared a story that illustrates both individual problem-solving effort and openness to support from an international collaborator. Initially working alone to resolve a coding issue, Amani described exhausting online resources before reaching out to a peer in the U.S., who offered direct support through a video call: “He told me it’s as easy as saving... then he showed me how to use an autosave feature that saves a lot of time.” This seemingly simple interaction highlights an important shift: from self-reliance to relational learning, rooted in trust and exchange across geographic and cultural boundaries. Amani also appreciated learning about global traditions during cultural presentations, recalling one in particular: “Ther e was a student from Brazil with a presentation about some leaves used to make this beverage... I thought one day I should visit and perhaps have a taste.” His curiosity to experience the beverage firsthand illustrates how STEM-related learning can intersect with cultural exploration, fostering both intellectual growth and global awareness. These students exemplify how students moved beyond viewing STEM as a static knowledge skill set. Instead, they came to see knowledge as situated, culturally shaped, and worth engaging with on its own terms. The competence of Embracing Other Ways of Knowing thus reflects not just a collaborative practice, but a shift in how students see themselves and their place in STEM. Building community Students also developed competencies in creating inclusive, supportive, and collaborative learning communities. This competency involved more than simply joining a group; it required intentional actions —inviting participation, affirming contributions, and offering support. Students learned to take initiative in fostering psychological safety and belonging, even across language and time zone barriers. Mary, a U.S. based-student reflecting further on her team’s efforts, described how she and her partners found ways to connect despite language challenges. Mary and her team members had a willingness and openness to work through this together in order to reach a shared understanding of communication and effectively collaborate through their project. Through conversation, shared Slack messages, and even exchanging words in Portuguese, her team built trust and clarity. Mary describes this in detail during her interview: “...all three of us [in the group] wanted just to get to know one another and actually talk with each other. Although we may have a main language barrier but we found a way to talk…We, we talked about our ideas on the way we wanted to work on our projects, the sections that we each got, and the way that we want to do the work, and they were also nice in teaching a  few words in Portuguese and go back and forth with them. And then we also did text a lot through Slack, to just re-assure ourselves that we did understand what we were talking about and the way that we were going to do our project… with some of my peers.” This is also reflected in Mary’s discourse during global meet-ups over time, as illustrated in her ENA models in Figure 2 from January to November. In January, Mary’s discourse was primarily content-oriented October February 311 (Description). Connections to Social Support were in support of content and supporting others with their lack of knowledge (Uncertainty). By November, Mary more prominently associates  Social Support with Engagement, and Resilience, demonstrating how mutual encouragement and cross-cultural communication enabled her group to navigate language barriers, collaborate effectively, and sustain confidence throughout the project. Liza, a Brazilian student, spoke about how mutual openness and willingness to help were essential to completing group work. She appreciated how others consistently showed up, offered support, and accepted one another’s contributions. Her post-assessment in terview comments emphasized the importance of shared responsibility and care in making collaboration possible: “Acho que sim acho que essa comparação com sendo aberto para pessoas que quiserem participar do projeto assim como a por todos e eu me ofereci para ajudar. Acho que isso ajuda muito na cooperação. A gente tem que estar aqui para que consigamos fazer um projeto juntos.” English Translation: “I think so, I think this comparison with being open to people who want to participate in the project as well as that of everyone, and I offered to help. I think that helps a lot with cooperation. We have to be here for us to be able to do a project together and you can give a specific example of how you helped another club member or you were helped by another member of the club.” In another individual reflection, Benny, a Mexican-American male learner from the United States, expressed how his consistent attendance at global meet-ups was not only for personal growth but also to support and encourage others, emphasizing a mutual commitment to the group. He discussed how the value of a nonhierarchical, welcoming space was important. Additionally, roles and credentials are secondary to shared curiosity and kindness: “If people, if new kids or teens or adults want to join … they don't have to be a very knowledgeable person. They don't have to be in the highest program in their school. They don't have to be the club president. They don't have to be the school president, don't have to be the principal to join because we will a ll learn from each other and only through kindness will we learn more about each other.” These accounts show that Building Community was not simply a product of the project’s structure, but a competency developed through deliberate relational practices. Students learned to create environments where difference was welcomed, support was reciprocal, and everyone felt invited to contribute. In learning to navigate difference and foster belonging, students cultivated a critical competency for working across borders in STEM and beyond. Their experiences highlight that community building is both a social and intellectual endeavor—one that expands what it means to collaborate meaningfully in global contexts. This study demonstrates how youth, when engaged in culturally diverse, informal STEM contexts, candevelop boundary-crossing competencies vital for 21st-century collaboration. The competencies of Embracing Other Ways of Knowing and Building Community illuminate how adolescents build relationships, navigate complexity, and co-construct knowledge in ways that mirror the demands of global STEM careers. These findings point to the importance of educational designs that center human connection, relational learning, and cultural responsiveness. As educators and institutions seek to prepare learners for participation in international, interdisciplinary STEM fields, this study offers insight into the potential for  young people to  co-construct knowledge, bridge cultural boundaries, and reimagine collaboration as a deeply human endeavor.
