# Parents’ Information Seeking for Children’s STEM Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
An understanding of information sources parents find useful and trustworthy can inform the development of supplementary learning materials for at-home STEM kits. This study investigates types of information sources parents seek out to support their children’s STEM learning at home and their perceived level of trust for the information sources. Through participants’ information horizons map, follow-up interviews, and surveys, preliminary findings demonstrate that information sources that were frequently used were not always the sources that were trusted. Implications for supplementary materials for at-home STEM kits that align with parents’ information seeking practices are discussed.

## Introduction

As demand for effective STEM education grows, involving parents in children’s engineering learning at home is crucial (DeWitt & Archer, 2017). One way to support at-home STEM learning is through the use of STEM kits (Simpson et al., 2024). While using the  kits, parents may seek additional information (e.g., search Internet  and books) as supplementary learning materials to maximize the educational potential  of the kits. This need for supplementary resources raises questions about the types of information sources parents turn to. However, the effectiveness of the information sources in supporting STEM learning often depends on users’ ability to critically evaluate and navigate the information (Savolainen &  Kari, 2004). By investigating  information sources parents find useful and trustworthy, we seek to inform the development of supplementary learning materials for at-home STEM kits. As such, this study investigates: (1) What information source s do parents seek for STEM learning, and why? (2) Which sources do they perceive as trustworthy?

## Methods

This is an intrinsic case study (Stake, 1995) of 20 parents who are residents in  five different States in the U.S., recruited as part of a larger project that provided  family engineering program (Kim & Choi, 202 3). Each participant (1) completed an information horizons m ap, (2) engaged in a follow-up interview, and (3) completed a survey. We used the information horizons map methodology developed by Sonnenwald et al. (2001) and asked participants to draw a map of information sources they seek out when the ir children have a question related to STEM. We asked participants to consider different t ypes of information sources and rank the order of each information source by the degree of use or preferen ce. The follow-up interview was conducted in Zoom individually to understand why the participants sought out the i nformation sources, in what order, and how the information was used. Then, participants completed an online survey that asked to rate the level of trust towards each information source included in the list and describe the reasoning.  The list included information sources mentioned in the information horizons map, and a few other sources that we considered relevant (e.g., TikTok, AI speaker). The information horizons maps were analyzed by noti ng down which information sources participants had on the map  and the order of use or preference for each information source. Then, in the Excel sheet, we added transcription of the interviews to understand why each information source was sought a nd how they were used for STEM learning. All names were ma rked with pseudonyms. Lastly, participants’ survey data was merged into the Excel sheet to document the level of trust towards each information source. To analyze data, we conducted a thematic analysis using the constant comparison method (Glaser & Strauss, 1967).

## Findings

While there was a variety of different paths and se quences of using information sources to support chi ldren’s STEM learning, many parents followed the path of starting with Google, then finding more resources by checking out books at the library, checking out YouTube videos, or finding people who may have knowledge in the STEM topic. Participants described the ease of access as  the primary reason for starting their information seeking with Google. YouTube was frequently utilized because it provided visual information that are engaging for c hildren. Parents also mentioned the importance of recognizing human knowl edge and STEM expertise within their own community to teach their children.

Analysis of information horizons map and survey showed that participants’ most preferred or frequently used information sources for supporting children’s STEM learning was Internet/Google while the least preferred or used were information sources with specialized e xpertise (see Figure 1). Analysis of online survey further demonstrate that p articipants placed more trust on information from e xperts, credible STEM institutions (e.g., Smithsonian), as exemplified by Olivia’s remark: “This information is fact-checked and supported with research.” On the other hand, participants had mixed trust tow ard the Internet platforms such as Google and YouTu be as well as personal networks, TV shows, and documentar ies. Participants found AI speakers and TikTok to b e the least trustworthy (see Figure 2).

## Discussion

Our preliminary findings demonstrate that information sources which  were frequently sought out by parents to support children’s at-home STEM learning were not always the sources that were trusted. Parents frequently used Google and YouTube for children’s STEM-related inquiries due to their ease of access and convenience of mobile technology, but shared challenges of evaluating the information sources. While many parents preferred the use of curated information from trusted organization (e.g., library) or professionals, these information sources were the secondary sources that they sought out after using the internet as it would take longer to identify and utilize these information.

Based on our preliminary findings, we discuss several implications for developing supplementary materials for at-home STEM kits that align with parents’ information seeking practices. When researchers design at-home kits to be used without any educators on site, we suggest including a curated list of reliable online resources and books  to guide parents to trusted information. Additionally, incor porating links to engaging instructional videos from platforms such as YouTube could make learning more interactive.  Given parents ’ preference for credible sources, collaborating with  STEM experts to provide fact-checked materials can increase trust in the kits. Lastly, our findings highlight that connecting families with local STEM events or knowledgeable individuals in the community could be one way to provide credible information so urce that parents and children can additionally learn from the kit. Finally, we acknowledge that this exploratory study is based on a small number of parents from our ongoing research. While we do not have par ticipants’ full information, we expect that many of the participants have high motivation to support  their children’s STEM learning, as evidenced by th eir willingness to participate in our research. Our participant sample also does not represent the full spectrum and complexities of parents’ information behavior, limiting the representativeness of our findings. Future stud ies would benefit from a more diverse sample of participants.
