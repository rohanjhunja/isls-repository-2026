# Not Just What to Do, But How to Know: Epistemological Messages in Science Notebook Prompts

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the relationships among preservice teachers’ notebook entries and the epistemological messages in instructors’ prompts. We analyzed 24 students’ digital notebooks from an introductory physics class and compared responses to two types of prompts: empirically-focused and design-focused. Empirically-focused prompts elicited resources such as knowledge-as-propagated and accumulation, while design-focused prompts more often elicited knowledge-as-fabricated and knowledge formation. Our findings highlight ways that features of prompts influence students’ sensemaking approaches in science notebooking tasks.

## Introduction

Students’ multiple productive ways of knowing and producing knowledge can be highlighted by attending to their epistemological resources—their ways of thinking about the nature and source s of knowledge (Hammer & Elby, 2003). These resources include learners’ views of  knowledge as received, inferred, constructed, or di rectly perceived (Hammer & Elby, 2012). Epistemological resources shape how students make s ense of the world and are differentially enacted based on context. Likewise, t he ways that learners perceive what  approaches to knowledge and kinds of knowing are appropriate for a particular learning activity comprise their epistemological frame (Scherr & Hammer, 2009; Patterson & Ding, 2025)  of a learning activity. Relatedly, epistemological messages refer to “implicit or explicit messages teachers communicate to students about the nature of knowledge and knowing” (Russ, 2018, p. 95).  These messages shape students’ epistemological fram ing and, consequently, their approach to science learning.

However, there has been limited research on how teachers’ messages and students’ framing are taken up in science notebooks, and how the design of notebook p rompts may more reliably send messages to students to pursue sensemaking about content. This study addresses this gap by examining preservice teachers' science notebook entries that were completed in an introductory physics course. We focus on instructors’ epistemological messages and students’ resources  in order to explore the following: What is the relationship between the epistemological messages instructors convey in science notebook prompts for physics inquiry act ivities and the epistemological resources evident in students’ notebook entries?

## Context and methods

This study analyzes 24 preservice teachers' science notebooks from an introductory physics course for education majors at a large research university.  Course content includes energy, waves, sound, and l ight phenomena with explicit goals of scientific modeling and inquiry. Both authors contributed to teaching the course. Throughout the semester, students engaged in structured formative assessments and documented their investigations, explanations, and reflections using the Book Creato r platform. Their entries incorporated multimodal representations, including text, photographs, and s hort video clips. We conducted a multimodal content  analysis (Serafini & Reid, 2023) to systematically examine t he text and visual content of instructor prompts and student notebook entries for 19 entries from each of the 24 students. Our initial analysis of the messages that underlie the prompts le d to two top-level categories: (1) Empirically-focused prompts (13 entries) emphasized observation, modeling-based explanation, data collection, and reasoning  from evidence. For instance, students created visual models to explain wave beha vior using evidence to support claims about the relationship b etween the angles of incidence and reflection.  (e.g., Does reflection of water waves in the ripple tank behave  like the reflection of light rays at a mirror? Create a visual model of the reflection of the wave at least one an gle; Write a claim about what can you conclude abou t the relationship between the angle of incidence and the  angle of reflection and support your claim with evidence). Similarly, the shadow activities prompted students to document deta iled observations and explanations based on firsthand evidence (e.g., Create a shadow to generate one or more images or videos of interest; Consider the timebased ways shadows change if the flashlight rises and sets like the sun…Can you make a projection that is smaller than the object? Explain). (2) In contrast, Design-focused prompts (3 entries) highlighted iterative design, modeling, and reflective reasoning on how tools med iate conceptual understanding.  For instance, students used Google Teachable Machine to train a model that clas sified sound and were asked to reflect on how the d esign process influenced their understanding of sound properties (e.g., pitch, loudness, timbre) and artificial intelligence. We asked, “In what ways did your design of your teachable machine change or extend your learning about sound and its properties?”. Three additional prompts did not clearly fall into either category and were left unclassified. To analyze how students responded to these prompts, we applied  a coding framework adapted from Hammer and Elby (2003) and Hammer et al. (2005). We systematically reviewed each notebook entry and coded for epistemological resources across three dimensio ns: (1) knowledge source resources (e.g., knowledge as fabricated, propagated, and free creation), (2) epistemological activities (e.g., accumulation, formation, and checking), and (3) epistemological stances (e.g., acceptance, understanding, and puzzlement). In what follows, we describe patterns of the epistemological resources evident in students’ entries for empirically-focused prompts and design-focused prompts and illustrate with representative examples.

## Findings and discussion

Instructor prompts across the inquiry activities co nveyed epistemological messages that shaped how pre service teachers approached their tasks and activated epistemological resources. However, the alignment between these messages and students’ responses varied.  For instance, in the shadows investigation, students were prompted to manipulate light sources and objects to generate and explain novel  shadow phenomena. Most students  activated knowledge-as-propagated and engaged in epistemological activities of acc umulation and checking, treating knowledge as something passed on from an authoritat ive source (e.g., an instructor, prior instruction,  a known principle) and focusing on gathering and verifying information. For example, one student wrote, “The angle at which a light strikes an object also affects the size and shape of its shadow… longer shadows when light is lower,” while another student described how “moving the light back and forth or up and down” changed the length of the shadow. These entries demonstrate that the  prompt elicited evid ence-based reasoning through embodied manipulation and repeated observation. Similarly, in the ripple tank work on water waves, the prompts asked students to compare the behavior of water waves to the reflection of light. In this prompt, the message emphasized analogical reasoning grounded in empirical pattern recognition. One student wrote, “The angle of incid ence and the angle of reflection are the same,” suggesting a ctivation of epistemological activity of formation and understanding, as she developed a new understanding  by synthesizing observational data with an underly ing principle and making sense of the phenomenon. Howev er, not all students accepted this analogy to visible light: Another student reflected, “I don’t think it behave s like a mirror… in water it created a ripple,” sho wing an epistemological stance of puzzlement as she questioned the fit of the mirror model for water reflection. In contrast, the prompts that were design-focused elicited responses that indicate a perspective that knowledge can be constructed, refined, and mediated  through iterative tool use. For example, i n the AI sound classification activity, students were asked to  create and populate categories in Teachable Machine by rec ording sounds, test their own models, and then reflect on their conceptual understanding of sound and AI. Students took up the role as designers and evaluators of knowledge systems. S tudents activated knowledge-as-fabricated and engaged in epistemological activities of formation. In other words, they viewed knowledge as something that could be constructed through iterative exploration  or inferred from prior understanding and refined through experimentation. One student, for example, wrote that pitch “can be singled out” and reflected that “AI learns more than just simple sound…it takes in all the outliers”. Another student noted, “This taught me how frequencies and pitch play a big role in determining what sound  is what.” These responses demonstrate how this des ign task effectively prompted students to think beyond obser vation, toward the structure of classification syst ems and function of machine learning and the properties of sound as data. Importantly, some epistemological messages that the  instructor intended  were largely missed. For example, w hile the instructor invited creative use of materia ls to generate artful shadows, the predominant message that was taken up in the notebooks was one in which knowledge can be directly perceived and refined through systematic observation. Our preliminary analysis highlights the need for additional work to investigate the design of notebook prompts, how students’  epistemological resources may demonstrate change across the course of the semester, and the relationship between different content foci and students’ notebook entries.
