# Teach or not teach? Impacts of Objective Explanation in Game-based Learning: Insights from Gaussian Hidden Markov Analysis

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We investigated the impact of teachers' explanations of objectives in game-based learning on students’ learning outcomes and processes. The research involved 206 participants, divided into a control group (n=114), which learned objectives through reading, and an experimental group (n=92) receiving teacher explanations. The study incorporated Gaussian Hidden Markov Models to analyze learner states during game-based learning. Findings revealed that teacher explanations direct their learning states towards efficiency, helping learners quickly enter and maintain an efficient learning state. This work provided instructional insights for implementing technology-enhanced learning methods in authentic educational settings.

## Introduction

This study explored the impact of teachers' explanations of objectives on student game-based learning outcomes and processes. We established two groups for this purpo se: one in which teachers explained the objectives and another where students learned learning objectives autonomously. We employed a Gaussian Hidden Markov Model (G-HMM) to analyze process data, comparing the learning states of learners of the two groups. Related works Previous research has extensively explored the bene fits of game-based learning, which include stimulating learners' motivation (Hayward & Fishman, 2020), engagement (Kessner & Hayward, 2021), and enhancing their learning outcomes (Karakoç et al., 2022). Realizing the benefits of gam e-based learning necessitates a close collaboration between teachers and games  (Squire, 2005). However, well-designed games may feature game elements that overlap with pedagogical design eleme nts in function (Gillern & Alaswad, 2016). A quintessential example is objectives: games can integrate learning  objectives, and teachers can also elucidate these objectives. In this context, how can we appropriately manage th e relationship between teacher s’ activities and game s? Specifically, when well-designed games include objectives, is it still necessary for teachers to explain them? Addressing this question requires empirical researc h to provide evidence, analyzing the impact of whether or not teachers explain objectives on learn ers' outcomes and learning processes. In analyzing learners' processes, G-HMM has been employed. The G-HMM posits that it is possible to infer a sequence of hidden states from a continuous sequence of observations  (Rabiner & Juang, 1986). We collect multiple gaming sequences from each student, such as attempts or the number o f stars. These sequences collectively serve as obse rvations, contributing to a holistic understanding of the student's learning process.

## Participants and learning environment

Participants included 206 fourth-grade students. The students were selected by using a convenience sampling strategy. The classes were randomly and evenly allocated into an experimental group (N=92) and a control group (N=114). Before engaging in game-based learning with the educational game Cube Elimination, students in the experimental group received instructions from their teacher. These instructions were about the learning objectives, including the goals and gameplay. For example, the teacher instructed the students that the objective for the second map was to select the front face of the reference plane w ithin the cube. In contrast, students in the control group began the game-based learning without any introduction to the objectives. All cla sses were taught by the same experienced mathematics teacher, who had received prior training on the game. Cube Elimination is an educational game designed to enhance students ' spatial abilities. The game features three maps, comprising 33 levels. None of the students had previously played this game.

## Data collection

The pre-test and post-test data were gathered using Ekstrom's (1976) Mental Folding Ability Test, comprising 20 questions to assess spatial cognition in transforming 2D representations into 3D objects. Game log data from Cube Elimination  captured interactions with the game. Each log indicated one attempt, including level_id, identifier, timestamp, star rating, time to complete, hint views, and probe clicks.

## Result

RQ1: The teacher's explanation and students' self-learning of objectives in gamebased learning similarly bolstered learners' mental folding abilities The pre-test and post-test measures of mental folding abilities did not pass the normality test. Therefore, the MannWhitney U test was employed.  T here were no significant differences in mental folding abilities  between the control and experimental groups' scores before and after the game-based learning. This suggested that the effects of teacher explanations and student self-learning through reading were similar. RQ2:Learners tended to maintain efficient learning states under the teacher’s explanations of objectives As shown in Figure 1, the numbers in the circle represented the initial distribution probabi lities of the states; the arrows indicated the transition directions, with the numbers alongside representing the transition probabilities. A comparison between the experimental and control gro ups revealed that learners in the experimental grou p had a higher likelihood of maintaining their states, espe cially those who entered the independent state (wit h a self-transition probability of 84%). This suggested that  once learners entered this stat e, they were likely to remain in this efficient learning mode. Conversely, the control group showed more dynamic state transitions, implying that learners' states in a self-learning environment were more affected by immediate challenges and conditions. ## Discussion and conclusions

The study highlights the crucial role of teacher explanations in technolog y-enhanced innovative learning environments. Teacher explanations not only amplify  the benefits of game-based learning but also help learners quickly adapt to efficient learning states. This re search contributes significantly to game-based learning theory from the instructional design perspective. However, we adopted a single game and limited student groups. Future research should employ a variety of games and learn ers from diverse grade levels and cultural backgrou nds to enhance the generalizability of the study's conclusions.
