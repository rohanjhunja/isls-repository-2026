# Decolonial and Postcolonial Possibilities for the Learning Sciences

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium brings together Learning Sciences scholarship rooted in diverse geopolitical contexts from different histories of coloniality. We engage with decolonial, anticolonial, and postcolonial theory within specific contexts to reconsider normative ideas about learning and knowing. Together the papers serve as an invitation and a provocation to unsettle normative colonial knowledge practices within the Learning Sciences. We ask, ‘how might the Learning Sciences rearticulate theories of learning that serve life rather than capital accumulation, and racialized and gendered hierarchy?’

## Introduction

Over the last decade, critically-oriented scholarship in the Learning Sciences has i llustrated the ways learning is a non-neutral engagement in the world, where cultural, political and historical powered dynamics shape both how we study learning and towards what ends (Philip et al., 2018). Through a focus on the role of powered dynamics as part of learning processes, this realm of resear ch has opened critically-oriented pathways in our field marked by efforts to disrupt, desettle, and decolonize our  scholarship—a departure from the colonial ways of knowing and being that undergird foundational assumptions about what is knowable and how. In this symposium, we aim to take up pieces of this  movement towards decoloniality, exploring the theory and consequentiality that underpins decoloni al, postcolonial, and anti-colonial theory when brought into conversation with Learning Sciences research. We bring together several emerging scholars within the field of Learning Sciences who address coloniality and power through their work across different geopolitical, historical, and cultural repertoires of practice. As part of this endeavor, we articulate when we join in the ‘sibling disruptions’ of postcolonial and anticolonial approaches (though these are robust fields which can hardly be encapsulated in a single session). We consider an anticolonial stance as one that is “committed to naming, to oppose settler colonial logics and practices… to reimagine and, falteringly, begin to put into practice ways to be in relation ” that move away from coloniality while recognizing there is mu ch to do to meet promises of decolonization (Patel,  2014, p. 360). Postcolonialism signals not a so-called “end” to colonialism, as many postcolonial theorists clearly identify the ongoing colonial relations within "postcolonial " states, but rather signals a rejection of colonia l domination and the echoes of formal colonial rule (Loomba, 2002). A postcolonial stance, then, draws attention to the specificities of time, place, history in acknowledg ing and unmaking imperialist relations (though even  this basic definition could be contested in the field). Indige nous and settler colonial studies are resonant, tho ugh ontologically distinct, sets of fields whose work similarly identifies the harms of the colonial project as a structure, rather than an event (Wolfe, 2006) while actively r ecentering toward Indigenous sovereignty and resurg ence (Simpson, 2011) as alternatives. Therefore resonant across the papers in this sympos ium is a vision of working from the margins to unsettle the macro-narrative that is dominated by white Eurocentric no rms of learning. Disrupting the ongoing move towards globalized western modernity (Mignolo, 2011), we push to create space for geopolitical and epistemic diversity. We assert tha t there is no single unique approach to addressing coloniality. Instead, we orient the conversation to highlight ou r diverse lens es anchored in specific identities, places, and histories of resistance as we each engage with distinct enunciations and practices of decoloniality. Colonial approaches to research value a human-centric ontology where knowledge-creation occurs through human control of the world towards aims that are often determined by dominant group bodies and towards dominant group needs and desires – witnessed through the (re)production of Eurocentric  and heteropatriarchal ideologies. These ideologies are often unmarked and  go unremarked on because they align with the domin ant onto-epistemic norms of our field and society. That is, to do research within the norms and ideals of academia is to be part of reproducing colonial ways of knowing and being whether explicitly stated or not. Indeed, as scholars from decolonial theory, postcolonial theory, anticolonial theory, and Indigenous and settler colonial studies have argued across decades of work, these norms significa ntly limit how we create theory, where we focus ana lytic attention, and whose lives and practices are worthy  of attention (wa Thiong'o; 1998; Smith, 1999; More tonRobinson, 2004). Within the Learning Sciences, colonial norms also s hape what we think of as good design, trustworthy methods, and ethical participation in and through o ur research, often resulting in practices of extrac tion, erasure, and exploitation. Our field has felt tensions in re cent years over the limits of epistemic heterogenei ty and the possibilities (and desirability) of objectivity, concerns that are tightly woven into Eurocentric ideas of science and reliability, in contrast to other traditions of rel ationality (Wilson, 2005). Many of our theoretical touchstones  in the field are rooted in whiteness as an a priori as sumption (Moreton-Robinson, 2004; Curnow, 2022). Only recently have we seen significant shifts to incorpo rate Indigenous ways of knowing and being int o our core theoretical canon (i.e. Bang et al., 2015; Marin, 2018, 2020), let alone inviting those ontologies towards radically reshaping how we orient to sensemaking and field-building. Reorienting ourselves away from the hegemonic (colonial) norms of the  field takes a radical repositioning anchored in dec oloniality, anticolonialism, and postcolonialism. That is, decolonial, postcolonial and anticolonial theory, and Indigenous and settler  colonial studies offer tools for fundamentally desettling the Learning Sciences. For example, scholars fighting for gender equity attune to ways that learners are dynamically positioned in ways that can support, trouble, and deny learners dignity (Espinoza & Vossoughi, 2014), their opportunities to learn, and their rightful presence in learning spaces (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2019). Much of this work attuning to gender, however, takes colonial constructions of binary gender as given realities. However, as Lugones (2007) illustrates, this binary construction of gender is a creation of and tool for the maintenance of Weste rn colonial power. That is, research in the Learning Sciences a imed at supporting gender equity, undergirded by ta ken-asassumed colonial constructions of gender, (re)produces colonial ways of knowing and being despite being equityoriented. Instead, Lugones (2007) offers a decolonial analytic for attuning to the intertwined mechanisms of power at work (i.e. heterosexuality, capitalism, and raci alization) that (re)produce colonialism through bin ary gendered relations. This  is one of many possible examples, where anchoring ourselves in decolonial and postcol onial ontologies offers new directions for our approach to learning, community, and research. In this symposium, we draw on decolonial and postco lonial work within and beyond the Learning Sciences to rearticulate theories of learning that serve more liberatory futures for us all; specifically, by re-orienting ourselves away from the colonial ways of knowing and being that shape our taken-as-assumed approaches to studying learning. For example, drawing on Indigenous ontological and epistemological definitions of time, space, and place enables us to recognize the complex relations between humans, more-than-humans, and technologies that shape learning (Bang & Medin, 201 0; Marin et al, 2020). This emphasizes the role of lands/waters and non-human participants in the study of learning and att ention (Marin, 2013, 2019, 2020; Marin et al., 2020), and it expands our definitions of learning spaces to include forests, creek and rivers and the embodied observational practices that Indigenous and tribal communities rely on to make sense of place (Bang et al., 2015; Elliott-Groves & Meixi, 2022; Henne –Ochoa et al., 2020; Hermes et al., 2023, Marin, 2013; 2019; 202 0). Studying learning from a decolonial stance entails drawing on ontological and epistemological definitions of time, space, place, and lands/waters into the study of daily phenomena associated with moving bodies and local/placebased literacies. The papers in this symposium reco nsider the colonial id eologies and relations at the core of Learning Sciences theory and practice by engaging w ith non-Eurocentric ways of knowing and being towards more expansive and decolonial understandings of learning. Decolonial and Indigenous perspectives also emphasi ze the ethical dimensions of research with non-dominant communities, exploring researchers’ roles and learning trajectories while attuning to communi ties’ sense-making practices (Vossoughi and Gutiérrez, 2014). T hese moves toward decoloniality pay close attention to the ethical dimensions of participatory  knowledge co-construction (Rahm, 2019; Vossoughi et al., 2020) a nd have sparked interest for better understanding and equitizing the roles of communities in studies of l earning, specially within research-community partnerships and co-design. Pushing back against deficit-based perspectives that attempt to fix non-dominant communities, these efforts stress the importance of mutual trust as a foundation for unlocking the transformational power of communities while honoring their everyday practices, subjectivities, and knowledges (Aldemir et al., 2023; Lezotte et al., 2022; Vakil et al., 2016; Jadallah, 2024). They emphasize leveling the status between researchers and participants, practicing respectful listening (Meixi et al., 2022; Rahm, 2019; San Pedro et al., 2017), and valuing particip ants’ stories and experiences (Meixi, Marin, et al., 2022; Windchief & San Pedro, 2019). Therefore, community members are positioned as experts whose wealth of knowledges are key to identify, address and reconfi gure challenges within their realities (Barajas -López & Ishimaru, 2020; Booker & Goldman, 2016; Gutiérrez et al., 2009; Ishimaru et al., 2019). The decolonial work that emerges from these commitments advocates for disman tling colonial dominance by valuing diverse ways of knowing, grounded in local and marginalized communi ties' experiences and knowledge practices, as well as rematriating land, waters, and other resurgent prac tices (Bang et al, 2010; Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Co ulthard, 2014; Tuck & Yang, 2014; Simpson, 2011).

Still, there is much work yet to be done; our field  has only begun to explore the consequentiality of decoloniality and other ontological and epistemological positions which fundamentally unsettle and are ultimately incommensurable with colonial ways of knowing and being. As Learning Scientists c ommitted to the disruption of colonial norms that shape what we know about knowing, what we learn about learning, and how we teach both, we are also guided by Tuck & Yang’s (2014) reminder  that decoloniality (and its sibling disruptions) a re not metaphorical: they demand material change in relations. This symposium is an invitation and a provocation: if we understand colonialism as central to many of the understandings of learning in our field, as wel l as many of the practices and orientations that ar e hegemonic within social sciences, what is to be done to reorient ourselves and re-embed in relations other than extraction, exploitation, and domination? How might the learnin g sciences look to other relations to rearticulate theories of learning that serve life rather than capital accumulation and racialized and gendered hierarchy? Process This hybrid symposium will begin with an initial framing by the session discussants to orient Learning Scientists to ideas of coloniality, decoloniality, anticolonia lism, and postcoloniality. Recognizing that these c oncepts may be new to many in the field,  the primer (10 min) will be an invitation to explor e key themes and bodies of scholarship. Symposium papers will present a brief overview of the context and questions of their rese arch, as well as their decolonial, postcolonial, and anticol onial theoretical commitments in turn (7 min each, 35 min). Symposium members will then form a fishbowl convers ation (45 min) to engage in a rich discussion acros s geopolitical, historical, and theoretical engagements to identify the consequentiality of decoloniality for theorizing and analyzing learning. Presenters will be invited into a discussion about key questions, including:

1.  How do decolonial, postcolonial, and other counter-hegemonic theories “show up” in their design,

analysis of learning, and findings?

2. Why do decolonial, postcolonial, and other counter-hegemonic theories matter for our field?

3. What might we collectively imagine as a future for Learning Sciences that is less rooted in colonialit y,

and instead attuned to decolonial visions?

Throughout the conversation, audience members will be invited to join the fishbowl and participate in imagining what more robust engagement with coloniality, decol oniality, anticolonial, and postcoloniality might a fford the field of the Learning Sciences.

Resisting relying on categorization in efforts toward anticolonial queer and trans solidarities Ali R. Blake In this paper I discuss how a decolonial perspective shaped how I (as a white settler and queer and trans educatorscholar) approached languaging gender and sexuality in a queer and trans communal clothing making space called QTthreads. QTthreads gathers in a city in what is colonially known as the Northeast U.S. QTthreads is situated in histories of multidimensional struggles for self-determination. Participation did not require explic itly identifying as queer or trans; many participants in the intergenerational and interracial group did dynamically take up this and more language in relation with gender and sexuality. Through a lens of the colonial/modern gender syst em (Lugones, 2007), I share how resisting relying on c ategorization of queerness and transness strengthen ed our capacities for participating in intertwined movements.

Affinity spaces premised on shared identification c an be valuable. Bounding can also sever potential relations across peoples from varying histories, ea ch with vast expanses of articulating ourselves wit h language in action. As Azuira and colleagues (2014)  discuss in their introduction to a special issue on  decolonizing the transgender imaginary: the term transgender itself does not begin to encompass the radically different relationships that gender nonconforming populations across the world h ave to health care, basic rights, safety from criminalization or stigmatization, and legal p rotection or regulation of bodies,  identity, and space (p. 308).

The term queer, while forged with multidimensional politics, similarly evades political precision (Fer guson, 2019). Spaces broadly characterized as for queer and trans folks often assume whiteness, as racist histories ascribe precarious possibilities for s elf-making to whiteness (Schuller & Gill-Peterson, 2020). Moving away from requiring individuals align with language borne pri marily from Eurocentric contexts can open for pluralities of struggles in relation with gender and sexuality whi le navigating “a different kind of relationship amo ng people, of making decisions, making and spreading resources  and information, and learning from each other” (Br anson, Ornelas & Rajala, 2024, p. 76). This can open possi bilities for nurturing solidarities with, for examp le, travestis of Argentina (Azar et al., 2024), joyas as particular Two-Spirit peoples of ‘California’ (Miranda, 2010), Sts’iyóye smestíyexw slhá:li or Twin-Spirited women of the Coast Salish peoples (Wesley, 2014), and more peoples seeking justice for histories of gendercide.

We sought to align with a coalitional vision organi zed around “one’s relation to power, and not some homogenized identity” (Cohen, 2005, p. 21). It was important to resist static and potentially univocal framings of gender/sexuality in language that can serve to deny  folks’ gender self-determination (Stanley, 2014) through the maintenance of in/out boundaries. Uttamchandani and  colleagues (2024) highlight how representational p olitics can thwart justice-oriented learning sciences scholarship, as categorization is unable “to account for the dynamism of people and the instability of these categories” (p. 268). I share how attending to queerness and tr ansness in a project working to align with a coalitional politic s around multidimensional struggles for self-determination that doesn’t rely on categorization can offer ways to ga ther around many material conditions in which “some  bodies in some places are allowed to move more freely than others” (Ritchie, 2015, p. 632). Decolonial relationality in the abolitionist futures project Gera Villagrán Becerra, Joe Curnow Winnipeg, MB is Indigenous land with strong traditi ons of Nêhiyawak, Inninew, Anishinaabek, Oceti Sako win Oyate, Anishininiiwak, and Michif governance practices that shape the day-to-day lived experience of city life in material and symbolic ways (Sinclair, 2024). At the same time, colonial violences and r acial capitalism are normalized, and the violence of policing and imprisonment are consistent markers of the landscape (Toews, 2018). Within this context, the Abolitionist Futures Proje ct has gathered prison and police abolitionists fro m Winnipeg to engage in transformative learning experiences de signed to shift the “abolitionist horizon”(Kaba, 20 20), and change what participants believe is possible in terms of decolonial, non-carceral futures based in relations of care. Over the last four years, the Abolitionist Futures Collective has asked how “we keep us safe”, taking a movement slogan and actualizing it through alternat ive approaches to community safety like training fo lks to respond to drug toxicity emergencies, build  alternatives to calling the police, and design non-punitive safety practices within our activist communities. As we ha ve begun to analyze data from the workshops, the co llective has struggled to find or create tools to measure the most enduring impact of the research project-- the relationships of trust and familiarity that have been built acros s groups through ongoing shared work. While we have  video data of hundreds of minutes of multi-camera data to analyze interaction within, relation ality has been har d to capture using the tools available to us (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). Though learning scientists have increasingly been a ttuned to the significance of relationships (Jackso n, et al., 2022; Teeters & Jurow, 2018; Uttamchandani,  2020; Vea, 2021), the tools and frameworks availab le to us under-appreciate how significant building community is as  a learning outcome and a learning process concurrently. In this conceptual paper, we initiate  a dialogue with the Latin America project coloniality/decoloniality (Maldonado-Torres 20011, Mignolo 2007) in search of new possib ilities for en gaging with the concept and praxis of community in the learning sciences. Through this dialogue, we  explore tensions between future/speculative oriented discourses in the learning sciences (Garcia & Mirra, 2023) and the underlying premises of modern, scientific inquiry (Castro-Gomez, 2019); as well as t he relationship between knowledge production in the Learning Sciences and the possibility of building and sustaining community. We conclude with emerging analysis on the Abolitionist Futures project’s relationship development as both a learning outcome and a learning process through the conceptual framework of decoloniality. For learning scientists, taking decolonial theory seriously requires more than just superficial engagement with ideas of relationship, it requires an epistemi c reorientation (Martin, 2011) away from establishe d Eurowestern, modernist ways of bounding and making sense of data. How might we reconsider learning sciences tools alongside other ontological frameworks where life and relationality are at the center, rather th an absorbed into the dominant canon (Dussel, 2001, Santos, 2024)?

Redefining the silent period: From silent to silenced—Disrupting anti-colonial othering and deficit narratives Sophia Thraya For linguistically minoritized, multilingual refugee youth and children, formal educational settings often function as assimilative spaces shaped by policies and pedag ogies rooted in colonial logics and the linguistic dominance of English. I situate this w ork within the broader discourse around the “Englis h Language Learner” label as a colonial project (Takeuchi, 2021) and the ways in w hich it positions learners within our educational s paces (Kayumova & Harper, 2020). Within these spaces, the  "silent period" phenomenon (Dulay & Burt, 1974) — emerging from applied linguistics, cognitive psychology, and language acquisition—has historically pathologized multilingual learners. This discourse, often framed  through a learner-focused deficit lens (Gutiérrez & Orella na, 2006), attributes silence to personality traits, so cial skills, or cognitive abilities (Brown, 1994; G ranger, 2004), while ignoring broader sociopolitical and instituti onal contexts. Such framings neglect the implicit m onolingual norms institutionalize d within educational policies and pedagogical practices, which uphold English linguistic superiority and marginalize learners' rich multilin gual repertoires. Building on Said's (1978) work on  otherness, this study, situated in and emergent from observati ons within the field and literature, examines how the  “silent period” functions as an extension of colonial other ing, where language learners are oppressively silenced rather than inherently silent. This silencing, reinforced by education policies, teacher training programs, a nd Englishonly mandates, mirrors broader systems of epistemic  violence that erase rich linguistic and cultural k nowledge. As an educator and researcher, I critically analyzed the damaging notion of the "silent period" through a discourse analysis of "silent period" and "translanguaging" (García & Leiva, 2014; García et al., 2017; Li & Gar cía, 2022) in Alberta's educational policies and conceptualizations of multilingual learners. I also conducted an interactional analysis of co-constructed linguistic fluidity and anti-colonial practices manifested in land-based relational spaces, during Soil Camp (https://soilcamp.ca; Takeuchi et al., 2021), a longitudinal research-partnership involving the participants directly impacted by deficit and colonial linguistic ideologies. Based on my findings, I seek to disrupt the widespread acceptance of this phenomenon and of fer a reima gination of alternative possibilities. This reimagination highlights the transformative power of embracing fluid and agentive linguistic practices to carefully listen to the historically silenced voices of multi lingual refugee children. Focusing on video-based interaction analyses of data collected during the first year of Soil Camp when many of the children were often considered to be in “silent period,” this work demonstrates how t ranslanguaging stances candisrupt these dynamics. By validating learners’ full linguistic repertoires, such practices challenge colonial constructions of language learning and reclaim spaces for multilingual agency. In resp onse to the call for anti-colonial approaches to languages in disciplinary learning contexts by Takeuchi et al. (2022), this work seeks to illuminate the transformative potential of anti-colonial pedagogies. It offers pathways to decenter  monolingual norms, amplify historically silenced voices, and foster linguistic dignity (Espinoza & V ossoughi, 2014) and agency in educational settings.  By situating the “silent period” within a broader critiq ue of colonial narratives, this study invites the L earning Sciences community to critically engage with the geopolitics of language and its implications for epistemic justice in our spaces for teaching and learning.

Nationalism, decolonization, and indigeneity: A lens into cases from the global south Arundhati Velamur, Rishi Krishnamoorthy Increasingly, scholars in the Learning Sciences are  exploring how we might decolonize what we recogniz e and value as learning, the roles of the More Than Human  (MTH) (Bang et al., 2015) world as part of learnin g processes, and the methodologies (Krishnamoorthy et  al., 2021; Marin, 2020) we draw on to engage in re search. Resonant across this literature is an understanding  of the human/MTH divide as a colonial artifact tha t enacts powered boundaries around the ontologies, epistemol ogies and methodologies that  guide our work (Lugones, 2010). Decolonial work necessitates desettling Euro centricism—and, as we will argue in this session, affiliated local supremacist ideologies—in what counts as research, how we enact those proc esses, and towards what ends (Smith, 1999). We learn from scholarship in the Learning Sci ences that attends to learning as emergent in and with the land (Bang et al., 2015; Marin & Bang, 201 8), through methodologies that are grounded in a re lational ontology (Pugh et al., 2019; Krishnamoorthy et al., 2021), and where learning emerges through land and kinshipbased decolonial practices and knowledge systems towards more liberatory ends (Marin et al., 2021; Meixi, 2022). As scholars with ancestral ties to South India, our  contribution examines how calls for decolonization are taken up in contexts outside Turtle Island. We interrogate how moves to ‘decolonize’ take on a col onial character on lands with competing accounts of colonization and postcolonial nation-building. That is, what counts as the beginning of (settler)colonial rule, or who counts as ‘Indigenous’ on lands with many conflicti ng and overlapping histories of occupation and migration, is often contested (Osuri, 2017). This paper draws on examples from contemporary India to illuminate how such contested accounts of history and place are recruited into serving Hindu nationalist aims, under the guise of ‘decolon ization’. Proffered as a rejection of western thought and domination, we show how these interpretations of de colonization are grounded in the very ideologies th ey claim to reject, and in local brahmanical (i.e., caste-based) hierarchies, to achieve a Hindu-centric vision of postcolonial India. Drawing on data from a larger study in a rur al South Indian school, we illustrate how the curre nt Hindusupremacist government has co-opted the fight for decolonization by claiming Hind u Indians as the only people “rightfully” ‘Indigenous’ to the Subcontinent, with all others (e.g., Muslim and Adivasi communities) as foreign others without legitimate claim to the land. Throug h critical interaction analysis (Philip & Gupta, 20 20) of informal interactions in the school, we illustrate how these sociopolitical ideologies are woven into everyday interactions and leveraged towards garnering public sanction to justify postcolonial settler colonial projects, such as the military take-over in Kashmir (Mushtaq & Amin, 2021). We will dis cuss its implications for other local geographies in the Global South that have faced ong oing settler-colonial violence. We argue decolonization practices within education and learning must be situated within complex place-based histories of colonization and immigration. Overall, this paper explores the sociopolitical and historical powered dynamics that inform learning and reproduce nationalistic relations, and how a decolonial lens can mobilize learning towards other, freer ends. Centering epistemic justice in learning sciences: Methodological reflections on working with caste-marginalized communities Ishita Pradhan Methodological approaches in learning sciences have  often been shaped by Western epistemologies, sidel ining marginalized knowledge systems, especially in conte xts marked by deep social hierarchies like caste. E pistemic injustice persists in these contexts through the exclusion and misrepresentation of caste-marginalized communities (Patel, 2015; Guru & Sarukkai, 2012). This paper dr aws on the concept of "activism for dignity" among Dalit students, to propose a methodological framework for advancing epistemic justice in learning sciences. Dalit students, through activism, resist oppressive structures while creating spaces that foster new forms of knowing, being, and doing. Their activism challe nges exclusionary practices, emphasizing community-oriented, historically informed, and identity-reclaiming ways of learning. These findings necessit ate re-envisioning research methodologies to honor the cul tural knowledge, agency, and lived experiences of marginalized communities. This extends the field’s methodological toolkit by centering Dalit stud ents as active producers of knowledge, rather than passive subject s, through collaborative analysis, contextualized k nowledge production, and community-led research agendas.

Recognizing caste's historical and sociopolitical c ontext is critical for understanding Dalit experien ces (Patel, 2015; Guru & Sarukkai, 2012). Grounded in c ritical sociocultural theories, this approach chall enges dominant, often Brahminical narratives in education research (Rawat & Satyanarayana, 2016). I  used a critical sociocultural framework to study Dalit students’ activism in higher education. While effective, capturing the full depth of their experiences and epistemic contributions demands further methodological considerations. Practices like co-design and participant ownership are essential to r ecognizing marginalized communities as active agent s (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003; Nasir & Cooks, 2009). My research revealed that Dal it students navigate oppressive structures by leveragi ng community practices and embodied histories. Meth ods rooted in dialogue, collaborative design, and narrative-based research better reflect these experiences. A key finding is that Dalit students employ “activism for dignity” as both a resistance strategy and a way of learning, contesting invisibility within academi c institutions and reshaping discourses on agency a nd identity (Pradhan, 2024). This activism reflects community-centered, relational epistemologies rooted in caste-marginalized values. Research methodologies must honor collective narratives, historical memory, and relational agency, which are often overlooked by conventional approaches (Rawat & Satyanarayana, 2016). This paper proposes a framework for advancing epistemic justice in learning sciences by centering Dalit students’ activism as a form of knowledge production. It underscores the need for inclusive research practices that challenge conventional paradigms and reflect the diverse realities of marginalized commu nities. The framework offers a pathway for researchers to authentically e ngage with caste-marginalized communities, integrating their ways of knowing into the broader field of learning sciences.
