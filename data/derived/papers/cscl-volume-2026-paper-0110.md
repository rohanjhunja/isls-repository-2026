# Iterative Design for Distributed Embodied Collaboration

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To effectively support remote maker sessions, tools are needed that allow distributed embodied collaborations to emerge naturally rather than forcing conformity to existing video conferencing. We present an overview of a multi-year iterative design, testing, and evaluation process of REACH (Remote Embodiment for Augmented Collaborative Help), offering insights into developing similar systems to support distributed embodied collaborations.

Iterative Design for Distributed Embodied Collaboration Casey Smith, Mike Tissenbaum cjsmith0@illinois.edu, miketiss@illinois.edu University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign Abstract: To effectively support remote maker sessions, tools are needed that allow distributed embodied collaborations to emerge naturally rather than forcing conformity to existing video conferencing. We present an overview of a multi-year iterative design, testing, and evaluation process of REACH (Remote Embodiment for Augmented Collaborative Help), offering insights into developing similar systems to support distributed embodied collaborations. Collaboration between learners at a distance has long been an interest in the learning sciences (Dillenbourg et al., 1996); however, the ways typical technologies mediate these exchanges can make activities like remote making difficult (Jayathirtha et al., 2020). During co-located exchanges participants use embodied gestures around physical artifacts to  guide attention (Goodwin, 2003; McNeill, 1992), communicate shared meaning with out domain-specific language (Roth, 2001), and help cue learning (Lindgren, 2014). However, video calls often limit embodied interaction with physical objects, making explanation, debugging, and collaboration  more difficult (Licoppe et al., 2017).

In response, REACH is a low-cost camera– projector system that merges remote workspaces by overlaying projected gestures and artifacts  to enable real-time interaction around shared objects (Smith & Tissenbaum, 2022). Each participant’s workspace is captured and projected onto their partner’s surface, allowing users to gesture around and manipulate artifacts as if co-located (Figure 1a). Developed through iterative designbased research in makerspace camps, REACH has evolved across multiple cycles to improve functionality  and explore distributed embodied collaboration during authentic making practice. The first REACH prototype, developed in early 2020, was tested in  a remote circuit-building activity between a mentor and student. Early observations suggested its potential for remote gesturing but also revealed three key design limitations: time-consuming manual camera –projector alignment, visual artifacts from overlapping camera-protector views, and limited  networking support. To  address these issues, we developed calibration software to improve alignment between physical and projected objects and migrat ed from WebRTC (n.d.) to Twilio (n.d.) for more reliable connectivity and recording. A web-based dashboard was also introduced to support remote setup and debugging. Attempts to mitigate visual echo artifacts in software (Liu et al., 2024), proved computationally intensive and ultimately unnecessary under typical lighting conditions. With these design updates, we piloted REACH in an afterschool program using a short puzzle activity (Figure 1b), where pairs of students used gestures to coordinate pieces across locations. Year one – building and debugging circuits with remote peers Adding a Zoom (n.d.) session at each station, we deployed REACH in a jigsaw activity during a 2022 study, where two pairs of students  collaborated remotely to assemble and debug a circuit  (Figure 1c). Using the Divergent Collaborative Learning Mechanisms  (DCLM) framework (Tissenbaum et al., 2017)  to analyze 12 cases, we showed that REACH extends key affordances of co-located making into distributed settings, enabling effective remote peer collaboration (Smith & Tissenbaum, 2025). Participants reported that, “it was easy to transfer skill” but noted that “most communication happened through talk” due to the quality of the projection. The projectors' low resolution and small image size made circuit details difficult to see and limited participation  from local 650 partners. Additionally, u sing a Chromebook for Zoom required headphones in the noisy camp environment, effectively excluding the second student on each side.

Year two – supporting making through remote mentorship In the second iteration in 2023, s tudents engaged in open-ended maker projects while receiving just-in-time support from remote mentors to troubleshoot, refine, and extend their designs. Based on feedback from year one, we improved the REACH Dashboard so sessions could be launched directly from the web interface, removing the need to start each projector manually, and simplifying setup in the field. Zoom sessions were shifted to iPhones with Bluetooth headsets making it easier for students to present their projects to remote mentors (Figure 1d), but remained cumbersome due to complex recording settings, account  management, and frequent accidental disconnects. Using DCLM to examine 11 cases, we showed that REACH supports remote mentorship by enabling embodied multimodal interaction, giving rise to distinct mentoring patterns such as guidance, brainstorming, and debugging (Smith & Tissenbaum, in review a). Users again noted resolution issues, though improved lighting and larger project scales made these less disruptive. Students also struggled to fit larger projects under the projector and share laptop screens when seeking programming help. While they worked around these limitations using Zoom, the challenges highlighted the need for a more flexible stand design and integrated screen sharing. Year three – shifting roles in distributed embodied collaboration In the 2024 offering, students participated in a distributed maker camp, collaborating with remote peers and mentors to build and share projects while shifting between roles across different interaction types.  Lessons from Year 2 informed us of two key improvements: custom video conferencing and a redesigned stand. We developed REACH Observer, a two-way video app integrated with the  projector’s Twilio backend to streamline setup, recording, and post-processing. Audio clarity remained challenging in the noisy camp environment, leading to a switch to noise-cancelling headsets. This improved clarity, but if worn backwards would mute students. Labeling helped, but additional training and automated prompts for low microphone volume could improve reliability. To better support  larger projects, we redesigned the stand using a VESA-compatible mount, improving stability, image size, and workspace access (Figure 2a). This enabled students to share larger artifacts, including full laptop screens, within the projected workspace.

Ten REACH stations across two camps and a remote mentor site generated over 100 GB of data per day with multiple video channels per link supporting the “always-on” interaction. To manage this dataset  (3.7 TB total), we used computer-vision tools (Bradski, 2000) to help automate segmentation. Face detection identified active sessions (Figure 2 b) and motion tracking isolated worksurface gestures (Figure 2c). H owever, gaze estimation (Figure 2d) using MediaPipe (Lugaresi et al., 2019), proved unreliable as students’ eyes were often out of frame, requiring manual coding. Analysis of 10 cases using DCLM, showed that participant roles shaped the use of DCLM, with mentor –student interactions emphasizing boundary-spanning act ion and coordinat ed joint attention, and peer interactions relying more on boundary spanning perception to coordinate peer  visibility and recognition (Smith & Tissenbaum, in review b).

This paper presents the design-based research journey of REACH over several years, highlighting the importance of iterative design for effectively supporting learners  distributed embodied collaboration with physical artifacts, even when they are not co-located. These kinds of design stories are critical for the fields of computer-supported collaborative learning and the learning sciences broadly, as they outline the often messy and non-linear progression of technology and theory (Kali, 2016). By extending the benefits of co-located makerspaces into distributed contexts, the design trajectory of REACH presented here is intended to offer a possible touchpoint for researchers investigating new tools for distributed embodied collaborations that offer a new avenue of support for hybrid and remote education grounded in constructionist, sociocultural, and embodied learning theories. 651 Bradski, G.
