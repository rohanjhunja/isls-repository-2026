# Convincing Novices: The Effect of Notifications on Student Motivation in a Blended Learning Support Environment

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Notifications have become a common tool enhancing the interaction between users and different online systems. Their main goal is to inform users about a particular fact, event, or upcoming activity that needs to be taken care of. This paper explores the use of notifications in a learning support system developed for blended courses with the aim of encouraging students to practice at home. The system has been evaluated in an undergraduate university course. The results show several positive effects of notification. In particular, they indicate that notifications enhance the engagement among novice students in learning activities; that is, students engage in a greater number of exercises throughout the course consistently. The findings provide valuable insights on several aspects of the design of notifications for blended learning support.

## Introduction

Effective learning support in a large university co urse can be challenging as the link between a teach er and a student grows thin (Ward & Jenkins, 2014). It becomes hard for a teacher to get an accurate estimation of the overall level of understanding in a class, let alon e recognize strengths and weaknesses of individual students. As a consequence, students can rarely receive timely feedback and guidance. The situation worsens when the course population is considerably diverse in terms of rele vant background, which is often the case in large B Sc-level courses. Large differences in students’ initial kno wledge of the core concepts makes it very challengi ng for the instructor to properly cater the course material for all categories of students. For some students, the course material may look banal, while for others it is challenging, and individual support is needed.  Supplementary learning resources can help when provided on different levels of difficulty. Many university cou rses embrace a blended model, where students are supplied with large quant ities of auxiliary online learning resources (Alammary et al., 2014). In such setting s, students’ motivation becomes a crucial factor, es pecially with regard to self-regulated learning activity at home (Wolters, 1998), and especially for novice students  who suffer from surface-level understanding and limited grasp of fundamental concepts, which makes them feel disoriented in a blended course (Robins et al., 2003). Supporting students’ motivation helps them stay engaged with learning material and resolve many learning difficulties on their own. Such a sup port can come in the form of declaration of learnin g goals, explanation of the current progress, or formative feedback. This information can be communicated to students in different ways including progress indicators (Brusi lovsky et al., 2009), badges (Mah, 2016), leaderboards (Landers & Landers, 2014), open student models (Bull, 2020)  and learning analytics dashboards (Verbert et al., 2020). In this paper, we explore the use of notification s as a medium to inform students about available le arning resources and help them stay engaged with these resources. Notifications have been used before to provide simi lar type of assistance (Tabuenca et al., 2015). The purpose of these notifications is to help the stude nt make a timely decision about when to engage with  available content, task, or activity (Hartley et al., 2020). Research conducted by (Damgaard & Nielsen, 2018) has revealed that notifications can be beneficial in enhancing student performance. A large body of the existing research on the use of notifications for supporting students has been done in the context of mobile learning apps. This study aims to explore the impact of notifications on student learning performance and behavior within the general framework of a blended course. It is especially focused on investigating students who might need help the most – the novices. Quizitor As the blended learning support environment, we used the tool called Quizitor. It is a quiz platform that provides assessment and practice material in two modes: in-class and at-home (Hamzah & Sosnovsky, 2023). In the in-class mode, Quizitor allows a teacher to organize real-time in-lecture assessment, immediately display summative results and engage students in a brief remedial dis cussion. This is an effective way to  raise awareness and engagement during lectures. In the at home mode, Quizitor is used as a typical self-assessment platform. It is a student-centered activity designed to support students' pra ctice and preparation for the exam. It is carried o ut asynchronously outside the lectures. From the point  of access, Quizitor has two versions: browser-based and a mobile app (available both on Apple and Android platforms). We have observed, that on average, students present  at a lecture are readily participating in the in-class quizzes, considering them fun, social and interacti ve way to break off the (sometimes) mundane and pas sive routine of listening to a lecture and assess their understanding of learning material on the spot. However, when it comes to the at-home quizzes, some students are less engaged with t he material than we would want them to be, they do not spend enough time with the quizzes and do not attend to them  on a regular basis. Hence, we have decided to employ a solution that has been successfully applied to inform users of online systems about important events, promote desirable actions, and overall, inc rease the frequency, regularity and amount of their  interaction with the system-notifications (Shirazi et al., 2014).

Quizitor uses notifications to provide students with informa tion and reminders about their performance and available quizzes. Notifications are sent in two ways simultaneously: as in-app alerts displayed once a student logs into the system next time (Figure 1a & 1b) and as push notifications displayed on the mobile app's icon, providing a quick indication of relevant notifications for the student (Figure 1c). For this study, we have designed seven types of notifications prompting students to participate in the at-home assessment. End-Of-Lecture notifications were sent to students after each lecture. Missed-In-Class notifications were sent to students who did not participate in an in-class quiz. Low-Score-In-Class notifications were sent to students who had received a low score on the last in-class quiz. Exam-Reminder notifications were sent to all students a week before the exam. New-Quiz-Introduced notifications were sent to students when a new at-home quiz become available. Inactivity notifications were sent if a student had not logged into the system for t wo weeks. Worst-Quiz-At-Home notifications were sent to inform a student on the quiz that has the lowest number of attempts. Figure 1 Notifications in Quizitor: (a) In-App Alert, (b) Active Notification List, (c) Mobile Push Notifications Evaluation The participants of this experiment were 190 underg raduate students enrolled in the Web Technology course taught in 2023 in Utrecht University. 155 of them access Quizitor to practice at home. Participation was voluntary and students could withdraw at any time. Quizitor was introduced to students during the first lecture of the course. After that, six following lectures were used for da ta collection. The students were randomly divided i nto the experimental and control groups. Students in the control group had access to all functionality of Quizitor excluding notifications. The students of the experimental gro up received regular notifications from Quizitor encouraging them to practice at  home and guiding them to optimal quizzes. Quizitor recorded students' in-class and at-home attempts and notification clicks.  We categorized the students' activity into two phas es: the lecture and exam periods. The lecture period represents the activities from the first lecture until one week before the exam. During this period, students regularly worked on quizzes, both in class and at home. For further analysis, we filtered out students who have not worked with at-home quizzes during the lecture period. This left us with 67 students. 48 of them belonged to the notification group and 19 – to the control group. We further divided students into two groups: novices and non-novices, based on their performance on the first three in-class quizzes evaluating students understanding of the three basi c topics of the course (HTML, CSS, and JavaScript). As mentioned before, the spectrum of initial knowledge in this course is rather wide. Many students taking this course have had substantial We b Development experience before the start of the co urse. For such students, the three initial in-class quizzes prove to be trivial, unlike the novices. The novice group consisted of students who achieved the aggregate score of less than 50% on these quizzes. Based on this selection, we have had 28 students in the novice group and 39 students in the proficient group. Out of 28 novice students, 18 belonged to the notification group and 10 to the control group. Finally, we classified the students based on their interaction with the notifications. Students were classified as  following notifications if they followed three or more notifications. Altogether, 8 novice students have been categorized as following notifications. As seen from Table 1, none of the parameters helping us to measure students’ motivation is significantly different among the groups (according to the Mann-Whitney U tests). Moreover, there is no obvious tre nd indicating any positive influence of notifications on students from the experimental conditions.  However, as we start comparing the target parameters between the novice students in the notification group and the control group, the situation starts to change  (Table 2). The overall trend shows that novice students from th e notification group have been much more engaged with the learning material provided by Quizitor for at-home practice. On nine out of eleven target parameters notification group outp erforms the control group.  Finally, when comparing novice students in the notification group that followed no tifications against the novice students in the cont rol group, not only we see the positive trend on all parameters, the results of Mann-Whitney U tests also indicate significant differences on eight of them (Table 3).

## Conclusion

Novice students have only surface-level understanding of the subject matter  (Robins et al., 2003). In blended learning settings, such learners often lack guidance and motivation to keep working on the course material. The obtained results demonstrate the potential value of notifications in a blended learning environment, for novices. Novice students who did not ignore notifications have spent more time with the system, attempted more questions, logged into the system more often and more regularl y, accessed a greater diversity of questions and to pics, mastered more questions and topics, and were more persistent when it comes to working with individual questions. While the experiment was conducted in a realistic e ducational setting and a reasonable number of stude nts participated, the current analysis was based on a re latively small subset of students. The potential to  improve the robustness and generalizability of the findings lies in expanding the participants to encompass a more substantial number of students, thus allowing for a more comprehensive examination of the impact of notifications.
