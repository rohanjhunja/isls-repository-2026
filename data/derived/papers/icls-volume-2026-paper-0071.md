# From Implementation to Innovation: Re-imagining the role of Practice in Research

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Reverse Translation (RT) is a conceptual framework repositioning educational practice as a generative source of knowledge. Drawing from Design-Based Research (DBIR), Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs), and Improvement Science (IS), RT challenges the conventional theory-to-practice pipeline by beginning with practitioner-identified tensions and insights. It offers a structured process for elevating practitioner insights into theoretical discourse, fostering reciprocal learning, and context-specific innovation. This model is especially relevant for knowledge mobilizers, such as instructional coaches and teacher leaders, who often support others ’growth without sustained opportunities to develop their own practice. RT contributes to the learning sciences by expanding methodologies for practice-initiated inquiry, deepening commitments to equity, and strengthening the relevance and adaptability of contextually grounded interventions. Using vignettes and applied examples, this paper identifies how RT can inform professional development and research design, bridging the research-practice divide in more effective ways.

Research and practice occupy complementary and distinct roles within the educational ecosystem, each contributing in meaningful ways to the advancement of teaching and learning. Research typically focuses on generating evidence-based insights, evaluating p rogram effectiveness, and analyzing complex educational phenomena (Biesta, 2007; Farley-Ripple, 2018; Farrell et. al., 2021), while practice engages directly with the realities of classrooms, schools, and communities (Boser and Mcdaniels, 2018; Brown, 2021). These roles are frequently described as complementary, though they are not structured to be mutually generative or hierarchically equal. Since educational translational research often analyzes teachers or practice there is a unidirectional flow of knowledge, from research to practice, with practice implementing externally developed insights rather than actively contributing to their formation.

This pattern is not unique to education. Similar approaches have been observed in fields such as agriculture and medicine, where translational models have historically prioritized the dissemination of expert knowledge from universities and research institutions to local communities. For example, the agricultural extension model focuses on improving crop production by training farmers and sharing best practices through participatory learning and community engagement. Described as “taking the university to the people” (Anderson & Feeder, 2007; Norton & Alwang, 2020; Rogers et al., 1977; Wamae et al., 2011), this model has expanded access to innovation while also reflecting hierarchical dynamics that may limit integration of local, experiential knowledge into theoretical frameworks.

Insights from agricultural research suggest that top-down “transfer of technology” approaches can be less effective than those that engage directly with practitioners. Studies have shown that meaningful adoption and adaptation of new techniques often occur when farmers are involved in identifying problems, shaping solutions, and participating in iterative feedback loops (Norton & Alwang, 2020; Landini et al., 2023; Passioura, 2020). These findings prompt a reconsideration of how knowledge is mobilized across the research-practice divide. They challenge traditional assumptions and invite educators and researchers to act as knowledge mobilizers to explore more reciprocal, context-responsive approaches that elevate practitioner insight to theoretical and practical innovation (Rycroft-Smith, 2022).

This paper introduces Reverse Translation (RT) as a conceptual framework and co-design method that reimagines the flow of knowledge in researcher-practitioner collaborations. RT is a practice-anchored research approach that illuminates practitioner-surfaced tension and uses these insights to inform theory. It represents a joint effort between researcher and practitioners to intentionally link research and practice making the work informative for both. Rather than beginning with theory and moving toward application, RT starts with practicebased tensions and insights, recognizing educators ’work as a generative space where new questions, innovations, and understandings emerge. In doing so, RT provides a model for informing theory through the lived experiences of practitioners, fostering reciprocal learning, expanding the relevance of educational research, and increasing the likelihood of uptake.

Collectively, DBR, RPPs, and Improvement Science challenge paradigms of research dissemination that casts educators as passive recipients of knowledge. Alternatively, each of these approaches treat educators as coconstructors of knowledge, centering the problem of practice as the starting point for investigations, building systems for continuous learning and adaptation. Reverse translation extends these traditions by explicitly inverting the flow of knowledge: beginning with practice-based tensions, it privileges practitioner expertise, and seeks to inform theory from the ground up.

Conceptual model description This paper introduces the Reverse Translation (RT) model as a conceptual framework for educational research, grounded in practitioner experience and informed by empirical reflections. RT begins and ends in practice, challenging the traditional research-to-practice pipeline. While the RT model is presented in sequential stages for clarity, its implementation is inherently nonlinear. Stages may unfold concurrently, recur over time, or emerge organically in response to shifting practitioner needs and context. The first stage, practice-based problem identification, centers the authentic challenges surfaced by educators, recognizing that in practice tensions can emerge in locally specific ways that are context dependent. This phase is driven by practitioner observations and wonderings; however, because the problems resist generalization and require situated inquiry they remain under explored in research. In the second stage, fieldinformed observation and documentation, these problems are situated in context allowing the researcher to gain a full understanding of the problem by classroom observation, reviewing student artifacts, or collecting field notes, which capture the nuances and constraints of the local environments. The third stage, collaborative framing and 603 prioritization, requires a balance of power between the researcher and practitioner. It involves researchers and practitioners co-constructing the problem into answerable questions, ensuring alignment between theoretical inquiry and practical relevance.

The model then moves to theoretical exploration and knowledge synthesis, where researchers deepen the alignment and connect the collaboratively framed questions to theories, frameworks, and empirical findings; identifying gaps or points of intersection between  the practitioner’s experience and existing literature. Stage five, design and local testing of solutions, emphasizes the iterative co-design of interventions, tools, or practices piloted in authentic educational settings, with ongoing practitioner feedback shaping refinement. From here, the process transitions into generalization and model-building, synthesizing insights from localized interventions to generate broader theoretical contributions and design principles that articulate what works, for whom, and under what conditions. For this stage to be successful insights from localized interventions are compared across contexts to identify recurring patterns and contextual influences. These are abstracted into generalizable principles, integrated with theory, and co-validated with practitioners. The models are iteratively refined and re-tested until they are theoretically sound and practically relevant.  In and after this stage,  traditional testing models could be included to provide evidence for new research-based practices (Cook et al., 2012). Finally, scaled knowledge mobilization ensures that the outcomes of reverse translation are disseminated in forms accessible to practitioners and policymakers, thereby closing the loop by returning knowledge to practice in usable and impactful ways. In this stage, findings are translated into multiple formats for diverse audiences and shared through professional networks and practitioner communities, in ways traditional research projects don’t do. Workshops and PLC's support capacity building efforts during implementation, while feedback loops ensure ongoing refinement. Dissemination is embedded into existing infrastructures and, when relevant, translated into policy recommendations for broader, sustainable impact. Collectively, the RT model positions practice not as an endpoint of research but as the generative starting point and ensures knowledge mobilization. RT was developed in response to limitations observed in traditional translational research and seeks to increase the relevance and uptake of evidence-based practices (Dragnić-Cindrić & Fusco, 2025). The initial stages (stages 1-5), focusing on broader knowledge mobilization and systematic adaptation, are more clearly conceptualized. Stages 6 and 7 remain emergent and warrant further exploration to determine how they can be implemented with fidelity and to understand how they unfold in real time across diverse educational contexts. This study employed a retrospective application of the Reverse Translation model to examine the design and enactment of an existing initiative originally designed independent of RT. By layering the RT framework onto current activities, this project explores how practitioner-identified tensions and insights were surfaced, interpreted, and mobilized in localized settings. This approach enabled the assessment of the model’s utility in real-world conditions, its alignment with emergent practice, and its potential to enhance the relevance and uptake of researchbased strategies. The Hearing Each Other’s Voices (HEOV)  project was designed to foster equitable collaboration (CrainDorough & Elder, 2021; Lee et. al., 2022), between researchers and practitioners. Participants were organized into dyads, each pairing a STEM instructional coach (who serves as knowledge mobilizers; Rycroft-Smith, 2022) with a researcher to address a locally relevant educational challenge faced by the coach (Victor et al., 2025). The project's structure combines a series of virtual workshops and in-person work to support trust building, problem refinement, and collaborative knowledge generation. Each dyad co-authored a Research Practice Brief (RPB), a short, practical document that translates research into usable strategies for teaching and learning. These briefs were grounded in authentic classroom challenges and co-authored to reflect both practitioner expertise and research-informed perspectives (McClain et al., 2024; Miller et. al., 2025). Data analysis included project documentation, design artifacts, and records of implementation activities. Participants were selected from a network of approximately 600 Title I schools, which receive federal funding when at least 40% of students are classified as low income, and included STEM instructional coaches who were matched with researchers with complementary expertise in the area of the problem of practice. Three coachresearcher dyads were selected as the foci of analysis. Two dyads represented urban school districts in the Northeastern United States, while the 3rd dyad was situated in a suburban Midwestern district. The data was analyzed in three steps. First,  project activities were identified across the lifespan of the project, with particular attention being placed on moments such as the matching process, professional learning sessions, school visits, inperson writing intensive, and dissemination efforts, where practitioner choices, inquiry and problem articulation were anticipated and surfaced as central to the work. Next, those activities were mapped onto the seven stages of the RT model to explore how well the model illuminates the project's underlying structure, tensions, and opportunities for knowledge mobilization. Finally, the RT model was used as an interpretive lens to examine the 604 project's activities, surfacing mechanisms, illustrative moments, and examples that align with or expand the models stages. This analysis helped clarify how practitioner-led inquiry unfolded in practice and where the model offered useful scaffolding or required adaptation.

Preliminary findings from the HEOV project demonstrate how the RT model can be actively applied to address modern classroom challenges. In the project, practice-based problem identification originated directly from the practitioner’s context, establishing practice as the origin of inquiry, positioning practitioners to be innovators in the collaboration, and ensuring relevance and grounding in local realities. Knowledge synthesis and theorybuilding occurred as researchers and practitioners collectively examined problems of practice, prompting researchers to reconsider their assumptions. One researcher stated,“ I had to learn how [things] were defined. I hadn’t really thought about the problem from the practitioner’s perspective or the resources that they might have.” This allowed participants tonegotiate meaning, build mutual understanding and shared ownership. Finally, the RPBs, which translated local solutions into formats that could inform other classrooms and communities, provided a way to mobilize knowledge. While testing is ongoing, scalability has not yet been achieved. To illustrate the practical application of the Reverse Translation (RT) model, a series of vignettes were drawn from real challenges raised by instructional coaches. Each vignette demonstrates how locally grounded practitioner concerns were translated into research-informed resources and supports. These vignettes, in turn, informed broader understandings of research-practice integration. By using vignettes to trace the stages of RT, the model's capacity to capture authentic practitioner needs is highlighted to engage research evidence responsively.

When applied across the three vignettes, the RT model served as a retrospective lens for interpreting how practitioner-identified challenges were addressed through collaborative inquiry. While not used to drive the initial design, RT helped surface core elements that contributed to meaningful change and offers a promising framework for future design. In each case, the process began with problems surfaced directly from practice, which were refined through researcher-practitioner collaboration, connected to relevant theoretical insights, and translated into actionable resources such as research-practice briefs. The application also revealed areas where the model may require adaptation or extension. By tracing the seven stages of RT, the vignettes suggest that bidirectional learning: in which practitioners deepen their engagement with theory and researchers develop a richer understanding of classroom realities, is possible when inquiry is grounded in practice-based tensions and supported with intentional interactions. Taken together, these examples highlight the potential of RT to reorient research-practice partnerships toward models that begin and end in practice, fostering equitable collaboration, scalable innovations, and sustainable pathways for knowledge mobilization in future educational research. 607

## Limitations and considerations

As an early-stage project, HEOV represents a promising but preliminary retrospective application of the RT model. Its scope was limited in terms of the number of participants, duration, and contexts represented. These constraints mean that findings cannot yet be generalized across educational systems or settings. Furthermore, the reliance on dyads, while generative, raises questions about scalability: how might the model function within larger or more complex teams?

A key design consideration of the RT model is its intentional centering of practitioner-led inquiry rather than focusing on initiatives led by administrative and district-level personnel. This decision reflects a strategic focus on surfacing and addressing practitioner challenges that may not always align with broader institutional priorities. By centering the lived experiences of educators and knowledge mobilizers, RT seeks to illuminate tensions that are often overlooked in top-down reform efforts. Knowledge mobilizers, such as instructional coaches, have extensive expertise and contextual knowledge, but often occupy a hybrid space within the education ecosystem, functioning as bridges between classroom practice and system-level goals. Their unique positioning allows them to cross boundaries and share knowledge, navigating both worlds while maintaining the flexibility to implement change responsively and relationally (Coburn & Woulfin, 2012). While this approach may limit direct engagement with policy actors, it strengthens the model’s capacity to generate grounded insights and foster adaptive learning within the spaces where teaching and learning unfold most directly. As such, I hypothesize that the uptake of evidence-backed practices will increase as a result. RT’s focus on practice-based tensions may not align with institutional research priorities, funding structures, or policy mandates. This can limit support for practitioner-led inquiry, or make it difficult to sustain RT efforts in traditional academic settings. Conversely, since RT centers practitioner lived experiences and tensions, the use of non-standardized data sources such as coaching conversations, classroom observations, and reflective journaling, may be undervalued thus impacting how knowledge is legitimized, disseminated, or rewarded in academic systems. Furthermore, no training programs exist to teach researchers and practitioners to work together in respectful and effective professional collaborations (Dragnić-Cindrić & Fusco, 2025). For this model to work, it requires intentional design, and skilled facilitators who can navigate power dynamics, support reflective practice, and translate practitioner insights into actionable research questions. Future research should examine how RT can be adapted to different educational contexts, including varied school systems, subject areas, and policy environments to improve the generalizability of the model. Longitudinal studies will be needed to assess the sustainability of outcomes and the durability of partnerships. Additionally, further methodological refinement could explore how the RT framework integrates with other design-oriented approaches, such as improvement science or design-based research, while maintaining its distinctive focus on beginning and ending in practice.

Contributions to the field HEOV contributes to the growing body of work on equitable research–practice partnerships in three notable ways. First, it centers practitioner expertise by positioning educators as the originators of research problems and the coauthors of solutions. This stands in contrast to traditional models where practice is often a site of application rather than knowledge generation. Second, HEOV demonstrates the potential for bidirectional learning, in which researchers gain a deeper understanding of classroom realities while practitioners engage with research-based frameworks and tools. As one practitioner reflected, “I have things to contribute… but this experience has helped me identify small points that I am missing here and there to really refine my practices,” underscoring how the structure supported critical reflection and growth. By centering educator’s expertise and lived experiences as legitimate sources of knowledge RT disrupts hierarchical dynamics in research collaborations and opens space for educators to shape the questions, frameworks, and outcomes of learning research. A second researcher stated, “I know we are thinking about ways to get our research into practice, but I’m thinking how do we get practitioners into what we do? Having a network of really informed practitioners is amazing.” Third, the use of ResearchPractice Briefs represents a new pathway for knowledge mobilization. Unlike academic publications, RPBs are tailored to practitioner audiences, bridging the accessibility gap while still drawing from theoretical insights. Together, these contributions expand both the conceptual and practical possibilities of collaborative educational research.

## Implication for research

The HEOV project offers important lessons for both researchers and practitioners. First, intentional design decisions were made to support mutual understanding during the initial stages of implementation. For example, the dyadic structure created space for trust-building, while the combination of virtual and in-person sessions 608 allowed participants to iteratively refine problems of practice over time. The co-authorship of RPBs further ensured that knowledge was mobilized in forms directly usable by educators. Additionally, the reverse translation model provides a structured approach for bringing practitioner insights into theoretical conversations. A researcher described this shift, noting, “Sometimes I think researchers miss the point… When I go back to my own research I will keep the practical hands-on activities in mind as I pursue the problem at a theoretical level,” highlighting how engagement with practice informed and expanded their theoretical lens. For researchers, HEOV illustrates how engaging deeply with practitioner contexts can lead to more nuanced and grounded theoretical contributions. For practitioners, the process provided structured opportunities to critically reflect on their own practice w ith research-informed support. This dual impact is reflected in participant reflections. A researcher shared “I actually do have ways to translate research to practice… which a lot of times I feel is missing in my academic writing,” suggesting that the proximity to practice helped surface actionable insights and challenge assumptions about the utility of academic work. A practitioner noted, “I grew in confidence knowing I do have good ideas that are research-based,” indicating that the process provided critical reflection and affirmation through research-informed dialogue. These comments suggest that the process fostered both theoretical grounding and practitioner reflection. Looking forward, future iterations of HEOV should consider how to scale this model beyond individual dyads, perhaps through networks of practice or district-level adoption. Sustaining the model will require institutional support, both in terms of time for practitioners and recognition of co-produced outputs within academic reward structures. The RT model creates meaningful professional development opportunities for knowledge mobilizers, such as instructional coaches and teacher leaders, who play a vital role in translating research into practice across schools and districts. These professionals are often responsible for designing and facilitating professional development, mentoring colleagues, and guiding instructional improvement. Despite their central role in building teacher capacity, coaches frequently lack access to sustained, reflective, and practice-informed development opportunities for their own growth. Practitioners have noted that budget cuts have significantly reduced funding for professional development activities. Funds originally set aside for teacher growth and development have been reallocated to other, high priority areas leaving coaches to “get creative” about addressing teacher challenges but also to navigate their own learning in isolation. That process can be time-consuming and limiting without clear direction or support. The RT model offers structured thought partnership, time for reflection, and collaboration with peers who share similar passions and challenges, and who possess the expertise needed for effective brainstorming. Practitioners also state that participating in the HEOV project was an uplifting experience. RT addresses this gap by creating a mechanism through which knowledge mobilizers engage in inquiry that begins with their own practice-based tensions. It offers a practical response to the enduring question: “Who trains the trainer?” Instead of relying on external expertise, RT builds internal capacity through collaborative reflection, iterative learning, and reciprocal knowledge exchange. By keeping the expertise in-house, and developing skills locally the model ensures that implementation remains contextually grounded and responsive to the realities of practice. This localized approach supports fidelity, as coaches and knowledge mobilizers are positioned to monitor, guide, and adapt implementations in real time. Their embedded role allows for ongoing oversight and nuanced support, helping to ensure that ideas are not only taken up but enacted with integrity and sustained impact. This shift not only strengthens individual practice but also contributes to a more dynamic and responsive professional learning ecosystem.

The HEOV project demonstrates the promise of the RT model as a practice-centered approach to educational research. By beginning with authentic problems of practice, moving through collaborative framing and theoretical synthesis, and ultimately returning knowledge to classrooms through Research-Practice Briefs, HEOV enacts a vision of equitable and reciprocal knowledge production. This model not only challenges the traditional researchto-practice pipeline but also advances a more democratic and contextually grounded form of educational inquiry. The HEOV project, while still in its early stages, explores what becomes possible when research partnerships truly hear and respond to the voices of practitioners, offering a pathway for bridging the research-practice divide through mutual learning and shared action.
