# Designing for Child-Centered Computing and AI Education Through Slow Pedagogies

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Educational technologies with young children are currently a highly contested area. Meanwhile, the need for understanding computing and AI has perhaps never been greater. This study takes an alternative viewpoint and examines what slowness afforded a seven-month design project and ethnographic work with a preschool. It is shown that slow pedagogy provided teachers with critical perspectives on technology, enabled children to experience, relate to, and establish boundaries with computing and AI.

Designing for Child-Centered Computing and AI Education Through Slow Pedagogies Robin Samuelsson, Mälardalen University, robin.samuelsson@mdu.se Abstract: Educational technologies with young children are currently a highly contested area. Meanwhile, the need for understanding computing and AI has perhaps never been greater. This study takes an alternative viewpoint and examines what slowness afforded a seven-month design project and ethnographic work with a preschool. It is shown that slow pedagogy provided teachers with critical perspectives on technology, enabled children to experience, relate to, and establish boundaries with computing and AI.

Recently, technology, and screen time in particular, have been topics  of debate in children’s lives. Educational systems, such as the Swedish system of the current study, are now undergoing a significant step back from digital initiatives and imposing screen-time recommendations (Díaz et al., 2024), thus imposing a new set of educational challenges (see Samuelsson, 2025).  Simultaneously, the need for people growing up today to understand computing, data, and algorithms in a deep and critical manner has arguably never been greater. Thus, understanding how educational technologies can be ’degrown’ (Selwyn, 2024), while still enabling young people to comprehend and relate to the machines they grow up with, becomes a key challenge for future work with children and computing. One arising question is thus how to create child-centered computing and AI alternatives for children, allowing them to playfully and in an age-appropriate manner engage with basic concepts of computing (Samuelsson, 2024a, 2024b; Kafai & Grover, 2026), while also increasing their agency as actors that can influence their digital space (Lodi & Martini, 2021). This paper explores alternative designs for fostering computing and AI education in a child-centered manner, building on the concept of ’slow pedagogies’. Computing, AI and slow pedagogies in childhood education While it is clear from the literature that computing can be introduced in early childhood education (Bers et al., 2014), even for young nursery children (Samuelsson, 2024a), a pertinent challenge in the field is how educational designs for early childhood education should look like  (Angeli & Valanides, 2020;  Yang et al., 2022). As most computing educational concepts have been adopted from primary education, scholars have highlighted the need to better ground computing concepts in early childhood pedagogies in a child-friendly manner (Su & Yang, 2023), where inclusive and diverse educational outcome s are crucial (Tissenbaum et al., 2021). Playful robotics and activities have had success in presenting children in early childhood with basic concepts of computational thinking and even basic understanding of machine learning (Yang et al., 2024). Currently, several educational initiatives are starting to create curriculum and activities that incorporate the basics of AI into early childhood education, for example, with trainable robots and games  (Su & Yang, 2023), and even through children’s play (Samuelsson, 2023).

Educational technologies are often bound to discourses of speed, particularly  in how Ed-Tech should provide faster or more effective learning  (Selwyn, 2024). An alternative challenge is instead to take one’s time with technology. This study builds on Alison Clark’s (2023) work on slowness in early childhood education and applies it to  computing and AI. Clark (2023) shows how slowness has capacities for learning that come from taking one’s time with children and education, critically opposing the hurriedness that is part of  contemporary education. T o explore new pedagogical alternatives for this shift, the current study employs the notion that slowness in relation to the ethnography conducted during a 7-month design project for children’s computing featuring educational and play activities with a programmable robot. The primary artifact in this work was an educational robot for children’s programming, called the Blue-Bot, which was used for educational purposes, played with, and creatively redesigned during the project. One of the groups consisted of a preschool department with 22 3-5-year-olds, one head teacher, and two assistant teachers. The second group consisted of 14 1-2-year-olds, one head teacher, and two assistant teachers. The design process and outcomes of the study have been described in other studies (see Samuelsson 2023, 2024a, 2024b), here, the focus is on the ethnographical data collected during the project. Over the seven-month period, thirty-two field days were conducted, collecting field notes, memoing (Emerson et al., 2011),  and video ethnographic data were collected 2895 during educational and play activities for a total of 25 hours. Four focus group design sessions with teachers have been conducted, along with both formal and informal start-up meetings and closing interviews. Children have also been interviewed about their perceptions of robots, computing, and automation (see Samuelsson, 2023).  The current study takes an abductive approach to data analysis  (Timmermans & Tavory, 2022). Being a theoretical and iterative approach to data, it contrasts with the standard inductive analysis of ethnography. T he aim here has been to interpret what slowness and slow pedagogy afforded, which also involved looking for “surprises” (Agar, 2006; Timmermans & Tavory, 2022) within the dataset, as technology and slowness are not typically related in educational inquiry. As such, the discovery of slowness during the project has been the key analytical aim. Slow reflection and design The project involved design sessions where teachers could not only spawn ideas for new pedagogical designs, but also critically reflect on the role of digital media in early education. As the project lasted more than half a year, the teachers could take their time not only to ’implement’ technologies but also to evaluate their educational-technological choices critically. Taking time with technology allowed teachers to critically consider what technology in early childhood c ould become and to rethink how to use it  in a more child-centered manner. This also included critically reflecting on and excluding some technologies, such as the widely used tablet, as it was perceived as not coherent with the practices and values deemed less compatible with the DIY spirit and arts-andcrafts-based preschool activities aligned with this preschool’s view of learning. Time for experience In the playful, exploratory spaces that emerged when children were allowed to engage with robots beyond educational activities, they had time to play with and experience  the robots. With the younger group of children, there was significant time that could be classed as ”unproductive,” such as when children sensorially felt the robot, pressed buttons seemingly haphazardly, or inspected the robot’s inner workings through the transparent shell. However, over time, these experiences proved  important for training on using the computational device (see Samuelsson, 2024a), but they also provided a relational aspect that grew  out of the  slow, extended exploratory engagement with the robots.

Relating and forming boundaries toward computing and AI In the slow approach, teachers could spend time with children and explore the new technology. Children had time to express feelings  about technology, such as perceiving the robot as scary, as well as  to consider what robots could do in future scenarios (cf. Samuelsson, 2023). Over the time of the ethnographic observation, as well as during interviews, it became clear that children had developed a well-conceived sense of integrity regarding robots and the potential of autonomous technologies.

The ethnography of this project offers insights into slow pedagogies, enabl ing teachers to use technologies more reflectively with children. The technology could be used in a more child-centered manner (Samuelsson, 2024b), where play, exploration, and meaning-making could come to the forefront. Aligned with the notions of computing for all and wider goals of computing education being realized today (Tissenbaum et al., 2021), slow pedagogies here offered a ground for new pedagogical developments. The extended time allowed children to relate and form boundaries toward the robots. This goes well beyond the often limited aims of similar projects to develop children’s computational thinking (e.g. Bers et al., 2014). The embodied and relational quality afforded by the slower approach, instead, allowed children to form well-grounded perspectives on what robots and AI agents could offer (see Samuelsson, 2023). The increased “time for play” (Clark, 2023) and exploration further fed back into the educational process, where the seemingly “unproductive” play and exploration with robots created new, unexpected agendas for the educational project.

In sum, the study holds educational implications for work with children and technolog y, where the alternative stance of slowing down and relating during technology use can be a major facet that opens up unexplored potentials for learning, providing a much-needed critical strategy for engaging with technology (Selwyn, 2024). In a world where computing has become almost ubiquitous and digital devices have entered even the earliest educational stages, a future challenge can be when devices should, and when they should not, be used. Thus, how we ’degrow’ (Selwyn, 2024) and reconceptualize educational technology will be a future challenge.
