# The Practices of Documenting in Research-Practice Partnership

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Documenting is critical for shaping and codifying processes in research-practice partnerships (RPPs). Prior work highlights a need for further theorizing of how documenting can support or undermine equitable partnership work. Drawing on practice theory, we examine how RPP documenting practices relate to participation and outcomes. Findings indicate a continuum spanning two affordance-constraint dyads: durable/static versus dynamic/ephemeral. This continuum illustrates how documenting surfaces and addresses tensions, which can inform decisions in RPPs regarding practices of documenting.

## Introduction

As research-practice partnerships (RPPs) have emerged as an approach to educational improvement, so too have calls for close analysis of techniques, strategies,  and enactment to examine how these partnerships op erate. (Coburn & Penuel, 2016). To date, scholars such as Bang and Vossoughi (2016), Philip and colleagues (2 018), and Vakil and colleagues (2017) have examined how partnerships come to be enacted through power dynamics. These papers also call on RPP researchers to examin e how issues of power and equity come to be lived i n partnership work. This poster draws inspiration fro m these scholars by attending closely to the enactm ent of partnerships and, more specifically, to the facilit ation practices that come to constitute them. By at tending to facilitation practices, we argue that insight can b e generated into how partnership work comes to be c arried out while creating opportunities to interrogate the practices of partnership work to improve them. In our poster, we seek to surface, interrogate, and  complexify documenting as a central facilitation practice for RPPs; here, documenting refers to the practice of inscribing actions, words, and relations into artifacts that represent and shape partnership work. While se emingly mundane and commonplace, the practice of documenting is fraught with contradictions that shape how people participate in partnerships. We illustrate how documenting simultaneously excludes and includes, produces durability and enables dynamism, and centralizes and peripheralizes perspectives and people. In this submission, we offer a brief theoretical framing, drawing on practice theory and actor-network theory to focus o n practices generally and on documenting specifical ly. We then illustrate the practice of documenting using cases from two RPPs: one focused on curriculum development and another on participatory design. We argue that attending to the complexity of documenting is essen tial for talking about, studying, and enacting partnership work, particularly as it pertains to issues of power and equity.

## Theoretical framework

Our focus on practices and the practice of document ing in particular is framed by practice theory and actornetwork theory. Practice theory is centrally concerned with understanding how social life and organizations come to be produced through situated and recognizable actions (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011). A core theoretical move in practice theory is to transcend dualisms; rather  than viewing two phenomena as opposites (i.e., str ucture vs. agency), practice theorists view them as relational  and mutually constitutive, where structure constra ins and enables agency and agency (re)produces, modifies, a nd combats structure. We use this conceptualization  of practice to highlight documenting as a situated and  patterned action that is simultaneously inclusive,  exclusive, durable, and dynamic (Latour, 1990; 2005).

## Methods

We use qualitative case study methodology to examine the effects of internal documenting practices in two RPPs. The first case focused on implementing culturally relevant science curriculum included four university researchers and six high school chemistry/physics teachers. In the second case, educators and researchers co-desig ned an interactive web-based tool for pre-service teacher education. Partners included three university researchers, two secondary mathematics teachers, and one community e ducator. For both cases, videos, transcripts, meeti ng agendas, field notes, and researcher memos were ana lyzed using thematic coding and descriptive narrati ve strategies to represent how practices of documentin g took place within each RPP and how they influence d facilitation, processes, and outcomes (Charmaz, 2006; Langley, 1999). ## Findings

The first case illuminated practices of documenting that promoted durability of overall RPP processes. University researchers created meeting agendas, feedback surve ys, and PDs enabling the creation of routines, know n expectations, and decreased workload on partners. However, the lack of documenting partners’ needs ultimately led to withdrawal. Partners experienced the static aspect of documenting practices in this RPP as alienating and noted that the concepts and decisions made durable in the process were misaligned with their working contexts. In the second case, participants experienced the co mmitment to dynamic, open-ended activity as an obst acle to design work due to the ephemeral character of docum enting progress. In response, facilitators provided support through a design template intended to increase the durability of participant contributions while still  affording opportunities to revise, rearrange, and recontextua lize their ideas. The practices of documenting in these cases indicate a continuum that spans between two affordance-constraint dyads (See Figure 1). Figure 1 Continuum of affordances and constraints in practices of documenting On one side, the affordance of durable documentatio n comes with the constraint of being static; by ins cribing ideas and choices in permanent forms, RPPs simultaneously enable future activity to refer to past work and limit participants’ agency to revisit or change the premi ses of that activity. On the opposite side, the aff ordance of dynamic documenting entails the constraint of being ephemeral. By keeping the practices of documenting open to change and revision, RPPs both prioritize participants’ voice and power in the working process and limit the ability of future activity to build on a stable foundation.

## Implications

Our findings indicate that the opposing affordances of durability and dynamism are not only both valua ble, but coexist to varying extents across different approaches to documenting. As a documenting practice approaches one affordance, however, the attendant constraint exert s a stronger effect, e.g. emphasizing durability en tails increasingly static products and participation, while over-valuing dynamism can lead to problematic ephemerality (Latour, 1990). The impact of these constraints manifest as issues of participant agency, inclusion or exclusion, and the centering or marginalizing of participant v oices (Vakil et al, 2016; Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). This indicates that while effective and equitable docume nting might prioritize one of these affordances, pa rtnership work benefits from cultivating deliberate balances between the two, treating them as a continuum as opposed to mutually exclusive properties. We suggest that faci litators consider which affordance (if either) bett er supports the context or aims of the activity at hand to inform their decisions about documenting work.
