# Toward Critical Joy in Learning: Lessons from Trans Youths’ Placemaking in Virtual reality

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, I analyze the film Creative Futures to illustrate how trans youth expressed trans joy, a form of critical joy, in their virtual reality art by challenging societal control and visualizing community belonging. In my analysis, I draw from speculative education, particularly marginalized youths’ practices of envisioning more just futures and research on restorying by marginalized youth to challenge harmful narratives. My analysis focuses on the public artifacts of the two-part documentary film, with particular emphasis on the 3D virtual art created by the three transgender youth and their explanations of their art. The youth show in their art that supporting trans youths’ survival and joy requires life-sustaining practices—celebration, acceptance, collectivity, and togetherness—supported by communities and trans kinship. The film further demonstrates how trans youth can be supported by providing social movement learning spaces with access to creative technologies to envision their futures.

## Introduction

In this paper, I analyze the film project Creative Futures to illustrate how trans youth expressed critical joy in their virtual reality art by challenging societal control, visualizing community resilience, and imagining their futures. I position critical joy as an approach grounded in cr itical phenomenologies and across a collection of m ovements, including Black joy, queer joy, trans joy, and disa bled joy. Critical joy encompasses marginalized peo ple’s resistance to oppression and life-sustaining practice s to support community resilience. It also serves as a counterbalance to an overemphasis on narratives of marginality. Transgender, nonbinary, and gender-nonconforming people face rising transphobia as ant i-trans movements are growing alongside and as a core strat egy for mobilizing voters within conservative polit ical movements (Barrow, 2024; Martino & Kuhl, 2024). As life becomes increasingly unsafe and challenging for many of us in these times, making space for joy will be essential for survival and persistence toward justice. This work builds on research in the learning sciences about LGBTQ+ people and learning (Paré et al., 2019; Shrodes, 2021; Uttamchandani, 2021) by looking at how learning to design for the futures we want involves not only so cial critique but also critical joy as a tool for social  movement learning. It builds on this work by bring ing it into conversation with le arning sciences perspectives on speculative educati on (Garcia & Mirra, 2023; Lizárraga, 2023; Shaw et al., 2023).

## Theoretical background

I approach this work by investigating trans joy, examining how  transgender youth envision  joyful futures for themselves. Trans joy is a movement alongside simul taneous efforts “focusing on the joy of being a mem ber of marginalized groups, including those celebrating Bl ack joy, queer joy, and disabled joy” (Westbrook & Shuster, 2023, p. 16). Centering trans joy is a form of resistance to trans antagonism and violence and the disempowering and harmful narratives told about trans people. I describe the collective movements of joy by and for marginalized people as critical joy, grounding my approach in critical phenomenologies  that center marginalized experiences of gender and sexuality, as well as  the interlocking identities and power structures that shape those li ved experiences (Ahmed, 2006; Shrodes & Paré, 2022). Critical joy focuses on the life-sustaining practices that celebrate and uplift marginalized people’s lives and includes recognizing that to fully live in joy, w e must also resist oppression. However, critical joy orients our attention from a solitary focus on oppression toward imagining how we can survive together. Ahmed's (2006) phenome nology of queer orientation shows us how normativit y comes from the pressure to walk the straight path, orienting our attention to acceptable objects and people. Finding those moments where we can veer off the path, or perhaps stray from it entirely, while potentially dangerous, can help us discover joyful ways to live with those who also veer off the path.

## Methods

The author and their collaborator created the film in 2018 as part of a competitive funding program for immersive films in Western Canada (Paré & Windsor, 2018). The film is a two-part documentary consisting of a short, linear film (10 minutes) and a 360º companion film (5 minu tes). It brings together three transgender youth to  design their ideal futures using a virtual reality paintin g application. The linear fi lm follows the youths' processes of trying the virtual reality art application for the first time, creating their 3D spatial painting in VR, and then sharing their art with family and friends. The 360º film immerses the viewer in each youth’s art, allowing them to experience it alongside the youth as they give a tour of their art and its meaning to them. The VR art application Tilt Brush was created by Google. Tilt Brush enables users to paint in a 3D space using various virtual brushes, including animated ones. Then, Tilt Brush art could be exported and pre pared as a 360º film that is viewable on YouTube.  The film was distributed on TELUS TV, a Western Can adian television network, and on YouTube by TELUS Storyhive for six years, receiving over 20,000 views on YouTube. Creative Futures included three documentary subjects: Terrance, Edyn, and Levin. Terrance (he/him) described himself as a musician, artist, poet, acti vist, and a transmasculine, bisexual male. Edyn (she/her) described herself as a drag queen, mechanic, and trans girl. Lastly, Levin (they/them) described themselves as an artist interested in exploring gender and sexuality in their artwork, as well as a transmasculine, nonbinary person. The youth do not describe their race, but Terrance is Black and Levin and Edyn are white. My analysis focuses on the public artifacts of the two-part documentary film, with particular emphasis on the 3D virtual art created by the youth and how they discussed their art in both the linear and 360° films. I paid particular attention to how the youth engaged in social critiques and how they envisioned more just ways of living in their art, as portrayed in the 360° film alongside their tours. I analyzed the film through a phenomenographic lens (Marton, 1981; Paré et al., 2019), which attends to people’s experiences, conceptualizations, and inter pretations of a phenomenon. Phenomenography is also  based on the premise that a structural dimension shapes e xperiences and underlies the diversity and variabil ity of participants’ experiences and sense-making (Marton, 1986, pp. 41-42). Attending to the structural dimension in sense-making is important to this analysis, given that ou r experiences of gende r and sexuality are oriented (Ahmed, 2006) by social, political, and historical forces, as well as intersectional (Crenshaw, 1991) dimensions of power. Therefore, my analysis focuses on the fil m subjects’ artifacts —their 3D virtual art and stories —and how their depictions and explanations reveal their sense-making and the underlying structural forces that sh aped their experiences and stories. In my analysis, I explored the following questions:

(1) How were the youth critiquing the worlds they found themselves in, and what challenges did they

surface as problems that needed to change?

(2) How did the youth speculate about the kinds of worlds they wanted for their futures, and what qualities

did they represent in their art as more socially just ways of being?

## Analytic approach: Speculative imagination and restorying as methods of

resistance toward critical joy I draw from speculative education, particularly the  practices of marginalized youths in envisioning mo re just futures. Garcia and Mirra (2023) frame speculative education as “an exhortation to educational theory,  research, and practice to think urgently and creatively about the worlds in which we currently find ourselves and the worlds we can learn to create” (p. 1). Speculative educational frames that incorporate digital technologies into their design work with youth (Lizárraga, 2023; Shaw et al., 2023) highlight how marginalized youth can learn to cre ate with emerging technologies while also learning to challe nge dominant narratives and represent new possible worlds and ways of being that center their experiences. This approach is further evidenced in the work by Shrodes (2021), where LGBTQ+ YouTubers employed humor to create reaction videos in respons e to harmful anti-LGBTQ+ narratives, thereby creating  public, social critiques while also imagining new p ossible worlds unrestrained by dominant ideologies. Additionally, Uttamchandani’s (2021) ethnography of LGBTQ+ youth in the youth group Chroma demonstrates how the youth prefigured, imagined and acted according to the future they wanted, in order to create a more just and inclusive future for LGBTQ+ youth. I also draw from education literature on restorying, a concept and practice that arises from marginalized communities' resistance to oppression and the erasu re of their experiences from the media. Through res torying projects, marginalized people reclaim their agency and assert their perspectives in shaping the narrat ives that define their lives (Stornaiuolo & Thomas, 2018). Th e film is presented as a necessary counterbalance t o the plethora of news media about trans youth that prese nts their lives negativel y and thus silences or flattens trans youths’ experiences (Paré & Windsor, 2018). In contrast, the youth constructed virtual worlds that give them hope for the future and consider the conditions of livability for trans youth. Where trans-antagonist dominant narratives have appropriated and silenced trans youth, the trans youths’ creative and artistic restorying through virtual worlds created pathways for them to “write themselves into  existence” in the media (Stornaiuolo & Thomas, 201 8, p. 346).

## Findings

Challenging societal control and oppression In articulating their ideal futures, two of the thr ee youths first shared the problems they wanted to cha llenge in order to move toward their ideal futures. The youth s’ experiences and artistic approaches are, of cour se, not homogenous simply because they are all trans youth.  However, this heterogeneity presents one of the fi rst challenges to the homogenizing nature of media repr esentations of trans youth. Going beyond heterogeneity, the youth delve into deeper critiques of power ideologies to unearth the foundations of societal control that limit their lives and cause them harm.

Figure 1 Terrance’s Art (left), and Edyn’s Art (right) Terrance’s art (Figure 1) begins by placing the vie wer in what he calls the “bad side,” confronting th e viewer with images of police cars, barbed wire, and electrified fences that keep people from leaving. He described the tall, grey buildings as a representation of the  experience of getting a job and becoming part of t he system of oppression in society and the monstrous figure in t he doorway as people who had been in his life who t ried to pressure him into conformity. Finally, he explained that the bad side is a “very cookie cutter, binary, Eurocentric, whitewashed” place. Terrance’s art of the bad side captures the feeling of being oppressed and trapped in others’ expectations of how one should be, with frightening  forms of punishment and control that keep us from freedom and liberation. He aptly critiques the legal and social forms of policing that disproportionately affect trans people, especially trans people of color (LaChance & Dwyer, 2023). Edyn’s art (Figure 1) invites the viewer “inside her head,” where she visualizes her emotions as a young trans woman in high school. Words surround the viewer— “Lost? “Alone,” “Scared,” “Anxiety,” and “Different.” She explained that as a trans person, she struggled to figure out what bathroom she could safely use. She pointed out how her school administration used transmisogyn istic, sexist thinking in their decisions about trans students’ access to bathrooms. Edyn explained that they “let trans men use whatever bathroom they want,” while Edyn, the only trans woman at her school, was required to use  the staff bathroom. This policy singled her out and caused her additional fear, confusion, loneliness, and distress. Underlying these administrative decisions was a core belief that women and girls are in danger from men and boy s in bathrooms and that trans boys are not the “rea l” boys who endanger girls in bathrooms—a sexist and transphobic belief. However, not allowing her to use the bathroom of her gender communicated that they did not believe her to be a woman and that she was a threat to other girls in the bathroom. Edyn was identifying transmisogyny —prejudice and discrimination toward trans women. Th e administration’s transmisogynistic policy placed the unfounded feelings of cisgender girl students above a young trans girl student’s safety from violence (Jones & Slater, 2020). Visualizing community, kinship, and belonging The youth all restory the future for trans youth by sharing visions for the future that visualize community spaces where people of different identities, expressions, and bodies are welcomed. These speculative futures envision worlds in which trans people can thrive.

Figure 2 Edyn’s Art (left), Terrance’s Art (center), and Levin’s Art (right) Edyn countered her story of transmisogyny in school, which made her feel alone and unwelcome, with her story of finding inner peace by doing dance per formances as part of a local drag show. Under a dra wing of a stage (Figure 2) are the words, “All the world is a stage,” and beside it, “Who am I? You’re whoever you want to be.” She shared that her vision for the future was “non-binary, where someone can just go on stage, be what ever they want to be.” Performing dance in a space that welcomes and celeb rates Edyn’s full expression of herself demonstrates the importance of LGBTQ+ and drag performance spaces. Similarly, Rogers (2018) found in a study of drag kings that “drag is an outlet for a gender identity already present but stifled in the broader community” (p. 892). Edyn shared that this sense of freedom of expression, without gendered limitations, and a sense of belonging is what she hopes for everyone.

Terrance shared the other side of his art —the “good side” (Figure 2), which can only be acces sed by being part of a collective to escape the bad side. The good side represents freedom and unity, featuring trees, grass, and water, in stark contrast to the grey and lifele ss bad side. The good side also included people who  Terrance described as all looking and thinking differently but who work together to be accepting and loving. As Alexander

(2023) explains, “Black trans life is inherently pe dagogical, teaching a way of being and knowing that exists

beyond the logics of punishment as a fundamental tenet of social organization” (p. 233). Terrance highlighted the importance of collectivity in the struggle against oppression but said that this struggle should bring us together across differences to experience love, unity, and freedom. Levin explained that they had not imagined having a future beyond 1 8 or 30, but they felt that life was looking brighter, so they depicted “an old person’s patio,” their future patio (Figure 2). They envisioned this space as open and welcoming, with LGBTQ+ and trans pride flags and other fun decorations, such as lights and bubbles. Ahmed (2006) explains that objects  and people orient us and affect how and whether we can extend ourselves into the world. Trans  and queer objects allow the extension of transness and queerness into  these spaces more readily. Levin also included a small, in-progress painting of Terrance, Edyn, and themselves doing virtual reality, illustrating how trans kinship is essential for ext ending trans people into the future. They explained  that they imagined themselves in the future, reflecting on this “cool” moment in history where the three of them participated in this film together.

Across these speculative imaginings, the three youths portray community belonging as essential to their ideal futures, with the elements of community taking on various qualities. Terrance presented symbols of diversity and unity in his portrayal of community, but he emp hasized that conversation is necessary to work across differences toward a collective goal of love, unity, and freedom for all. Edyn focused on the necessit y of an LGBTQ+ community space where people are celebrated and encouraged to be who t hey want to be. Levin designed a space that was welcoming of queer and tr ans people, as well as people with physical disabil ities who use ramps, suggesting the importance of accessibili ty and the celebration of identities. Levin also sh ared the significance of trans kinship to an ideal future, where having  trans friends with whom to create joyful memories can be another integral feature for trans youths’ s ense of community and belonging. Each of these comm unity qualities demonstrates what these trans youth feel they need and want to feel a sense of belonging and  joy and provides indications of what other trans youth may also require to feel supported.

## Summary and discussion

Marginalized youth face additional barriers to envisioning a future for themselves. To imagine one’s future in the face of oppressive forces is an act of resistance, but not an easy one, especially if one lacks suppor tive people. Every imagined future of the three youth artists in volved envisioning community spaces. In contrast to  an individualistic notion of resilience, the youth emphasized the importance of community belonging  for resilience. The life-sustaining practices the youth identified —celebration, acceptance, collectivity, and togetherness —were supported by communities. It required being with pe ople who celebrated each other, worked collectively toward freedom and unity, and designed for togetherness ac ross diverse identities and needs, such as age, rac e, ability, gender, and sexuality.

Given the rise in hostilities toward trans people, and especially trans youths’ rights to equitable education and health care, work that offers ways for trans yo uth to express their perspectives while finding rea son for hope and joy is ever more necessary. Already, the incr ease in US state laws restricting trans youths’ rig hts has led to an increase in their risk of suicidality (Lee et al., 2023). Educational researchers and practitioners must find ways to support trans, gender nonconforming, and questioning youth. The youth in this film showed us that supporting trans youths’ survival and joy will require communities where they feel accepted, have a sense of belonging, and are supported to challenge the oppressions that limit their lives. Furthermore, this film illustrates how trans youth can be supported by providing social movement learning spaces with access to creative technologies, where they can restory harmful narratives about their lives and engage in the speculative imagining of joyful and more just futures.
