# Apt Epistemic Vigilance

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In an increasingly complex information landscape, students can face challenges when evaluating scientific information due to discrepancies between the nature of classroom information environments and the unregulated nature of online information environments. This paper introduces the concept of apt epistemic vigilance, a framework that integrates epistemic vigilance and apt epistemic performance to address these challenges. Epistemic vigilance involves critically assessing both content and sources, while apt epistemic performance emphasizes achieving epistemic aims through competence, adaptability, and meta-epistemic awareness. By fostering skillful and adaptive engagement with both content-focused and source-focused evaluations, the apt epistemic vigilance framework prepares students to navigate epistemically unfriendly environments effectively. The paper concludes with implications for instructional approaches to enhance and promote apt epistemic vigilance in science classrooms. The goal of this approach is to equip researchers and educators with a framework for promoting informed information evaluation in civic and scientific contexts.

## Problem statement

In their everyday lives, people increasingly rely o n the Internet to learn about a range of topics, in cluding news, politics, and scientific issues. The widespread use  of the Internet for learning is relevant for educa tors because sources of information fo und online have different “epistemic characteristic s” compared to those presented by teachers and textbooks in schools (Barzilai & Zoar,  2012). In classrooms, information is often curated  by the teacher or other educational professionals, and it is assumed  to be trustworthy such that students can focus on understanding, building, and applying new informati on (Duncan et al., 2018). However, there is a discr epancy between the intellectually “friendly” sources in th e classroom environment and the intellectuall y “unfriendly” sources found online (Chinn et al., 2021). On the internet, and especially on social media, one cannot assume that information is credible, has been vetted, or comes from an authoritative source. Unlike in the classroom, the onus is on the individual to verify the credibility of s ources when surfing the web. This discrepancy is co ncerning, because research indicates that students can struggle to evaluate the credibility of online sources without evidenceinformed instruction (Pimentel, 2024). Despite feeling confident about their abil ity to evaluate sources, students often rely on “weak heuristics” for doing so, such as attending to professional-appearing aesthetic features or other characteristics that are designed to mimic professional communication (McGrew, 2021). Additionally, when students aren’t provided with opportunities to learn to evaluate sources, they might not develop a sense of agency or self-efficacy to challenge sources that appear authoritative but are, in fact, not. A second challenge lies in the fact that research s uggests people tend to overestimate their ability t o evaluate scientific information directly, especiall y among those with intermediate levels of science k nowledge (Lackner et al., 2023). Such a finding poses an interesting tension for K-12 science education, which rests on the assumption that students candevelop scientific lit eracy by attaining some levels of proficiency with using and applying scientific practices and core ideas. K-12 science education is (understandably) not focused on developing students’ expertise in all areas of science and, at most, it seeks to develop intermediate levels of science knowledge among students. Given these twin challenges-overconfidence when evalua ting science directly and difficulties with evaluating the credibility of sources of scientific information, we turn to the field of epistemic education for tools to address these concerns. Epistemic educatio n is a field of education focused on how students t hink about knowledge and knowing, and how this informs both th eir learning and their approaches to interacting wi th information (Chinn and Barzilai, 2018). In this con ceptual paper, we bring together  two concepts concerning epistemic education, epistemic vigilance and apt epistemic performance, to propose an initial framework that has the potential to inform educational approaches aimed at both improving stud ents’ sense of agency for evaluating sources in a way that is sensitive to issues of overconfidence. Theoretical approaches pursued In this paper, we draw from two conceptual areas, e pistemic vigilance (Sperber et al., 2010) and apt e pistemic performance (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018) to propose an initial framework for apt epistemic vigilance. To do so, we first discuss each of these concepts and then describe how unpacking the concept of epistemic vigilance using the apt epistemic performance framework might provide u seful insights into supporting students in navigati ng epistemically unfriendly environments, where some (or much) of the information might push beyond the l imits of their background knowledge. While we see the development of apt epistemic vigilance being useful for a range of settings and domain areas, we spe cifically focus on its application in evaluating sc ientific information in everyday media sources (i.e., the Internet and social media) as this is the primary domain expertise of the authors. Epistemic vigilance Epistemic vigilance is an awareness that informatio n communicated by others might be, intentionally or unintentionally, false or misleading (Sperber et al., 2010).  Vigilance can be applied to the content of the information or the source of the information  (Sperber et al., 2010, Tseng et al., 2021). For exa mple, a content evaluation might involve determining whether a claim is sufficiently supported by evidence or whether a proposed mechanism is plausible (Tseng et al., 2021). Such a n evaluation is focused on  evaluating the quality of the information itself. Source evaluations, however, are guided by questions about the source of the information such as “Is this source knowledgeable about the topic?” and  “Is this source likely to be communicating honestl y?” In the science classroom, most of the skills emphasize d are designed to develop students’ abilities to en gage in content evaluations (whether this focus is intentional or n ot), emphasizing framings centered on thinking like  a scientist, doing science, and engaging in scientifi c practices (e.g., NGSS Lead States). However, a crucial limitation is that it is not possible to teach students all the science they’ll need to know to make direct evaluations of information (Polman et al., 2014). The recent CO VID-19 pandemic is one such example of a society caught off-guard.

Fortunately, the epistemic vigilance framework sugg ests another path to support critical engagement with information: source evaluation. In this case, individuals scrutinize not the content of the messa ge, but its source. Often, individuals can use social indicator s to evaluate the trustworthiness of the source, su ch as the source’s level of expertise or their adherence to f ollowing vetted processes fo r producing and disseminating information (Duncan et al. 2018). Source evaluations can be a powerful tool for increasing students’ agency when they are not in a position to evaluate information content directly, which is often the case in today’s inform ation age. By learning to effectively engage in source evaluations, students can seek evidence about the potential quality of a source and use this to decide whether to criti cally ignore the information or investigate further. Some of the ways that students can engage in source evaluations involve evaluating the source’s potential conflicts of interest, their track record and areas of expertise, and thei r alignment with the current scientific consensus a bout an issue (Osborne & Pimentel, 2022). Individuals can also lea rn to c orroborate information against other verified and reliable sources, a process Caulfield and Wineburg (2023) have labeled “reading the room.” W hile source evaluations might be especially useful in everyday communication, students may be able to engage in some content evaluation of information that they have previously learned about. For example, when students encounter claims denying the existence of climate change, having some previous understanding of the mechanisms of climate change, the varying evidence supporting its existence, and the multiple impacts it has on society might enable individuals to engage in some direct critique of those denialist claims (e.g., Zummo et al., 2021). Nevertheless, it is not possible to prepare students to learn all of the science that they will need to know to be able to engage in such content critiques. Instead, students will need to be in a p osition to learn new scientific information as it arises. To do so, students will, again, need to make decisions about which sources a re credible for learning that information. This is a process that also involves making evaluations about which sources to learn from. So, in everyday contexts, students will sometimes be in a position to engage in content evaluations and often in other cases, they will need to make source evaluations. How do students learn to assess which approach(es) are most appropriate, given the context and their own knowle dge of the subject? How can students employ both pathways of epistemic vigilance competently and adaptively? Apt epistemic performance Apt epistemic performance refers to the ability to achieve epistemic aims through competence, rather than by chance alone (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018). When considering how students can learn to evaluate sources of scientific information skillfully, the apt epistemic performan ce framework provides a useful set of guid elines for the characteristics that can be fostered in students and the kinds of support that can be provided to students to achieve such competence. Apt epistemic performance draws fr om the field of virtue epistemology, particularly t he work of Sosa (2015) who argues that epistemic success should arise of competence and skill rather than by mere chance or luck. In their work, Barzilai & Chinn (2018) ide ntify five aspects of apt epistemic performance tha t lead to competent epistemic performance. These five characteristics are: ● Cognitive Engagement in Epistemic Performance: actively applying reliable epistemic processes (e.g., skills, practices, or methods) to achieve particula r aims (e.g., epistemic products) and ideals (e.g. desirable qualities in the product).

● Adapting Epistemic Performance:  flexibly tailoring epistemic strategies to diverse contexts and tasks, recognizing situational demands, and selecting appropriate tools and methods to achieve epistemic aims. ● Regulating and Understanding Epistemic Performance: using metacognitive skills to plan, monitor, and evaluate epistemic processes, while reflecting on and improving one’s assumptions and reasoning. ● Caring About and Enjoying Epistemic Performance:  cultivating dispositions for achieving epistemic aims (e.g., intellectual curiosity), valuing those aims, and fostering motivation to pursue knowledge thoughtfully and ethically.

● Participating in Epistemic Performance with Others:  engaging in collective knowledge-building and evaluation while also leveraging diverse perspectives to promote shared understanding In the following section, we apply each of the five  aspects of apt epistemic performance to the concep t of epistemic vigilance to explore how science classrooms (and education more broadly) might foster apt epistemic vigilance in the digital information age.

Contribution: Promoting Apt epistemic vigilance We propose the concept of apt epistemic vigilance to refer to the skillful and adaptive use of reliable processes associated with both “pathways” of epistemic vigila nce-content-focused and source-focused-to critically evaluate information in alignment with epistemic aims and ideals. Apt epistemic vigilance expands on the concept of epistemic vigilance by integrating it with the p rinciples of apt epistemic performance, emphasizing  not only the cognitive act of scrutinizing claims and sources but also the competence to do so effectively and appropriately across varied contexts. Such a set of competencies is particularly relevant in today’s "epistemi cally unfriendly" information environments, where misinformation is p revalent, and the credibility of information often cannot be assumed. Apt epistemic vigilance is not merely abou t spotting falsehoods or biases; it involves naviga ting complex informational landscapes with intellectual humility, metacogni tive awareness, and a disposition toward critical engagement. Educational approaches that fo ster apt epistemic vigilance are likely to support students in becoming informed information-seekers, empowered w ith the skills and confidence necessary to discern information reliability, even in unfamiliar or contentious domains. Students engaged in apt epistemic vigilance would be able to skillfully choose to engage in content or source-focused evaluations (or a combination of the two), depending on their c urrent state of knowledge about the topic and context in question. For example, when encountering a new claim about climate change on a social media post linking to an associated webpage for mor e detail, students may feel as though they have eno ugh knowledge to evaluate the claim, however once they start to investigate, they may realize that they do not. In such a case, rather than continue with a content evaluation approach, attempting to evaluate the information being presented, students might decide to  investigate the sources to determine whether or not  the information is likely to be coming from a credible source.

As a brief example of some of these aspects, we turn to Ahn’s response from a recent study on students’ evaluations of science information on the internet.  In the study, students were asked to summarize and  then evaluate the credibility of a blog post abou t sustainable energy (Pimentel, 2024). Ahn was able  to accurately summarize the arguments made in the post, which arg ued against the use of certain renewable energies. When asked to evaluate whether he thought the post was l ikely to be credible or not, he first attempted to evaluate the claims and evidence provided in the post. However, as with other students in the study, he struggled t o evaluate the information therein because many of the claims and evidence surpassed his current background knowledge on the topic. While other students either gave up in th eir evaluation efforts or chose to accept the blog posts claims because they “sounded” well-informed, Ahn chose another strategy. After trying to evaluate the content of the post, he adapted his approach saying, “I can see th e big logo here… so I want to look into them… Iwant  to do a search on [the organization], because there are some sites that can give their input on whether a site is trustworthy or not.” Ultimately, he was able to discredit the source with the help of other websites who identified it as a climate denialist webpage.

In this example, we can see some of the elements of  apt epistemic vigilance  at play. He was adaptive (aspect 2) in his performance, shifting from a cont ent-focused to a source-focused approach once he realized the first was no longer productive for him. He also was  able to adapt due to his regulation (aspect 3) of his own performance, realizing that limits in his own backg round knowledge were acting as a barrier to him eva luating the information directly. And, he had clearly developed some knowledge of how to cognitively engage (aspect 1) in reliable processes for both content and source-evaluation, as demonstrated in his use of processes, like verifying across multiple internet sources, to check the source’s credibility.

## Significance and implications

Our proposed framework for apt epistemic vigilance seeks to address gaps in educational efforts to foster students’ ability to evaluate information online, where the c redibility of sources and claims cannot be assumed.  By integrating principles of epistemic vigilance and apt epistemic performance, this work aims to equip students with the skills to navigate the complex and often mislea ding digital information landscape by emphasizing b oth cognitive and adaptive capacities.

The apt epistemic vigilance framework has several i mplications for educational approaches in general and science instruction specifically. For brevity, these implications are listed here and will be described further in our presentation, including: providing students opp ortunities to engage with authentic information sou rces of varying quality (Duncan et al., 2018; Pimentel, 202 4), preparing students to use multiple reliable pro cesses to evaluate information (both content and source-based), supporting students in reflecting on their own level of knowledge in relation to information being evaluate d, and supporting students in reasoning about evalu ation across different information contexts. In doing so, apt epistemic vigilance can be useful in guiding educators with scaffolding metacognitive strategies and creating l earning experiences that emphasize both content and  source evaluation. Ultimately, this work will contribute to culti vating informed, critically engaged citizens capabl e of making evidence-based decisions in an increasingly information-saturated world while reducing susceptibility to mis and disinformation.
