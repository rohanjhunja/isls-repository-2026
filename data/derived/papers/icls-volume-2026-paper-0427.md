# Semi-Formal Learning Groups as Design Interventions in Doctoral Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
A semi-formal learning group of four doctoral students and one faculty member in art education met biweekly over two years (2023-2025) to share artworks, written reflections, and personal narratives. The group formed organically and later adopted design-based research to identify the design choices that shaped emotional safety and creative risk-taking. Data included written reflections, meeting transcripts, photographs of artworks, and reflexive journals. Qualitative analysis produced three initial design conjectures: lightweight routines that stabilize participation without pressure, emotion-centered feedback that foregrounds empath y over correction, and shared documentation that links private reflection to public pedagogy.

Doctoral students in art education juggle studio work, teaching, dissertation writing, and professional identity formation across cultural and linguistic boundaries. Women of Color in art education face additional pressures, navigating racial microaggressi ons and language challenges alongside these academic demands (Lee & Maken Ali, 2025). Few doctoral programs offer structured spaces where all of these commitments can come together in a single conversation. Courses address disciplinary content; informal ha llway talk addresses personal concerns. The gap between the two leaves doctoral students to manage vulnerability, creative risk-taking, and professional growth largely on their own.

Semi-formal learning groups occupy the space between coursework and casual interaction. Members meet on a regular schedule with shared routines, yet participation remains voluntary and agendas emerge from the group. In 2023, four doctoral students from South Korea, China, Iran, Pakistan, and the United States invited a faculty member to join biweekly meetings in a studio classroom or online. No syllabus guided the sessions. Over time, the group developed its own rhythm: short personal check-ins, artefact sh aring, and open dialogue. What began as an organic support network gradually became an intentional learning environment. The members later decided to study the design elements that contributed to the group’s significance in their doctoral lives. Communities of practice depend on mutual engagement, joint enterprise, and shared repertoire (Wenger, 1998). Participatory design research expanded the scope of design-based research by foregrounding power, relationality, and reciprocity among co-designers (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Recent learning sciences scholarship on emotion demonstrated that feelings are not merely conditions that enable or block learning; feelings can also become learning targets in their own right (Vea, 2020). Educational intimacy, a  construct describing relationships that support inclusive engagement while prefiguring desired social futures, showed how relational trust shapes what becomes possible within a group (Uttamchandani, 2021). Arts-based learning, long underrepresented in learning sciences scholarship, gained visibility through a special issue of the Journal of the Learning Sciences  arguing that artistic practices can transform teaching and learning by centering creativity, embodiment, and multimodal expression (Halverson & Sawyer, 2022). The current study drew on these perspectives to ask: what design elements supported emotional safety, creative engagement, and professional growth in a semi-formal doctoral learning group? Answering the question required honesty about where the work stood.  The group did not begin as a design-based research project. Members formed a community, recognized its value, and then looked back to identify the design choices that seemed to matter. The three principles reported here are therefore best understood as in itial design conjectures (Sandoval, 2014) emerging from a first exploratory cycle, conjectures that future iterations will test and refine.

The study combined design-based research (Brown, 1992; Collins, 1992) with narrative inquiry (Clandinin & Rosiek, 2007). One methodological cycle structured the work. During an exploratory phase, participants documented experiences through journals, recorded discussions, and artworks including ceramics and drawings. In a co-design phase, the group developed pedagogical prototypes from personal stories. In a reflective phase, the group analyzed how those prototypes addressed inclusion and emotional safety. Personal narratives provided the content that the group then shaped into shared routines and teaching strategies. 2710 The group consisted of one faculty member and four doctoral students. Three members contributed to the writing of the current paper; two others participated through related presentations and publications. Participation remained voluntary, with no obligation or hierarchy, and members could join or pause involvement at any time. The faculty member joined at the invitation of the doctoral students. Because the project involved self-reflection among authors, institutional review board approval was not required. Confidentiality and emotional safety were maintained through mutual agreement (Ellis, 2007). Data included 7,500 words of written reflections, four meeting transcripts, photographs of artworks, lesson prototypes, and reflexive journals (2023– 2025). Thematic coding identified recurring ideas such as belonging, creative risk-taking, and emotional gr owth; themes were revisited collaboratively (Clandinin & Rosiek, 2007).

Three design conjectures emerged from the analysis: lightweight routines, emotion-centered feedback, and shared documentation. Each conjecture described how a small, deliberate design choice contributed to emotional safety and creative engagement.

Lightweight routines.  Each meeting opened with a brief check-in: members shared one word or one sentence about how they were feeling that day. No explanation was required. On some weeks, the group used artefact prompts, bringing a small artwork, a photograph, or a short written reflection to share. These low-stakes openings reduced performance anxiety and helped members arrive in the space emotionally before deeper conversation began. One participant described the experience as providing “a sense of belonging that did not require performance or approval” (R. Gu, personal communication, September 12, 2025). The predictable rhythm of the check-in stabilized attendance and helped members who had been absent rejoin without awkwardness. Emotion-centered feedback. During one session, a student placed handmade ceramic tiles on the table, each tile representing a daily emotion from the previous week. Group members did not critique the tiles as artworks. They responded to the feelings the tiles conveyed, sharing their own emotional associations. As one participant observed, “I see people respond in a more welcoming and open environment than traditional art critique because they don’t judge the works; they share perspectives that are honest and equal” (A. M. Ali, personal communication, September 13, 2025). Emotion-centered feedback shifted the purpose of response from evaluation to recognition, creating conditions where creative risk-taking felt possible because failure was not the point.

Shared documentation. After several months of meeting, the group began co-authoring reflections and presentation materials. A conference abstract would draw on journal entries from three members; a lesson prototype would incorporate ideas from a group conversation. One participant explained that these collaborative records “made visible how ideas moved from conversation to classroom application” (A. M. Ali, personal communication, September 13, 2025). A concrete example: one member adapted the check-in routine for a university ceramics course, opening class sessions with students sharing an object that represented their week. Shared documentation served as the bridge between private narrative and public pedagogy, making the group’s learning available beyond its own meetings.

The three conjectures reported here are preliminary. The group did not begin with explicit design hypotheses, and each conjecture emerged from a single exploratory cycle. Future work will test these conjectures through deliberate implementation in new contexts, track how the conjectures change through iteration, and include a wider range of participants. The study's small scale and self-study design limit generalizability. Despite these limitations, the findings offer a practical contribution. Lightweight routines, emotion-centered feedback, and shared documentation are low-resource strategies that require no special funding, technology, or institutional approval. Any group of doctoral students with a shared interest and a regular meeting time could try them. For the learning sciences, the study reinforces a growing body of work arguing that emotion is central to how people learn, not a distraction from cognition but a driver of engagement and creative practice (Uttamchandani, 2021; Vea, 2020). The findings also align with calls to position the arts as spaces where multimodal expression advances learning beyond traditional STEM outcomes (Halverson & Sawyer, 2022).
