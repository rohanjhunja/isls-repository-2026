# Reimagining Informal STEM Through Radically Community-Based Learning Spaces

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This practice-oriented paper explores how informal STEM learning (ISL) can address inequities and broaden participation by leveraging nontraditional, culturally relevant spaces. Many ISL activities occur in science centers that fail to engage underserved communities, resulting in the "Lost Einsteins" phenomenon—missed potential innovators due to systemic barriers. Grounded in culturally responsive pedagogy, this pilot project used community-centered methodologies to transform informal spaces into hubs for STEM learning. Through partnerships with local organizations and retailers, ISL experiences were delivered in environments reflecting community values, reducing access barriers and fostering belonging. Early findings from post-activity surveys revealed significant first-time exposure to STEM technologies and increased interest in further learning. By prioritizing cultural relevance, the project reimagines STEM accessibility, presenting a scalable ISL model that builds equitable pathways for historically excluded groups. This initiative demonstrates how rethinking STEM learning spaces can catalyze diversity, inclusion, and innovation within STEM fields.

## Introduction

Envisioning futures for a shared world should inclu de all voices, but this is unfortunately rarely the  case. Even though we know that global grand challenges are bes t addressed with a diversity of perspectives and id eas, structures and systems of limited participation con tinue to persist in innovation activities-particularly in the technological domain. The likelihood that a child will grow up to be an innovator is influenced less by aptitude or interest than by socioeconomic status and childhood environment, specifically their proximity to innovators who look like them (Bell et al. 2019, Cian et al. 2022). The many “Lost Einsteins” that result from this exposure gap— specifically racial-ethnic minorities, low-income individuals, and women, represent both a  loss of potential innovation and growth in STEM fields and a community-wide loss of opportunity for upward mobility (Chetty et al., 2017). Educational actors are at a crucial pos ition to create pathways that can reach the Lost Ei nsteins and extend the world-making ability to a wider group of participants. What this requires, however, is the ability from the part of such actors to envision nontraditional pathways, forge nontraditional alliances, and desig n for the participants that are not currently sought out.

For the last decade, StreetCode Academy, a tech-education nonprofit, has worked towards bridging th e gap that exists between communities of color and ST EM (Science, Technology, Engineering, Mathematics) education and professions. With its efforts, Street Code Academy has successfully increased active commun itywide engagement and sustained interest in STEM lear ning, which has further created pathways to profess ional opportunities. The approach developed by StreetCode is a community-centered, holistic model of Informal STEM Learning (ISL), which the organization has found to  be impactful in creating cultural relevance for ST EM in historically marginalized communities. Cultural relevance is key to emphasize here as too often opportunities are built with the idea that as long as access is provided, participants will opt in. However, as we know from work on development and empowerment (e.g. Robeyns, 2005; Se n, 2005), opportunities are real only if they are r elevant and obtainable in the social context of the indiv idual. We argue that this cultural relevance is cur rently not taken seriously enough in the efforts of educational actors to reach nontraditional participants in STEM/tech education, and, further, world-making.

In the interest of closing academic achievement gap s to increase STEM diversity, a large body of resea rch has been conducted on how self-selecting participants react to and engage with ISL  activities (Harlow et al, 2019; Brahms and Crowley, 2016). Considerably less research addresses the factors preventing marginalized youth from participating in STEM activities that provide on-ramps to STEM pathways. Many existing ISL activitie s are offered in museums and science centers —not intrinsically informal spac es—and target those seeking STEM enrichment, who presumably already benefit from exp osure and access to such resources. Studies on incr easing equity or diversity in STEM set in these environmen ts inevitably overlook community members who may fe el uncomfortable, unwelcome, or discriminated against in such spaces. The project presented in this practice-oriented paper de-emphasizes formal teaching structures, roles, and locations, focusing instead on strengthening the ta rget community’s creative energy and agency. The fo cus is rather on how informal learning, facilitated by com munity-centered events, workshops, and design challenges, can not only promote STEM engagement but also contr ibute to the broader goals of equity, community-driven methodologies, and the emergence of more diverse STEM-informed individuals and communities. More specifically, this paper depicts a pilot proje ct that has developed and tested innovative ways of delivering ISL experiences to hard-to-reach populations—using locations where they feel deep ownership and belonging. The rationale of this project is that tr ue diversity, equity, and inclusion in STEM will be  achieved through radically informal STEM learning approaches  rather than through adjustments to existing academic pathways. More concretely, the pilot project aims to increase the accessibility of ISL by blending STEM learning experiences with local community values in cultural ly relevant public spaces. Offering ISL in such an idiosyncratic environment removes barriers to acces s, increases the sense of belonging, and encourages  familywide engagement.  This paper also represents a research-practice partnership where StreetCode Academy, in charge of the pilot project, works in collaboration with a professor at the University of California, Merced to both apply existing and create novel academic knowledge in the field of learning and innovation sciences. Theoretical grounding This work is grounded in culturally responsive STEM  learning (Ashcraft et al., 2017; Eglash et al., 20 13; Kafai et al., 2014), Learning Ecologies (Barron, 2004; Jackson, 2019; Pinkard, 2020), Community of Practice (Wenger, 1998; Holland and Lave, 2019), and Sociocultural Th eory (Carrino et al., 2016). First, the work aligns  with a growing body of research on culturally responsive S TEM education that highlights the importance of cen tering the lived experience of minority learners and treating these experiences as valuable assets, rather than hindrances to be overcome (Ashcraft et al., 2017; Eglash et al., 2013). Transforming a deficit framework that pos itions marginalized groups at an inherently weaker positio n to engage in STEM into one that views their cult ural background as an asset for creativity, is essential  in extending world-making opportunities for everyone. Recent research has indicated that a sense of belonging is  a key mediating factor in improving learning outco mes, particularly for under-represented youth (Walton and Cohen, 2011; Strayhorn, 2018), and fostering it through culturally situated learning environments is centra l to our approach. We know that engagement and agen cy are more likely to happen when the sites of learning ar e inviting, culturally relevant, and comfortable, a nd that processes of intrinsic motivation for sustaining en gagement are more likely to be initiated in such si tes (e.g. Amabile, 1998; Freeman et al., 2007).

Three assumptions are key to understanding the conn ection between such communities of practice and learning: knowledge is a social process, knowledge is situated in a real-world context, and learning takes place not only in formal settings but also through  interactions with others and the development of mea ning, cultural languages and artifacts of their practice (Wenger, 1998). Catalyzing cultural shifts towards a future where STEM opportunities and world-shaping innovation activity is equitably shared, ca pitalizing on these facets of social learning is essential, and has the ability to disru pt traditional teaching methods and conventions tha t reflect the dominant culture. Existing structural inequalities, including class discrimination, shape the way people feel about STEM (Dawson, 2014) - creating a major motivational hindrance for minority participants. Conversely, when ISL is community-centered and designed to reflect the cultures of minoritized communities, ISL is made accessible to ethnically, linguistically, racially, and economically diverse families (Garibay et al., 2017). Existing research indicates that patterns of nonpar ticipation in STEM education programs are not the result of lack of interest, cost, or barriers to ac cess, but are due to ethnicity and social class, wi th minoritized individuals and those from lower socioeconomic levels feeling excluded from both science and high-status cultural institutions like museums (Godec et al. 2022). Because most ISL takes place outside of schools, universities, and the labor market, less attention is paid to equity than in formal settings (Dawson, 2014). Additionally, North American research suggests that social variables, s uch as age, class, ethnicity, gender, or language s poken may play an even greater role in ISL experiences than i n formalized learning environments (Hinojosa et al. 2021). Many existing ISL activities are currently offered in museums and science centers, which are not intri nsically spaces that are perceived as open or informal by mi nority learners. Hence, they may not represent ‘rea l opportunities’ as described by  Robeyns (2005) and Sen (2005). As such, the informa l learning spaces are likely to target those seeking STEM enrichment, who presum ably already benefit from exposure and access to su ch resources.

Informed by these perspectives, our project explore s how community-centered learning frameworks contribute to increased STEM interest and engagement for segments of the population that are frequently excluded from ISL opportunities. At a higher level, we apply  a sociocultural understanding of how scaling a hol istic, community-driven, ISL model can catalyze culture shifts, providing on-ramps to lifelong learning. Design and implementation The “Who’s Next Tour: STEM Learning Reimagined” pro ject began in the spring season of 2023, aimed to increase the accessibility of ISL by blending STEM learning experiences with local community values in culturally relevant public spaces. This multi-city tour including ISL events took place in multiple sta tes, at 11 different Shoe Palace locations. Our retail chain p artner on this project, Shoe Palace, was an ideal l ocation for introducing ISL to a hard-to-reach demographic. Shoe Palace is a family-owned business that has grown into a well-recognized apparel brand and is deeply connected to  popular urban and youth culture. The audience and employees of Shoe Palace reflect a similar participant community at StreetCode Academy: 88% of customers are people of color; 82% of employees are people of color; and 90% of employees are under 35 years old. Interactive pop-up tech stations were installed in the various Shoe Palace locations, featuring augmented reality, virtual reality, and artificial intelligen ce technologies. Shoe Palace customers and event at tendees were invited to “play” with the various tools to explore  their functionality while engaging in conversation s with StreetCode staff and ShoePalace employees about the ir applications in professional and personal uses. Live tech demonstrations and hands-on activities were co-designed with local community organizations to cura te stations that might better resonate with attendees’ cultural  contexts and interests, helping participants envision how these tools could be leveraged to play meaningful roles in their individual and communal lives. This project focused on delivering ISL in nontraditional spaces to a large,  geographically diverse population segment not typi cally engaged with or represented in STEM. It advanced co llaboration and innovation by uniting a STEM educat ion nonprofit organization, a research university, and a national retail chain considered a cultural pilla r of our target audience. More than 1600 people attended and partic ipated in this initiative, including casual Shoe Pa lace shoppers who discovered the installations during th eir visits, as well as local student-facing organizations and others who were invited to partner with StreetCode in each city. Working with an equity lens, the proj ect has the potential to implement and evaluate a novel ISL approach in national locations. Participants were asked to complete post-participation interviews to gauge interest level and potential for longer-term engagement. Around 74 percent of participants reported that this was their first time encounterin g these technologies, suggesting the im portance of bringing tech-exposure experiences to general audiences. At each station (Augmented Reality, Virtual Reality, and Artificial Intelligence), participants were surveyed on their interest in learning more, on a scale of 1 to 5, wi th 5 being the hi ghest. Participants at all three stations reported between 4 and 5. Across locations, between 20 and 4 0 percent of participants reported an interest in e nrolling in a formal class to deepen their knowledge of the various technologies they explored, varying mostly by the type of tech exposure they experienced.

These preliminary findings suggest a positive corre lation between culturally complementary ISL locations and the receptivity of hard-to-reach segments of the population to STEM learning. From a theory-building perspective, these findings highlight the importance of socio-cultural factors, specifically that informal STEM engagement in culturally supportive environmen ts may contribute to a greater sense of belonging t hat increases interest and participation. Future iterat ions of this project aim to further ex plore these potential contributions that may inform innovations in the delivery of ISL in informal STEM learning environments. Practice-based learnings from implementation As a practice-oriented initiative, this project contributes to in creasing knowledge around the diversification of informal STEM education by challenging conceptions of what counts as a STEM learning environment. It d oes so through the application and evaluation of approaches that deliver ISL in settings that are culturally relevant to underrepresented community members who do not normally participate in STEM learning. The project puts forth a holistic, community-centered approach to creating cultural releva nce in ISL, which includes intergenerational learning environments, radically informal and famil iar learning spaces, cultural modeling by instructo rs, and community-based innovation projects. This approach aims to deliver ISL in a way that addresses potential barriers as well as resources on the level of the individual, community, and social environment. As such, the p roject contributes to the current discussion on culturally  responsive STEM education (Ashcraft et al., 2017; Eglash et al., 2013) as well as diversity and belonging in the technology fields (B uzzeto-More et al, 2010; Nugent et al, 2010; Outlay, 2016). Current approaches in this dom ain still tend to attribute scarce engagement of un derserved communities to a lack of interest or aptitude whereas there is a pertinent need to critically examine the norms and structural set-up in the current learning environments (both informal and formal), and find ways to redesign these as more radically inclusive.

The role of ISL is significant here, while still un derdeveloped. Whereas inequity in informal STEM learning and the shortcomings of traditional ISL en vironments to reach minoritized people have been re searched individually, this project aims to integrate those perspectives in making informal STEM learning more equitable, accessible, and able to reach a wider, currently un served audience. The project highlights the role of  site-based, community-centered programming in achieving this. We are inspired by theories of Learning Ecologies (Barron, 2004; Jackson, 2019; Pinkard, 2020), which take int o account histories of inclusion and exclusion to c reate opportunities that are inviting, relevant, responsive, and intellectually engaging to diverse learners. Currently, StreetCode Academy is seeking additional  funding to scale this model of radically informal STEM learning. If funded, the organization will be able to provide ISL opportunities in 20 different c ities over 4 different regions. Using their culturally relevant teaching model, StreetCode Academy will apply, refine, and test a scalable approach to ISL that creates pathways in to STEM for those who are historically the most exc luded: underserved students of color and their families. O ur objective is to expose around 3,000 individuals to STEM learning while collecting data on mindset shift, se nse of belonging, and enthusiasm for further learni ng, with the ultimate goal of proving a scalable teaching method ology able to bridge historical gaps in STEM. This familyinclusive, community-centered approach to ISL seeks to increase equity in STEM, contributing to both community well-being and a STEM sector where diverse representation drives innovation growth, to the benefit of society at large.
