# Promoting Cognitive Strategies for Processing 360° Videos in History Learning Contexts

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
360° videos can be characterized as immersive representations that may cause an illusion of reality. This illusion can be overwhelming or lead to a lack of distancing while processing the contents presented, which is exacerbated when learning with history-related 360° videos containing emotionally charged content. One way to counter these dangers could relate to the use of cognitive strategies. Cognitive strategies could help students to use a structured approach for processing the immersive and emotionalising videos and, thereby, to focus on aspects that are fundamental for reflective interaction with the content. Cognitive strategies can be promoted through self-regulated learning (SRL) trainings. Thus, the present study investigates whether a SRL training can promote school students´ acquisition of cognitive strategies for processing history-related 360° videos. The results of our quasi-experimental study (N = 164) suggest positive effects of the training on both students’ use of and knowledge about cognitive strategies.

## Introduction

In recent years, 360° videos have been developed to  intensify learning processes in history learning c ontexts. These videos can be characterized as immersive representations that may cause an illusion of reality (Cummings & Bailenson, 2015). This illusion of reality might be particularly problematic in history learning con texts. Specifically, history-related 360° videos may not only create immersion, but are also often characterized by highly emotional content. This emotionalized and immersive presentation of the content could overwhelm the recipients or lead to a lack of distancing, whereby they may n ot realize that the content shown in the video is o nly a representation of the past (Bunnenberg, 2020; Brauer, 2019). Thus, the impression of actually experiencing history could hinder a cognitive and critical engagement with what is being presented. Consequently, watching immersive videos with emotionally charged content in history learning contexts requires recipients to critically analyse the contents presented (Gautschi, 2015). Therefore, in history classes, students need to be supported in a pplying dedicated methods for analysing the videos, for instance, by using cognitive strategies. Cognitive strategies refer to learning techniques f or taking up, identifying, classifying, memorising, elaborating as well as critically examining and sto ring information (e.g., Schiefele & Wild, 1994). If  students learn to use cognitive learning strategies adequate ly, they can use a structured approach for processi ng the immersive and emotionalising history-related 360° videos and, thereby, focus on aspects that are fundamental for reflective interaction with the video content. In r esearch on history education, it is also assumed th at activities, such as summarizing, elaborating and contextualizin g the learning content, aid students’ reflective and critical processing of history-related content. While these activities are denoted as competences or skills in research on history education (e.g., Gautschi, 2015), they are conceptualized as cognitive strategies in research on selfregulated learning (SRL). SRL research on how to be  acquire such strategies has revealed that students  need to know how and why to use them, and they need to be able to apply them (e.g., Wischgoll, 2016). Moreover, we can learn from SRL research that both cognitive and  metacognitive processes should be trained together  (e.g., Dignath & Büttner, 2008; Stebner et al., 2015).

SRL trainings may be a promising way to promote the  cognitive as well as metacognitive strategies required when learning with 360° videos in history education. However, while ample research has invest igated the effects of SRL trainings in the context of the natural sciences (e.g. Stebner et al., 2015), there are barely any studies on such trainings in history education and, more specifically, for learning with history-related 360° videos. To the best of our knowledge, only one study has in vestigated self-regulated learning in museum sites (Dold, 2020), but this study did not focus on learners’ processing of immersive and emotionalising 360° videos. Against this background, the present study aims to investigate the effect of a SRL training on students’ knowledge about and use of cognitive strategies when examining history-related 360° videos. Building on relevant characteristics of SRL trainings uncovered by previous research (e.g., Stebner et al., 2015), we developed a SRL training that combined cognitive and metacognitive strategies. To examine the effects of our training,  we conducted a quasi-experimental study comparing two conditions: explicit SRL training (experimental condition) ICLS2022 Proceedings 1365 © ISLS with explicit instruction on the functionality of c ognitive and metacognitive strategies and explicit guidance on using these strategies for analysing history-relate d 360° videos, and implicit SRL training  (control condition) without instruction on the names and functionality of cognitive and metacognitive strategies and implicit guidance on the use of cognitive strategies for analysing hi story-related 360° videos. In the analyses presente d here, we focus on examining the effect of our SRL training on students’ use of and knowledge about cognitive strategies. We hypothesize that students who receive an explicit SRL training will – in contrast to students who r eceive an implicit SRL training – improve their use of cognitive strategies (H1a) and their knowledge about these strategies (H2a) when examining a history-related 360° video. Consequently, we further hypothesize that – after the training

– students of the explicit training condition will use (H1b) and mention (H2b) more cognitive strategi es for

analysing a history-related 360° video than students of the implicit training condition.

## Method

Design and Sample 164 9th to 13 th graders (52% female) from 10 classes of 10 German secondary schools participated in our study that took place in the form of an online video conf erence due to the pandemic situation. The 10 classe s were randomly assigned to either the explicit training c ondition (n = 70) or the implicit training condition (n = 94). Figure 1 depicts the procedure of the present study.

## Procedure in both conditions

In the explicit training condition, students first received a brief introduction to historical terms and then participated in a strategy training that is based on a SRL training from previous research (see Stebner et al., 2015) and includes a strategy lecture and an explicit str ategy exercise. During the strategy lecture, the su pervisors discussed the definition and usefulness of the foll owing five strategies in history-learning contexts with the participating students: (1) summarize content, (2) check medium, (3) check perspective, (4) check mood and (5) formulate conclusions. Afterwards, the participating students practised to analyse 360° videos with the help of the supervisors. This exercise explicitly included guidance on the use of cognitive strategies. In the implicit training condition, students received a more detailed introduction to historical terms than students in the explicit training condition. Afterwards, in contrast to the students of the explicit training condition, the students of  the implicit training condition did not receive explicit instruction on cognitive and metacognitive strategies (i.e., no strategy lecture) and only indirectly practised to analyse 3 60° videos with the help of cognitive and metacogni tive strategies. Specifically, the supervisors did neither explicitly name the cognitive and metacognitive strategies nor explain their functionality during the exercise.

In addition to the 360° video that students watched  and analysed during the exercise, all participants watched – as part of the strategy use tests – a 360° video before and after the training session (see Figure 1). We administered a strategy use test as well as a strategy knowledge test before and after the training session in order to assess the number of cognitive strategies used w hen analysing a 360° video (strategy use test) as w ell as the number of cognitive strategies mentioned when expla ining how to analyse 360° videos in general (strate gy knowledge test) as our dependent variables.

## Measures

The strategy test is based on the multi-strategy te st by Stebner et al. (2015) and measures spontaneou s recall as well as the intended use of cognitive and metacognitive strategies. In our study, we adapted the test to the historyrelated context and divided it into two parts: The strategy use test measured the use of cognitive strategies by asking the participants to watch a 360° video and to examine its presentation of the past. The strategy knowledge test measured the knowledge about the cognitive strategies (indicated by stude nts’ mentioning of the cognitive strategies) when examining a 360° video in general.  The instructions in the strategy use test differed  slightly depending on the video content between pre-and posttest, but included the same type of task. In the p retest, we used the video “A VR journey to the Middle Ages”. In the posttest, we used the video “What did they [member of the German State Security Service] want in Berlin?!”. The videos are available on YouTube. We coded the student answers in the strategy tests by using a system of categories that builds on the definition of historical deconstruction competence (Gautschi, 2015), elements of film analytics (Kühberger, 2013) and processes of self-regulated learning (e.g., Schiefele & Wild, 1994). For example, applying the strategy check the medium in the strategy use test was defined as describing the medium and its influence on the representation (example of a respective student answer: “The whole situation is presented as a 360° video, which means that the viewer can look around and has an all-round view during the event.”). In the strategy knowledge test, check the medium was defined as mentioning that the medium and its influence on the representation have to be taken into account when analysing 360° videos in general (example of a respective student answer: “It´s very important to look at the video critically and to recognise inter pretations. With these videos it´s very important t o get an allround view of the situation and also to pay close attention to the sounds.”). We coded the answers in a binary way. Thus, we only coded whether one of the strategies occurred (1) or not (0), and did not analyse the frequencies of the occurrences of the individual strategies within  a student answer. Two independent raters coded 30%  of the pretest and posttest datasets and reached, on average, a satisfying agreement (κ = 99,88). To test our hypotheses, we calculated the sum of the coded strategies, leading to a total number of 0 to 5 strategies that students could use or mention in the strategy tests.

## Results

To test H1a and H2a, we investigate the pre-post di fferences within each of the two conditions by cond ucting Wilcoxon tests. To test H1b and H2b, we compare students’ use of and knowledge about cognitive strategies after the training between the two conditions by conducting Mann-Whitney U tests. Prior to the interventions, students’ performance in the two pretests did not significantly differ between the two conditions, neither with respect to the total number of applied strategies in the strategy use pretest (explicit training condition: M = 0.91, SD =.93; implicit training condition: M = 0.93, SD =.79) nor with regard to the total number of strategies mentioned in the strategy knowledge pretest (explicit training condition: M = 0.36, SD =.61; implicit training condition: M = 0.27, SD =.56).

With respect to our H1a, the results of the Wilcoxo n test show a significant pre-post difference in th e total number of strategies applied by students of the explicit training condition (n = 44, r =.56, p <.001, M(pretest) = 0.89, M(posttest) = 1.73) and, as expected, no significant pre-post difference in the implicit training condition (n = 50, r =.25, p =.07, M(pretest) = 0.98, M(posttest) = 1.28). Thus, in line with our H1a, while students of the explicit training condition improved their use of cognitive strategies for analysing a history-related 360° video by the end of the study, students of the implicit trai ning condition did not improve their use of these s trategies. Consequently, in line with our H1b, the results of the Mann-Whitney U test show a significant differen ce (U = 2092.00, Z = -2.70, r =.22 p =.007) in the number of used cognitive strategies between the students of the explicit training condition (n = 66, M = 1.62, SD = 1.21) and their counterparts of the implicit training condition (n = 83, M = 1.07, SD =.85). Thus, students who received the explicit SRL training used significantly more strategies for analysing 360° videos than students who received the implicit training. With regard to our H2a, the results of a Wilcoxon t est show a significant and large-sized pre-post difference in the number of mentioned cognitive strategies in the explicit training cond ition (n = 26, r =.75, p <.001, M(pretest) = 0.42, M(posttest) = 2.42), but also a significant and medium-sized pre-post difference in the implicit training condition (n = 30, r =.69, p =.000, M(pretest) = 0.37, M(posttest) = 1,63). Thus, against our H2a, students in both conditions increased their knowledge about cognitive strategies by the end of our study. However, looking at the differences in participants´ knowledge about cognitive strategies between the two conditions (H2b), the results of the Mann-Whitney U test show a significant and small effect of condition (U = 543.50, Z = -2.26, r =.25, p =.024) on the total number of mentioned cognitive  strategies after the training. Thus, in line with our H2b, students who received the explicit SRL training (n = 32, M = 2.28, SD = 1.75) mentioned significantly more cognitive strategies than students who received implicit training (n = 48, M = 1.4, SD = 1.44). ## Discussion and conclusion

The present study investigated the effects of an explicit SRL training on the knowledge about and use of cognitive strategies when watching 360° videos with history-related content. We found that students who participated in the explicit SRL training used (H1a) and mentioned (H2a) a higher number of cognitive strategies in the posttest than in the pretest. However, against our hypothesis, students in the implicit training condition also improved their knowledge about (but not their use of) cognitive strategies. This finding may be due to the fact that, at the time of the pretest, students did not exactly know what they were supposed to do in the task. During the intervention, however, students in both conditions got at least an impression that the examination of 360° videos requires some k ind of analysis. Consequently, students with implic it SRL training might have activated relevant prior knowle dge they had previously gained by performing simila r tasks (e.g., film or text analysis) in school. Neverthele ss, students of the implicit training condition wer e seemingly unable to transfer this knowledge to their actual video analysis, as they did not use these strategies for analysing the history-related 360° video. However, although s tudents of both conditions improved their knowledge  about cognitive strategies by the end of the study, our f urther results suggest that students of the explici t training condition acquired more cognitive strategies than s tudents of the implicit training condition. This fi nding is supported by both the total number of strategies that students used in the strategy use posttest (H1b) as well as the total number of strategies that students mentioned in the strategy knowledge posttest (H2b). This find ing is particularly interesting in light of the fact that we designed the training in a greatly reduced form due to the pandemic situation. The duration of an intervention can be crucial for its effect on learning outcomes, or in other words, the effectiveness of an intervention can be affected by the number of training sessions and the number of months one participates in it (Dignath & Büttner, 2 008). But as our study indicates, a 60-minute train ing can already evoke beneficial effects on especially the use of cognitive strategies. Future research on SRL  in history education could build on this finding, for example,  by providing students with multiple lessons in a h istory learning project in which students practice the use of strategies for examining different forms of presentations of the past.

To conclude, our results are in line with previous studies on the effectiveness of SRL trainings (e.g., Stebner et al., 2015, Dignath & Büttner, 2008), and  may provide a new impulse for empirical history ed ucation research on learning with 360° and virtual reality media. Moreover, our study demonstrates the potenti al of interdisciplinary research in this area, as we attempted to build a bridge between history education with its mainly theoretical approaches on how to teach historical c ompetences on the one hand and empirical research i n educational science with well-researched approaches for promoting cognitive strategies on the other hand.
