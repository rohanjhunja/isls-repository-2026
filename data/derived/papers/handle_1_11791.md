# Pluriversal Frameworks for Equitable and Sustainable Learning Designs. Knowledge Co-construction Experiences along Lands, Territories, and AI in Colombia

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores how Arturo Escobar’s decolonial concepts of the pluriverse and autonomous design can contribute to learning sciences research. We draw from Escobar’s designs for the pluriverse framework, focusing on three principal components: the pluriverse, political ontology, and autonomous design, to guide learning scientists toward more sustainable and inclusive designs. Initially, we discuss these concepts and their current applications in the learning sciences. We then present three vignettes that illustrate the application of this framework in community-based research projects and instructional practices across various urban and rural contexts in Colombia. Through these examples, we examine the potential of Escobar’s proposal to facilitate equity-oriented, culturally sustaining learning designs that foster inclusion and resilience in diverse educational settings. By highlighting how local knowledges can challenge dominant Western ontologies and epistemologies, we advocate for a more pluralistic approach in learning sciences research to address the ecological and social crises we currently face.

## Introduction

The learning sciences have advanced our understanding of learning by recognizing the practices and literacies of non-dominant groups (Uttamchandani  et al., 2024). However, despite the progress in int egrating non-Western epistemologies, there is still a need for increased socio-epistemic heterogeneity in the learning sciences. Tsovaltzi and colleagues (2022) stress the importance of stre ngthening conn ections with justice-oriented theories and methodologies from historically non-dominant communities, including minority groups and  Global South perspectives. In response to this call, the Brazili an Affiliate Group of ISLS has committed to featuri ng South American scholars whose unique perspectives can contribute more nuanced understandings of the situated nature of learning in formal and informal educational settings (Tsovaltzi et al., 2022). We join these efforts as Colombian scholars in teac hing, learning, and literacies currently engaged in research between North and South America. We propos e that integrating Latin Americandecolonial theory, particularly Arturo Escobar’s concept of the  pluriverse, can enrich learning sciences research by providing a framework for analyzing and designing research with  non-dominant groups. Furthermore, bringing in Global South epistemologies like the pluriverse into a field historically dominated by Global North  scholars addresses calls for epistemic justice (Stroupe, 2022). It emp hasizes the horizontality between the knowledge sys tems and ways of living in the Global North and the diverse subjectivities and worlds emerging from the Global South. Learning scientists already consider frameworks tha t value learners’ lived experiences rather than imposing external frameworks. For instance, Gutierrez and colleagues (2008, 2017, 2020) have provided nuanced insights into the learning processes of migrant students often labeled as deficient. Similarly, others have integrated Indigenous epistemologies into learning research, h ighlighting the importance of storytelling, observa tion, and community involvement within Indigenous and peasant communities (Bang et al., 2015; Elliott-Groves & Meixi, 2022; Marin, 2020; Meixi et al., 2022). This body of work raises critical questions about the ethical and ontological dimensions of designing for learning (Vossoughi et al., 2020; Zavala, 2016). Recognizing the pressing nature of ecological, political, and social crises, it is urgent to develop learning designs that promote care for both human and more-than-human lives. This is particularly important for communities whose worlds are threatened by systemic violence, inequity, and racism. In this paper, we draw from Escobar’s (2017, 2018) designs for the pluriverse framework to explore how it can guide learning scientists toward more sustainable and inclusive research and learnin g designs. We begin by discussing Escobar’s concept  of the pluriverse and its current applications in the learning scienc es. Following this, we present three individual vignettes that illustrate how this concept is appli ed in community-based research projects and instructional practices across various urban and rural contex ts in Colombia. Then, we discuss the potential of t his framework to facilitate equity-oriented, culturally sustaining learning designs that challenge dominant Eurocentric ontologies and epistemologies.

The designs for the pluriverse framework We approach Escobar’s (2017, 2018) designs for the pluriverse as a relevant framework for navigating the transitions caused by the crisis of the capitalist model. Based on Zapatistas’ definition, Escobar des cribes the pluriverse as “a world where many worlds can fit” (Escobar, 20 18, p. xvi). Understanding this concept requires unpacking the intricate relationship between practi ces, cultures, and territories, as Latin American s cholars like Fals-Borda (1979) and Mignolo (2012, 2002) elucidated.

Escobar's work on the pluriverse and post-development practices has significantly influenced fields such as decolonization, design, and the recognition of d iverse ways of knowing (Chaves 2017, 2018). His sch olarship critiques dominant Western modes of knowledge production and development, advocating for an ontological shift toward recognizing diverse worlds and ways of livin g. Traditional livelihoods, such as fishing in mang roves and ancestral mining, have sustained human and more-than-human lives in the territories of Black and Indigen ous Colombian communities for centuries (Escobar, 2014). However, as with many other communities, these locally enacted practices are often overlooked and endangered by modernity. Drawing on activist research with community grassro ots in the Colombian South and Pacific regions, Escobar theorizes the pluriverse to disrupt the universal models that pervade Wester n modernity, what he refers to as the “One-World World” (OWW) ontology (Escobar, 2014, 2018). The pluriverse rejects the OWW’s dualist ontology that otherizes differences and instead see ks to acknowledge the non-dualist ontologies present in our planet's various worlds. This shift “implies a transition from concepts like ‘globalization’ and ‘global studies,’ to concepts centered in the pluriverse, understanding it as a multiplicity of worlds mutu ally entangled and co-constituted but different” (Escobar, 2014, p.146). Furthermore, the challenge lies in envisioning “oth er forms of knowing~being~doing” (Escobar, 2018, p. 146) while questioning the profound beliefs about the individu al, the real, the science, and the economy that underpin modernity. Escobar proposes a three-level framework for analyzing ontologies to elucida te further this ontological shift and its implications for understanding divers e worldviews (Escobar, 2014). The first level addre sses the premises different social groups hold abo ut the entities existing in the world. The second l evel examines the concrete practices through which these premises are  enacted. The third level focuses on the stories or  narratives used to describe entities and relations in the world. Our vignettes below draw on these three levels to illustrate the pluriversal perspective in practice.

Buen vivir: a social philosophy A central reference in Escobar’s work is the concep t of buen vivir (from Quechua’s expression sumac kawsay). While acknowledging the epistemological challenges of translating such concepts (Rojas-Lizana, 2024), Englishbased scholarship uses the terms living well, or good life to refer to it  (Chavez et al., 2018; Ribadeneira, 2020). This social philosophy views humans as stewards of earthly resources within a communal project, departing from modern development narratives and subordinating economic objectives to “humandignity, social justi ce, and ecology” (Escobar, 2018, p. 226). It rejects linear progress approaches, challenges Western knowledge centrality, recognizes more-than-humans’ intrinsic value, and adopts a relational conception of all life (Escobar, 2018). Designing for the pluriverse entails a radical practice-considering design in its broadest sense-of reimagining ways of living that allow all beings, h umans and more-than-humans, to inhabit the world in dignity. This approach embraces cultural or ontological diff erences, nurturing alternative forms of knowledge i n nondominant communities that transcend modern Cartesian logic. Escobar characterizes design ontology as a primary function of human-world relations, introducing autonomous design as “a design praxis with communit ies that aims to contribute to their realization as  the kinds of entities they are” (Escobar, 2018, p. 279). This  perspective understands that communities design th emselves, recognizes people as practitioners of their own knowledge, considers community designs as inquiries or learning about the system, and acknowledges that every design process is a statement of problems and possibilities. These principles align with Freire’s (2018) critical peda gogy approach, emphasizing the interaction between humans and their world as a requisite for learning and shaping their reality.

## Expanding the pluriversal perspectives

The pluriverse has gained relevance in studies of learning and des ign that challenge hegemonic Western ontologies and epistemologies. As an ontological co ncept, it has gained momentum in research aimed at making science, language, and education more equitable for individuals from non-dominant communities. The pluriverse has inspired theorizations of pluriversal education  (Williams & Bermeo, 2020), including pluriversal l iteracies (Perry, 2023) and applied linguistics (Becerra Posada, 2024; Ortega, forthcoming). Empirical research has also been informed by the pluriverse concept, such as the design of STEM education programs that challenge Western ideals of  science and instead affirm the identities of margi nalized youth (Kayumova & Dou, 2022). Researchers are incre asingly questioning what it means to design for the pluriverse. For instance, St John (2023) explores this wi thin Africandesign education programs, examining h ow local design practices in Ntaria can reconfigure cu rricula and educational approaches. St John argues that while local design practices can lead to mor e sustainable outcomes, such a shift requires “adju stment or a full abandonment of habitual expectations, timelines, or understandings” (p.23). Moving away from dominant educational and design pr actices is a concern for studies informed by the pluriverse. These studies aim to recognize and inco rporate the knowledge of non-dominant groups in areas such as data activism (Rodima-Taylor, 2024), agroecology (Chavez et al., 2017), a nd sustainable development education (Macintyre et al., 2024). Drawing from ca se studies in Latin America, they advocate for radi cal alternatives to extractivist practices.

The pluriverse in the learning sciences Learning scientists have emphasized the need for alternatives to Western extractive models that dominate science, knowledge construction, and learning, which limit our acknowledgment of the pluriverse (Takeuchi et al., 2020). This section will examine how the pluriverse has gained traction in the learning sciences, resha ping educational research and practice. For example, Mirra and García (2023) draw upon Escobar’s ontologically oriented design, suggesting we exist in a world-within-the-world, both influencing and being influenced by our  designs. Their pluriversal approach recognizes that designing spec ulative futures helps us understand how we are shap ed by present possibilities, encouraging learning sciences to embrace being both designers and subjects of our co-created world. Also, Correa et al. (2023) introduce the creative pluriverse  framework, reimagining maker education through multispecies and multimattered creativity. They emphasize the interconnectedness of human and morethan-human entities in creative production, demonstratin g how learners engage in embodied dialogues with materials and organisms in biomaking with fungi.

Moreover, Escobar's notion of the pluriverse has be en used to inform calls to advocate for the learnin g sciences community to promote an approach in which many worlds can coexist. Takeuchi et al. (2022) urg e learning sciences scholars to build communities tha t resist homogeneity and assimilation, proposing an epistemological, ontological, and axiological expan sion in learning sciences inspired by laborers’ exp eriences. They argue for an inclusive approach committed to c reating an inclusive field that embrac es the coexistence of diverse ways of being and knowing. Sengupta et al. (2022) envision pluralistic frameworks countering s ettlercolonial legacies, respecting and fostering knowledge rooted in lands and communities. They call for recognizing epistemic and ontological contributions from historically non-dominant perspectives, particularly from the Global South.

Existing frameworks like social design experiments (SDEs) (Gutierrez & Jurrow, 2018) align with pluriversal approaches, challenging assimilation and bridging community knowledge with educational des ign. SDEs connect youth cultures, community knowledge, a nd institutional practices to co-create futures, integrating every day and scientific knowledge to promote expansive, consequential learning that deepens understanding and addresses social inequities. Syncretic learning (Gutiérrez, 2014) values horizontal learning, integrating knowledge across contexts, fostering new ways of knowing and being that prioritize justice and equity. Escobar’s perspective emphasizes varied conceptuali zations of success beyond singular educational models, focusing on students’ literacies, knowledge, and cultural practices. For him, learning is a way to live with dignity and relate meaningfully to one’s local cont ext. Building on these foundations, we propose a pl uriversal perspective informed by Escobar’s work as an ontolo gy that enriches the plurality of epistemologies in  the learning sciences. This approach contests uniformity and assimilation, encourages diverse ways of knowing, and prioritizes learning designs serving diverse commun ity lifeworlds. It pushes learning sciences toward research designs that recognize and respect the diversity of  learners’ worlds, fostering a more equitable, incl usive epistemological landscape.

Three vignettes on pluriversal learning designs and research in Colombia We draw on three vignettes to conceptualize the pot ential of the pluriverse in studies of learning acr oss three different contexts and disciplines. Co-author 1 draws on his work exploring alternatives t o financial literacies among Colombian farmers. Decolonizing financial education through a pluriversal political ontology Financial literacy is increasingly recognized as es sential in a world where "money is the measure of a ll things" (Freire, 2018, p. 58). However, the conventional approach to financial education has been criticized from different perspectives (Björklund & Sandahl, 2023). For examp le, some scholars argue that it perpetuates colonia l and capitalist worldviews that prioritize individualism, competition, and extractive practices (Blue, 2019). In this vignette, Jose (co-author 1) aims to contribute to the discussion proposed by th ose advocating for an alternative critical approach to financial literacy. Drawing on  Arturo Escobar’s and Paulo Freire’s theories, he e xamines diverse financial worldviews and practices emerging  from non-dominant communities and outlines a liberatory approach to financial education.

According to Colombia’s National Policy for Financi al and Economic Inclusion and Education (CONPES, 2020), integrating financial services into citizens' daily lives  is an effective pathway  to generate economic opportunities and address the needs of tho se in poverty conditions. However, this approach may be perceived as an attempt to impose a singular worldview (Escobar, 2018) driven by the extractivist interests of the global financial system (Lazarus, 2020), potentially worsening inequality. The shortcomings of this strategy were evident in the tensions between governmental Discourses and the discourses of Colombian rural communities shared during workshops and interviews between the Summer of 2022 and 2023. In these activities, we met with members from four different Colombian rural communi ties to examine the impact of finance in their everyday activities. It became clear that they don’t trust traditional financial institutions because these institutions prioritize their corporate interests over the community's well-being. During one of the workshops, Juan, a member of a farmer’s association, pointed out, "With all these fees and other charges, you end up losing money when you put your money in the banks." In another session, Luisa, a community leader, declared, "I do not trust banks. I prefer to keep my money under the mattress." Despite the power imbalances, individuals can resis t oppressive dynamics by adopting everyday practices that help them operate outside the formal  system. For example, Jose observed how Andrés, who grows crops on a rented farm in the Valle del Cauca regio n, applies financial concepts such as risk diversif ication and cash flow planning. Although he doesn’t consider hi mself financially savvy, he makes sound decisions t hat are appropriate for his context. He cultivates various crops, including corn, spinach, sweet peppers, gree n beans, turmeric, and tomatoes. Andrés strategically choose s these crops to minimize risks associated with agr icultural practices like pest and disease damage. Additionally, this diversity allows him to manage his cash flow effectively. While sweet peppers have a short growing cycle and provide quick returns, corn takes longer to mature and demands higher resource investments over time. By b alancing short-term and long-term crops, Andrés can manage the funds needed to maintain his operations while generating sufficient income to meet his needs without relying on credit from financial institutions. Understanding and valuing the lived experiences of these communities is necessary. However, modern Western epistemologies continue to assert that participation in the financial system is the only way to thrive, implying that we must all learn to engage and use it. Imposing financial literacy programs on individuals  within an exploitative system that has eroded thei r trust serves only to reinforce oppression. Rather t han socializing students to accept the current fina ncial and political systems or presenting neoliberal ideologies as objective truths (Björklund & Sandahl, 2023), a liberatory approach to financial literacy should empower non-dominant communities to challenge and transform the systems that perpetuate financial injustice and economic in equality (Freire, 2 021). In this context, Escobar’s (2018) concepts of autonomous design and political ontology become crucial for shifting the dominant financial literacy paradigm. Simultaneously, Freire’s (2018) critical consciousness offers a valuable framework to guide community engagement, fostering social transformation. By col laborating with communities to d evelop strategies for integrating into the economic system on their terms instead of merely adapting to it, we can more effectively challenge neoliberal notions of individualism and c ompetency while promoting empathy, solidarity, and collectivism.

Ontological design: valuing communities' knowledge and literacies Through this vignette, Tatiana (co-author 2) describes dialogue excerpts from a partic ipatory, community-based project that intends to decolonize languages and technology curricula in a rural community in the Colo mbian Caribbean region. Three secondary school teachers and she, as an allied researcher, listened to these stories while walking with community members and then shared them with students and other community neighbors. Centering local stories in our dialogue enabled the recognition of local knowledge and literacies, which then led the team to co-design curricula around the need to perpetuate the community’s traditional storytelling, plant weaving, and crafting practices.

During one of our dialogue sessions, Macarena, a ni nth-grade student, read aloud this story we had collected during previous walks around the community: Up there, where you can see the trees, there was a pond. It was the only pond around here. That was years ago, like in the 40s, and the ladies would come to wash clothes there, and they would invite each other, and they would bring the wooden tubs on their heads, wooden tubs, and they would make a little donkey with three sticks and wash, the ladies would invite each other, there was a day when it was time to wash, and they would meet there (story told during community dialogue session in July 2022).

After listening to the story, community members started responding to it. Ruben, one of the adult neighbors, said, “These traditions and customs have been eroded by technology, and this is the knowledge that we used to have.” Ruben’s comment prompted Jairo, the school’s techno logy teacher, to defend technology: “Today, we have  a misconception of what technology is. We are always relating technology only to computers, but technolo gy has always existed. For example, the mere fact of makin g a pylon is already applying technology.” Jairo pointed out the misconception of technology that has become prominent, and when referring to the pylon, he reminded us that the village's traditional crafting practices are ex amples of technology. Such a move ignited several c ommunity members to add examples of local technologies like balays, a craft made out of Bejuco used to vent rice after it is husked, and Juncus mats to put children to sleep.  Naming these crafts as examples of traditional technologies and their intricate relation with native plants like Bejuco catabre, which is now more challenging to find in the village proximities, also led to a recognition of knowledge  holders. Yeinis, another community member, referre d to our previous visit to Juana and the pond where sh e collects juncus for crafting mats. Drawing on thi s visit, Yeinis commented on the value of plant-weaving knowledge that elders like Juana still conserve and which has become less prominent in the village, thus changing their ways of living into stories of the past like the story shared in this vignette.

We continued telling the stories collected during walks in the community and its surroundings. Our storysharing and story-listening led to recognizing traditional artifacts and crafts –balays and esterillas –as valuable technologies intricately related to knowledge of the land. Similarly, community elders were recognized as keepers of indigenous knowledge and practices that should b e passed on to the younger generations. The move fr om acknowledging traditional crafts as valuable techno logies also led to the recognition of Indigenous kn owledge holders and their relationship with plants and the natural world, thus shedding light on the intricate  socionatural relations that configure worlds beyond or outside t he idealized Western, modern world (Escobar, 2018).  This sustained community dialogue became essential to ou r participatory community-based project, thus enabling us to acknowledge these stories and the worlds they em bed as valuable assets for contextualizing educatio nal curricula and as essential elements f or preserving the community’s historical memory, lo cal knowledge, and literacies.

Our curricula co-designs centering local, land-based literacies like land storytelling and plant p icking and crafting are instances of autonomous design that “recraft[s] communal forms of being” (Escobar, 2018, p.166) to disrupt the imposition of a colonial national cu rriculum that ultimately aims to homogenize student s and assimilate them into a human capital ontology. Our educational co-designs ultimately aim to sustain worlds and ontologies like the ones in the territory of the Tropical dry forest inhabited by this community. Generative AI and biodesign in the pluriverse: a student’s vision for Colombia in 2050 Informed by Escobar’s notion of the pluriverse, San tiago, Nicole, and Giovanna (co-authors 3, 4, and 5) share their instructional design insights from a biodesig n course at a private university in Colombia. Biode sign undergraduate students were tasked with using generative AI to envision sustainable futures. One student focused on addressing the environmental and health hazards posed by chromium tanning in leather production, a longstanding practice in Colombia. Her project became a  powerful example of pluriversal thinking, emphasizing the coexistence of multiple worldviews, challenging the  imposition of a singular path to development or modernization. The student’s work, framed by specul ative design (Garcia & Mirra, 20 23), invited her to use AI to envision a future for the leather tanning indust ry in 2050, not by eliminating the craft but by tra nsforming it through sustainable practices. Through AI-generated imagery, she illustrated how biosustainab le methods could preserve artisanal tanning without the toxic impact of chr omium. Reflecting on her project, she noted, "Our solution would achieve better health quality for the workers who are directly exposed to chromium," emphasizing that her vision retained traditional craftsmanship while introducing safer, more environmentally frien dly processes.

This approach aligns with Escobar’s pluriversal fra mework, which critiques the dominant One-World World ontology—a mindset that imposes a singular, universal pathway to progress on all communities (Escobar, 2014). Instead of viewing the tanning industry as s omething to be replaced by modern technology, the s tudent resisted this binary logic. "In 2050, Colombia may not be able to become very technological," she explained, “but our solution would still improve the quality of lif e for workers handling chromium directly.” Her desi gn exemplifies Escobar’s idea of autonomous design, where communities are empowered to shape their fu tures according to their values and needs, maintaining local identity while addressing new challenges (Escobar, 2018). The AI-generated images visualized a future where leather tanning had become safer for  workers and the environment. One image depicted a sustainable facto ry by a clean lake, surrounded by green landscapes symbolizing ecological recovery. Another showed workers in healthier conditions, free from exposure to harmful chemicals. Her final image  depicted a biosustainable tanning process that reta ined artisanal techniques while replacing toxic chromium. "I believe that our solution would still allow for a functional small town for the families of tannery workers," she remarked, recognizing the importance of preserving these communities' social and cultural fabric.

The student’s project embodies Escobar’s notion of the pluriverse (Escobar, 2018). Her design did not impose a universal solution but rather built bridge s between tradition and innovation, ensuring that a rtisanal craftsmanship and sustainability could thrive toget her. Escobar’s vision of a pluriversal future rejec ts the one-world model of progress, advocating for autonomous, commu nity-driven solutions instead. However, the use of generative AI in this context raises important ques tions about sustainability and autonomy. While AI w as a tool to imagine a sustainable future and to engage in cr iticality about the values of youth communities, it s environmental costs and socio-political implications cannot be ignored. Costanza-Chock (2018) argues  that we must move beyond inclusion and fairness to center a utonomy and sovereignty.  The author ask,  “How do AI systems reproduce colonial ontology and epistemolog y?” and “ What would AI look like if it were designed to support, extend, and amplify indigenous knowledge and practices?” (p. 10). This critique is highly relevant to the student’s project, as it invites a deeper inquiry into how AI may inadvertently reinforce colonial structures. In line with Escobar’s critique of the One-World World, Costanza-Chock calls for a decolonized approach to design, emphasizing place-based, collaborative practices that recognize the i nterdependence of all living beings. Escobar’s idea of a world where many  worlds fit resists the homogenizing forces of neol iberal globalization, advocating for diverse lifeworlds to coexist. As we reflect on the next steps for this study, students must critically engage with the limitations of AI itself. Future research should ask: how can AI be decolonized to support plural worlds, amplifying indigenous knowle dge rather than imposing a singular worldview? We c an center autonomy and sovereignty in creating sustainable futures by doing so.

## Discussion

Enacting sustainable solutions to global crises requires adopting ontologies that challenge the dominant forms of modernity. This conceptual paper argues for the pluriverse as a decolonial concept that counters Western thinking in learning studies and advances equitable design-based research by re-centering non-dominant community epistemologies and ontologies (Bang & Medin, 2010).  Our research methodologies reposition participants  as knowledge holders and critical actors in knowledge co-construction and analysis. Escobar’s (2018) pluriverse framework has enabled us to recognize rural community members ' and biodesign students' sense-making practices as resistance against Western defi nitions of literacy, education, and economic success. Concepts such as political ontology, autonomous design and the definition of the pluriverse as "a world where many worlds can fit" (Escobar, 2018,  p. xvi), acknowledg e the intricacy between practices, cultures, and territories. Our vignettes engage wit h Escobar's philosophy of transformational learning  design, which privileges "practices of engaged, experimental, and open-ended design research, including prototyping and scenario building" (Escobar, 2018, 177). Each vignette illustrates key aspects of Escobar's philosophy in practice: ● Vignette 1 showed researchers immersing themselves in farmers' daily routines, gaining valuable insights into their challenges and practices. This approach acknowledges collective autonomy and resists the influence of Western experts, particularly in tradi tional financial literacy approaches that perpetuate global inequalities. It illustrates political ontology’s pertinence in addressing oppressive practices. ● Vignette 2 demonstrated how teachers engaged with the community by listening while walking through their territories. This led to understanding the on tological designs underlying traditional practices and technologies, informing the redesign of school curr icula to sustain the pluriverse within the educatio nal community. It acknowledges autonomous design's relevance in incorporating traditional knowledge into current educational initiatives.

● Vignette 3 report ed on collaborative learning practices between teacher s and students, revisiting and expanding students' designs. This process allowed s tudents to reimagine traditional leather tanning practices alongside AI, recognizing the value and limitations of local knowledge while resisting pressures to erase them and the livelihoods they support. It highlights the importance of the pluriverse as a critical element for speculative design.

These vignettes attempt to disrupt power hierarchies that are often central to research designs, an ongoing concern in our field (Adams et al., 2022; Rahm, 201 9). Our pluriversal framework has enabled us to cha llenge these hierarchies by developing mutual,  trusting relationships with community members and i nvolving them as central knowledge-holders in our projects.

We acknowledge the power dynamics inherent in our positions as researchers affiliated with institutions in the geopolitical Global Norths – which can be located in both the geographical North  and South (Andreotti, 2021). Our power and privilege, impossible to erase, are noted by the individuals we serve. This ongoing tension underscores the importance of reflexivity in our re search practices. We constantly strive to balance our roles as researchers with our commitment to empowering and a mplifying community voices, recognizing this challe nge as integral to our pursuit of more equitable and in clusive research practices. This approach aligns wi th Escobar's concept of autonomous design, which emphasizes comm unities as practitioners of their own knowledge and  the importance of collaborative inquiry.

Our pluriversal framing has allowed us to bridge disciplinary limits, crossing borders between languages, financial literacy, and biodesign, overcoming the t raditionally compartmentalized perspective of knowl edge construction. We hope that this pluriversal  framework sparks more transdisciplinary research in  the learning sciences, addressing the need for “the integration and transcendence of disciplinary fields leading to  the emergence of new worldviews” (Markauskaite et al., 2024, p. 216) beyond K-12 classroom settings. Through community partnerships, we have engaged in collective learning that enables new ontological and epistemological understandings critical to sust aining non-Western ontologies. Escobar’s design for the pluriverse approach informs intentional research de sign, valuing communities’ knowledge, literacies, a nd everyday experiences. It also serves as an analytic al tool for examining artifacts from learning-through-design experiences, encouraging designs that respect commu nity ontologies and epistemologies rather than subs cribing to universalized notions of development and modernity.

Future steps Building upon the pluriversal perspective presented  in this paper, future research in learning science s could explore several promising directions. Longitudinal studies could examine the long-term impacts of pluriversal approaches on community empowerment,  sustainable development, and educational outcomes, providing valuable insights into their transformative potenti al. Developing assessment methods aligned with plur iversal perspectives could move beyond traditional metrics to capture the richness and compl exity of learning within diverse cultural contexts. Additionally, exploring ways to integrate pluriversal thinking into formal educational curricula and teacher training programs could foste r learning environments that reflect the multiplici ty of worlds learners inhabit.

By pursuing these avenues, the learning sciences ca n continue to evolve towards a more inclusive, equitable, and globally relevant field that honors and sustains the pluriverse of human knowledge and experience. This approach aligns with Escobar's vision of design for the pluriverse and has the potential to reshape educational research and practice in profound ways. As emergent  scholars in the learning sciences, we are inspired by the influence of Escobar's ideas on decolonial and post-developmental scholarship. We look forward to expanding on these ideas to help  build equitable and culturally responsive learning experiences that challenge dominant Western paradigms and embrace the richness of diverse epistemologies and ontologies.
