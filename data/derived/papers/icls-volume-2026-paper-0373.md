# Beyond One-Sided Arguments: Evaluating Perspective-Taking in Middle School Essays with GPT-4o and Human Scoring

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this exploratory study, we compared GPT-4o zero-shot scoring with human ratings of middle school students’ essays collected across multiple studies. Results showed that GPT-4o achieved acceptable reliability with human raters, highlighting the potential of GenAI for assessing perspective-taking. Further, we compared linguistic features of essays with a single perspective versus those with multiple perspectives. Future research should examine the quality of perspective-taking, moving beyond recognition of alternative perspectives to evaluating attempts at rebutting counterarguments.

Perspective-taking, particularly the ability to anticipate alternative perspectives and address counterarguments, is widely recognized as a cornerstone of argumentation skills. Across educational standards  and argumentation theories, this skill is essential for preparing students to engage in critical thinking, evidence-based reasoning, and constructive civic discourse (Litman & Greenleaf, 2018; Song et al., 2024). Although students learn to generate claims and reasons relatively early, many  struggle to move beyond one-sided arguments toward advanced skills such as anticipating counterarguments and formulating rebuttals (Ferretti & Fan, 2 016). Students’ essays focus on defending their own position without considering opposing views (Ferretti & Graham, 2019), limiting the sophistication and quality of their arguments  (Taylor et al., 2019).  Developing perspective-taking places high cognitive demands on students, requiring them to integrate multiple views and evaluate counterarguments (Leitão, 2003). These challenges are pronounced in wri ting, where students often find it difficult to construct rebuttals or synthesize conflicting ideas (Nussbaum & Kardash, 2005). Thus, the development of perspective-taking tends to lag behind other aspects of argumentative writing and requires targeted support and sustained practice. Reliable assessment of perspective-taking is crucial for advancing both research and instruction in argumentative writing. However, scoring this skill is challenging because it involves identifying nuanced features in students’ texts, such as subtle acknowledgments of opposing views. Human scoring provides valuable insight but is often resource-intensive and difficult to scale. Automatic essay scoring (AES) can provide timely feedback that helps teachers make informed instructional decisions (Gerard & Linn,  2016). Recent advances in generative AI (GenAI) have expanded the potential  for automated scoring of complex writing skill s through a more comprehensive analysis (Latif & Zhai, 2024; Mizumoto & Eguchi, 2023). Scholars have examined GenAI-human scoring relationships using correlation, regression, and agreement statistics (Awidi, 2024; Bui & Barrot, 2025; Mizumoto & Eguchi, 2023; Suhan & Wolf, 2025; Tate et al., 2024).  Prior studies have focused on overall writing quality or essays written by non-native English speakers. In contrast, our research examines essays produced by U.S. middle school students and focuses on perspective-taking. Our research questions are: 1) How well does the GPT-4o model, using zero-shot prompting, perform at scoring perspective-taking skills in middle school students’ essays, compared to human scoring?  2) How do the linguistic features between essays that present a single perspective and those that incorporate multiple perspectives?

## Method

The dataset included 410 essays collected from three studies  with middle school students. Studies 1 and 2 implemented a dialogic argumentation approach to promote awareness of alternative perspectives, while  study 3 provided a brief scaffolding on incorporating alternative perspectives into essay plan ning. Study 1 included 42 essays on the topic of Junk Food, and study 2 included 151 essays on Cash for Grades. In study 3, students wrote two essays: one on C ash for Grades (N = 109), and one  on Lengthening School Year (N = 108). Students were required to write an essay to communicate their opinions and arguments  on the given controversial issue. For example, should middle school students be rewarded with money for getting good grades  (Cash for Grades)? The perspective-taking scoring rubric is as follows:  If an essay anticipates a reason for the alternative perspective or a potential counterargument, it receives a 2 for including two perspectives. If an essay just discusses reasons that support one’s own position, it receives a 1 for having a single perspective. If an essay does not include any position, it receives a 0. Two raters independently scored all the essays. Using the same scoring rule, the GPT4o model with a zero-shot prompt and a temperature setting of 0.0 was deployed. 2545

## Preliminary findings

Among the 410 essays, GPT-4o classified 267 (65.1%) as single-perspective, 137 (33.4%) as presenting multiple perspectives, and 6 (1.5%) as no opinion. Table 1 presents agreement levels between human raters and GPT-4o, measured by percent exact agreement  and quadratic weighted kappa (QWK). Agreement between two human raters and between rater 1 and GPT-4o reached a satisfactory level. Agreement between rater 2 and GPT-4o was somewhat lower but still within an acceptable range. For Junk Food, neither human-human nor human-AI agreement was strong, maybe due in part to the small sample size. Across the two Cash for Grades sets, we observed variability in consistency within the same topic, with human-human agreement fluctuat ing more than human-AI agreement. These findings indicate that GPT-4o using a zero-shot prompt  can approximate human scoring performance on perspective-taking in students’ essays. Across topics, the GPT-4o zeroshot prompting method yielded an acceptable range of reliability in terms of QWK  and percent exact agreement. This result  shows the potential of ChatGPT, notably GPT-4o, for automatic scoring the perspective-taking in argumentative essays. Our analysis of linguistic features revealed interesting findings. Longer essays were more likely to include multiple perspectives, suggesting a link between writing fluency and perspective-taking, as fluent writers face less cognitive load and can allocate resources to planning and revision (McCutchen, 1996). Essays with multiple perspectives also showed higher interactivity  (i.e., a conversational style), particularly in the Junk Food topic, likely reflecting the dialogical treatment in Study 1.  The absence of clear patterns in academic vocabulary, sentence complexity, and text cohesion suggests that perspective-taking may involve argumentative strategies more than linguistic sophistication, highlighting the importance of explicitly teaching strategies focused on addressing counterarguments.
