# AI-Scaffolded Summarization in STEM: How do Students Differ in Writing and Revision?

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Summarization is a key learning strategy in undergraduate STEM, yet students often struggle without timely, effective feedback. This study investigates how a n AI-scaffolded feedback system (SMART) relates to students’ revision behaviors and how these patterns vary across diverse student backgrounds. We analyzed 1,081 summary revisions from 49 undergraduates in an introductory physics course using a suite of mixed-effects models. Results show that adding concepts and words while maintaining semantic coherence predicted stronger concept learning, whereas excessive deletion corresponded to weaker outcomes. Revision patterns differed meaningfully by gender, race/ethnicity, and academic level, suggesting that AI-scaffolded feedback needs to be tailored to support diverse learners toward productive revision strategies.

A core challenge in undergraduate STEM education is helping students move beyond surface-level reading to build deep, integrated knowledge. The writing-to-learn paradigm offers a powerful framework for this: when students summarize a text, they must select, organize, and connect key ideas, effectively externalizing their current mental model (Bernacki et al., 2021; Dunlosky et al., 2013; Kintsch, 1988; Hayes, 2012). A summary thus reflects where a learner’s conceptual understanding currently stands, and when students revise it in response to feedback, they are actively restructuring that understanding (Kim & McCarthy, 2021). Providing such timely, specific feedback, however, is difficult in large STEM courses, particularly because students of different genders, racial/ethnic backgrounds, and academic levels may engage with feedback in systematically different ways (Cataldi et al., 2018; Yeager et al., 2014). Automated Summary Evaluation (ASE) tools such as SMART (Student Mental Model Analyzer for Research and Teaching) offer a promising solution. SMART analyzes students’ summaries against an expert model and delivers multimodal feedback  such as visual concept maps, targeted feedback identifying missing concepts and propositions, and GenAI-generated definition examples to scaffold the revision process. A p rior study confirms that this AI-scaffolded approach improves concept learning overall (Kim et al., 2020); however, it remains unclear which specific revision behaviors drive these gains, and whether different learner groups engage in systematically different revision strategies. Addressing these questions is essential for designing feedback that is both effective and equitable. This study focuses on two core questions: RQ1. Within the AI-scaffolded environment, how are students’ revision behaviors associated with  their changes in concept learning?

RQ2. Within the AI-scaffolded environment, how do students’ revision behaviors differ across learner characteristics?

This study was conducted in a 16-week introductory physics course (Spring 2025) at a large, diverse public university in the southeastern U.S. Across 7 pre-class modules, 49 undergraduates composed and iteratively revised 250– 300-word summaries using SMART, yielding 1,081 revision pairs after data screening. Demographics (n = 45  survey respondents): 59.2% female; Asian (28.6%), non-Hispanic Black (28.6%), non-Hispanic White (14.3%), Hispanic (14.3%); 69.4% upper-level; 30.6% first-generation. Concept learning was assessed via four SMART semantic indices: Recall-C and Recall-P (proportion of key concepts/propositions recalled), and Concept Matching and Relational Matching (semantic alignment with an expert model; all scaled 0–1). Revision behaviors included the number of concepts, added words, removed words, changed sentences, proportion of sentences changed, and cosine similarity between consecutive drafts (computed using OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small). To model this longitudinal nested data, we used Linear Mixed-Effects Models (LME) for concept learning outcomes, Negative Binomial GLMMs (NB-GLMM) for count-based revision behaviors, Binomial GLMMs (B-GLMM) for proportional outcomes, and Beta Regression for cosine 2851 similarity. Within-and between-student effects were statistically decomposed to distinguish individual-level changes from group-level differences.

Revision behaviors and concept learning At the within-learner level, additive behaviors such as adding words (β =.0004–.001, all p <.05) and concepts (β =.003–.005, p ≤.002) consistently predicted higher scores across all four concept learning indices, while changed sentences were associated with weaker propositional recall (β = −.009, p =.027). Notably, greater cosine similarity between drafts, indicating smaller semantic changes, predicted better Recall-C, Concept Matching, and Relational Matching scores (β =.122–.335, all p <.05), suggesting that coherent revision is more effective than large-scale rewriting (Cong, 2024). At the between-learner level, students who, on average, removed more words gained less from additional revision attempts on Recall-C, Recall-P, and Relational Matching  (all β < -.001, p <.05). Conversely, higher average number of concept s amplified the benefit of revision for Relational Matching  (β <.001, p =.044), and higher average cosine similarity (β =.415, p =.047) was associated with stronger Relational Matching.

Revision behaviors across learner background Revision behaviors varied by learner background. All coefficients represent Incidence Rate Ratios (IRR) for count outcomes or Odds Ratios (OR) for proportional and similarity outcomes, calculated as exp(β). Upper-level students showed the greatest gains in writing volume across revision attempts  (IRR = 1.043–1.119, all p ≤.002) alongside progressively increasing draft-to-draft similarity (OR = 1.3 95, p <.001), suggesting they expanded content while preserving semantic coherence. Male students added about 22% more words (IRR = 1.217, p =.007). They made 12% more sentence changes (IRR = 1.123, p =.007) per attempt but showed slightly weaker gains in number of concepts  (IRR = 0.981, p  =.04 5), suggesting surface-level expansion over conceptual elaboration. White students expanded content richly, producing 8% more concepts per attempt (IRR = 1.082, p <.001), but made 26% fewer word additions (IRR = 0.744, p =.040) and 39% fewer proportional sentence changes (OR = 0.6 06, p <.001), suggesting a more selective, efficient revision  process. Black and Hispanic students showed reductions of 14–31% in sentence-level edits (all p <.01). This study examined how revision behaviors in an AI-scaffolded summarization environment related to concept learning and how these behaviors varied across learner backgrounds. Adding concepts and words while maintaining semantic coherence was a consistent predictor of stronger concept learning, suggesting that careful, selective addition of relevant ideas appears to be the most beneficial revision approach. Conversely, students who tended to remove more words than their peers may have been editing at a surface level, removing content indiscriminately rather than selectively refining it, which limited the benefit of further revision attempts. Similarly, frequent sentence-level changes corresponded to weaker propositional recall. These patterns suggest that surface editing may disrupt key concept relations rather than strengthen them (Yang et al., 2024). Revision patterns also differed by learner background. Upper-level students revised more extensively and refined conceptual relations more rapidly. Male students added more words but showed weaker gains in concept use, suggesting surface-level expansion over conceptual elaboration. White students achieved content enrichment through fewer, more selective edits. Black and Hispanic students showed reductions in sentence-level editing, possibly reflecting selective feedback engagement (Cohen et al., 1999; Yeager et al., 2014), though other explanations remain possible. These differences indicate that AI feedback may be most effective when guiding different learners toward productive revision strategies. Several limitations should be noted, including the correlational design, the single-course context, and underpowered subgroup analyses given small racial/ethnic group sizes. Future research should replicate with larger samples and incorporate richer text analytics to capture the nature of students’ edits alongside non-cognitive factors (Greene, 2015). Despite these limitations, the findings highlight that realizing the equitable potential of AI-scaffolded summarization requires feedback that responds not only to what students write but to how they revise.
