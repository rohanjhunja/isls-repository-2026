# Using Magic Performance to Facilitate Empathy of Design Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This exploratory study investigated the use of magic performance to promote empathy and perspective-taking skills of design students. The results suggest that magic not only increased students’ awareness of their bias but also encouraged them to form a user-centered mindset. This study highlights the potential of using magic as an innovative pedagogy to facilitate critical thinking, empathy, and perspective-taking.

## Introduction

Empathy toward users is essential for designers to create meaningful experiences and products that benefit users (More et al. 2016). Empathy can also enhance the cr eativity of designers and help them select creative  concepts (Alzayed et al., 2021). As such, empathy has been recognized as a fundamental component across various design methods, including Design Thinking (Jobst & Meinel, 2012) and Creative Problem Solving (Treffinger et al, 2005).

An increasing number of d esign educators and researchers  emphasize the need to improve students’ empathy and perspective-taking skills (Howcroft et al., 2021; Altay, 2014). Design researchers  have explored approaches such as co-design, which encourages design students to collabo rate with users to enhance their understanding (Tumala & Boxter, 2019), and reflective practices, such as writing design journals, to help students critically analyze u ser experiences and develop a deeper understanding of user perspectives (Chen & Forbes, 2014). Although these studies have shown the positive influence of related approaches on students’ empathy and perspective-taking, most of them focus solely  on teaching students tools and strategies to better understand audience needs, while neglecting to  raise their awareness of their implicit biases  and to cultivate an empathetic mindset.

Previous research has shown the effectiveness of using magic performance to pro mote critical thinking (Dougherty, 2004). A successful magic trick not only relies on the magician’s sleig ht of hand but also on the assumptions made by the audience. Watching magic and learning about the related principles can raise students’ awareness of personal biases  and mental model (Li et al., 2023) and encourage critical examination of recei ved information (Goodin, 2010).  It is worth noting that  critical thinking can prom ote empathy and interpersonal understanding (Guamanga et al., 2025).  Reframing implicit bias or personal assumptions as a habit can result in long-term reductions in bias and facilitate both empathy and perspective-taking (Devine et al., 2012). Moreover, this critical mindset can encourage designers to re-examine conventional design solutions and come up w ith creative design ideas.  For instance, a  case study conducted by Wood et al. (2023) suggests that learning about magic props and their mechanism can inspire designers to create patient-centered medical devices. In addition to watching and learning magic, performing magic has been shown to benefit designers by promoting their flexible thinking and helping them recognize the importance of the audience to their design (Li, 2020). We argue that learning and performing magic could  promote an empathetic mindset that centers on the needs of the audience. Performing Magic is an embod ied behavior where the performer not only needs to know “how” but also  constantly analyze the audience’s thinking, observe their reaction, and cu stomize their performance. This dynamic interaction  makes magic an ideal tool to promote perspective-taking and empathy. Therefore, this qualitative study explored the influence of magic performance on design empathy and perspectivetaking of design students.

## Methodology

This study was conducted in a graduate-level user experience design class at a public university in the Midwestern United States. 15 students (7 male and 8 female)  participated in this study.  All of them are first-year graduate students. Magic tricks were selected and organized based on their relatedness to the topics of the class. Each magic trick was implemented based on the framework propos ed by Li (2020). For each trick, the participants first watched the magic effect performed by the magician as a group. Each trick lasts about 4–5 min. Then, they were asked to brainstorm the methods used by the magicia n to achieve the trick. After that, the actual meth od was revealed to the participants. Related principles of  how magicians create, execute, and improve magic tricks were also discussed. Participants were also provided with actual design products that adopt sim ilar principles and techniques of designers so as to understand how similar design principles and skills were shared by designers and magicians. Finally, they were given related props to perform t he magic tricks for each other. Following this structure, 15 participants completed all five sessions and participated in a 30-40 semi-structured interview. Each interview lasts about 30-40 minutes. Thematic analysis was used to analyze the interview  data. The data was coded using NVivo 12.

## Results

Theme 1 Watching and Learning Magic helps students be aware of the limits of “mental model”: Watching magic tricks in the design class helped students recognize their mental models and assumptions. One student said, “We all have mental models  and preconceived assumptions about something.  With the magic trick, we're able to see how our mental model makes us think in a certain way. Because our perception makes us believe  this can’t be done.”  Watching a magic trick that defies logic or expectation  helped students realize how their  mental models and assumptions  influence their beliefs about what is possible.  The quote “make us think in a certain way” indicates that  students also became aware of how their assumption limit the m from envisioning alternative possibilities. In addition, learning about the magic props created by magicians to achieve the magic tricks inspired students to re-examine their own mental models and become more critical about what they observed. One student commented on the everyday magic props used by the magician, saying, “ Well, I think in general all magic tricks relate to mental models because we have a specific idea of how things work, like the Oreo Restoration trick. The magician used a fake [gimmicked] Oreo with a rubber band but made it look real, making us believe it was a solid normal Oreo. We all have an idea of how things work, but the magician changes how things work.” Similarly, another participant noted, “After watching magic, I always think, h ow do I break out of my mental model ? How do I break away from what I originally th ought.” On the other hand, students also considered the men tal models of their audience, aiming to make their designs easier to follow. As one student explained the simplicity in design., “First, it’s the mental model. It influenced me in this concept of simplicity —just make things simple. Unique is fine, but make it simple for users to understand.” Theme 2 Performing magic helps students form an “audience-centered” mindset: In this study, students not only watched magic but also learned magic tricks and performed magic for each other in the class. This hands-on magic experience helped students understand – the illusion is created in the audience’s mind and that  it can fail if the magician neglects the audience's needs and perspective. One student said, “If you want to perform magic, first of all, you should put your audience in mind because they are the focus. You need to know what type of audience you are performing t o.” Another student also highlighted the importance of considering audience’s perspectives to magicians: “For magicians, magic is all about your perspective, your angle, and your position. Sometimes the audience might notice the little tricks and subtle things you are doing to perform those tricks. So it's also important to keep the position and the angle right to your audience.” It is worth noting that such perspective-taking and empathy toward the audience also influenced students’ empathy toward their users.  Students were able to see the connection between ma gic performance and design practice. One student said, “Without being more conscious or being more aware of our assumptions, we couldn’t be a good magician or a good designer.  As a designer, we need to be a good observer and a good listener of the user.” In addition to users’ needs, similar to how magicians skillfully direct the attention of their audience, students were also influenced to focus on their role as desi gners in guiding users’ focus and ensuri ng effective communication, as suggested by the following comments: “As a designer, your main goal is to communicate with your audience. You have to catch their attention and call the attention of the audiences in your design.”  “We need to make it appealing for the audience but practical for ourselves.”

## Discussion

This study highlights the potential of using magic performance as a pedagogical tool to promote empath y and perspective-taking among design students. Two key themes emerged: the impact of watching magic on students’ awareness of mental models and the influence of performing magic in fostering an audience-centered mindset. Watching seemingly impossible magic effects challen ges students’ previous assumptions. Under such infl uence, students were motivated to reexamine and question their ment al models and biases. This critical self-awareness is crucial for designers, as it  encourages them to critically examine their personal assumptions for the users and approach design with an open and creative mindset.  In addition to watching magic, performing magic required students to adopt the perspective of their audience, emphasizing the importance of understanding and m eeting audience needs. This hands-on experience encouraged students to draw parallels between magic performance and design practi ce. They recognized the benefits of perspective-taking, effective communication, and empathy to both magicians and designers.
