# Rotating Leadership of Transactive Discourse through Synchrony and Desynchrony of Multimodal Interaction

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Rotating leadership emerges in successful collaboration. This study investigates the extent to which their multimodal interactions are synchronized or not, along with the rotating leadership of transactive discourse (LoT). Nine groups of three members participated in a 45-minute co-located CPS task, and their spatial positions were collected. The results suggest that in addition to the synchronization of the spatial positioning, desynchronization was observed in rotating LoT. The potential use of the kinesthetic modality is discussed.

Rotating leadership can be observed in successful collaborative activity, as one form of shared leadership. Successful collaboration is supported by shared agency. Thus, the leadership is emergent, and the temporal leader is not fixed but rotates (Ma et al., 2016). In this study, we investigate rotating leadership from the perspective of transactivity (Teasley, 1997). T ransactivity is  a social mode of knowledge co-construction and has been considered vital for productive collaborative problem-solving (CPS) (Vogel et al., 2023; Weinberger & Fischer, 2006). Previous studies have operationalized the leadership of transactive discourse  (LoT) as an engagement to the discourse moves in which ideas are connected to the existing discussions (Oshima et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2025). While transactivity and rotating LoT are considered one of the important aspects to understand productive CPS, previous studies suggest that multimodal synchrony may also influence the CPS productivity and the transactive discourse (Li et al., 2022; Aoyama Lawrence & Weinberger, 2022; Nasir et al., 2021; Popov et al., 2017). In this study, we shed light on multimodal interactions in rotating LoT. To capture and analyze multimodal interactions in co-located collaboration, it is important to define and integrate the modalities appropriately (Esterhazy et al., 2025; Schneider, 2023). Esterhazy et al. (2025) proposed the Multimodal Collaboration Analytics (MMCA) framework and defined five categories of modality; here, we address the auditory, visual, and kinesthetic modalities, excluding the tactile and physiological ones, as our interest lies in dynamic group interaction.

Previous studies suggested that the synchrony of auditory and visual modalities is potentially crucial for transactive discourse (Nasir et al., 2021; Aoyama Lawrence & Weinberger, 2022; Sharma & Molinari, 2023). However, the kinesthetic modality remains underexplored. Kinesthetic modality, such as spatial positioning, may shed light on embodied teamwork in co-located collaboration (Zhao et al., 2023). In contrast, Popov et al. (2017) argued that synchrony of action might be important but not significant in transactive discourse. F urther investigation is required on the synchrony of the kinesthetic modality to uncover the multimodal interplay in the transactive discourse. In this study, we employ spatial position as a kinesthetic modality to analyze the synchrony in rotating LoT. Specifically, we address the following research question: To what extent does the synchrony of spatial positioning differ between rotating and fixed LoT? Nine groups of three undergraduates, 27 in total, at a Japanese university participated in a CPS task. The task was from the Jasper series (CTGV, 1992), and participants were asked to watch a 17-minute video storytelling before the 45-minute CPS session. During the CPS session, they had access to two artifacts: a computer and a whiteboard. The computer was available for watching the video, and the whiteboard was available for writing and organizing information. The objective  of the task was to consider possible routes to bring the eagle to an animal hospital located in town  as fast as possible. The whole CPS session was recorded  and transcribed. A stereo camera was also installed for spatial position analysis.

## Methodology & Study Context

We analyzed the temporal trajectory of the LoT based on the transcripts. Following previous studies (Lu et al., 2025; Oshima et al., 2023), the group’s transactive discourse was modeled as a word co-occurrence network, and the individual LoT is operationalized as the contribution to the network. We extracted task-related words from the transcripts as the nodes in  the network. These words (nodes) are linked through the conversation turns  by transactive discourse moves, such as integration and conflict resol ution, which can be operationalized as the co-occurrence of words. In addition, we operationalized the individual LoT based on Lu et al. (2025), in which LoT 638 is defined as the extent to which the nodes and links added by an individual contributed to the development of the network. The temporal trajectory was captured by the conversation turns, using KBDeX (Oshima et al., 2012). To extract spatial position information, we developed a stereo capture system and conducted 3D skeleton detection. A stereo camera was placed  in front of the artifacts, capturing 3D image s of all three members. Their spatial position was automatically annotated with three labels: in front of the computer (computer), in front of the whiteboard (whiteboard_front), and around the whiteboard (whiteboard_around). The results suggested that the dyadic synchrony was frequently reconfigured in the groups with rotating  LoT, while there were fewer reconfigurations in the groups with fixed LoT. The temporal trajectories of LoT suggested that rotating LoT emerged in groups 1, 2, 3, and 5, implying collective contribution to the group’s knowledge coconstruction. Conversely, groups 4, 6, and 8 were led by two of the three members, and groups 7 and 9 were led by only one member, both implying unbalanced contributions. In the following sections, we compare the members’ spatial positioning between groups 5 and 9, where significant rotating and fixed LoT were observed, respectively. Figure 1 shows the distribution of spatial position among members in groups 5 and 9, suggesting the different patterns of dyadic synchrony. Figure 1(a)  suggests frequent reconfiguration of dyadic synchrony in group 5, where rotating  LoT is demonstrated. Dyadic synchrony was first observed between members 1 and 2 around the computer, then member 2 reconfigured the synchrony with member 0 around the whiteboard. After a while, member 0 moved in front of the computer  and was in sync with member 2. Several reconfigurations were then observed among all members. The frequent reconfiguration suggests that members’ engagement was actively self-organized and emerged. In contrast, group 9, where a fixed LoT was demonstrated by member 2, experienced less reconfiguration of dyadic synchrony, as illustrated in Figure 2(b). While members 1 and 2 both reconfigured their dyadic synchrony by switching their spatial position, member 0 stuck to the whiteboard and never interacted with the computer.

We argue that desynchronization of dyadic synchrony would be influential to the collaboration quality. Here, we define desynchronization as leaving synchrony and interacting with another member(s). Collaboration requires not only synchronous work but also asynchronous division of labor (Dillenbourg, 1999), and members’ actions are sometimes distributed (e.g., Popov et al., 2017), based on t heir shared agency (Damşa, 2010). From the perspective of embodied teamwork (Zhao et al., 2023), members’ shared agency is embodied, which could lead to distributed multimodal interactions. In terms of MMCA, it would be said that as a result of shared agency, desynchronization occurs in the kinesthetic modality, and rotation of LoT occurs in the verbal modality. Therefore, desynchronization might be another indicator of collaboration, along with the rotating LoT. Our study contributes to the transactivity research by adding empirical implications that collective engagement to transactive discourse could be embodied not only in verbal and visual modalities but also in the kinesthetic modality. In addition, our methodological contributions also allow us to quantify the multimodal interaction in transactive discourse, implying the potential importance of desynchrony. Despite the implications, s everal limitations remain. Due to the small sample size and coarse temporal granularity, the causal relationship is unclarified. In addition, the kinesthetic modality includes actions and body movements, which were not analyzed.
