# Teacher Instructional Adaptations to Support Language Learning in Elementary STEM

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research supporting adaptations for multilingual learners (MLLs) in elementary science classrooms, particularly within project-based learning (PBL) frameworks, remains limited. This qualitative ethnographic study investigates how a fourth-grade STEM teacher in a dual language immersion (DLI) classroom adapted the Multiple Literacies in Project-Based Learning (ML-PBL) curriculum to support students’ science sensemaking across language. Data were collected through teacher observations, interviews, and video recordings, focusing on instructional adaptations. Findings show that the teacher recognized one adaptation, strategic grouping of students, but did not mention two others that were identified by researchers, supporting translanguaging and using multiple modalities.

## Introduction

There is little empirical research addressing curricular adaptations for multilingual learners (MLLs) in elementary science and STEM classrooms. Most research centers on a specific type of adaptation, such as translang uaging (Pierson & Grapin, 2021), scaffolding (Walqui, 2006), integrating computer visualizations (Malone, 202 3), or modeling (Pierson et al., 2021). In this study, we investigate a teacher's instructional adaptations w ithin a dual language immersion fourth-grade class engaged in project-based learning (PBL). Supporting multilingual learners through project-based learning PBL supports collaborative group work among student s to create a physical outcome by applying understa nding of their learning (Halvorsen et al., 2019). Sociocu ltural theories of language development (e.g., van Lier, 2004; Lee et al., 2013) suggest that the  features of project-based curriculum can provide opportunities for stud ents to learn science and language simultaneously. The curr iculum that is the focus of this study, Multiple Li teracies in Project Based Learning (ML-PBL) (Educurious, 2024), engages students in building solutions to problems in their communities, which creates a need for students to s hare local knowledge through their existing languag e practices. For example, each unit begins with students conduct ing family interviews in their home language and sharing with the class. Engaging with the curricular driving questions provides students with a meaningful context for language use and encourages sensemaking through interactions with others. Moreover, ML-PBL consistently offers students multiple modalities, such as investigating using specific ma terials, acting out ideas, and student-engineered artifacts to support sensemaking through science practices (Reigh & Miller, 2020). Teacher curriculum adaptation to support MLLs’ sensemaking Adaptations are the teacher’s departure from moves and prompts as written in the curriculum materials (Adah Miller et al., 2022; Lowell et al., 2024). Teachers adapt science lessons to scaffold student learning (Davis & Miyake, 2018), differentiate instruction (Drake & Sherin, 2006) and center students’ ideas and questions (Hammer et al., 2012). Research has shown that teacher adaptations can foster an equity-centered learning environment by engaging students’ culturally-based intellectual and linguistic re sources (Marshall et al., 2021; Severance et al., 2016). For example, teachers can leverage local social jus tice issues (Miller et al., 2022) and encourage collaboration and support equitable sensemaking (Ko  et al., 2024). Such adaptations deepen students’ collaborative engagement in the practices of scienc e, w hich offers opportunities for them to use language in meaningful ways (Cook, 2023). Given that research s upports student scientific sensemaking as authentic  and situated context for language development through c ommunication in science practices, the follo wing question guides this research: In a classroom with multiple languages, What adaptations does a teacher make during class to support student science sensemaking?

## Methods

This ethnographic qualitative study occurs in an elementary school in the southeastern United States (Fetterman, 2019). The elementary school is located in a midsiz e urban area with approximately 570 students, of wh ich 30% are classified as MLLs (School District, n.d.). The  school offers a Dual Language Immersion (DLI) prog ram in which students receive equal amounts of instruction  in English and Spanish. The focal teacher, Ms. Smi th, is a STEM teacher with 16 years of classroom teaching ex perience and ESL certificate. Students across the s chool's grade levels (K-5) come to her STEM classroom once a week for 45 minutes. Ms. Smith speaks only English. Ms. Smith uses the ML-PBL science curriculum, open sourced through the Sp rocket platform (Educurious, 2024). ML-PBL is designed to meet the Next Generation Science  Standards through the principles of PBL (Halverson et al., 2019). It also supports e quity and social justice-oriented goals, topics, and teaching practices. This study focuses on five days of instr uction, centering balanced and unbalanced forces fr om a unit entitled Toys. Each lesson includes contexts for pr oblems, ways to support collaboration in small grou ps, and teacher prompts. The focal class is a fourth grade DLI classroom, where the STEM class is taught in English. Data collection included several semi-structured interviews with Ms. Smith. The first and  second were to gain more information about her background, and her initial ideas about adapting the curriculum to her class structure and student population. The fin al interview occurred after each lesson to discuss her adaptations and lesson reflections. During the lesson the classroom was audio recorded and three researchers observed the teaching of the lessons by taking field notes. Afterwards, they debriefed the lesson to discuss the teacher’s adaptations. All debrief sessions and interviews were audio recorded. All audio recordings were transcribed using an on line platform.

Data analysis began by using a grounded approach to identify the teacher’s adaptations. Using field notes and audio of teaching, the research team created in dividual memos for each of the five days of instruc tion to identify adaptations from the curriculum. The team discussed the memos as a group and identified the m ost common adaptations. The team then analyzed the post-lesson teacher interviews for additional insight on  the adaptations they identified and additional adaptations from the teacher’s perspective.

## Findings

The analysis identified three types of adaptations. The research team identified two adaptations, translanguaging and use of multiple modalities. The teacher identified an additional adaptation, strategic grouping of students. Ms. Smith supported translanguaging by encouraging students to use the language with wh ich they felt most comfortable. For example, she said “Y'all are all bilingual at this point, right? So, you can all talk in both languages to help them, okay?” Additionally, Ms. Smith suppor ted the use of multiple modalities by encouraging students to use hands-on materials, drawing, and note taking to support c ollaboration and problem solving. One example involved a collaborative class activity where students worked together to engineer a balloon powered car. During the activity, Ms. Smith circulated the classroom, monitoring students' engagement and focus. One group consisted of six members, five of whom have Spanish as a first language. Ms. Smith checked that the group understood the assignment and how to begin. Materials were set out for the stud ents consisting of cardboard, plastic wheels, balloons and metal axles. Ms Smith prompted student s by pointing to materials and asking “Where should  you start? …come up with an idea…write down what materi als… go get them and get started”. In one post teach ing interview Ms. Smith acknowledged using multiple mod alities to support all students, not just MLLs. She  did not describe this as an adaptation. Ms. Smith does not assign student groups, allowing student choice and recognizing the importance of strategic grouping for successful collaboration. During several post t eaching interviews, Ms. Smith noted the students self selected into groups helping to support communication between their peers. Overall, she said she was pleased with the groupings, howeve r she reorganiz ed a few students to better facilitate communication among Spanish speakers.

Ms. Smith purposely added to the collaboration problem-based contexts from the curriculum to focus on language of collaboration, supporting translanguaging explicitly. She demonstrated the use of materials to support talk, a teacher move that was not included in the curricular prompts. Finally, Ms. Smith changed the composition of the groups during the lesson to support collaboration.

## Discussion and conclusion

This analysis shows a range of teacher adaptations that support students to engage in sensemaking and use language for meaningful purposes, including the use  of multiple named languages and modalities to supp ort communication.  However, the teacher seemed at times to be unaware of some of the productive adaptations that she was making. Thus, a valuable direction for teacher education is to support teachers to recognize the productive adaptations through guided reflection. By centering  sensemaking, language use, and collaboration as science goals, teachers can be supported in deepening their  analysis of the extent to which their adaptations support students. Despite the teacher’s explicit prompts fo r students to use their home language, she only spo ke English and the curricular materials were in English; as a result, students preferred English in the classroom, to the exclusion of students who are newer to speaking this language. Teachers may need additional time and support to reflect on how their instruction al moves are taken up by students, the extent to wh ich these moves promote equitable collaboration and sensemaking to generate insights that can inform further adaptations.
