# Interest vs. Identity: Navigating Methodological Challenges to Coding Learners’ Funds of Identity

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines the methodological challenges of coding student identity within classroom activities using the Funds of Identity (FoI) framework. Analyzing eighth-grade students’ engagement in a network visualization activity, we explored distinctions between expressions of interest and identity. Results highlight complexities in interpreting student participants’ identity within brief classroom interactions, offering nuances in categorizing student contributions and prompting refinements in coding practices.

## Introduction and theoretical framework

Scholarly research on culturally relevant and susta ining pedagogies emphasizes the importance of recog nizing learners' backgrounds and identities to enhance the ir learning experiences by connecting them to the c urriculum (Ladson-Billings, 1995; Gay, 2002; Paris, 2012). We analyzed learners’ Funds of Identity (FoI) — “historically accumulated, culturally developed, and socially distributed resources that are essential for people's self-definition, self-expression, and self-understanding" (Esteban-Guitart & Mo ll, 1994, p. 37) —expressed through humanities classroom activities integrating network visualizations and data literacy (Authors, under review). These activities aimed to help students articulate their identities within collaborative networks. While the research team anticipated students might not fully  represent their identities  during brief (2-3 class period s) activities, we  expected expressions of their interests. This distinction raised questions about whether student contributions reflected core identity or situational interests. Although identity and interests are historically accumulated and socially situated, research distinguishes identity as core to a person’s self, whereas interests may be a part of identity or situational (Renninger, 2009; Azevedo, 2011). This poster underscores the methodological challenges of coding participants’ funds of identity in  brief interactions, examining how momentary expressions of interest align —or not—with identity as currently defined and how these distinctions influenced connections within the network (Steinberg et al.,2025).

## Methodology

The present methodological analysis emerged from a broader intervention study with 89 eighth-grade students in a Greek Mythology network activity using Net.Create (Craig &Danish, 2018)—a collaborative visualization tool for creative interactive networks  in real-time. Students mapped connections among their interests, their peers' interests, and the mythological characters. Although the broader study explored pedagogical outcomes  (reported in detailed elsewhere, Steinberg et al.,2025), this research  specifically examines methodological challenges  in coding brief student expressions into Funds of Iden tity categories (i.e., geographical, practical, cul tural, social, and institutional). To investigate these challenges, the research  team analyzed classroom video recordings  and student-generated network visualizations. Initially, the researchers independently coded stud ent references to personal characteristics, interests, and identities  using FoI categories. The  team then  engaged in iterative discussions to clarify, refine, and achieve consensus on coding decisions. Additionally, students completed a postsurvey about their identity expressions following all network  activities, providing further context for analysis. Ultimately, this paper highlights insights from our  collaborative, iterative self-reflection (Clarke et al., 2023)  on methodological decisions, particularly addressing h ow to differen tiate identity  from interest within brief classroom interactions.

## Results

Our team identified three key challenges in coding learners’ identities during brief interactions: Consensus building around dimensions of identity While coding student utterances, we realized our interpretations of identity were influenced by our own identities and biases. To address this, we engaged in iterative discussions and refined our coding scheme to explicitly define each FoI categor y. For example, when  a student connected themselves to the Greek Prometheus with the descriptor “thief,” we debated whether this reflected a practical category (a significant activity or inter est for the student) or  a personality characteristic  (defined as personal qualities, appearance, or view s of oneself)  they identified with. In this case, the researchers needed tonegotiate between these two categories based on their own identities and opinions regarding whether being a t hief is an activity or a part of someone's personal ity. After reviewing the coding scheme where each category was previously defined, we reached a consensus of classifying it as practical because it was more aligned with an activity rather than a quality. This consensus-building process led us to  create explicit guidelines in our codebook,  standardizing our analysis and  reducing potential bia s. Methodologically, this highlights the  necessity of clear, collaboratively defined criteri a when coding ambiguous identity data, especially when researcher positionalities may shape interpretation. Exploring the tension between whether students are sharing their interests or identity We also encountered challenges distinguishing whether students’ contributions represented core identity markers or situational interest. Existing literature acknowledges overlaps between interests and identity (Renninger, 2009; Azevedo, 2011), yet our brief classroom interactions magnified this  ambiguity. To address this, we refined our codebook to include nuanced categories accommodating diverse student expressions. For instance,  a student stating they and a god “liked sports ” was categorized  as practical  (meaningful activity), whereas describing themselves and a god  as “athletic” was coded under personality (an added category related to the idea of self-definition and expression), reflecting a personal quality. This adjustment helped us acknowledge diverse forms of self-expression while  identifying meaningful patterns in how students related to the FoI categories.  This methodological tension suggests that rigid separations between identity and interest are problematic, underscoring the need for flexible, context-sensitive coding schemes or expanded definitions within FoI to analyze brief student contributions effectively.

Reconciling our codes with student reflections about sharing An additional data point complicating the assumption that students' contributions represented core aspects of their identities emerged from our post-survey results. In this survey, students were asked to list the three most important aspects of their ident ity and rate how often they shared these in the net work activities. The data showed a misalignment between what students deemed significa nt, what they expressed during the Greek Mythology network activity, and their self-reporting of expressing these aspects of their identity. Although more than half of the students indicat ed they shared important personal elements,  the coded  network data  rarely matched these explicitly. This mismatch may suggest that the activity structure limited opportunities for me aningful identity sharing or that students articulated their identiti es and interests in ways we, as researchers, did no t recognize. Methodologically, th is discrepancy highlights  the importance of incorporating student reflections  or memberchecking processes into identity analyses, ensuring that  coding categories authentically represent students’ perspectives.

## Conclusion and discussion

The methodological challenges raised by this study demonstrate the complexities  inherent in coding students’ identities within brief network-based activities using the FoI framework. Specifically, our findings suggest the importance of flexible, nuanced coding approaches capable of capturing diverse, transient expressions of identity and interest. Given these methodological insights, we intentionally raise critical questions to stimulate dialogue in the learning sciences field:

1. Are identity and interest bidirectional? Are all in terests a result of identity, or donew interests

build/change identities? Is this an important distinction for researchers to attend to?

2. How can teachers and researchers together navigate bringing student identity into the classroom with the

need tonegotiate the connections and differences between interests and identities?

3. What types of activities provide opportunities for students to share/exhibit their identities in a way  that

allows teachers to learn about their students and build more student-relevant learning opportunities?

4. How do opportunities for collective identity sharin g, such as within this network visualization activi ty,

provide opportunities for students to share their i dentities or get to know their classmates’ identiti es above and beyond individual interests, and is that distinction meaningful? By explicitly raising methodological questions, our study contributes to ongoing discussions on culturally relevant and sustaining pedagogies, emphasizing the importance of designing coding frameworks and analytical processes that authentically represent students’ lived experiences. Future research should explore methodological innovations, particularly  engaging students directly in validating, refining, and expanding identity-coding schemes suitable for brief class interactions.
