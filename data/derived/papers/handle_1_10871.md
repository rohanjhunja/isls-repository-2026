# Towards a Longitudinal, Qualitative Study of Computational Identity in K-12 Computer Science Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Broadening and deepening participation in computing requires a richer understanding of the impacts of K-12 CS education on learners’ lives and identities. We propose that qualitative, longitudinal research can contribute to this understanding and help produce better CS learning experiences. This poster presents our proposed methodology for conducting such a study along with pilot results. Our findings create space for discussion about these methods and the nuance they can provide in understanding computational identity.

## Introduction and background

In 1980, Seymour Papert asked a prescient question:  how would computers change the way people think? In the 43 years since, computer science (CS) education research has work ed to identify and shape these changes, from Wing’s (2006) definition of computational thinking (CT) to Kafai et al.’s (2020) identification of cog nitive, situated, and critical framings of CT. As CS educat ion moves into mainstream K12, however, Tissenbaum et al.

(2021) propose re-examining its goals and broadening possible endpoints beyond becoming a software engineer.

We argue that these shifting ideas about what CS of fers K-12 students motivate longitudinal investigations that look beyond individual CS class rooms and seek to understand the ways learners deve lop and make use of their CT throughout their educational, professional, and personal lives. Such research can  broaden access and deepen participation in computing by exploring how learners’ experiences wi th CS education contribute to their CS identities (Shaw and Kafai, 2020). While existing work has used phenomenographi c methods to investigate learners’ experiences of CS education (Kinnunen and Simon, 2012), little has explored K12 learners’ experiences across computing contexts (i.e. classrooms, social media, etc.) and over time. In previous work, we contributed to this line of research by documenting how K-12 CS learners begin to “re-see” technology throughout their lives and how this process contributes to the computational identities learners author for themselves (Wolf et al., 2023) We have since conducted additional interviews with the students from our original study two years after their initial in terviews and just before they graduated from high s chool. This poster proposes an extension of our analysis into a longitudinal study of the influence of CS education on learner’s lives and identities. We share our proposed methodo logy and report findings from piloting these method s on a subset of our data to spark conversation around methodologies and longitudinal, qualitative studies of K-12 CS.

## Methods

We study a cohort of students at a private bilingua l PreK-12 school in Hong Kong who participated in the firs t iteration of a 2-year Constructionist CS course designed and taught by the authors. In 2021, we interviewed 13 students (three girls, ten boys; 14-16 years old) at the end of their Grade 10 course. In 2023, we re-interviewed 8 of these students (3 girls, 5 boys; now 16-18 years old). Both interviews began by asking students to share their “learning journey with CS” as if they were outlining the chapters of a book (Dziallas and Fincher, 2018). We then asked students to define computer science and to re flect on their relationships with particular techno logies (e.g., phones, the internet). In 2023, we asked participants to member-check findings from the 2021 interviews. In analyzing the 2023 interviews, we focus on: What themes and experiences from the earlier interviews still stand? What new themes and experiences emerge ? How have existing themes and experiences extended  or shifted in some way? To answer these questions, we propose a flexible, abductive coding approach (Detering and Waters, 2018) that treats the data from our previou s interviews as related but distinct and allows us to look for themes in the data that reflect our earlier finding s while still creating space for open coding of new themes. This poster represents the pilot efforts to trial this a pproach on interviews from two participants who ser ved as case studies in an earlier paper and were thus more fami liar to the authors (Authors 2023a). One author rev iewed the findings from our existing publications and coded t he twonew interviews with both an open, inductive coding approach and one that drew on previous findings to create codes for: (1) examples of students re-seeing technology in their everyday lives, (2) feelings of respect for the creators of technology, and (3) feelings of activation to “do more” with computing. The resulting codes include t hose from previous analyses and those which arose i n a grounded fashion from analyzing the new interviews.

## Findings from pilot and discussion

Piloting these methods produced insights into theme s that persisted across interviews as well as ways students’ experiences had changed since the first interview. One student, Tina (all names anonymized), shared in  her first interview that, after our 2-year course, she felt that she “wasn’t built for CS” but that she would benefit from her CS education throughout her life. Our original analysis grapples with how to reconcile Tina’s lack of confidence in her CS ability with her intentional decision not to pursue CS further based on her lack of interest in the subject (Authors Blinded, 2023a). However, in Tina’s second  interview, we were afforded a new perspective on h er computational identity. Tina described starting an online business with peers from our CS course that sells clothing based on personalized fashion recommendations. Tina shared how each member of the team took on roles, and in her role as a designer of the company’s website, “because [she] know[s] the process of computer science… there's certain things that it's gonna be tougher to do. Knowing th at helps to come up with ideas for the website that  are still very useful or very aesthetically pleasing. B ut it's also doable for the computer scientists in [their] team.” Though she identifies other students in her team as  the “computer scientists,” Tina’s ongoing relation ship to CS demonstrates how her computational identity has led  her to another “endpoint” of CS education where he r CS education still plays a beneficial role in her life (Tissenbaum et al., 2021). Another student, Noa, shared in her initial interview how her experience with CS education allowed her to do personal projects outside of our courses related to her interests in sustainability. Noa shared how this process contributed to a holistic sense of her growth as a person and her desire to do more for the world with  what she had learned in CS. Our original analysis interprete d Noa’s experiences as contributing to a highly act ivated computational identity where Noa saw herself on an inbound trajectory into the discipline of CS (Authors Blinded, 2023a). In Noa’s second interview, she maintains th is desire to pursue CS, but, after experiencing mor e CS education through another course and an internship,  she now realizes “how, unless you have a big amoun t of people that will push for sustainability, it's really hard to go on that movement on your own,” and shared how she now tries to “think realistic” about what she cand o with her CS learning. This development in Noa’s t hinking demonstrates how her notions of CS have been comple xified through their situation in context and indic ates the development of CT from a more critical framing (Kafai et al., 2020).

## Conclusion

This pilot shows nuances that develop as students’ author their computational identities over time and  context: Tina’s imagined future usefulness of CS comes to fruition and Noa’s activated but idealistic vision meets reality. These insights into learners’  rich trajectories offer direction for the larger, l ongitudinal study of computational identity we propose. The learning sciences remind us that learning is an ongoing process rather than a destination to be reached, and our work aligns computational identities with this insight. Computational identities are mobile, dynamic, and pluralistic, and design our curricula and methods accordingly.
