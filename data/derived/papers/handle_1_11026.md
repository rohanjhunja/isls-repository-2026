# Professional Learning Integrating Computer Science and Storytelling through Culturally Responsive Computing

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study presents a critical case study of a redesign of a professional development activity for elementary teachers to understand design decisions that lead to opportunities to integrate computer science and computational thinking aspects with social/societal dimensions in learning. We conducted qualitative analysis of culturally responsive-sustaining aspects of the original and redesigned activities, along with reflections and debriefs about the activity. Findings from the case study highlight potential affordances and limitations of taking an adaptation approach towards computer science professional development design in order to better reflect critical and/or culturally responsive computing in elementary grades.

## Introduction and literature review

A common approach to supporting computer science le arning at elementary grades often involves integrat ing computer science and computational thinking (CS/CT) into other subjects, often in STEM. This integratio n is challenging for teachers, such that professional learning is important to support this work. However, research on professional learning that is focused on critical a nd/or culturally responsive computing are less comm on (Jayathirtha et al., 2023), and typically have not included elementary grades. Thus, this study aims to explore how the design of professional development (PD) for K-8 teachers that has focused on supporting teachers to integrate computational thinking and literacy can be re-oriented towards culturally responsive computing. We draw on previous research on culturally sustaini ng pedagogies and culturally responsive computing that together provide a framework for how “students  have opportunities to learn that can sustain their  cultural ways of being but also shape their access to power in oppressive systems” (Madkins et al., 2020, p. 9)  and “situating technology ideas within a sociopolitical  context and giving students opportunities to criti que and explore issues that are relevant to them” (p. 10). Previous research on culturally responsive computing has noted the role that textiles and crafting can play in broadening concepts of computing and incorporating powerful CS/CT ideas (Jayathirtha & Kafai, 2019). Recent work that has shown how computer science idea s can be interwoven with social/societal perspectives in PD with high school teachers (Jayathirtha et al., 2023).

## Study context and methods

This work was done in the context of a research-practice partnership with educators in Eastern Tennessee, which previously developed collective goals around supporting K-8 grades teachers to include CS/CT in integration with literacy through storytelling. This approach was driven by partners who emphasized the history of oral and written practices in the region, including Appalachian creative works such as stories. Currently, the partnership is using design-based research to investigate how to adapt PD activities in order to foreground this culturally responsivesustaining teaching perspective. As most participating teachers were new to CS/CT co ntent, a primary design structure used in the original PD was teachers experiencing K-8 CS/CT activities as learners led by facilitators, and reflecting on the student thinking and pedagogical aspects of using those activities in the classroom. This study uses a critical case study approach. Dat a used include original and revised professional development materials, notes and video recordings o f design and debrief meetings, and written reflecti ons from designers or facilitators of the professional learning. Data from design and debrief meetings was used to select the case; h was selected because it was discussed across several meetings, becoming a focal example for discussing design revisions for the PD activities. Qualitative coding of the case was completed on the original and revised activity, along with written reflections on the design and use of the activity. A deductive coding scheme developed from the culturally sustaining-responsive STEAM curriculum scorecard dimensions on  representation and social justice (Peoples et al., 2021) was used. The coded data were then examined to identify differences in coding between (a) the original and revised activity, (b) the revised activity and (c) written reflections.

## Findings

The revised version of the focal activity was set in the context of quilting as a cultural activity within Appalachia, with a rich history and ties to abolitionist moveme nts (Culturally Situated Design Tools, n.d.) and storytelling. The revised activity provided photos of quilts within Appalachian traditions, with a task to create instructions for a quilt pattern as an “unplugged” CS/CT activity. The original activity involved creating instructi ons for a toothbrush robot, which was adapted from a family STEM activity drawing on brushing teeth as a family routine (Tech Tales, 2019). In both versions, teachers completed the activity as  learners  and discussed pedagogical implications. The decision to change to the quilting context resp onded to the difficulty identifying “social situations and problems'' that the toothbrushing robot could be a solution to, and  one that is “embedded within a societal and/or systemic context” (Peoples et al., 2021, p. 14) related to Eastern Tennessee. The design aimed to focus on the same CS/CT concepts, algorithmic thinking. However, the quilt context provided additional space to connect to decomposition as Appalachian quilts incorporated multiple instances of common visual elements. Based on qualitative analysis of the quilt activity, there was some alignment to the representation and social justice elements of the coding scheme. There was a connection to a regional/culturally-specific practice (i.e., quilting), but it did not use an action-oriented approach (i.e., in terms of how quilting can or has been use d to take actions that combat inequity or promote equity, and connect learning to social, political, and/or environmental concerns) and did not have explicit support to critique the impact of STEAM (i.e., promote or provoke critical questions about STEAM and the societal status quo). Written reflections after the PD highlighted how the purpose and impact of quilting within a socio-historical context was abse nt, despite being a main motivation for revisions. For example, a quilt pattern could connect to how  a community engages in storytelling through quilt making, passing on cultural knowledge and practice, and the potential risks of including design and story details in a pattern to tell stories to combat oppression (e.g., in abolitionist movements). Finally, reflections posited that more in-depth engagement with anticipating students’ quilt pattern decisions using decomposition and algorithmic thinking may be generative for developing connections to critical consciousness in quilting.

## Discussion

Changing the context of the activity to one that had cultural significance, while attempting to maintain continuity in CS/CT content, provided an opportunity to expand and deepen the CS/CT content of the activity. This reflects previous research that found technical, social, and  individual aspects of critical computing can be in tertwined in teacher PD (Jayathirtha et al., 2023), and addition ally suggests that using an authentic, culturally s ignificant context can provide opportunities for richer  CS/CT content in PD. However, the case study also highlights how designing for critical consciousness as a goal of PD was a challenge, despite these aspects of the original activity being a motivation for redesign. This reflects prev ious research highlighting that critical consciousn ess is often challenging for teachers to incorporate when working to take up culturally sustaining pedagogies (Madkins et al., 2020). Finally, future work could examine the hypothesis that stronger support in the PD for anticipating student thinking about the CS/CT ideas may be generative for developing connections to critical consciousness.
