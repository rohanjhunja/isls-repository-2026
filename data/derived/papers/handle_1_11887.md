# Interaction Analysis between Generative AI and High School Students in Scientific Projects

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates interaction patterns between generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) and high school students as they develop science research projects. Specifically, it examines GenAI’s behavior, focusing on interaction patterns and response appropriateness. The analysis indicates that GenAI frequently provides unsolicited advice and generates responses eight times longer than student inputs despite predefined constraints. This study proposes a visualization method for the interaction imbalance between learners and GenAI, providing valuable insight into the relationship between interaction balance and learning in future computer-supported collaborative learning research.

## Background and research questions

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI), particularly large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT (OpenAI, n.d.), has garnered significant  attention in computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) research as a promising educational tool. While GenAI has diverse applications, this study focuses on its potential to support collaborative problem-solving of unstructured problems. Recent studies suggest that GenAI enhance s problemsolving by providing structured explanations and promoting scientific thinking (Borge et al., 2024; Kasneci et al., 2023), contributing to scientific research projects.

This research examines GenAI’s role in collaborative problem-solving. The 2022 revision of Japan ’s high school education standards emphasized collabor ative inquiry-based learning (MEXT, 2018), prompting the development of novel teaching materials. Approximately 300 selected high schools offer courses in scientific research projects  (JST, n.d.), where  students collaborate to create and address their research questions  (RQs). Teachers typically supervise 9–17 groups per class, limiting opportunities for dialo gue-driven guidance. Consequently, students often develop overly simplistic questions that can be answered via internet searches or overly complex ones that exceed their capabilities. Although teachers employ tools such as textbooks, checklists, and worksheets (Okamoto, 2021a, 2021b), these static resources often fail to stimulate students’ critical thinking, underscoring the need for interactive GenAI support.

However, implementing GenAI in collaborative learning presents certain challenges. First, GenAI often disrupts conversations with unsolicited advice, resulting in context-insensitive interactions that inhibit problemsolving. In addition, its stochastic response patterns may deviate from pedagogical objectives. Second, excessive support risks undermining cognitive engagement and knowledge construction, given GenAI’s limited conceptual understanding and difficulty in tailoring scaffolding to users’ learning levels (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023). These limitations are particularly problematic in collaborative settings, where interactive knowl edge construction is crucial (Borge et al., 2024). Recent studies have highlight ed these concerns. Borge et al. (2024) inferred tha t GenAI messages became redundant and monopolistic du ring dialogues with learners, necessitating correct ions from learners  to maintain appropriate levels of support. Similarl y, Chu et al. (2024) observed that university students primarily considered GenAI an information-gathering tool rather than a collaborative learning  partner. These studies emphasize the importance of learners’ explicit strategies for utilizing GenAI as a learni ng support tool. However, due to their primary focus on one-on-one interactions rather than group learning scenarios, further research is necessary to explore the functions of G enAI in classroom settings and how students use it collaboratively.

This study addresses two RQs. RQ1: Can GenAI maintain appropriate interaction patterns in collaborative problem-solving, specifically during theme selection for scientific projects ? Using qualitative analysis, we investigate whether GenAI provides uns olicited advice or information and, if so, the nature of such advice. RQ2: Does GenAI provide concise support proportional to user inputs ? We examine this question via quantitative analysis, focusing on the relationship between input length and GenAI responses.

## Methods

Lesson design We designed a learning activity as part of the RQ development phase within a one-year study project. It was also the students’ first opportunity to use GenAI in the  course. Prior to the activity, teachers provided instructions on operating GenAI, covering privacy settings, handling personal information, and hallucination risks. The students were informed that the GenAI-based tool employed in this study had two primary functions: identifyin g gaps in user-provided information and generating prompts to refine their RQs. The lesson was structured into two distinct phases:  (1) student interaction with the GenAI tool and (2) the documentation of interaction data within shared group repositories. During the first phase, students submitted segments of their research plans and engaged with A I-generated prompts to refine their RQs. Subsequently, they documented their interactions in shared documents v ia Google Classroom (Google, n.d.) to maintain a record of group activities. Each group utilized a single computer to access the chatbot, thereby facilitating collaborative discussion of their research project based on GenAI’s responses. The learning support tool was a customized GPT mode l (OpenAI, 2023) based on ChatGPT (OpenAI, n.d.a) named “Inquiry Support AI. ” Developed by educators and researchers specifically for this instructional context, it had three primary objectives: (1) collect data on learners’ inquiry processes, (2) facilitate and enhance inquiry activities while refining RQs, and (3) organize and tabulate collected data. The AI was programmatically constrained (OpenAI, n.d.b) from: (1) provide explicit ideas or proposals for  research, experiments, or surveys,

(2) generate reports or materials for students, (3) fabricate experimental or survey results, (4) autonomously

determine inquiry processes or conclusions, and (5) create survey instruments or specific questionnaires. The tool’s response format was designed to be concise a nd precise, reflecting contemporary communication s tyles among youths (Pae, 2020).

Dataset Data were collected from a lesson at a Japanese pub lic high school, with approval from the Ethics Comm ittee at the second author ’s institution. The activity involved 280 students in 111 groups, e ach with 2–5 students. After examining all shared files, we identified 34 groups with valid interactions between students and the GenAI-based learning support tool. Excluded files included blank documents, those containing only tables, and those containing only research proposal drafts. This study focused on user-GenAI interactions, excluding files containing only GenAI-generated messages. The final dataset comprised 25 groups’ discourse data, totaling 195 lines of interaction.

Analysis approach To address RQ1, user and GenAI messages were analyzed from two perspectives: response and content. We specifically performed qualitative data analysis  using five codes: Thought, Request, Answer, Advice,  and Information (Table 1), which categorized user and GenAI messages. The first author, who designed the learning support tool, created these codes by analyzing the data, assigned them, and conducted network analysis. Both users’ and GenAI’s messages were constructed with m ultiple sentences. Hence, a single message might contain several codes.

Epistemic network analysis (ENA) and ordered networ k analysis (ONA), which analyze code responses, are widely used in the field of learning  sciences.  ENA generates an undirected network graph representing the co-occurrence relationships of codes on a two-dimensional plane (Shaffer, 2017). In addition, ONA visualizes the frequency of response directions  as a directed graph, utilizing  chevrons and the shading of links (Tan et al., 2022). Recent CSCL research uti lized ENA (e.g., Ohsaki & Kasai, 2024; Özbek et al., 2024). However, because this study utilized different codes for each user and GenAI, it was unnecessary to calculate the direction between codes and represent their co-occurrence relationships in a two-dimensional plane. Furthermore, we had to visualize responses between the users’ and GenAI’s messages (directed network graph) and the  cooccurrence of each message’s content (undirected ne twork graph) in the same space to observe conversat ion patterns. As illustrated in Figure 1, co-occurrence relationships were calculated using a window size of one for the undirected graph and two for the directed graph.

To address RQ2, we analyzed all GenAI responses to user messages by calculating character counts. Furthermore, we excluded formatted tables in the GenAI’s message s due to their automatic generation and lack of relevance to discourse redundancy.

## Results and discussion

Regarding RQ1, Figure 2 presents the network analysis results of user-GenAI messages. Orange and blue nodes represent user and GenAI message codes, respectively. In addition, black and blue-dotted edges represent directed and undirected graphs, respectively. Co-occurrence frequencies are displayed around each link, and GenAI’s responses to user requests (Request) were categorized into three types: Answer, Advice, and Information. GenAI provided 38 Answers, 18 instances of Advice, and 8 instances of Information. Among the answers, 19 included Advice, and 9 included Information. In response to user comments (Thought), GenAI pro vided Advice 13 times and Information twice. Notably, Request was included in Thought only twice, indicating that GenAI proactively offered Advice in 11 cases (approximately 85%) without explicit user requests. Table 2 illustrates an example of GenAI’s proactive advice.  The messages were originally written in Japanese and subsequently translated into English by the first author. The values in the brackets in Table 2 represent the character count of the origin al Japanese text.  Herein, the user shared their survey plan in response to a previous question. However, GenAI initiated comprehensive advice on questionnaire design, providing a detailed response of over 500 characters that required the user to scroll. This highlights GenAI’s tendency to offer inordinate support. For RQ2, the results of a quantitative analysis involving 85 user-GenAI interaction pairs revealed substantial differences in message lengths. GenAI’s messages averaged 436.3 characters (SD = 516.2), while user messages averaged 54.8 characters (SD = 127.3), ind icating that GenAI’s messages were approximately ei ght times longer  on average. Figure 3 presents the message length ratios for each user –GenAI interaction pair. GenAI’s messages were typically 2–6 times longer than the user’s messages (27 cases),  with one pair reaching a ratio of 163. Furthermore,  GenAI’s messages accounted for 89% of the total cha racter count, underscoring its significant contribution to message volume.

This study has two significant limitations. First, a potential sampling bias exists in the analyzed data due to the reduced final dataset, which comprised only 34 of the initial 111 participating groups. While the exact reasons for this data exclusion remain unclear, the  analyzed data likely represent the successful inte ractions of these groups following the teacher’s instructions. Future research should mitigate this bias  by incorporating a broader range of group data. Second, the study’s exclusive focus on GenAI–user interactions neglects the impact on group collaboration. Future studies should investigate how GenAI support affects students’ RQs and project plans to elucidate its effects on collaborative learning. Despite these limitations, this study significantly contributes to GenAI-supported collaborative learning research, both in terms of content and methodology. Although existing studies emphasize the importance of analyzing GenAI–human interactions in CSCL contexts (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023; Borge et al., 2024; Chu et al., 2024), this study extends the analysis to include group–GenAI interactions in collaborative settings. Our findings highlight two key features of GenAI support: its pr oactive assistance and the substantial volume of messages  it generates. Furthermore, this study proposes a novel quantitative analysis method for GenAI–student interactions. In the future, with advancements in LLM inference technology, pre-output control of response content and length is expected to become increasingly prevalent (e.g., Wu et al., 2024). Consequently, approaches such as this study, which attempt to confirm optimal interaction patterns quantitatively, will become increasingly significant. The insights obtained from this study  are valuable for analyzing GenAI –student interactions in education and developing practical GenAI-enhanced learning tools.
