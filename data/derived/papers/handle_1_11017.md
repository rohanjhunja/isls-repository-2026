# Exploring the Relationship Between Students’ Adherence to Active Learning and Self-Regulated Learning Processes

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite the effectiveness of active learning, STEM learners often face challenges in navigating diverse activities and resources. Addressing this, we investigated how students with varying adherence to high-structure active learning exhibited distinct SRL processes during think-aloud protocols. Using epistemic network analysis, we identified differences in students’ verbalized SRL processes between groups who differed in their level of adherence. Future work will be broadened to explore the relationship between student adherence and learning outcomes.

## Introduction

In STEM education, many instructors employ high-structure course design with active learning (Sathy & Hogan, 2022), an educational practice demanding students’ intentional engagement in learning activities for knowledge construction. Despite its effectiveness compared to traditional lectures (Freeman et al., 2014), STEM learners often struggle to navigate the abundant learning activities and digital resources  as they prepare for, attend, and review course lessons (Bernacki et al., 2020). In this context, students’ self-regulated learning (SRL; Schunk & Greene, 2018) ability plays a pivotal role in  academic success. Understanding how students self-regulate in the early stages of active learning STEM courses is crucial for providing support throughout their subsequent learning.

To address this, we explored whether students’ adherence to structured pre-class activities, which reflect their preparedness for in-class learning, measured through digital trace data, serves as a reasonable proxy for their SRL processes. Specifically, we ask ed: How do students with different adherence to high-structure active learning exhibit distinct SRL processes during think-aloud protocols?

## Research context

We utilized data from a technology-mediated introductory biology course in Spring 2020  at a southeastern US public university. Our data came from two sources: actual in-course behaviors via digital traces and SRL processing captured during a lab study that occurred during the course. The course employed high-structure course design with active learning across pre-class, in-class, and after-class phases. In each phase, students were assigned specific learning tasks to be completed within that  phase. Our focus was on the pre-class phase, where students were instructed to complete: (1) guided reading questions (GRQs) following reading the assigned chapter in their digital textbook, and then (2) a homework assignment. During the lab study, participants (N=42) were asked to verbalize their thought processes while engaging in a specific lesson from the course and they received a $75 gift card for their time upon completion.

Identifying high and low adherence student groups We extracted students’ time-stamped digital trace data to track students’  completion of two pre-class activities (i.e., GRQs and homework assignment). By comparing the submission times, we assessed  whether students completed these activities in the order recommended by instructors. Binary coding value s for adherence were cumulated for each lesson and then summed for  lessons 1 to 14, which covered the first half of the course. The average total adherence score over the period was 9.07 (Min = 0, Max = 14, SD = 3.92). We used an average split to cluster students: high adherence (above, 24 students) and low adherence (below, 18 students). Coding SRL processes during think-aloud protocols We incorporated a codebook adapted from  Greene et al.  (2015) to code students’ SRL processes during think-aloud protocols in the lab setting. Macro-level SRL codes included PLANNING (e.g., (re)stating learning sub-goals), MONITORING (e.g., monitoring understanding, with valence), DOMAIN-GENERAL STRATEGIES (e.g., re-reading), DOMAIN-SPECIFIC STRATEGIES (e.g., mathematical problem-solving), and ASSESSMENT STRATEGIES (e.g., ruling out answers). Finally, we coded READING to capture the occurrences of students reading learning contents, prompts, or tasks. For all codes, inter-rater reliability surpassed the threshold of adequate agreement (>80%). We then analyzed the coded verbal data using epistemic network analysis (ENA).

## Results

The ENA scatter plot (Figure 5, Panel A) indicated statistically significant differences in SRL processes between low and high adherence groups  (x̄  Low = 0.08, x̄  High = -0.06, t(37.42) = 2.14, p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.66). The subtraction (Panel D) of the mean ENA networks of both groups (Panels B and C) revealed the differences in the connections among the macro-level SRL codes. L ow adherence students (red network) exhibited more links between MONITORING and DOMAIN-GENERAL STRATEGIES, whereas high adherence students (blue network) exhibited more links between DOMAIN-SPECIFIC STRATEGIES and PLANNING. ## Discussion and future work

In this work, we demonstrated that low adherence students were more inclined to prioritize metacognitive judgement of their learning and employ domain-general strategies, whereas high adherence students were more likely to focus on task-oriented goals and utilize domain-specific strategies to attain these goals. These differences imply that, in this context, relatively sparse data regarding completion of pre-class activities in the instructed order could provide insights into students’ SRL processing in a STEM course with high-structure active learning, where students’ SRL skills significantly impact academic success. In future work, we aim to broaden the analytic scope to investigate how students with varying adherence  levels relate to their subsequent learning performances in an online learning setting and overall learning outcomes.
