# The Components to “Make it Your Own”: A Framework to Study Youth Agency via Modification in Science Center Demonstrations

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Science center demonstrations allow science to come alive on the floor through youth facilitators. However, they replicate presentations of epistemic hegemony in science that perpetuate inequitable access and identification with STEM. This paper centers youth agency, as studied through modification of science demonstrations, as a learning process with possible contribution to transforming epistemic practices in out-of-school science. In a science center where youth work as facilitators of science demonstrations, we present a framework for places of agency in science demonstrations: introduction of ideas, session scaffolding and framing, physical environment and materials, and incorporation of audience. We discuss how differences in these categories across cases led to unique affordances and constraints to modification and present implications for the design of facilitative science sessions and professional development for facilitators.

## Introduction and related work

In science centers, science demonstrations show pro mise as sites of interaction bridging eye-catching activities paired with explanations of scientific concepts and  audience engagement (Taylor, 1988) —but like other ISE activities, are criticized for reprod ucing “narrow and authoritative representations” th at “alienate people from non-dominant social groups” and perpetuate inequities in access to, identification with, and participation in STEM (Dawson, 2014, p. 227). One approach to combat these inequities is a push to define and design for youth agency in museums, inviting diverse knowledge and experiences toward epistemic practices that reshape ISE (Letourneau et al., 2024). This paper operationalizes agency as multifaceted, and relating to the “physical environment, social engagement, choice and auton omy, and relevance and empowerment” among constitue nts in museums (Letourneau et al, 2024). Facilitators of demonstra tions in informal science education (ISE) settings are often youth or young adults who hold relevant perspectives and experiences that could make science more inclusive for diverse audiences in museums (Letourneau, 2021). As  such, youth agency in science demonstrations prese nts “the possibility to intervene in, and transform the  meaning of, situated activities” in ISE toward bro adening epistemic perspectives and practices in STEM (Mäkitalo, 2016, p. 64). In this context, youth are both experts and learners of science and its sociocultural norms—their learning can be characterized as shifts in their agency in s cience demonstrations, and shapes and is shaped by the interactions and environments around them (Nasir & Hand, 2006). This study focuses on modification as an observable feature of agency across these four feat ures in application to youth staff facilitating dem onstrations, and explores what factors create or constrict the m odification. We study facili tative science for its potential to subvert portrayals of science on the museum floor to broaden accessibility, participation, and relevance of science for facilitating youth staff with varying understandings, identification, and comfort with science. We  pose the following question: What components of existing facilitative science se ssions in a science center provide or constrain opportunities for modification?

## Context

The study is set in a science center where local yo uth staff facilitate science demonstrations as part  of a career development program aimed at boosting youth agency while supporting visitor STEM learning.  Demonstrations used to be scripted, but in an institutional effort toward sharing authority, program managers shifted focus toward emphasizing broader scientific practices and habits  of mind, providing demonstration guides rather tha n scripts, and encouraging youth to create their own script —to ‘make it their own’ (Mohabir et al., 2021). Youth staffs’ experience ranges between three months and six years with the museum. Youths’ experience with each facilitative science session also ranges between two and six mon ths. Youth staff are referred to in the sing ular “they” to preserve anonymity.

In this context, facilitative science takes two forms: demonstrations and stage activities. Demonstrations entail multiple activities and more scientific conc epts, while stage activities focus on one activity and its related science concepts. In this study, we observe two sta ge activities and one demonstration. We chose cases  that present lines of contrast between the structural differences stage activities and demonstrations present, as well as between two stage activities.

Data and analytic procedures Data include observations, conversations with youth  facilitators following demonstrations, conversatio ns with adult training staff, and facilitative guides to cr oss-check how youth staff facilitated the session in re lation to guiding material. The lead author coded data deductively with two initial themes: affordance, constraint (Saldaña, 2011). From the coded excerpts, she deductively cod ed for facilitative actions with the following star ting points from extant literature on agency in museum contexts: introduction of ideas, materials selection, and responses to visitors' questions and interests (Letourneau et al., 2024). She wrote reflective memos from the coded excerpts to inductively generate child codes of what was modifi ed or constrained from modification across cases. S he grouped the child codes and used the groupings to inductively iterate on parent codes. Authors then corroborated and iterated codes in conversation. Finally, the le ad author coded observations with the resulting cod ing scheme (see Table 1) to surface specific features allowing and constraining modification across cases.

## Findings and discussion

In this section we present the derived parent and c hild codes as features of modification (see Table 1) across two stage activities (“Tiny Investigations” and “Air Cannon”) and one demonstration (“Cool Chemistry”). Tiny Investigations is a tool-based stage activity using a digital microscope wit h up to 960x magnification on everyday objects. Materials-wise, Tiny Investigations utilized a microscope, a box of items that facilitators could use the microscope on, and any o ther materials either from the pockets of themselve s or the crowd. The youth facilitator asked the audience for their observations on texture, color, structure, or anything that might seem surprising or unusual. Air Cannon is a s tage activity derived from another demonstration ca lled AirPlay, which explored various concepts around air. This stage activity introduces concepts of air pr essure and air vortexes, and visualizes the latter with a fog machine and using the air cannon to knock over a to wer of cups. Materials-wise, this stage activity involved an air cannon, a  fog machine, and paper and plastic cups. “Cool Chemistry” focuses on phases of matter in chemistry  and how they change through changes in heat energy. Materials-wise, Cool Chemistry utilizes  printed diagrams of molecules for different phases of matter, balloons, hot water in an electric kettle, plastic and glass beakers, a pot, a mallet, tongs, and a tank of liquid nitrogen. Youth staff added balloons to a pot with liquid nitrogen to demonstrate how adding or taking away heat from gasses can change the amount of space atoms and molecules take up. They then compared boiling water to liquid nitrogen to demonstrate how adding heat could cause liquids to boil regardless of being scalding hot or freezing c old. They then submerged a balloon in liquid nitrogen and smashed it with a mallet to demonstrate how removing heat could change its elastic properties. Finally, the youth f acilitator asked the audience to count down togethe r (“5, 4, 3, 2, 1!”) and added hot water to liquid nitrogen to demonstrate h ow quickly phases of matter could change with heat energy, creating an exciting “poof” that drew gasps and laughter from the audience. ## Conclusion, implications, and future work

This work presents implications for professional de velopment and structuring facilitative science for science educators in and beyond ISE. These emergent findings surface the strengths and challenges of certain formats and structures, and points to a close r look of what different session formats accomplish. Understanding the unique affordances and challenges point to possible future  iterations of sessions that leverage novel combina tions of affordances towards more agential formats for youth facilitators and their audiences. Experiment-based structures like Cool Chemistry present more linear experiences, with opportunities to solicit hypotheses from audience members, and check  those hypotheses after each activity. On the other  hand, sessions that involve activities that are tools or practice-based with malleable content like Tiny Investigatio ns, allow for an experience more along the terms of a makerspace. Such experiences may allow for more choice from both the youth facilitator and audience. These flex ible formats seem to require more expertise on the end of the youth, but it is possible to frame facilitative science sessions to also invite expertise from audience members, and to position youth as exploring inquiry along with a udience members. Incorporating some components of experimental-based formats, such as a set of possible hypotheses  and questions for the audience relating to tools or practices might allow for agential exploration of science practices/tools alongside collective hypothesizing. Furthermore, modifications from youth facilitators present a slew of practical expertise that can be compiled and folded into professional development for other youth staff and science educators. Encouraging youth staff to observe their peers’ strategies, and creating a toolkit of effective practices can allow for a more systematic sharing of this expertise across youth facilitators. Furthermore, we can consider how to design formats or features of facilitative science sessions that invite audiences to guide inquiry—and how this could manifest across a variety of session formats. All sessions presented unique a ffordances for inviting audience input, but this di ffered depending on whether the goal was content learning or engaging in a scientific practice. A practical tension in this process is designing around audience expectations o f demonstrations: namely, a visitor’s expectation t o receive knowledge from a demonstration rather than participate in co-constructing it. Future work can consider this factor to design a session that incorporates audience asks and  scaffolding/framing based on what constitutes welc ome participation from visitors’ perspectives.

In science learning formats that inherently privilege authoritative scientific presentation,  understanding where facilitative science can be modified and enco uraging agency across different formats presents de sign possibilities toward more inclusive practices and presentations of science in informal space s—for diverse youth staff and their audiences, both of whom comprise fu ture generations entering STEM. The next step of th is work is to incorporate cultural practices and ways of kn owing through the outlined agential components in facilitative science through participatory design with youth sta ff and families, toward co-creating portrayals of science in out-of-school spaces that span beyond narrow, historicized  representations that continue to perpetuate in equities in access, identification, and participation.
