# Diagnose Academic Emotions from Facial Expressions: Relationship with Science Learning Performance in Web-Based Self-directed Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study delves into elementary school students emotional engagement in web-based, self-directed science inquiry. Through the lens of Facial Action Coding System (FACS) analysis across 96 video recordings of 53 students, we investigate the prevalence and patterns of emotions exhibited during three distinct science experiments. We seek to understand the emotional combinations and how these affective states transition in relation to academic performance. Our methodology includes using z-score of each type of emotion (happiness, surprise, sadness, neutral) as threshold to discern the primary 12 emotion combinations. Using Markov Chain sequential pattern analysis, we found notable differences in emotional transitions between higher and lower-performing students, indicating a potential link between emotional trajectories and learning outcomes. High performers appear to have more positive emotional experiences, as indicated by transitions to 'happiness.' In contrast, low performers might keep staying at negative emotions. These differences may impact the learning effectiveness.

## Introduction

Web-based platforms are increasingly used to support self-directed science learning by offering interactive environments that foster independent inquiry (Linn et al., 2003). However, they also pose challenges i n personalizing instruction and supporting students w ith low self-efficacy or poor self-regulation, who may disengage or exhibit gaming behaviors (Gerard et al., 2022; Baker et al., 2013). Emotions play a criti cal role in these challenges, shaping students’ engagement, eff ort regulation, and persistence (Pekrun, 2006). To design emotionally responsive learning environments, we mu st better understand how emotions influence learnin g processes and outcomes.

Ekman’s Basic Emotion Theory offers a framework for  examining how discrete emotions —such as happiness, sadness, and surprise —affect behavior and learning (Ekman, 1992). These e motions serve adaptive functions and can be measured using tools like the Facial Action Coding System (Ekman & Friesen, 1976). Emotions guide attention, motivation, and decision-making, making this theory especially relevant to self-directed science learning (Pekrun & Linnerbrink-Garcia, 2012; Camacho-Morles et al., 2021). Positive emotions enhance engagement and persistenc e, while negative emotions can signal frustration or disengagement (Pekrun, 2006). Surprise may indicate cognitive conflict or discovery, promoting curiosity and deeper learning (D’Mello & Graesser, 2012). Yet emo tions often co-occur and fluctuate, reflecting the complex nature of affect (Forgas, 1995). The Affect Infusio n Model (AIM)  suggests that mood can influence cognitive processing. It’s important to examine how combinations and transitions of emotions impact learning. Despite growing interest, there is limited research on applying AI-based facial expression recognition in real-world educational settings (Kingsley et al., 2022). While such tools candetect discrete  emotions, their potential to capture dynamic emotional changes duri ng learning —especially in self-directed environments — remains underexplored (Lu & Luo, 2023). These setti ngs, with minimal teacher support, require students  to selfregulate, persist, and manage emotions. Understandi ng emotional transitions, such as shifts from frust ration to curiosity, can reveal key mechanisms of cognitive and emotional regulation. This study analyzes emotional patterns and transitions during self-directed science inquiry to inform the design of emotionally adaptive learning systems. It addresses the following research questions: RQ1: Which emotion combinations are predominantly expressed by students during self-directed science learning tasks?

RQ2: Which emotion combination can significantly predict students’ post-test score? RQ3: What are the differences in emotion combination frequency between high and low performance students?

RQ4: What emotion combination transition patterns emerge among high and low performance students?

## Materials and methods

The participants were 53 fifth-grade students (31 male students and 22 female students) from an elementary school located in Inner Mongolia, China. All participants were of the same age group and Chinese ethnicity. The learning context was an interactive, self-directed science inquiry using the Web-based Inquiry Science Environment (WISE; Linn et al., 2003). Students explored whether the same material could insulate both hot and cold drinks by forming hypotheses, designin g experiments, running NetLogo simulations  (Figure 1), interpreting charts, and drawing conclusions. They completed three experiments: Hot Drink, Cold Drink,  and Mixed conditions.

Figure 1 Science Inquiry Activity on WISE We analyzed 96 student videos from three science inquiry tasks using the Facial Action Coding System (FACS; Ekman & Friesen, 1978), a validated method f or detecting emotions through facial muscle movemen ts. Emotions were classified every five seconds across 55,956 frames, with each frame coded for anger, disgust, fear, sadness, happiness, surprise, and neutral (Ekman et al., 2002). To answer the first research question regarding the  prevalent emotional combinations, the following methodological steps were taken:

1. Initial Emotion Data Analysis: We began by calculating the mean values for the seven recorded

emotions. Due to the infrequent appearance of anger, disgust, and fear, we excluded them from the subsequent analysis to focus on the more commonly occurring emotions.

2. Calculation of Z-Scores: Z-scores were computed for each emotion per student to standardize

emotional intensity. Only emotions with z-scores above zero—indicating above-average expression— were retained.

3. Identification of Emotion Combinations: Emotions were ranked by z-score at each frame. The highest

was labeled the primary emotion, followed by secondary emotions if applicable. a. 4. Frequency Calculation: We calculated the frequency of each emotion combination across frames and

included only those appearing in over 2% of frames for further analysis, highlighting the most common emotional experiences during learning.

To address RQ2 on the impact of emotion combination s on performance, we aggregated frame-level emotion data to the student level and linked it wit h test scores. Logarithmic transformation was applied  to normalize the data, and stepwise linear regression was conducted, controlling for gender and pre-test scores. For RQ3, ANCOVA was used to compare emotion combina tions between high-and low-performing students, controlling for video duration. For RQ4, we employed a Markov chain model to examine emotion al transition patterns. This model assumes that future  emotional states depend only on the current state, making it well-suited for capturing moment-to-moment shifts. Students were grouped by performance  on the simulation task, allowing us to compare emotional transition patterns across groups.

## Discussion

This study examined how emotion combinations influe nce self-directed science learning, focusing on their prevalence, links to performance, and differences between high and low-performing students. Students displayed a range of emotions —neutral, surprise, sadness, happiness —and complex combinations like “neutral, sadness” and “happiness, surprise,” reflecting the emotional ly demanding nature of minimally guided tasks. Inte restingly, the “neutral, sadness” combination positively predi cted post-test scores, su ggesting that moderate negative emotions, when balanced, may support reflective and productive engagement (D’Mello & Graesser, 2012). High performers showed fewer less adaptive combinations (e.g., “sadness, neutral”), indicating stronger emo tional regulation, while low performers more often cycled through neutral or negative states —patterns consistent with self-regulated learning theories that highlight the importance of emotional control (Zimmerman & Schunk, 2011).
