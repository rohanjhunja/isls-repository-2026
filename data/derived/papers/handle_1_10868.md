# Academic Buoyancy and Fear of Failure Differences Among University Students: Null Findings

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Academic buoyancy refers to a student's ability to successfully deal with academic-related setbacks and challenges that are typical in the ordinary course of school life. The current study investigated the relation between academic buoyancy and fear of failure and examined the potential differences in academic buoyancy and fear of failure among university students (N =118). Results indicated there was insufficient evidence of differences in academic buoyancy and fear of failure when considering other individual characteristics, such as gender, college-generational status, native language, college-attending status, and racial backgrounds. Further, most students experience similar levels of academic buoyancy.

## Background

Academic buoyancy refers to students' ability to handle day-to-day learning-related challenges and setbacks, such as dips in motivation, low exam grades, and receiving negative feedback (Martin & Marsh, 2008; Putwain et al., 2023). On the other hand, fear of failure is also known as fear avoidance, which is also known as the tendency for students to be uncertain about their ability to avo id failures and achieve success  (Conroy et al., 2002; Martin & Marsh, 2003). The present research study is guided by Fischer's dynamic skill theory, which suggests that personal traits, environment, self, and others are all interconnected elements that play a role in an individual's lifelong skill development (Lerner, 2018; Sigelman & Rider, 2018). In the present study, we expanded upon previous research by examining academic buoyancy and fear of failure differences among university students. To address g aps in the research, we developed the following hypotheses: H1: Academic buoyancy is associated with fear of failure, H2: There a difference in academic buoyancy levels among university students considering their gender, collegegenerational status, native language, college-attending status, and racial background, and H3: There is a difference in fear of failure levels among university students  based on their gender, college-generational status, native language, college-attending status, and racial background.

## Method

The current data came from a cross-sectional online research study. The study was reviewed and approved by the University of North Texas’s Institutional Review Board (IRB-22-831). Participants have been recruited from Education undergraduate and graduate classes at a p ublic Texas university in Spring 2023. To qualify for the study, participants must be 18 years of age or olde r and current college students in the United States. The questionnaire measures included: (1) a demographic questionnaire, (2) the Academi c Buoyancy Scale (Martin & Marsh, 2008), the Performance Failure Appraisal Inventory, Short-Form (Conroy et al., 2002).

## Results

A Spearman correlation coefficient was computed to assess the linear relation between academic buoyancy and fear of failure. Results indicated there was a stat istically significant negative correlation between academic buoyancy and fear of failure r(120) = -.437, p <.001, CI (-.570, -.281). Results for our independent sample t-tests and ANOVAs suggest that fear of failure may be part icularly relevant for all students regardless of ge nder, college-generational status, native language, college-attending stat us, and racial background.  Further, most students are likely to reported similar levels of a cademic buoyancy. However, on average, men reported  to have higher academic buoyancy than women, and this diffe rence was statistically significant. See Table 1 fo r all independent t-test and ANOVA results.

## Discussion

Findings extend the limited and mixed literature on academic buoyancy and fear of failure. However, these results must be interpreted with caution due to its cross-sectional nature and recruitment period (i.e., after the semester’s midpoint). Further, we employed convenience sampling and mainl y recruited participants from  Education department’s classes. Thus, findings may not be generalizable to studen ts from other disciplines.  Thus, future research should consider replicating the study at v arious time points in the regular and summer semest ers and include students from various schools and fields of  study to  see if the findings are consistent with the current findings.
