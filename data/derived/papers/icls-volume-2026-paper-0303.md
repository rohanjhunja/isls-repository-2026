# Building Trust in Scientific Reviews: Challenges and Insights from High School Science

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This exploratory study investigates how ninth-grade students evaluate scientific sources presenting varying forms of evidence. Grounded in the Grasp of Evidence framework, the study examines students’ epistemic ideals and reliable processes when appraising evidentiary quality. While students’ quantitative ratings showed minimal change following an instructional intervention, their justifications revealed emerging understandings of evidence integration and synthesis. The findings inform approaches for resear ch and i nstruction that develop learners’ capacity to critically evaluate evidence in real-world scientific contexts.

In today’s digital landscape, people are inundated with an unprecedented volume of information from social media platforms, news outlets, and online forums. Ongoing public debates about scientifically settled issues, like the safety of vaccines or the real ity of anthropogenic climate change, underscore a troubling epistemic gap: many laypeople struggle to differentiate between well-justified scientific claims and claims that merely appear credible (Cooke,2018; Flew, 2021, O’Conner & Weatherall,2019; Osborne,2023). This difficulty reveals a broader failure within current science education to cultivate the epistemic competencies necessary to navigate the current “post-truth” world (Barzali & Chinn, 2018; Chinn et al., 2020;2021). To develop scientifically literate laypeople, learners acquire a grasp of evidence. According to the Grasp of Evidence framework (Duncan et al., 2018), skillful reasoning with evidence includes five dimensions, one of which is the dimension of integration.  Evidence integration encompasses coordinating large, diverse bodies of evidence and employing systematic processes to evaluate multiple pieces of evidence. This dimension emphasizes the value of converging lines of evidence, emphasizing that multiple independent yet consistent sources strengthen scientific claims. Scientists must coordinate diverse evidence, evaluate each source individually, and reason about the relationships among them to develop reliable conclusions. (Duncan et al., 2018). The dimensions are further unpacked in terms of (Barzali & Chin,2018; Chinn et al., 2014): Aims  (e.g., having the goal to reach an accurate and the consensus  of experts as criteria for determining whether an accurate conclusion has been reached), Reliable processes  (RPs) that are used to achieve the aims (e.g., evaluating multiple sources of information, evaluating the expertise of sources).

Preliminary research suggests that science curricula often overlook the integration dimension (Chinn et al., 2023). To counter susceptibility to single lines of evidence, curricula should equip students to engage with the full complexity of diverse and converging sources. Therefore, in our exploratory study, we seek to investigate how students appraise and justify different sources, which present varying forms of evidence, including articles that reference single studies, scientific reviews, and personal experiences. We seek to uncover:

1) How do students evaluate high-quality evidence (e.g. reviews) versus other evidence types (e.g. singular

studies and personal anecdotes)?

2) To what extent does participation in our intervention promote shifts in their evaluation of the sources?

## Context

This study is part of a larger NSF-funded project aimed at developing teachers ’ and students’ apt epistemic performance in science classrooms (Chinn et al., 2023; Yoon et al., 2023). Apt epistemic performance is successful epistemic performance (e.g., reaching accurate conclusions about what counts as scientific evidence) achieved through competence (e.g., one’s ability to evaluate the evidence, identify scientific consensus, and integrate the relevant information) (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018; Chinn et al., 2021). 2006 In this particular study, we focus on a pre and post-assessment given before and after students participated in a 7-day nature of science unit titled the Epidemic Unit and a 90-minute learning environment titled Fuel or Fad: The Science Behind Energy Drinks (the Energy unit), which followed the Epidemic unit and served as a performance assessment of students’ epistemic understanding. The Epidemic unit featured a game-like, agentbased simulation (Yoon et al., 2016; 2017) where student groups conducted exper iments and gathered data to determine the most effective mitigation strategies for controlling an outbreak in a simulated town. This unit aimed to develop apt epistemic performances—including understanding peer review, evaluating evidentiary quality and quantity, and using robust evidence to construct models—through inquiry-based investigations of socioscientific issues. The Energy Drink unit aimed to develop students’ lay reasoning by emphasizing claims supported by relevant, high-quality, and sufficient evidence. Centered on the socioscientific issue of energy drinks and mental health, the task promoted authentic engagement by situating reasoning in a realistic context. Students were guided to distinguish stronger evidence (e.g., reviews) from weaker forms (e.g., single studies or anecdotes) using sources modeled after real-world media (e.g., social media posts, podcasts). They ranked each source’s credibility before and after an instructional video explaining how scientific reviews synthesize high-quality evidence to reach wellsupported conclusions.

## Participants

The study was conducted during the 2024– 2025 academic year with 12 ninth-grade students enrolled in two biology classes, an accelerated and an academic placement, taught by the same instructor at a public high school in north-central New Jersey. Six students were randomly selected from each class to participate in pre-and postinterviews and to be followed throughout the intervention. Of the 12 participants, four identified as boys and eight as girls. The classes were selected because their teacher, a long-term research collaborator, demonstrated strong alignment with the project’s instructional goals. Including students from both placement levels helped mitigate potential bias related to differences in academic achievement or motivation. Data sources and analysis Pre-and post-assessments were administered through semi-structured interviews. Each student participated in an approximately one-hour interview before the intervention and again two weeks following its completion. The interviews were designed to examine students’ ability to transfer epistemic performances developed during the intervention to a different “real-world” context, specifically, evaluating the safety and efficacy of low-carbohydrate diets. A standardized interview protocol was employed across all  sessions, and all interviewers received extensive training to ensure consistent implementation and high procedural fidelity. During the interviews, students were presented with three short articles that varied in the type and quality of evidence provided, including single empirical studies, review studies, and anecdotal claims (see Table 1). Students responded to the same set of questions in both the pre-and post-interviews. All interviews were conducted via Zoom, and the recorded sessions were transcribed verbatim for subsequent data analysis. Ultimately, students’ ideals and RPs were analyzed based on students' justifications in the interview. (Students seldom mentioned aims explicitly, so we did not analyze them here.) Ideals and RPs were coded using both top-down and bottom-up methods.

Changes in students’ ratings To examine changes in students’ evaluations of the three articles from pretest to posttest, paired-samples t-tests were conducted. A paired t-test was selected because the same group of students provided ratings at both time points, allowing for the assessment of within-subject differences in their judgments before and after the instructional intervention. Results indicated no statistically significant differences in students’ ratings of the three articles across time. For Article 1, the mean rating shifted  from pretest (M = 5.17) to posttest (M = 4.67), t(11) = 1.48, p =.166. For Article 2, the mean score changed from pretest (M = 5.58) to posttest (M = 5.17), t(11) = 1.10, p =.295. For Article 3, ratings showed a marginal decline from pretest (M = 4.42) to posttest (M = 3.58), t(11) = 1.84, p =.093. Overall, the statistical results did not show significant meandifferences. To examine changes in students’ evaluations of the most convincing article from pre-to post-intervention, we compared the proportio n of students who selected Article 2 as the best source. At pretest, 6 of 12 students (50%) identified Article 2 as the most convincing, increasing to 8 of 12 students (67%) at posttest. This reflects a 17% gain in students, recognizing Article 2 as the strongest source of evidence following the intervention—the change suggesting a non-significant shift in students’ evaluation of evidence credibility over time. Insights and challenges presented in justifications Students’ justifications for their source rankings and their reasoning regarding which article presented the strongest evidence provided particularly valuable insights, revealing the ideals and evaluative processes they prioritize when appraising the reliability of sources. Since the pre-and post-scores were not significantly different, for the qualitative analysis, we merged the justifications to present a portrait of the students’ reasoning as they evaluated the sources. The most prominent theme that emerged from the data was that students were valuing the mere presence of “numbers” or “details” over the number of studies as their ideals when evaluating the sources. Similarly, a student reasoned that a review study should include its own control group or viewed the inclusion of many experiments as potentially complicating interpretation. The students’ reasoning suggests an emerging awareness of how messy it is when scientists are synthesizing multiple studies, offering an opportunity to further emphasize how converging different experimental Despite the small sample size, this exploratory study provides insight into how learners evaluate scientific sources and the reasoning guiding their judgments. While students’ article ratings showed little change post-intervention, their justifications revealed the portrait of epistemic ideals and reliable processes they prioritize when evaluating sources with varying levels of evidence. These findings are significant for both researchers and educators, as they highlight how students’ existing reasoning can serve as productive starting points for instruction. This work points to the importance of building upon students’ emerging epistemic ideals, such as valuing numerical data or explicit detail, and guiding them toward recognizing that methodological rigor, evidence synthesis, and consensus formation contribute to scientific credibility. For the research community, these results contribute to a growing body of work on how learners develop epistemic competence in evaluating evidence and reasoning about complex socioscientific issues (Chinn et al., 2020; Duncan et al., 2018; Osborne & Allchin, 2024). For teachers, the explicit by helping students see how scientists coordinate diverse findings to construct reliable knowledge. Embedding opportunities for students to analyze scientific reviews, weigh competing forms of evidence, and reflect on the formation of scientific consensus, science education can m ore effectively cultivate the epistemic practices needed to navigate this “post-truth” world.
