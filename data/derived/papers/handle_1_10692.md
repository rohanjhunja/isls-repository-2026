# Representing Students: Teachers’ Conversational Stories in Workgroups

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Programs to support teachers’ learning often rely heavily on representations of classroom practice to afford opportunities for teachers to learn. Among the various forms of representation, teachers’ stories about their classroom practice have received little attention relative to their ubiquity in teacher workgroup conversations. This study focuses on teachers’ stories of their classroom practice and analyzes at what degree of generality those stories represented students as actors. It finds that students were most represented at the aggregate level of the class. These findings suggest that studying teachers’ stories as representations of practice can help us understand what aspects of practice are made visible in these stories.

Narratives as representations of teaching practice Representations of practice are an important part of teachers’ pre-service and in-service learning (Grossman, et al. 2009; Hall & Horn, 2012). This is because many designed settings for teacher learning are at some temporal or spatial distance from the act of teaching and re presentations extend the “horizon of observation” t o allow for learning at this distance (Hutchins, 1996, p. 52; Little, 2003). Thus, understanding the representations of practice that teachers are relying on is central to understanding teachers’ possibilities for learning. Of the various ways teachers go about representing practice for learning purposes, one important medium of representation has been far less explicitly studied until recently: narrative. This is somewhat surprising because brief narratives of teachers’ own practice are often the most readily available and easy to produce representations (Hall & Horn, 2012; Pulvermacher & Lefstein, 2016). A recent study (Segal, 2019) showed that teachers s till relied heavily on stories as representations of pra ctice to support their discussions even when other representations, such as classroom video, were read ily available. Thus, teachers’ use of narrative to represent practice to each other is a ripe area for study because of the ubiquity of stories in their conversations. Studies of narrative in talk have moved in recent y ears from an emphasis on highly structured, uninterrupted monologues to a focus on shorter, mor e flexible narratives, called small stories (Bamberg & Gorgakopoulou, 2008). The stories that teachers tell each other about their practice can be characterized as small stories (Pulvermacher and Lefstein, 2016). Research ers have taken a variety of approaches to understanding and defining these stories. Horn (2010) focused specifically on how teachers’ stories related to past and future teaching practices and how the y supported teachers’ learning through recontextualiz ation. Others have focused on how teachers use stories during conversations to create identities for themselves, their schools, and their students (Downey, 2015; Yoon, 2016).  However, two recent studies, which serve as the ins piration for this paper, cast a wider net to begin making sense of the breadth of ways teachers use narratives to represent practice to each other. These two studies drew on  recordings of  Israeli teachers to analyze those teachers’ use of stories (Pulvermacher and Lefstein, 2016; Segal, 2019).  Both studies coded the stories by content and how t hey were taken up, but Segal (2019) also included significant qualitative analysis of a segment of conversation that included several stories. In this analysis, Segal characterized in more detail what each included story represented, including describing how specifically or generally teaching practice was represented. Purpose Building on the existing literature, this paper see ks to analyze a corpus of stories from a teacher le arning setting that is significantly different from Pulvermacher and Lefstein (2016) and Segal (2019). Thus, this study will first seek to confirm whether narratives are as prevalent in a different setting. I will then move beyond current analyses by developing a framework to show how one aspect of practice was represented in these stories. Specifically, drawing on a concept from institutional theory, I will analyze at what degree of generality students are represented in the stories (Hwang & Colyvas, 2020). Understanding at what degree of generality students are represented will reveal what kinds of entities of students are publicly highlighted in teachers’ discussions, showing what resources are made available to support changes in practice. Thus, this study will seek to answer the following question: At what degree of generality do stories told in these workgroup meetings represent students?

## Methods

To carry out this analysis, I use data from a two-year, video-based professional development program for cohorts of early-career high school mathematics teachers from across the Un ited States. This analysis focuses primarily on a part of the program where teachers met together in groups of 3-4 using a videoconferencing platform during each school year to share videos of their teaching practice and engage in discussion to support improv ement in their teaching practice. Group meetings were carefully facilitated  by an instructional coach. The centerpiece of these meetings was a set of 20-minute discussions, with each discussion focusing o n a video recording of classroom practice from a different teacher participant. The discussions followed a consistent pattern. Some days prior to the group meeting, each teacher shared a video of their teaching practice in their own cla ssroom. The other teachers in the group and the fac ilitator then individually watched the video and left publicly visible, open-ended tags. When the group met for discussion, the video-sharing teacher first answered any context questions from other group mem bers, but the group did not directly review the video recording together. The group then shared strengths or highlights from the video  and discussed a question about the video posed by the v ideo sharer. While the times dedicated to each part of th e discussion varied somewhat, typically most of the time was spent discussing the video-sharer’s question. For this analysis, I draw on video recordings and transcripts of three randomly select ed group meetings from the first year of the prog ram. The transcripts were coded to identify instances of teachers’ narratives of teaching practice. Following Segal (2019:4), I define a story as “a re counting of a past or hypothetical event as unfolding action, with an element of specificity (r egarding time, place, and/or actors, or including a  replay of enacted or imagined speech).” However, unlike Segal, I include teachers’ recounting of events they viewed in the video for the conversation  because the video itself was not played for the  whole group. Once the corpus of narratives was created, the narratives were coded w ith the name and role of the participant sharing th em as well as the section of the meeting in which the narrative took place. The narratives were then coded using an iterative, inductive process to identify how students were represented in them. When students were represented in multiple ways during the same story, the story was double coded.

## Findings

The coding process captured 101 examples of narrati ves. Of these, 5 3 represented students at the class level, meaning that students were presented as a collectiv e entity of a school class;  38 at the individual student level, meaning that students were represented as acting individually; 29 at the general level, meaning that students were represented abstractly in a way that referred to al l students;  11 at the within-class group level,  meaning that students were represented collectively in small groups; 5 at the social category level, meaning that students were represented as social groups larger than a class such as age, racial identity, etc.; 4 represented the teller as student, meaning that the teller recounted their own experie nce as a student or hypothetically placed themselve s in a student perspective; and 5 did not represent students at all.  Stories were most common during the Strengths and Highlights and Question Discussion portions of the meetings. Story examples and analysis The degree of generality at which students were most often represented in stories was at the collective level of the class. Thus, the stories presented actions as being taken by the class as a whole or attributing attitudes or feelings to the entire class. For example, one teacher, Reina (all names are pseudonyms), asked the group a question about how she could better record students’ thoughts or c omments to represent their ideas on the whiteboard.  Aaron, another teacher, suggested that she could have the students write down their own ideas, then Reina told this story: Reina: In the beginning, when we did it, I was letting the m write it down on whiteboards. We have little whiteboards, just so I could see what they were doing, and get an idea of what the different strategies were. Then, I took it away pretty quickly, because I wanted it to really be more of a mental math thing, than them writing it down thing. When they write it down, they instantly want to put it in a calculator, and I really do have a pretty open calculator policy of like, “You alway s have one, so you should probably use it, if you have.” This is a little bit different, I want you to really think about what percents are, but, yes, I like that. Reina’s main purpose in telling this story seems to be signaling agreement with Aaron’s idea while also explaining why she did not use that approach in the  particular circumstance of the class activity that  she had recorded and sent to the group. Reina’s temporal pl acement of the story (“In the beginning, when we di d it”) indicates that she is referring to a specific event with a particular group of students rather than students in general. Throughout the story, Reina alternated between including herself in the class (“We have li ttle whiteboards”) and separating herself from the students (“So I could s ee what they were doing”), but the students were always portrayed in collective terms. Once Reina narrated the events of using and then taking away the whiteboards, she declared the reason she decided to take away the whiteboards  in this instance, then explained why calculators were not helpful in this situation. Thus, in this story Reina claimed that her class of students shares a desire to use calculators after writing numbers down and that she altered her instructional actions because of this. In another instance, Garima, the instructional coac h, asked Jesse, a teacher,  to provide a strength or highlight from a video Wesley, another teacher, had shared and Jesse said the following: Jesse: I already said it, like the hunger that your kids have and how eager and willing they are to go up and actually offer anythi ng, no matter how incomplete or tentative they've got of an idea, it's really cool that they're just tearing it apart. One of the ot her things that I noticed that you do well, that I think is great is for whatever reason and I think Garima left a similar comment, they keep coming back to, "Okay,  so what's the answer?" They keep coming back to it and you just keep deflecting like, "Oh, I don't know, we're still figuring it out." You jus t keep avoiding validating answers or even focusing on the right or  wrong answer and just bringing up as many possible strategies as kid s can come up with and recognizing all of their different ways of try ing to solve that problem, which is really cool.

In this story, Jesse has been specifically asked to  refer to the video and describe positive features of the events recorded in it. Thus, when Jesse said “your kids” it can be taken to be referring to the students Wesley was teaching in the video, again abstracting to the lev el of a class being taught. Throughout this narrati ve, Jesse continued to represent students in this collective way, descr ibing first their general “hunger” and willingness to share ideas, then describing in slightly more detail a repeated interaction where the students asked Wesley for the answer to a question and he refused to provide it. Thus, similar to Reina in the first story, Jesse he re presents a willingness to engage as shared by the entire class  as well as providing an action that is attributed to the class as a whole (asking for the answer) and portrays Wesley as acting in response to this collectivity. There were also a significant number of stories in which individual students were represented.  In the following story, Bernard, a teacher, had asked how he could do better supporting students to talk directly to each other during discussions instead of having to mediate and prompt other students for responses. Garima responded by narrating and commenting on an interaction from the video Bernard had shared: Garima: Bernard, there was one moment in your video that I noticed, I think I put a tag on it. Where, I can't remember the names, the student's name, said something and you were like, "Can anybody else  explain what he said?" Which was a fantastic move for getting kids to talk about each other’s ideas. Then another kid said something and you were about to ask the first kid. "Did he say it right?" Then you stopped yourself and repeated what the other kid had said, and I was lik e, "No, why didn’t you just ask the othe r kid if it was right?" Because that's building tha t listening. Then eventually you'll be able to step back more and the other kid would be like, "No, that wasn't what I said or yes, that was what I said." In this story, Garima places the main thrust of the  action in the hands of two individual students and Bernard. Garima principally identifies the individual students by their actions (saying something) rather than with names or other features. However, here the students are not framed as a collective class but are instead individual actors in their own right. While Garima does not claim to quote the students’ words, Bernard’s words are directed at these individual students rather than at the  whole class. At one point, Garima suggests a generalization about Bernard’s actions (“a fantastic move for getting ki ds to talk about each other’s ideas”) but this generalization is not an appeal to a larger set of students, but to a  set of teacher “moves.” While this statement does mention an abstract, generic group of “kids,” the main goal of  the story seems to be showing how a generally good  practice functioned in an interaction with these two particu lar students. In the end, then, this story from Garima treats students as individuals in a way that none of the other stories highlighted in this section have done.

## Discussion

This study analyzed a corpus of stories from teache r workgroup conversations. This set of stories came  from a teacher learning context that was substantially dif ferent from what others who have carried out simila r studies have focused on. The high frequency of stories supports the general c onclusion from other studies that narrative representations of practice are very common, even when other kinds of representations (such as classroom video) are directly available. Read together with the growing literature  on this topic, this finding means that continued study of narrative representations of practice is v ery warranted across professional development conte xts. Understanding what kinds of stories are being told and heard in teacher learning settings will cont inue to illuminate what kinds of learning are taking place and are possible in those settings. This study also found and described several differe nt degrees of generality at which students were represented in narratives about teaching practice. In this dataset, students were most commonly represented at the level of the school class, with individual  students and generalizations about all students bei ng the next most common categories. This suggests that these are the ways that students were made available to the participants in these discussions and that any learning that occurred revolved around perceiving and interacting with students at these levels, though it is well beyond the scope of this study to discover to what extent such learning took place. The approach developed here to analyzing how student s are represented in these stories can serve as a model for investigating how other aspects of practi ce might be represented in teachers’ stories. Furth er studies should also investigate how more specific interactional contexts influence how students are represented in stories. Finally, future research should take up the issue described at the end of the previous paragraph: how do different representations of students lead to different kinds of opportunities for teacher learning and how do teachers act on those opportunities? All these lines of inquiry cou ld produce fruitful insights into how teachers lear n and how to better design settings to support that learning.
