# Queer and Trans Theories as Praxis: Analyzing Power and Designing for Learning Toward Justice

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This structured poster symposium, co-chaired by an intergenerational group of scholars, brings together scholars whose work engages queer and trans theories as praxis. Amid intensifying political attacks, we foreground the historical persistence of queer and trans knowledge-making and centering futures in which learning is reimagined toward justice and livability. The contributions examine diverse contexts —including early childhood classrooms, community design spaces, nationalist and parental rights discourses, arts-based learning, and computational simulations —to show how queer and trans perspectives unsettle dominant definitions of learning, reveal the operations of ideology, and foreground desire, embodiment, and affect as vital dimensions of liberatory and justice-oriented learning. Collectively, the six papers demonstrate how queer and trans theories refuse normative closure and open possibilities for more expansive ways of knowing. By attending to the conditions that restrict educational dignity and the practices that envision otherwise, we underscore how learning might be oriented toward dignity, creativity, and collective flourishing.

At a moment when queer and trans communities face escalating political attacks, reflecting on how, across generations, queer and trans communities have resisted erasure, created knowledge, and nurtured life in the face of systemic harm is essential for our continued survival (Anzaldúa & Moraga, 1981; Combahee River Collective, 2014/1978). This symposium, co-chaired by an intergenerational group of scholars, begins with that historical truth, while looking toward the future, and centers on the necessity of historicizing dialogue. We believe that sustaining queer and trans futures requires spaces where we can share historical knowledge and current lived experiences to collectively imagine ways forward.

The contributions gathered in this structured poster symposium take up queer and trans theories as praxis—approaches that actively intervene in how learning is conceptualized, designed, and lived. Queerness and transness are not only categories of being but orientations toward the world that challenge normative logics and cultivate more just and livable futures (Ahmed, 2006). Extending further upon the ISLS 2025 Symposium, which centered queer and trans perspectives within the learning sciences (Uttamchandani et al., 2025), our proposed symposium focuses on methodological and design possibilities for queer and trans liberation, while also acknowledging the tensions inherent in conducting this work in the current political climate. Through the works of the co ntributing authors, queer and trans theories are taken up as praxis, animating resistant and imaginative practices that refuse normative closure. Our symposium illuminates the methodological and design possibilities grounded in queer and trans desire, bodies, and presence, while drawing on the diversity in methodologies, contexts, and disciplinary areas. The varied practices featured in our symposium collectively make visible the contingency of dominant systems and nurture possibilities for more just and livable futures. These offerings extend the concerted effort to articulate how queerness and transness can support and expand justice-oriented scholarship, both in our analyses of learning (e.g., McWilliams & Penuel, 2015; Uttamchandani, 2021; Shrodes & Paré, 2022; Learning Towards Justice Team, 2025) and in our designs for learning (e.g., McWilliams, 2016; Paré et al., 2019). Across the papers, we delve deeper into the following interrelated themes. First, queer and trans theories unsettle the terms through which learning is defined and constrained. They disrupt the taken-for-granted categories 2192 that delimit what counts as knowledge and who is recognized as a learner (McWilliams & Penuel, 2017; Shrodes & Paré, 2022). This includes analyses of how dominant logics in education and design naturalize exclusion and foreclose possibilities, as well as contributions that articulate alternative orientations rooted in queer and trans sensibilities (as seen in Keenan’s analysis of how teachers’ learning about how to support trans children misaligns with children’s own learning about gender, thus foreclosing meaningful support for gender self-determination). Second, the symposium traces how power operates through discourse, institutions, and political projects to render queer ways of knowing (in)visible (as seen in the analysis by Jackson, Vea, and Harris on conservative parental rights discourse and Krishnamoorthy’s analysis on nationalist and caste logics). Together, these contributions build on scholarship that shows how ideology shapes learning (Philip et al., 2017) and reveal how macro-political ideologies are secured and reproduced in the microdynamics of learning, as well as how struggles over legitimacy and authority shape educational dignity (Espinoza et al., 2020). Third, the work emphasizes the entanglement of affect, embodiment, knowledge, and relationality (as seen in Blake’s design research rooted in pedagogies of queer and trans desire, Muscat’s studies of children coauthoring counter figured worlds to Euro-Western dance, and Paré’s Flocking QT Stories  simulation demonstrating affective orientations of sadness, desire, and hope). These design works highlight the politics of affect and bodily orientation (Ahmed, 2004, 2006), while extending further on the affecti ve and relational configurations of learning (Vea, 2020).

Taken together, our symposium deepens the discussion on how queer and trans theories reorient learning away from normative framings that constrain possibility and toward practices that recognize the complexity, vulnerability, and creativity of how people live, learn, and imagine otherwise. Against the tyranny of authoritarian regimes and autocracy that attempt to suppress the voices and lives of queer and trans people, our symposium collectively highlights multitudes of human conditions that queer and trans  scholars have historically held in the spaces of learning: hope, dismay, joy, and pain. Our symposium responds to Vossoughi and Ma’s (2025) call for scholarship as a collective form of resistance and reimagination, addressing “How candeep studies of lear ning and cognition shape policy and practice? How can our work address persistent inequities? What roles do research and writing play in educational innovation and change?” (p. 287). By focusing attention on the conditions that devalue educational dignity as well as visionary practices that envision more just futures, this symposium expands the possibilities of what learning can be and how it can be researched and supported. Designing for learning with queer and trans sensibilities: Offerings to the field from a communal clothing making space rooted in self-determination Ali R. Blake This analysis is from a social design experiment (Guitiérrez & Jurow, 2016) within a queer and trans communal clothing making space, QTthreads, rooted in commitments to multidimensional (Ferguson, 2019) struggles for self-determination. An intergenerational and multiracial group with varying relationships with dis/ability gathered on Sunday afternoons at QTthreads. A core dimension of inquiry was how the group cultivated relations (DiGiacomo & Gutiérrez, 2016) as part of developing political analyses of our  work. I expand on efforts to characterize principles for design, both in process and creations, rooted in queer commitments (McWilliams, 2016). To do so, I draw on ethnographic and microethnographic analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) of the experiences of 18 participants across 9 months (fall 2023-summer 2024) of design and weekly program sessions through fieldnotes, images, and video.

2193 I elaborate on two queered design principles –actual bodies, actual people and multiple entry points to relationships with queer and trans practices –that we (participants, including myself) developed. Taking fashioning as a valuable form of transgender cultural production (Galarte, 2015), we developed these principles from our queer and trans sensibilities (both through theory and life experience) while navigating ongoing interactions. Rooted in pedagogies of queer and trans desire (Slovin, 2024), the principles supported us in actively desiring queer and trans presence through making present practices  (Tan & Calabrese Barton, 2023) without requiring queer and trans people make ourselves known.

The principle of actual bodies, actual people grounded us in the realities and needs of the great variety of bodily shapes, sizes, and configurations. Standardized, colonial (Lugones, 2010) binary gendered and sized-based norms guide Western clothing design and leave many queer, trans, and/or fat folks with starved options for embodiment. Such conditions can be lackluster for anyone, with varying consequences. Instead, we set up systems that began with the actual bodies of actual people, aligning with our commitment to gender self-determination and bodily autonomy (Stanley, 2014), where both clothing and our bodies could be modified as desired. Starting with our own bodies also helped us imagine the bodies of potential others, making room for trans and queer people beyond the walls of the studio. The principle of actual bodies, actual people encourages us to root our activities in relevant specificities of the wide expanse of human embodied experiences. The principle of multiple entry points to relationships with queer and trans practices is one way we navigated valuing queer and trans practices without expecting participants to explicitly articulate alignment with them. We regularly engaged with queer and trans histories and practices, such as working with/in archives, sharing and inviting each other to events beyond QTthreads such as drag and theatre performances, and creating compression garments for a mutual aid project (Spade, 2020). We framed  these activities in ways that offered participants multiple ways to participate, through which they could choose to position themselves, or not, in relation with queerness and transness. In this way, the principle helped sustain a playful space for folks to explore dynamic possibilities of gender and sexuality.

Constructing the child, contesting the state: Inter-object stance in anti-trans parental rights discourse June Jackson, Tanner Vea, Audrey L. Harris This study examines how political claims within conservative parental rights discourse depend upon constructing children – gender non-conforming youth in particular – as epistemically untrustworthy and non-agential. Through discourse and interaction analysis (Jefferson, 2004; Derry et al., 2010) of one video episode (40 minutes) of Moms For Liberty’s The Joyful Warriors Podcast, we ask: how do characterizations of children within parental rights discourse interact with critiques of gender-expansive identities and the institutions that promote them? This discourse carries significant implications for the epistemic agency and educational dignity of young people, with a particular impact on gender-expansive youth, laying the ideological groundwork for learning spaces that are hostile to LGBTQ+ people. These conditions restrict whose knowledge is legitimized, what resources are made available, and whose educational dignity (Espinoza et al., 2020) is affirmed or denied. We draw on theorizations of “the child,” within queer theory (Edelman, 2004) and critical childhood studies (Castañeda, 2002), as a powerful cultural figure invoked by adults to justify broader political projects. Inspired by Philip et al.’s (2017) use of stance to demonstrate the role of ideology in shaping the content and trajectory of learning, we extend Du Bois’s (2007) stance triangle (see Figure 1) to examine how stances toward institutional authority (Object 1) are enacted through, and stabilized by, concomitant stances towards children (Object 2). We explore learning here as ideological elaboration, where existing beliefs about institutional threats are deepened and reinforced through recursive stance-taking toward children. This analysis, demonstrated through two excerpts, reveals how interobjective stances operate within political sensemaking. Classroom collisions: Good intentions and the foreclosure of gender selfdetermination at school Harper Keenan Trans-affirming school policies began to be established in the U.S. in the early 2000s. These policies followed the establishment of “safer schools” policies designed to protect gay and lesbian youth from bullying and discrimination. Trans-affirming school policy was often justified by the idea that trans and gender non-conforming youth were at risk of suicide, and therefore in need of adult protection. While all young people should of course have access to learning environments where they feel safe and secure, I suggest that the design of trans-affirming school policy had the effect of training teachers’ professional vision (Goodwin, 1994) into a view of young people that is out of alignment with the foundations of U.S. -based transgender social movements, which emphasize a collective demand for gender self-determination.

When the New York City-based trans activists of color now credited as the founders of the contemporary trans movement wrote the STAR Manifesto for trans justice in 1970, their first demand clearly stated: “We want the right to self-determination over the use of our bodies” (Cohen, 2007). Central to their model of community organizing was the idea that access to public resources —including education—should not depend on one’s conformity within government systems of identity regulation (Gill-Peterson, 2018). Y et, virtually all trans-affirming school policies focus primarily on making transness legible—and therefore manageable—within adultregulated administrative systems. Drawing from legal scholar Dean Spade, who argues that “in the face of significant resista nce to conditions of subjection, law reform tends to provide just enough transformation to 2196 stabilize and preserve status quo conditions,” (Spade, 2013, p.1035) I argue that the design of trans-affirming school policy enables adult-governed school systems to describe themselves as trans-inclusive while simultaneously retaining their power to regulate childhood gender. In this presentation, I draw from two years of multi-site ethnographic study in five kindergarten and first grade classrooms in San Francisco, including more than 50 interviews and 5 focus groups with teachers, 20 playbased focus groups with children, and over 500 hours of participant observation, I present a series of qualitative snapshots illustrating what I call classroom collisions  between teachers’ policy-informed ideas about childhood gender and the ways that children agentically assert their genders  in ways that challenge adult authority. I follow Ball et. al. (2011) in conceptualizing of teachers as policy actors  whose professional vision – in this case, their view of children – is shaped by school policy. Further informed by childhood studies scholarship, which views children as capable of “[acting] in a manner that enhances greater individual and community self-determination” (Campano et al., 2020, p. 224), I argue that even teachers of young children who actively seek to support gender diversity in their classrooms often struggle in their efforts, largely because they are primarily learning from adultdesigned policy about how best to support transgender and gender non-conforming children in school.  Ultimately, the findings from this study illustrate the need for educators and policymakers to consider children’s broader rights to self-determination in thinking about the design of equitable educational environments. Queer and Indigenous (Im)possibilities in the School Lunchroom: How Minorities Navigate and Subvert the Hindu Nationalist Project Rishi Krishnamoorthy In 2014, India voted into power a Hindu nationalist government, support for whom has been growing steadily over the last century (Banerjea, 2023; Das, 2025; Upadhyay, 2020). This brand of religious nationalism has instigated horrific violence against minoritized communities – particularly Muslim Indians – by claiming India as an Indigenously Hindu land, with all others who have resided there for centuries, considered foreigners (Subramaniam, 2019). Simultaneously, Hindu nationalists position Hinduism as welcoming and supportive of queer people; particularly visible with the government’s positive reaction to homosexuality being decriminalized in 2018 (Das, 2025; Upadhyay, 2020). While life for queers in India is by no means easy, scholars have traced ways that experiences with caste-privilege orient Hindu queers in/with/to space in ways that reproduce Hindu-nationalist-informed violence against minoritized Indians such as caste-oppressed Hindus, Muslims, and others (Banerjea, 2023; Das, 2025).

Set in contemporary India, this paper investigates how Hindu nationalist informed constructions of Indigeneity, religion, caste, and queerness intersect towards structuring how educators are oriented in/to school spaces. By recognizing how Hindu-nationalist and caste-based discriminatory logics contribute to shaping schoolbased interactions (Krishnamoorthy, under review; Nayak, 2023), this study additionally attends to how intersectional experiences with/of Indigeneity and queerness shape the interactional processes of inclusion and exclusion through ways that minoritized educators are allowed to be in relation with(in) school spaces. This work is part of a larger study of teaching and learning in a rural South Indian school. I draw on learning sciences scholarship that attends to learning as shaped by sociopolitical-cultural-historical powered dynamics, entangled across scales (i.e., macro-micro) (e.g., Danish & Ma, 2023; Philip et al., 2018). I examine how humans relate with each other in/with learning environments through a relational onto-epistemology informed by Indigenous and non-Eurocentric ways of knowing and being (e.g., Marin, 2020; Tigunait, 1983). I layer queer (Ahmed, 2006; Lugones, 2010) and feminist new materialist (Barad, 2007, 2012) theories to trace the pre-colonial histories and powered dynamics that shape how human (educators) are oriented (Ahmed, 2006) with(in) each other and the material world. Building on emerging data analysis methods in the field, I employ a radical relational approach to critical intra-action analysis (Gholson & Ma, under review; Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Krishnamoorthy, 2023) of two episodes where teachers and the lead researcher were informally socializing in the lunchroom. Each episode examines how educators’ relational histories with each other, the material world, and Hindu nationalism, cis-heteronormativity, religion, and caste, shaped the possibilities for minoritized educators to orient in the space. In doing so, this scholarship nuances how religious nationalist movements on the macrosociopolitical scale can infiltrate and structure informal interactions in learning environments. I show the processes through which: 1) Hindu caste-privileged and cis-heteronormative educators’ ways of knowing became inscribed into this school space, and 2) Indigenous and queered ways of knowing and being were rendered (in)visible. This study also illustrates the possibilities for building sociopolitical allyship (Tan & Calabrese Barton,

2023) between minoritized educators within learning spaces increasingly impacted by religious nationalism.

2197 She’s The Queen: A Child’s Reorientation of Gendered and Racialized Disciplinary Norms in Euro-Western Dance Through Embodied Authoring Luke Muscat This paper describes a critical arts-based (Finley, 2017) learning experience for a group of children between 5and 7-years-old during a 2-week summer dance camp in a major Northeastern metropolitan city in the United States. Tasked with co-authoring and enacting a short story through movement, drama, music, writing, and visual art, this group of children storied a world where gendered and racialized disciplinary norms of Euro-Western dance could be negotiated, resisted, and contested in their artmaking and embodied dialogues. By Euro-Western dance, I am referring to dance genres that originated in colonial Europe, specifically ballet and subsequent genres that emerged from its lineage in the West such as American modern dance (McPherson, 2023), rooted in colonial aesthetics of body, gender, story, and hierarchical/othering relationality of social groups (Ross, 2000; Stinson,

2005) that can value white cisheteronormative bodies over queer bodies of color.

Using figured world analysis (Holland et al., 1998) and queer phenomenology (Ahmed, 2006), I crystalize (Ellingson, 2008) the experiences of Arya (pseudonym), a Black, girl-identifying 6-year-old child, and her embodiment of the Fairy Godmother Queen (the story’s villain). Methodologically, this study draws upon video data from choreographic workshops, artistic artifacts (illustrations, writings, props, and set designs) produced throughout the day, and daily fieldnotes to piece together a multi-layered fragment (Ellingson, 2008) of Arya’s journey. This approach contextualized Arya’s experience while offering an entry point to theorizing how the figured world of Euro-Western dance can orient children’s minds, bodies, and spirits toward colonial ideas in explicit and implicit ways. Attending to Arya’s experiences led me to ask: How did Arya negotiate gendered and racialized disciplinary norms in Euro-Western Dance? Furthermore, because Arya’s embodied authoring was situated within children’s co-constructed storied world, I engaged with a secondary research question: How did children co-construct a counter figured world to Euro-Western Dance? To illustrate Arya’s embodied authoring, my first set of findings describe how children co-authored a counter figured world to the figured world of Euro-Western dance through storytelling, dance, music, and visual art. Through their use of movements, music, and artifacts as critical pivots (Vygotsky, 1998/1978) to enter and co-construct their storied figured world, the children cultivated an environment where negotiating and resisting embodied gender and racial dimensions of Euro-Western dance could be cont ested. My second set of findings attuned to Arya’s micro-movement negotiations during her choreographic process and the ways she pushed the boundaries of gendered and racialized disciplinary norms in Euro-Western dance across three major plot points in the  storytelling project. Across these three plot points, my micro-movement analysis highlights Arya’s embodied authoring – her reorientation of symbolically and culturally situated movements in Euro-Western dance to expand the possibilities of who she could be.

This paper contributes a deeper understanding of children’s complex sociocultural worlds, embodied identity formation, and learning while proposing the creative arts as a possibility for dancing together toward more liberatory, just, and equitable futures.

Rooting for the boids: abstraction and emotion in learning about systemic harm Dylan Paré Cisheteronormativity remains a pervasive force, yet most approaches to gender and sexuality education emphasize tolerance over critical disruption (Airton & Koecher, 2019). This paper introduces Flocking QT Stories, a multi-agent simulation designed to show how systemic marginalization and resilience emerge from interactions among individuals, groups, and institutions (Figure 2) (Paré & Craig, 2019). 2198 Figure 2 A screenshot of the Flocking QT Stories Multi-Agent Simulation. Drawing on queer theory (Butler, 1990/2006; Ahmed, 2006) and complexity education (Wilensky & Resnick, 1999), this study brings into dialogue the heterosexual matrix as an emergent system of constraint upon gender and sexualities and queer orientations as ways bodies and spaces are shaped through repetition and dis/orientation. These frames highlight how gender and sexuality are lived as complex, relational, and contingent phenomena, guiding the design of the simulation, which models normative and non-normative boids (bird-like droid agents). In the simulation, the proximity of normative boids or institutions drains their energy, while proximity to non-normative boids replenishes it. Each non-normative boid carries an audio story of gender-and sexuality-based marginalization or resilience. Although these stories frame the simulation in affectively resonant ways, my findings show that the abstractions —harm lines, stagnation, and emergent flocking—anchored participants’ sense-making and critique.

## Methodology & Study Context

Data were collected from interviews with sixteen participants who interacted with Flocking QT Stories at a public installation in a Western Canadian university and a workshop for middle school teachers in the Northeastern US. Sessions were video-recorded, transcribed, and analyzed using the constant comparative method (Glaser, 1965).

Across three cases, my analysis highlights how participants combined emotional orientations and abstract reasoning to engage with marginalized boids, critique systemic harm, and imagine alternatives. Participants' engagement with the simulation involved projecting personal and collective experiences onto the boids. Participants empathized with ‘stuck’ boids, sometimes re-enacting their stagnation with gestures or expressions to make sense of exhaustion under normative pressure. They rooted for the boids to be saved, when drained boids regained energy from a passing flock, likening it to queer people drawing “mental and spiritual energy” from community, an interpretation that framed resilience as a collective, not individual, phenomenon. Others questioned whether “one friend is enough to combat an institution,” highlighting how the simulation prompted them to weigh the limits of solidarity and the structural scale of harm. These patterns highlight how affective orientation and abstraction were entangled in participants’ reasoning. The boids in the simulation serve as transitional objects, enabling learners to project their identities onto the system and reason about self-other 2199 relationships (Hostetler et al., 2018; Papert, 1980). Rather than framing abstraction as a means of emotional distance, this study foregrounds emotional configurations—the interrelationships between feeling, sense-making, and practice (Vea, 2020)—as central to understanding social issues and the lived realities of marginalized people. Flocking QT Stories  exemplifies a queer-complexity pedagogy by designing affect-laden abstractions that center marginalized experiences and invite critique, redesign, and speculative hope. The design highlights the synergy between queer theories of the heterosexual matrix, queer orientations and embodiment (Butler, 1990/2006; Ahmed, 2006) and complexity education’s attention to emergence, projection, and mid-level reasoning (Hostetler et al., 2018; Papert, 1980; Wilensky & Resnick, 1999), offering new analytic and design tools for understanding systemic oppression and resilience. Further, by demonstrating how participants integrated emotions, personal knowledge, and desires for change into the simulation, the study shows that complexity simulations of queer and trans experiences can make explicit the conditions that perpetuate inequity, while also supporting learners' resistance and reimagination. Thus, learning about systemic oppression and resilience can emerge from the productive entanglement of abstraction, embodiment, and affect, reorienting participants toward marginalized per spectives and more just futures. Hybrid Facilitation Plan Our symposium is designed as an interactive hybrid poster session that engages participants across in-person and online modalities. The facilitation plan balances structured presentations, collective reflection, and open dialogue to foreground intergenerational perspectives and shared meaning-making. Co-chairs (3): Paré; Takeuchi; Moschkovich Discussants (3): Cruz; Leyva; Uttamchandani Presenters (6): 1. Blake; 2. Jackson, Vea, & Harris; 3. Keenan; 4. Krishnamoorthy; 5. Muscat; 6. Paré The three co-chairs will open the symposium by situating the session in historical and intergenerational contexts. Each co-chair will share a brief personalized history (5 minutes each), illustrating the perspectives and commitments we bring to this collective work.

2. Poster Overviews (15 minutes)

The six Contributors will each provide a two-minute synopsis of their poster, highlighting central arguments and contributions. This will orient attendees to the range of scholarship represented.

3. Silent Poster Exploration (15 minutes)

Attendees will view posters, which will be available in a shared Google Drive folder for in-person and online participants, ensuring equitable access.

- In-person attendees will receive a printed handout with short links and QR codes for the Google Drive

folder of presentations, as well as the Padlet.

- Online attendees will receive the short links to the Google Drive folder containing the presentations, as

well as the Padlet link, in the Zoom chat window.

4. Collective Padlet Contribution (5 minutes)

Participants will be invited to post comments, reflections, and questions to a shared Padlet wall, creating a collective record of engagement.

5. Author Responses (20 minutes)

The co-chairs will facilitate a round of responses by selecting questions from the Padlet. Authors will address selected questions verbally (2 –3 minutes for each question). Remaining questions will be answered asynchronously on the Padlet.

6. Discussant Panel (20 minutes)

Three invited discussants —from anthropology of education, math education, and the learning sciences —will share reflections on a common prompt: In your respective fields, how do the contributions from this symposium push boundaries or amplify current work? Their responses will further connect the symposium’s contributions to broader conversations across research communities.
