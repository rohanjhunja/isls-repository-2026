# Playful Uncertainty: Parents as STEM Educators in the Home

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examined playful uncertainties—moments when parents feigned uncertainty—to foster children’s engineering development in their home environment. Grounded in sociocultural theories of guided participation and family culture, we conducted a case study of four families. Findings suggest that playful uncertainties empowered children to take initiative, explore, and refine their understanding through collaborative discovery and problem-solving. This study also positioned parents as transformative agents, using playful uncertainties as creative epistemic support.

Purpose Parents and caregivers play a unique role in suppor ting their children as science, technology, enginee ring, and mathematics (STEM) learners, from the questions they pose (e.g., Simpson et al., 2022) to the epistemic challenges they present (Kim & Simpson, 2023) to the resources and access they provide (e.g., Dou et al., 2019).

In this paper, we add to this knowledge base by con sidering the role of  parents’ playful uncertainties when in engaging in engineering design tasks in home environments. Specifically, we address the following: What is the role of parents’ use of playful uncertainties in supporting children’s engagement as engineering learners? In this study, we define playful uncertainties as moments i n which parents feigned uncertainty (i.e., confusio n, lack of knowledge) during the engineering design process. Informed by Rogoff et al.’s (1993) concept of guide d participation and Keifert’s (2021) research on fami ly culture within moments of inquiry, we argue  that playful uncertainties within family interactions empower children to take  initiative, explore, and refine their understanding through collaborative discovery and problem-solving, particularly within an engineering context.

## Methods

An intrinsic case study was utilized to understand the particular nature of a situation of interest, t he manner in which parents supported their child’s learning as an engineer through playful uncertainties (Stake, 1995). The four cases in this study were selected from a larger  data set due to (a) the number and quality of video s and (b) the observation of parent’s common use of playful uncertainties through their engageme nt in the engineering kits. ## Results

Across all cases, playful uncertainty became a key approach through which parents provided an opportun ity for their children to explore ideas, take initiatives, and refine their understanding through collaborative discovery and problem-solving. Rather than  providing direct answers, parents often avoided ask ing questions (e.g., “I don’t know. You tell me.”) and/or hedged their certainty (e.g., “I don’t know if this will work.”.), allowin g children to make decisions that informed the design of the prototype. We provide two examples to highlight the results. The first is from the case of Amethyst (child) and Amy (parent), who were engaged in an engineering design task of an animal house for stray animals to survive extrem e weather conditions common to where they live. Amethyst and Amy are discussing how to build a house for flooding. Amy:...there would be wooden beams that hold up the house. Amethyst: Oooohhh. But how are they [animals] going to get out? Amy:        I don’t know. You tell me.

Amethyst: (drawing or adding to the sketch) Amy:        They would use stairs.

Amethyst: It’s a ladder.

It was not uncommon for Amethyst to ask questions o ut of her curiosity as an engineer (Line 2).  As exemplified in Amy’s response, “I don’t know. You tell me,” she avoided giving Amethyst the answer and instead asked Amethyst to think independently about the que stion. This playful uncertainty provided Amethyst  with agency to continue making decisions that would info rm the design of the house. In this instance, Amethyst took this up by adding her idea to the design sketch of the house. In the second example, Eden (child) and Amanda (par ent) were creating a rain gauge that will measure the amount of liquid precipitation over a set perio d of time. However, unlike typical rain gauges, thi s one will light up as an indication of how much precipitation has accumulated (e.g., 1 cm, 2 cm, 3 cm). Amanda:  Do you know aluminum foil conducts electricity? Eden:        Let’s test it (place aluminum foil over his head and move side to side). Amanda:  Oh, static electricity. If we rub it on your hair m aybe (rub aluminum foil on hair). Let's see (bending leg of LED light).

Eden:        Oh you're bending the light. You bent the light. Amanda:  Mhm. Which we’re gonna have to do when we put it in  the cup anyway (wrap aluminum foil around leg of LED). I don't know if this will work. Eden:        No.

Amanda:   You don't think so? Oh my goodness. Look at that. Eden:        OHHHH!(mouth wide open) Based on Line 1, it was apparent that Amanda knew aluminum foil was a conductor of electricity. However, she took this opportunity of Eden’s misconception (i.e., static versus current electricity) to engage in a playful uncertainty framed as an exploration as to whether or not aluminum foil is a conductor. Although, Eden was an observer in this exploration, he gained an understanding that aluminum foil is conductive. As noted in the last line, h e was rather in awe of this as it challenged his no tion of aluminum foil as a way to generate static electricity.

## Discussion

Although we only provide d two examples, through our analysis, we observed these playful u ncertainties as patterns in interaction, which may highlight playful uncertainty as a family practice utilized by the parents in this study to support their children as engineering learners (Keifert, 2021). Similar to guided participation (Rogoff et al., 1993), parents pretending to not know provided  opportunities for children to explore ideas through collaborative inquiry and take on a more agentive role in the design of the prototype. As such, we are unaware of any studies that consider playful uncertainties by formal and informal educators to support children’s  learning. As highlighted by prior scholarship o ur results  positioned parents as  unique educators  within their home environment (Duo et al., 2019; Simpson et al., 2022). This continues to underscore the unique nature of h ow parents support their children as STEM learners. We hypothesize that the home environment may be a safe place for parents to create and utilize playful uncertainties, but additional research shou ld be conducted to explore the epistemic and familial richness of playful uncertai nties for children in the home environment, as well  as other informal learning environments.
