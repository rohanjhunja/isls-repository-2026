# Reimagining Early AI Literacies: A Care-Centered Co-Design Study with Parents and Young Children

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While AI literacy has gained increasing attention in education, existing frameworks often privilege technical competencies and workforce preparation, overlooking the developmental, relational, and ethical concerns central to early childhood education. Grounded in socio-constructionist theories and an ethics-of-care lens, my dissertation reimagines AI literacy as a care-centered, co-constructed practice within family ecologies. This three-paper dissertation includes: (1) a care-centered critical review of AI literacy frameworks that advances a new conceptualization of early AI literacies in ECE; (2) a techno-ethnographic study investigating parents’ beliefs, practices, and contexts shaping children’s AI learning at home; and (3) participatory co-design with parent–child dyads to iteratively develop a Family AI Literacy Starter Kit. These studies bridge conceptual, perceptual, and design perspectives by identifying gaps in current frameworks, mapping parental engagement, and co-creating developmentally appropriate resources toward justice-oriented early AI literacies in families.

Artificial intelligence (AI) now permeates children’s daily lives through voice assistants, “smart” toys, and algorithmic recommendation systems, shaping a generation of “AI natives” who integrate intelligent systems into their cognitive, social, and imaginative worlds from an early age (Druga et al., 2017; Yang, 2025). While these technologies can support personalization and creative exploration, they also raise concerns about privacy, bias, overreliance, and the erosion of relational, human-centered learning (Berson et al., 2025). These tensions have fueled growing interest in AI literacy, broadly defined as the competencies needed to understand, evaluate, and engage with AI responsibly and ethically (Long & Magerko, 2020; Mills et al., 2024). However, much of the work targeting younger learners either adapts frameworks designed for older students (e.g., Su & Yang, 2023; Williams et al., 2019) or offers little actionable guidance for practice (e.g., Luo et al., 2024), often overemphasizing technical skills and workforce preparation while overlooking developmental appropriateness and the need for situated, meaningful sense-making in early childhood. Families, as a crucial “third place” for early AI learning, bring cultural knowledge, values, and relational practices that shape how children encounter and interpret AI (Druga et al., 2022). Yet, their lived expertise and everyday practices remain underexamined.

To address these gaps, this dissertation centers on the lived expertise of parents and children and adopts a co-design approach to surface meanings, practices, and needs that current top-down AI literacy frameworks often overlook. It begins with a critical review that introduces an ethics-of-care lens and proposes three interconnected framings of early AI literacies—functional, relational, and critical. Building on this foundation, Phase 2 adopts a techno-ethnographic approach to investigate how families negotiate and enact AI literacy in everyday routines and what factors shape these practices. Phase 3 then moves from perception to practice by engaging parent-child dyads as co-designers of a developmentally appropriate, low-burden Family AI Literacy Starter Kit for home use. Together, these phases address the overarching research question: How are early AI literacies conceptualized, negotiated, and enacted within family contexts?

## Theoretical framework

This dissertation is grounded in an integrated theoretical framework that bridges sociocultural and constructionist perspectives with contemporary framings of AI literacies. Rooted in the view that knowledge is co-constructed through making, dialogue, and participation in culturally meaningful activity (Vygotsky, 1978; Papert, 1980), this study understands learning as both a creative and relational process. Drawing on Freirean literacy and multiliteracies (Freire, 1970; New London Group, 1996), the study views AI literacies as multimodal, socially situated, and emancipatory practices that empower families to “read and rewrite” the technological world. Extending Kafai et al.’s (2020) computational literacies framings, the dissertation adopts three complementary lenses—functional, relational, and critical—to examine how learners understand how AI works, engage with it in human–AI and human–human relationships, and reflect on its ethical and societal implications. By foregrounding attentiveness toneeds, responsibility in action, competence in practice, and responsiveness to feedback, the ethics of care framework (Tronto, 2013; Porayska-Pomsta & Asare, 2025) repositions AI literacies as a socially situated a nd relational practice. Together, these integrated perspectives ground the study’s methodological, co-design, and analytic choices, enabling a care-centered approach.

This dissertation employs a three-phase, mixed-methods design. Phase 1 is a critical review (Paré et al., 2015) of AI literacy frameworks and review papers from 2015 to 2025, synthesizing their theoretical and pedagogical implications for early childhood education. Phase 2 uses a techno-ethnographic mixed-methods approach (Pink et al., 2016; Kien, 2008), combining an online survey and home-based inquiry with 8-10 parent-child dyads to capture both the breadth and depth of parents’ perspectives and mediation practices on family technology use. Phase 3 adopts a participatory co-design methodology (DiSalvo et al., 2017), engaging parent–child dyads in iterative, home-based activities that explore adaptable AI literacy themes. In this way, design functions as both a method for generating data and a means of refining practical supports. Data analysis follows an iterative, multi-layered strategy. In Phase 1, framework and thematic synthesis (Booth et al., 2016) map existing frameworks onto the functional, relational, and critical framings and interpret them through the ethics-of-care lens. In Phases 2, survey data are analyzed using descriptive and basic inferential statistics. Qualitative data, including open-ended survey responses, home visit notes, recordings, and interview transcripts, are analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis and in vivo coding (Braun & Clarke, 2006). In Phase 3, family-recorded interactions are analyzed through discourse and interactional analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Goodwin, 2000), triangulated with weekly reflections, interviews, fieldnotes, family-created artifacts, and researcher memos through artifact and content analysis. Across all three phases, the early AI literacies framework is continuously refined through movement between theory, empirical inquiry, and design.

## Preliminary findings

Phase 1 resulted in an Early AI Literacies Framework as shown in Figure 1. The functional framing, at the core, defines why AI is taught and which concepts, tools, and representations are emphasized. Surrounding this core, relational practices make functional ideas learnable and create space for critical reflection. The critical framing forms the outer layer, connecting to broader sociocultural and ethical contexts. An ethics of care may serve as connective tissue guiding implementation: it grounds the functional in developmental sensitivity, deepens the relational through empathy and reciprocity, and sharpens the critical through moral accountability and justice. Phase 2 and Phase 3 data collection will be conducted from Spring 2026 to Fall 2027, primarily in participants’ homes. Participants will be recruited through a Chinese school, a Girl Scout troop, a public school, and local libraries to capture a wide range of family experiences and perspectives on AI.
