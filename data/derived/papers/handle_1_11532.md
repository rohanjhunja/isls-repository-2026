# Co-Creation of Visual Stories: Representing Agency for Mathematics Learning with Forcibly Displaced Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines the impact of visual stories co-created with forcibly displaced children to express their agentic possibilities within the school context, particularly in mathematics classrooms. Using participatory co-design processes, six arts-based workshops were designed and facilitated for families to collaboratively illustrate stories of agency, identity, and belonging. Findings, in the form of co-designed visual stories, demonstrate students' use of visual representations to reclaim agency after experiencing forced displacement. The visual stories also highlight educators' roles in facilitating in-school mathematics learning experiences. This research underscores the potential of arts-based co-creation as a tool for children experiencing intersectional oppression to voice their lived experiences.

## Background

The field of learning sciences is increasingly emph asizing equity, diversity, and learning, particularly withi n the dominant discipline of mathematics. This shift is aimed at making mathematics more accessible and connected to racially and linguistically diverse communities to challenge and broaden mainstream norms (e.g., Boaler, 2002; Joseph et al., 2019; Leyva, 2016; Nasir, 2002; Seng upta-Irving & Vossoughi, 2019; Takeuchi & Ishihara, 2020). While research on equity and diversity in mathematics education is expanding, there appears to be a lack of methods that involve artistic participation. Artistic participatory approaches that engage participants directly in the process of visually depicting their challenges and experiences related to schooling and mathematics education appear to be less explored.  This approach could engage students directly in the process of creating art, allowing them to visually convey their own experiences and stories. Such an approach could make school experiences and mathematics more accessible and inclusive.

Drawing from participatory and co-design approaches (e.g., Gutiérrez, 2016; Ishimaru et al., 2018; Mawasi et al., 2023; Vossoughi et al., 2023), this study leverages co-creation of visual arts as both a communicative and analytical  tool. Co-creation approaches, which involve participants directly in art-making process, give participants a platform to share perspectives often dismissed in  text-based communications. Co-creation is a shift in visual art practices that fosters a more dialogical and social process by positioning participants as active contributors in artistic decision-making (Walmsley, 2020). By focusing on the embodied, racialized, and gendered experiences of forcibly displaced students, this research demonstrates how co-creation of visual stories can reveal the complexities of their experiences and their sense of agency in school settings. Intersecti onality is an approach that explores the interplay among  various systems of oppression such as gender, race, and class. It explores “the various ways in which race and gender intersect in shaping structural, political, and representational aspects of violence against women of color” (Crenshaw, 1991, p.1244). Utilizing an intersectionality lens as a key part of this study enables me to explore how being a racialized girl influences bodily experiences in school, particularly within mathematics classrooms.

## Methodology

The participants of this study are six  Afghan refugee/forced migrant families, which included 12 parents and 12 school-aged children (Grade 2 to 12).  Author 1 (Dadkhahfard) has the backgrounds in profe ssional graphic illustration and design. Based on her artistic enga gement, Author 1 (Dadkhahfard) sees illustration as a powerful form of communication that goes beyond the limitati ons of words and verbal expression as it can capture the nuances of emotions, the subtleties of moments, and the complexities of thoughts that are often lost in translation when conveyed through conventional verbal and textual methods. The co-creation process involved illustrationmediated workshops, where participants first analyzed the vi sual story illustrated by  Author 1 (Dadkhahfard), based on Author 2’s prior ethnographic work (Takeuchi, 2018), which depicted race, power, and embodiment issues in educational contexts. This visual story served as a catalyst for discussions, helping participants to express their own stories. They then drew their own stories, supported by guidance on artistic techniques. Various types of data were gathered during the workshops, such as observations with (visual) field notes, audio/video recordings (totalling 670 minutes), participants’ drawings at different stages of compl etion. Visual field notes taken during the workshops were to depict contextual information illustrating participants’ embodied stories expres sed during the workshops. Visual discourse analysis (Albers, 2013) was applied to capture complex racialized and gendered experiences. Using insights from visual discourse analysis, shared stories, participant drawings, and (visual) field notes, we collaboratively transformed these data into digital illustrations with particip ants. This iterative process continued in subsequent workshops, where participants' insights helped refine the final illustrations. Together, we co-created a visual story depicting shared experiences of forced displacement, gendered and racialized encounters with mathematics, and navigating school in a new country. Each phase was reviewed with partici pants, whose feedback shaped the final designs, making the illustrations a collective, visually rich portrayal of their stories and experiences.

Co-created visual story, “Gol Noor” Final co-created visual story is available through the project website (https://illustratestem.com/). Analysis of the visual stories and iterative process of co-creation of digital illustration highlighted key findings related to participants’ agency, identities, and interactions within school settings. Participants employed visual stories to assert agency, often positioning themselves as central, empowered figures within their artwork. For instance, student s illustrated their bodies in prominent ways, using  size and composition to convey their sense of agency. This form of self-representation enabled participants to depict illustrations that challenge stereotypes and resist marginalization, particularly within the context of mathematics classrooms where racial and gender inequalities are still prevalent. By centralizing their own images, they visually communicated resilience and confidence, symbolizing their agency within challenging educational environments. Through creating a story of a fictional character o f “Gol Noor,” participants collectively explored th e complexities of their identities as displaced students. The story co-created was a fictional story of an Afghan girl whose story is mirrored with the pa rticipants’ experiences. Gol Noor’s story, which portrayed her journey from her homeland to a Canadian classroom, offered a lens for students to explore themes of belonging and exclusion. For many  participants, the visual narrative allowed them to express the emotional complexity of displacement, contrasting cherished memories from their home coun tries with the often-isolating reality of adapting to a new school culture. These depictions highlighted the richness of their cultural identities, serving as affirmations of their lived experiences and emotional resilience.

Participants’ narratives depicted a teacher as playing dual roles: both reinforcing and challenging students’ agency. Some teachers were illustrated as  skeptical or dismissive, especially when students’  abilities challenged stereotypes, while others were shown as supportive, encouraging academic curiosity and vali dating students' intellectual contributions. This portraya l reveals the complex influence educators have on m arginalized students, emphasizing the need for awareness of factors that might impact how students from diverse backgrounds are perceived and supported.

## Scholarly significance

This research adds to the growing discussion on the  role of arts in learning (Halverson & Sawyer, 2022) by demonstrating the co-creation of (visual) arts as a method for understanding and addressing issues of equity in education. By centering on the voices of forcibly d isplaced students and through the design process le d b y artist/researcher, this study shows a possibility of art-based co-design for amplifying the voices that are often silenced in institutionalized educational settings.  By integrating visual storytelling with critical i nquiry into educational inequities, th is research offers new ways for scholars and educat ors to approach inclusivity in mathematics learning. The findings underscore the importance of participant-centered methodologies in revealing nuanced perspectives on identity and agency (Vossoughi et al., 2023). The co-creation of visual stories enabled forcibly displac ed students to express their embodied experiences in ways that traditional verbal methods migh t not capture (Albers, 2013). The role of co-creation of visual stories in this research  not only  democratizes the storytelling process  but also shifts traditional power dynamics by positioning students as co-creator. Critical arts-based research in the learning sciences have shown the power of arts to evoke questions to the hegemon y, multitudes of emotions, and taken-for-granted sceneries situated in places (Germinaro & Nickson, 2024; Nzinga, 2023). The co-created story that inscribes scars and pains from forced displacement but also depicts resistance and  agency invites educators to dialogue on pedagogica l possibilities for embracing humanity.
