# Investigating the Role of Perceived Intra-Unit Coherence for Science Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While instructional coherence is widely regarded as essential for effective science education, little evidence exists on how students’ perceptions of coherence influence their learning and motivation. This study investigates the role of perceived intra-unit coherence—retrospective, prospective, and in-toto coherence—in shaping learning outcomes. Drawing on data from 771 students across seven energy units, results indicate that retrospective and in-toto coherence positively impact immediate and unit-level learning but not broader conceptual understanding. Additionally, situational interest emerged as a key factor influencing coherence perceptions. These findings highlight the importance of designing instruction that aligns with students’ perspectives, enhancing engagement and achievement in science education.

## Introduction

A central goal of school science instruction is to enable learners to use their knowledge to make sense of the realworld phenomena and problems that they encounter in  their lives outside of school (e.g. National Resea rch Council [NRC], 2012). Traditionally, schools have t ried to meet this goal by prioritizing students’ de velopment of conceptual science knowledge with the assumption  that students would then be able to apply this kno wledge across a range of familiar and novel contexts. Yet, it has long been known that students commonly fail to use their school knowledge to make sense of novel contexts (D etterman & Sternberg, 1993). In addition, tradition al instruction has been shown to have a deleterious ef fect on student motivation to learn science and cho ices to engage in science activities (Liou, 2021; Vedder-Weiss & Fortus, 2011). One key reason for the shortcomings of traditional science instruction is a lack of instru ctional coherence. As instruction often starts over  again in many lessons, rather than b uilding on students’ prior knowledge and experience s, students cannot construct well-connected knowledge that that can be readily applied (Bransford et al., 2001), nor can they experience competence and hence develop motivation to engage in science.

Recognizing the shortcomings of traditional science  instruction, science educators have worked to develop instructional approaches specifically desig ned to be coherent, which has been defined as the a lignment of the specified ideas, the depth at which the ideas are studied, and the sequencing of the topics within each grade and across the grades” (Fortus & Krajcik, 2012). A growing body of empirical research suggests that in struction that is designed to be coherent better supports students’ learning about science than more traditional instructional approaches (e.g., Fortus et al., 2015; Schneider et  al., 2020). Instruction that is designed to be coherent follows a set of principles such as developing a small set of  ideas over time, contextualizing learning w ith meaningful phenomena and problems, and motivating new ideas and activities by a need-to-know (Nordine et al., 2021; Fortus & Krajcik, 2012): However, designing instruction to  be coherent has also been criticized. Sikorski and  Hammer

(2017) suggest that what is coherent for one student may not be coherent for another and that students might also

profit from a level of incoherence that may trigger  a need for accommodation and motivate them to lear n. However, the degree to which students actually perceive various aspects of coherence within instruction designed to be coherent, e.g., the extent to which they experience a need-to-know, has not been investigated. There is hence a lack of empirical evidence that directly links coherence perceived by students with learning outcomes.

To address the lack of evidence on the effect of pe rceived coherence on students’ learning and motivation, we used data from N = 771 students who studied in 7 different units on energy all designed  to be coherent and investigated aspects of students’ lear ning and motivation as a function of students’ perce ption of coherence. Further, we investigated how students’ p ersonal characteristics and features of the units w ere related to students’ experience of coherence. Better understanding the role that experiencing instruction as coherent plays in learning and better understanding which factors influence whether instruction is perceived as coher ent, are important to inform the design of instruction that effectively support science learning.

## Theoretical background

More than 40 years ago, a seminal report described school curriculum in the United States as “a mile wide and an inch deep” (Gardner, 1983), suggesting that the cur riculum covered too many topics in insufficient dep th for students to be able to develop deep understanding. In Europe, the picture was similar  and across the world, the focus on the knowledge of a broad range of isolated  ideas has since been considered a major reason tha t many students struggle to use their knowledge to solve c omplex, real-world problems; often also referred to as understanding (Bransford et al., 2001) or knowledge-in-use (Harris et al., 2019). The cognitive dimension, however, is not the only d imension of science instruction with issues. In fac t, some researchers even consider the declining interest in science the more severe problem (e.g.; Fortus et al., 2021; see also Osborne & Dillon, 2008, p. 25). Research showed that as they grow older, students become increasingly less interested in science (e.g., Gardner, 1975; Vedder-Weiss & Fortus, 2011) and hence less willing to engage in science or pursue a science-related career (e.g., Kessels & Hannover, 2002). It seems, students simply do not see science as something matching what they envision fo r themselves (i.e., their i dentity, Jenkins & Nelson, 2005). One reason is assumed to lie in the fact that school science, with its fragmented and incoherent nature, focuses on rote learning of factual knowledge, and does not ac curately reflect the endeavor that is the scientifi c enterprise. (Osborne & Dillon, 2008, p. 24). Another reason may  lie in the role that the experience of competence plays for the development of motivation (Deci & Ryan, 2012). When students repeatedly fail to use their fragment ed knowledge to make sense of th e real-world phenomena or problems that they encounter in their everyday lives, why should they continue engaging in an apparently futile effort? Research suggests that students decide science is not for them if they don’t experience competence  (Hazari et al., 2010). That is, they do not develo p a science identity and subsequently are less likely to continue engaging with science and to choose a science career (ibid.). Defining instructional coherence Recognizing the issues that fragmented curriculum in science creates, educators began to advocate for coherence in science curriculum (Kali, et al, 2008). Roseman et al (2008) characterized coherent science curricu lum as curriculum that presents a complete set of interrelated ideas in such a way that the connections between ideas are made explicit. Fortus and Krajcik (2012) expanded upon this definition by elaborating different types of coherence in science curriculum, including: content standards coherence, learning goals coherence, intra-unit coherence, and interunit coherence. Content standards coherence co nstitutes the degree to which educational goals (e. g. performance expectations for grades or grade bands) are arranged in a meaningful way. Learning goals coherence represents the fit between the learning goals for a n instructional activity and the actual activity (i.e. does the activity support meeting the learning goal). Intra-unit coherence refers to the extent to which the se quence of activities within a unit is suitable to support students in meeting the goals of the unit. Inter-unit coherence similarly refers to the extent to which different units build  on each other such that they support meeting overa rching educational aims over time. A particular  focus among science curriculum researchers and deve lopers, has been intra-unit coherence as it focuses on coherence at the scale of an individual instructional unit, which consists of a set of lessons connected around a single context that usually require a matter of weeks to complete. Many instructional models have emerged to support student learning and engagement around a particular context over the course of weeks, including: projec t-based learning (Blumenfeld et al., 1991), anchored instruction,  problem-based learning (Hmelo-Silver, 2004), design-based learning (Fortus et al., 2004), and modelbased inquiry (Windschitl et al., 2008). No matter their form, all of these instructional models are b ased on a shared set of learning principles: First, instead of learning about a broad range of isolated facts, students actively construct well-connected knowledge organized around a small set of  ideas (see Bransford et al. 2000). Second, learning is most effective when it is situated with in contexts that have relevance and meaning for lea rners (see Lave & Wenger, 1992). Third, learning is fundamenta lly social; knowledge is constructed through active collaboration and debate with peers and teachers (s ee Vygotsky, 1978). Fourth, students are supported in their learning through conceptual tools that resemble tho se used within the community of professional scient ists and engineers (e.g., learning technologies) (see Salomon et al., 1991). Coherent (science) instruction is typically designed to motivate and organize learning around the goal of figuring out interesting phenomena and solving mean ingful problems (Blumenfeld et al., 1991). That is,  rather than organizing instruction by topic (e.g., energy,  electricity), instruction is organized around rele vant contexts that require knowledge of a small set of consistent  ideas to be understood. Relevant contexts create a  need-to know for students. A perceived need-to-know about new ideas is critical for learning (Bran sford et al., 2000), as it stimulates learners to use their intuitive ideas, connect these ideas tonew knowledge, and to deve lop more expert-like understanding over time (ibid.). As students begin to use a consistent set of ideas across contexts, their ability to see the deep structure connecting contexts should be enhanced (ibid). In addition, relevant contexts can motivate students to engage in learning, and cohere nt instruction should be designed not only by logic ally sequencing a c onsistent set of ideas, but also through a careful consideration of how learners’ curiosity can be piqued and maintained. Further, as students build m ore sophisticated knowledge and successfully use th eir knowledge to engage with their surroundings in prod uctive ways, they should develop greater perception s of competence and self-efficacy, which are key factors in promoting students’ desire for continued learning (ibid). Effects of instructional coherence The positive effects of coherent science instruction on science achievement have been well-documented. Geier et al (2008) compared the standardized science assessm ent outcomes between middle school students who participated in project-based instruction and their in-district counterparts, and they found that students who learned in project-based classrooms outperformed their peers. Furthermore, they found a cumulative effect of participating in project-based science classrooms, indicating that additional opportunities to engage in project-based instruction further enhanced student learning. In randomized co ntrolled studies of project-based learning across K-12 (see e.g., Schneider et al., 2022), students who learned in project-based classrooms outperformed students in traditional classrooms on measures of science achievement. In meta-analyses, Furtak et al. (2012) and Chen & Yang (2019) found that inquiry-based and project-based science instruction, respectively, led to gre ater science achievement than in traditional instruction. Similar findings a re reported for problem-based learning (Hmelo-Silver, 2004). There is robust and growing evidence that coherent science instruction support s students’ science achievement compared to traditional approaches.

The effects of coherent science instruction on stud ent affect, as well as the mediating effects of stu dent affective states on student learning during coheren t science instruction, are less clear. The paucity of attention paid to student affect in coheren t science instruction aligns with a broader tendenc y to de-emphasize affective outcomes in science education research (Fortus, 2014). Yet, some important work has provided insights into how coherent science instruction can support student af fect. Inkinen et al. (2020) tracked high school stu dents’ situational engagement during teacher-developed units in which students engaged in scienc e practices to answer an overarching question over the course of six to t en lessons, and they found that the student involve ment in the practices of constructing explanations and developing and using models was associated with increased situational engagement. Kubsch et al. (2023) found that student s in units that were developed following the princi ples of project-based pedagogy (Krajcik & Shin, 2014) became more mastery driven and less performance oriented. In a systematic review of affective outcomes in K-12 science education, Potvin and Hasni (2014) found that “inquirybased” and “problem-based” interventions had positive effects on students’ interest, motivation, and attitude; they noted that most “hands-on” activities did not have such a positive effect. This discrepancy between the effects of inquiry-and problem-based approaches versus hands-on approaches hints at the role  of coherence in supporting positive affective outcomes, as the term “hands-on” generally refers to simply handling science mat erials rather than sustained engagement with a coherent sequence of investigations (Furtak & Penuel, 2019). In summary, there is consistent high-quality evidence that instruction designed to be co herent can promote student achievement in science. Yet, althou gh many researchers have built strong theoretical a rguments for why coherent science instruction supports student motivation, interest, and attitudes toward science (e.g., Blumenfeld et al., 1991), relatively little empiric al evidence exists to establish a connection betwee n coherent science instruction and affective outcomes. Instead, researchers have started  to question the extent to which the type of “premeditated coherence” that may accompany  pre-developed curricular materials bars students from seeking coherence for themselves (Sikorski & Hammer, 2017); effectively limiting learning and identity development, as pre-determined activities and outcomes threaten student s’ agency in shaping the knowledge production and practices of their classroom communi ty (Miller et al., 2018). Based on the existing emp irical evidence this critique is hard to answer, as investigations into the role of coherence have focused on the effects of instruction designed to be coherent and neglected s tudent perceptions of coherence (cf., Penuel et al., 2022). In this paper, we aim to address this gap in the current knowledge base of the field by focusing on the extent to which student affect and perceptions of coherence mediate  student learning during their participation in sci ence instruction designed to be coherent. We ask the fol lowing research questions:  (1) How do students perceive the intra-unit coherence of units designed to be coherent?  (2) How are students’ prior knowledge, interest, instructional context, and mode of instruction related to students’ perceptions of intra-unit coherence?

## Methods

This study draws on data that was collected during a large curriculum development project. The project produced a set of short (4-6 class period s long) instructional units to support students’ progressio n in developing understanding of energy across a range of different topics in middle school. Instructional units The design of the units followed project-based learning design principles (Krajcik & Shin, 2 014), i.e., all units were motivated by a driving questions and engaged s tudents in a diverse set of scientific practices, e mphasizing knowledge-in-use about energy (e.g., Harris et al., 2019). The units covered a diverse range of contexts (e.g., Roller-coaster, Laptop, Trampoline) and were used in grades 7 to 10. Contexts, phenomena, and activities were chosen and designed using storyline principles (Reiser et al., 2021), e.g., choosing relevant contexts and focusing on figuring out phenomena to support students in developing a need-to-know. Based on the energy learning progression proposed by Neumann et al. (2013), all units were designed to follow a three-phase structure: In the introduction phase (1), students engage with phenomena that lead to a driving question. During the work phase (2), students engage in scientific practices to answer a first sub driving question and reactivate prior knowledge about forms of energ y. Then, answering another sub driving question requires students to make connections between forms of energ y as they consider transformations between these fo rms. Addressing the last sub driving question helps to consolidate and deepen what was learned before. Finally, in the reflection phase (3) students use what they learned during the previous work phase to answer the overall driving question from the introduction phase. In sum, the units were designed with the aim that students experience a high level of intra-unit coherence. Inter-unit coherence was not a design-criterion, as the CRUs were intended to be used flexibly at various points in the state curriculum. The units were implemented using the digital learni ng environment moodle. The tasks within the units covered a wide range of task types, ranging from multiple choice tasks, to (short) constructed response tasks such as constructing explanations, and conducting experi ments. The units were designed to be used in a blen ded learning setting, i.e., there were frequent transitions between collaborative group work and classroom discussions and working in the digital learning environment.

Students’ learning was assessed  in three ways: 1) students’ learning during the unit was assessed using student artifacts from the activities preceding the assessment of coherence (i.e., the activities related to sub driving question 2;  T2), 2) students’ performance across all activities of the unit, and 3) students’  performance on the energy understanding test prior  to (T1) and after (T3) each unit. Interest included students’ interest in the upcoming unit (T1) and situational interest assessed at the same time (T2) as the coherence perceived by  students. ## Instruments and measures

Coherence Coherence as perceived by the students was assessed using three items adopted from Zivic et al. (2018). All items addressed the extent to which students perceived th at the activities they had just completed helped th em to meet the different goals of the unit. The first item (C1) focused on the extent to which students considered the activities they had just completed helpful for meeting the goal that these activities addressed (i.e., “I know how the activities I just did help me to answer [insert sub drivin g question 2]”). We refer to this aspect as the ret rospective aspect of coherence or retrospective coherence. The second item (C2) focused on the extent to which students considered the activities they had just completed helpful for directing them towards the next goal (i.e., “After the activities I just did, I know why I want to answer the question [insert sub driving question 3] next”). We refer to  this aspect as the prospective aspect of coherence, or prospect ive coherence. The third item (C3) focused on  the extent to which students considered the activities they had j ust completed helpful for making progress towards a nswering the overarching goal of the unit (i.e., “I know how  the activities I just did help me to answer [inser t unit driving question]”). This aspect is referred to as the in-toto aspect of coherence or in-toto coherence. Students had three answer options for each question: “Yes”, “No”, and “Not sure”. In this study, options “No” and “Not sure” were both considered to indicat e a lack of perceived coherence (i.e., “Not sure” w as recoded to “No” in the analysis). This decision was made because students who are not sure if they see connections between what they have been doing and different goals of the unit apparently do not perceive a sense of coherence. Learning during the unit All student artifacts recorded in the digital learn ing environment were scored as a measure of student s’ learning. Closed tasks were scored as either correct or incor rect. Tasks that required open-ended answers and emphasized conceptual understanding, such  as explanations, were scored based on the knowledge-integration framework (Linn, 2006) using a rubric adapted from Lee and Liu (2010). Tasks that emphasized the process characteristic of scientific practices (planning and carrying out investigations, analyzing data) were scored using a rubric based on Bernholt and Parchmann (2011). To obtain a measure of students’ learning in the activities immediately preceding the assessment of coherence, scores of their perfor mance on these activities were averaged. To obtain a measure of students’ learning across the units, scores of their performance were aggregated across all activities. Energy understanding Students learning across the units was  assessed using grade-specific versions of the energy concept assessment by Neumann et al. (2013). The tests were scaled by estimating a Rasch model for the combined pre-and post-test data. The model infit was within the range of 0.8 – 1.2 which is within the bounds recommended by Bond & Fox (2015). Students’ abilities were estimated using weighted likelihood estimation, with a reliability of.61. Interest and situational interest Interest was measured using an instrument by Gaspar d et al. (2015). Here interest in conceptualized fo llowing person-object theory which states that the interest relati on to an object (in this case the contents of the u nit) is primarily defined by value appraisals. The instrument consists of five Likert-type items (e.g., “I think the contents of the unit will be important for me.”). Students i ndicated their agreement with the statements on a s cale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much). The reliability of the instrument was satisfactory with ω =.94. Situational interest was measured by adapting the w ording of the instrument utilized to assess interes t such that it could be used during the unit (“The current contents of the unit are important for me”). The reliability of the instrument was satisfactory with ω =.74.

## Participants

The units were enacted by a total of 40 teachers and 1500 students. Teachers were free to choose the u nits they wanted to enact and did so based on their own and t heir classes needs. Further, due to COVID some teac hers implemented the units online. All tea chers participated in professional learning opportu nities offered by the development team previously to and during the implementation of the units. Due to teachers choosing which unit to implement, some units were implemented more often than others. Further, due to COVID, some unit implementations we re aborted, modified and shortened so extensively b y teachers that they no longer represented key elem ents of the design principles. COVID led also to ex tensive missing data due to students missing parts of a unit. To address these issues, we did not use the whole dataset for our analyses. Instead, we considered only data from implementations meeting the following criteria: 1) only from implementations which were, based on fieldnotes and  moodle logs, completed and faithful, i.e., teacher s enacted all parts of the units and followed the curriculum closely, 2) only when the students answered the coherence items,

3) only when the students completed the pre-and post-tests.

Analyses

## Research question 1: Coherence and learning

To investigate the relation between students’ perceived coherence and their learning, we conducted two analyses:

1) a variable-centered analysis and 2) a person-centered analysis.

For the variable-centered analysis we focused on the individual aspe cts of coherence and their relationship to students’ learning, statistically controlling for effects of the unit, i.e., which unit was implemented, and mode of implementation (online vs. in-class). To do so we ran multiple regression models. We used students’ learning during the activities preceding the assess ment of coherence, across the unit and, as measured  by the energy understanding post-test as outcome variables. These measures of lea rning vary in how close they are to the point in time when students’ perceived coherence was assessed – with students’ learning during the activities preceding the assessment of coherence being the clo sest and the energy understanding post-test being the m ost distal. Further we varied the measure of coherence by using the three different items C1, C2, and C3 to represent different aspects of coherence as an independent variable. In the person-centered analysis we investigated the relationship between the eight p ossible answer patterns that result from three dichotomously coded  coherence items on students’ learning.  Like in the variablecentered analysis, we ran multiple models (ANOVA) that examined the effect of students’ perceived coh erence patterns as the independent variable on different measures of learning as a dependent variable (students’ learning during the activities immediately preceding the assessment of coherence, across the unit, and as measured by the energy understanding test), while adjusting for eff ects of the unit and mode of implementation on stud ents’ learning. With eight different patterns of coherenc e, the number of possible comparisons becomes quite  large, potentially leading to error inflation. For this reason, we used post-hoc Tukey-tests to adjust for inflated error rates.

## Research question 2: Factors affecting perceived coherence

When investigating the factors that affect ed students’ perceived coherence, we considered students’ prior knowledge as measured by the energy understanding test administered prior to the unit (the pre-test), interest and situational interest, the mode of implementation, and the unit in which students were participating. Like in the variable-centered analysis to address research question 1, we performed separate analyses for each of the three coherence items C1  (retrospective coherence), C2  (prospective coherence), and C3  (in-toto coherence) using binomial logistic multi-level modeling. The models included students’ response to the different coherence items as the dependent variable and mode of implementation, unit, prior knowledge, interest, and situational interest as independent variables.

## Discussion

The aim of this study was to shed light on how stud ents’ perceived coherence is related to their learn ing and the contextual and personal factors that influence students’ experience of coherence. Researchers have suggested for some time that the coherence of instruction is an important promoter of students’ learning (e.g., Fortus & Krajcik, 2012). A range of instructional models have been proposed as a basis for designing coherent instruction. However, more recently other researchers have questioned the  extent to which instructional coherence supports s tudents learning or inhibits it – suggesting that learning goes along with the quest to establish coherence as the result of a perceived incoherence (Sikorski & Hammer, 2017). So  far, beyond reportedly positiv e effects of instruction designed to be coherent, little evidence has been provided that the coherence or lack thereof perceived by students supports learning. In our study we have investigated the effect of intra-unit coherence on students learning. More specifically we have investigated the role of retrospective coherence (i.e., the alignment between the instructional activities students have completed and the respective goal(s)), prospective coherence (i.e., the alignment between the instructional activities students have completed and goal(s) related to  the subsequent activities), and per-toto coherence (i.e., the alignment between the instruct ional activities students have completed and overar ching goal of the unit). We have investigated the effect of th ese aspects of coherence on learning at three diffe rent levels, students learning performance in the activities imm ediately preceding the assessment of coherence, lea rning across the unit and learning as measured by an energy understanding test administered prior to and after the unit. Our results reveal an effect of retrospective coher ence and per-toto coherence on student learning over the activities preceding the assessment of coherence, a s well as across the unit; not on learning as measu red by the energy understanding test. In addit ion, no effect was found for prospective coherence.  One possible explanation is that as students fail to see the how the activit ies they have just completed contribute to the goal  of the next set of activities, there is a perceived lack of coheren ce between the current activities and future activities, suc h that while students see how what they are doing contribu tes to the overarching goal they don’t seem how eac h set of activities is cumulatively building towards that goal. Students learn in each set of activities and across the different sets of activities (i.e., the unit) but fail to connect the knowledge they acquire in the different sets of activities with each other so that they develop a deeper understanding. Anothe r possible, and more simple explanation, may be that simply don’t progress enough in their energy u nderstanding that it could be explained by the cohe rence explained. Either way, our study provides evidence for the role that instructional coherence, and more importantly also that students experience this instructional co herence, positively influences their learning – suggesting that the idea that learning may require students to experience a certain level of incoherence. Although there is a slight chance that had students perceived greater incoherence, they may have not just learned in individual activities or across the unit, but also developed a deeper level of understanding. The fact that students’ learning both in the activities preceding the assessment of coherence and across the unit is correlated with their learning as measured by the energy understanding test casts substantial doubt on this conclusion. In terms of factors affecting students perceived co herence, a notable result of our study was that the factor most strongly linked with perceived coherence was students’ situational interest. As it stands, the experience of instructional coherence is closely linked with situational interest. This may suggest that instructional coherence may not only have a direct effect on students’ learning but also may have an indirect affect by increasing students’ situational interest and thus fostering their engag ement in future activities (although not necessarily th eir experience of coherence of these future activities as discussed above). The most important limitation of our work is that a lthough this is the first study to investigate stud ents’ learning as a function of the instructional coheren ce perceived by students, students’ perceived coher ence was only assessed once, roughly after two thirds of the unit. Hence, we can neither investiga te a possible variation in students perceived coherence across the unit nor th e extent to which perceived (prospective) coherence  at one point in time positively affects perceived (retrosp ective) coherence at a later point in time. As a results, we must refer to future studies with respect to the stabili ty of perceived coherence over time and the relatio nship between the perceived coherence at different points in time. This also limits our ability to draw conclusions about a lack of perceived coherence between sets of activities may have led to the limited progress in students’ understanding of energy. This also will have to be further explored in future studies, specifically designed to this purpose. Despite the limitations of the design and available  data, this study provides first insights into the effect of students perceived coherence on their learning, beyond a general effect of instruction designed to be coherent. This study, however, makes a major contribution to the discussion of whether it is important to design  for instructional coherence and for students to experie nce this coherence, or to design for students to ex perience a certain level of incoherence as that should drive t heir learning. Our study clearly supports the first  hypothesis, effectively providing support for future efforts to design coherent instruction.
