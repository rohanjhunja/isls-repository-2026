# Fostering Student Agency Through Game Redesign: A Discourse Analysis of Teacher Positioning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores the complex dynamics of teacher-student interactions that arise when grade 7 students redesign a tabletop game as culturally sustaining pedagogy. Situated in a larger design-based research project and set against the backdrop of the 202 2/2023 FIFA World Cup, this analysis focuses on one group of boys who leveraged their shared passion for football. Using positioning theory as the analytical lens, we examined how teachers’ discursive moves profoundly impacted students ’ agency, collaboration, and engagement. The findings illustrate that by ceding conversational control and positioning students as experts, teachers can create more equitable learning environments where students feel empowered to draw upon their own identities and culture to drive their learning forward.

In today’s increasingly diverse classrooms, there is a need for pedagogical approaches that actively sustain the rich linguistic and cultural resources students bring. Implementing Culturally Sustaining Pedagogies (CSP) (Paris,

2012) requires more than just innovative curriculum; its successful application often hinges on the subtle,

moment-to-moment discursive choices made by teachers. Within our overarching research of redesigning games as CSP, we investigate the role of the teacher-student discourse in realizing the full potential of this pedagogical approach. In particular, we ask how a teacher’s discursive positioning might impact student agency and engagement within a tabletop game redesign project. By conducting a micro-level discourse analysis of one group of middle school students  u sing Positioning Theory (Davies & Harré, 1990) as an analytical lens, this study illuminates how different teacher stances, from authoritative to facilitative (Grasha, 1994), profoundly shape how students leverage their cultural resources and take ownership of their learning.

## Theoretical background

This research is grounded in pedagogical frameworks that center students’ cultural and linguistic identities. CSP, as conceptualized by Paris (2012), calls for support ing “young people in sustaining the cultural and linguistic competence of their communities while simultaneously offering access to dominant cultural competence” (p. 95). Meaningful collaboration, a  central practice for enacting CSP, allows students to learn from one another,  while positioning their own languages and cultural knowledge as valuable resources (Le et al, 20 18; Stein et al., 2024). Implementing this approach, however,  presents a significant challenge that educators ha ve struggled to address for decades (Blatchford et al., 2003; Cohen, 1994): a fundamental transformation of the teacher ’s role from a “sage on the stage” to a “guide on the side” (Kaendler et al., 2015), who understands group dynamics and ensures diverse voices are heard.  Simply placing students in groups does not guarantee equitable outcomes; without intentional facilitation, existing social hierarchies can be reinforced (Barron, 2003). One powerful context for enacting CSP is through creative, student-driven activities like tabletop game design. This process invites students to infuse their personal narratives and cultural identities into their work, positioning them as expert creators (Gierus et al., 2024;  Kim et al., 2015). T he culturally sustaining potential of game design is realized only when students possess genuine autonomy to treat their designs as dynamic, evolving artifacts. However, the success of such an approach is not guaran teed by the activity alone  but deeply mediated by the teacher’s role (Vygotsky, 1978). A CSP-aligned environment requires a complex negotiation of classroom authority, where a teacher ’s moment-to-moment discursive choices can either validate student agency or unintentionally reproduce traditional, hierarchical classroom structures  between the teacher and the students or even among students (Cohen, 1994; Kaendler et al., 2015; Manke, 1997). To understand these shifting hierarchies, this study employs Positioning Theory (Davies & Harré, 1990) as an analytic framework. While CSP provides the equitable goal  and game redesign serves as the collaborative vehicle, Positioning Theory offers the analytic mechanism to observe how agency is discursively negotiated. By examining micro-level linguistic choices, such as pronoun usage, we can trace how classroom authority is dynamically shared or hoarded (Harré & van Langenhove, 1999). It is important to no te the boundaries of this lens: it captures the externalized performance of identity and power within discourse, rather than measuring 1372 internal psychological states or long-term academic outcomes (Green et al., 2020). Ultimately, it offers a precise vocabulary for examining how collaborative game redesign functions as a truly culturally sustaining environment. This inquiry is part of a multi-year (2020-2023) design-based research (DBR) project conducted in a culturally diverse urban middle school in Western Canada. DBR is an iterative methodology that seeks to develop theory and refine educational design through research in authentic learning environments (Brown, 1992). The data for this paper comes from the third iteration of the project, which took place over five months with approximately 100 Grade 7 students. Students worked in small, teacher-selected groups to redesign the cooperative tabletop game Forbidden Island (a game where players work together to collect treasures and escape a sinking island) to reflect their own interests and identities in interdisciplinary learning lessons (humanities and science). The project aligned with interdisciplinary curricular goals. Social Studies’ mandate for “Dimensions of Thinking” and “Deliberative Inquiry” (Alberta Education, 2007) mapped seamlessly to the creative and critical processes of inventing and analyzing game rules. Simultaneously, Science competencies (Alberta Education,

2003) were mirrored in the project’s demand for collaborative problem-solving; playtesting mechanics forced

students to engage in iterative cycles of hypothesis, testing, and revision to build a functional final product. The goal for the group of students in this paper was how they make a game about football, which was their common interest. As a central “problem” to solve, it translates into  “how can we transform the mechanics of Forbidden Island to create a new, functional game about football?” When students realized a rule did not work, they had to brainstorm a fix, engaging in a cycle of hypothesis, testing, and revision—a key scientific practice. This project forced students to explain their ideas to each other, listen to feedback, and work together to build a final product. A rich, multimodal dataset was collected, including approximately 20 hours of video recordings for each focal group, digital photographs of game artifacts, observational field notes, and semi-structured student and teacher interviews. This initial coding phase served to filter the expansive dataset and isolate key interactional episodes, defined as bounded interaction sequences where authority over the game design or group process was actively negotiated or contested. To investigate the nuances of these specific interactions, the second stage of analysis applied the Positioning Theory framework to key interactional episodes. Specifically, we conducted a micro-level discourse agency within the interaction (Wilkinson & Kitzinger, 2003). By examining these specific discursive moves, we analyzed how the language used by teachers and students positioned them in relation to one another and negotiated classroom authority in real time. We describe one group, Football Legends, to illustrate these dynamics in depth. The Football Legends (a student-chosen name) was a group of three boys, Milo, Mo, and Amir (pseudonyms), who were passionate about soccer (Figure 1a). Their shared knowledge about the sport became the central resource for their game redesign.  The interactions that unfolded revealed a tension between the teachers ’ attempts to formally structure these skill-building activities and the students’ more organic, passion-driven approach to the same tasks.  While this analysis focuses deeply on the Football Legends  to illustrate the m icro-level discursive moves, similar power dynamics and shifting teacher roles were observed across the broader dataset.  Analyzing interactions with their teachers, primarily Mr. Ken  (Social Studies) and Ms. Clara (S cience), revealed three key themes regarding the impact of teacher positioning on student engagement and agency. Theme 1: Authoritative positioning undermining student agency Early in the project, Mr. Ken took on the “ teacher-guard” role. His consistent discursive  patterns—frequently issuing directives such as “ Did you fill out the worksheet? ” while circulating among groups—demonstrated his treatment of  worksheet completion as a proxy for student engagement and curricular compliance. When he observed Mo filling out the required worksheet, he  positioned the rest of the group as off-task, inadvertently pitting them against one another:  Mr. Ken: “Milo, Amir, it’s not all up to Mo to write this. You need to work together.” / Milo (in a very upset tone): “ I literally did half the work. This guy just started writing.” / Mr. Ken: “OK. Make sure you’re all working together. ” / Milo: “ I always get into sh**. ” Milo immediately reacted defensively to this authoritative positioning, extensively using the pronoun “I” to reclaim his agency. At the same time, he alienated Mo by referring to him as “ this guy,” stripping Mo of his team identity and making him an outsider. The “shut down” of social participation and critical thinking was immediate ly observable. Field notes 1373 also indicate that soon after this interaction with Mr. Ken, collaborative game design ceased. The students reverted to off-task chatting, Mo stopped writing entirely, and the rest of the group actively excluded Mo from their interactions. By positioning himself as an enforcer of the worksheet, Mr. Ken inadvertently fractured the group’s cohesion, shutting down the collaborative skills the curriculum was designed to foster. In another early session, observing the group off-task, Mr. Ken initiated a public reprimand from across the room, shouting, “ Stop fooling around. Fix the game. ” This direct, authoritative command was immediately met with Milo ’s defiant, absolute retort, “ Nothing needs changing. ” As Mr. Ken approached the table, the discourse rapidly deteriorated into a power struggle characterized by short, staccato utterances and word choices: Mr. Ken: “I doubt nothing needs to change. There is … nobody makes a creation t hat’s perfect.” / Milo: “It is though.” / Mr. Ken: “It is not.” / Milo: “It is.” / Mr. Ken: “It is not.” / Milo: “We literally didn’t mess up on one thing.” The pacing of the teacher-student interaction shows neither party engaged with the game ’s actual design but rather fought for discursive dominance. When Mr. Ken subsequently shifted tactics to interrogate whether the game could be played by another group without verbal instructions, the rapid pace continued, with Mr. Ken repeatedly interrupting Milo’s attempts to explain their process. The interaction ended abruptly and dismissively when Mr. Ken issued a final directive (“You have to write it down”) and walked away, ignoring Milo’s response of “I already wrote it down.” Ultimately, Mr. Ken’s efforts yielded no productive outcome. The teacher’s role of a “guard” or “task monitor” proved ineffective; students not only stopped working on the project after th ese interactions but also stopped being a  part of a cohesive team. This “ teacher-guard” positioning, intended to promote engagement  and productive group work, proved counterproductive, creating a power struggle rather than a collaborative environment. These instances demonstrate that when teachers position themselves as enforcers, they can inadvertently shut down the very engagement  they seek to foster. Theme 2: Teacher-student expert-novice reversal fostering student agency During a walk-around, Mr. Ken approached the team and explained the importance of a game backstory. Without engaging in conversation about their game ideas, he urged them to connect their game to curriculum topics. Mr. Ken adopted an expert role without understanding students’ current process, which was met with a clear lack of students’ attention to his messages. The moment he turned away, students  reverted to distracting behaviors like bantering and throwing pencils at each other, largely ignoring his suggestions. A significant shift occurred later when the group appeared disengaged. At this time, he  accepted their answer of being “ finished” without further inquiry or insisting on compliance. Mr. Ken simply asked an inquisitive, non-evaluative question: “How many cards do you need? ” Following his accepting response, the students’ discourse shifted markedly from definitive closure to renewed deliberation: “ Let’s do the changes...”, “How do we set it up?” The teacher’s act of ceding control was pivotal. It gave the students the space and agency to reconsider their own work. Mr. Ken ’s inquisitive tone  and willingness to let the students own their process, empowered them to take greater responsibility for their project. He moved from positioning himself as the one who knew what was best for their project to positioning them as the capable authors of their own work. The seemingly most effective instructional moments  occurred when teachers positioned themselves as active listeners. The science teacher, Ms. Clara, exemplified this approach  when she approached the group to understand their work, asking, “can you explain to me how you play? ” She showed complete immersion in their words, frequently paraphrasing to ensure accurate understanding: Amir: “So if there’s four players, it’s two against two... you can collect the ones in that area... ” / Ms. Clara: “So it’s … if you would kick a goal on that side, you are trying to collect the treasures on that side ?” / Amir: “Yeah. And once something is gone... that’s a goal for them. Milo: “And if someone gets a treasure, no one else can get it, it's gone. ” / Ms. Clara: “It kind of stays the same, but you can’t get that treasure anymore?”  / Milo: “Or maybe we could… ” / Amir: “If this happens…” / Milo: “We could take the card out, so that there is no extra card. ” After this back-and-forth exchange of explanation (from students) and active listening and paraphrasing (from the teacher), the students began to identify holes in their design. They started brainstorming solutions in front of the teacher, saying things li ke, “Or maybe we could...” and completed each other ’s thoughts. The students were undeniably in charge of their game design iteration, evaluating it and holding full decision-making power. Ms. Clara did not lead them to these realizations 1374 but rather clarified what she heard, allowing their own insights to emerge. In this moment of student-led discovery, the team was actively engaged in the problem-solving and decision-making processes central to the Science and Social Studies curricula. By positioning them as experts, Ms. Clara created the conditions for authentic curriculum engagement to occur, driven by the students’ own inquiry and identity rather than by teacher directive. Theme 3: Teacher as translator leading to student creative authority Teachers at times adopted the role of a “ translator,” rephrasing formal or ambiguous instructions into accessible language that students could act upon. For example, when the team found a worksheet confusing, Ms. Clara translated “your new game’s theme” to “What’s your game about? What story are you telling?” While the original language was not overly complex, her composed demeanor and “translation” provided the reassurance the students needed. Clarifying instructions in this instance allowed the students to immerse themselves in the creative process as the designers of their game. However, the dynamic shifted when Ms. Clara offered a specific design suggestion, asking, “Are you going to name the players? Your favorite players? ” In this moment, she positioned herself as a contributor of ideas. N ot responding  to her  idea, the students continued  brainstorming with each other. They disregarded the suggestion at this moment as well as in their final game, demonstrating their strong sense of project ownership. While they valued her guidance, they were protective of their creative agency. Another example occurred when  students struggled to understand the task of listing their game pieces for their final game and quickly called over Mr. Ken. He  helped the group by rephrasing his lecture into simpler language that made immediate sense to the students.  As they resumed brainstorming, he remained nearby and answered follow-up questions with flexible, non-prescriptive responses like “ I would do that ” or “you can call them character pieces,” signaling that the instructions were open to interpretation. His consistent use of pronouns like “you” and “I” maintained a subtle professional distance, positioning the students as the authors. This contrasted with the students ’ frequent use of “ we,” which highlighted their cohesive group identity and shared ownership of the design decisions.  Ultimately, the “ translator” role proved most effective when it empowered students by clarifying tasks without infringing on their creative authority. Eventually, the teacher’s role in this context was to create the conditions for the students’ own knowledge and creativity to flourish.  T he students ’ peer-led collaboration was characterized by the frequent use of the pronoun “we,” signaling a collective ownership of the project. They drew on each other’s specialized knowledge, functioning as peer experts  when teachers empowered them as experts in their game. The group was able to identify Amir as the resident expert on the minutia of football while Milo taught Amir and Mo how to draw the different football stadiums on their game tiles (Figure 1b). This fluid negotiation of expertise within the group was possible when teachers supported creating a game that was authentic to their interests, moving away from the directives to fit their game into the curricular contents. A cross these episodes, a clear trajectory emerge d: when teachers defaulted to authoritative “ guard” positioning (Theme 1), student agency and collaboration collapsed. Conversely, when teachers consciously shifted their discursive positioning to that of an inquisitive novice (Theme

2) or a clarifying translator (Theme 3), they successfully navigated the tension between curriculum demands and

CSP, creating the necessary discursive space for genuine student expertise and shared ownership to flourish. The case of the Football Legends  provides a compelling microcosm of the power of teacher positioning in a culturally sustaining classroom. The analysis demonstrates a clear contrast between the outcomes of authoritative versus facilitative teacher discourse. While teacher monitoring and structure can be valuable, particularly for ensuring safety or managing scarce resources, our analysis suggests that when the pedagogical goal is fostering student agency and culturally sustaining collaboration, positioning oneself primarily as an enforcer of compliance can undermine these very aims (Theme 1). When the teacher positioned himself as a “guard,” seeking to manage and control student work, the result was defensiveness and disengagement. However, when teachers positioned themselves as curious facilitators, ceding epistemic authority and positioning students as experts, the students responded with increased agency, deeper critical thinking, and more authentic collaboration. The findings have significant implications for successful CSP. Educators must attend to the micro-level interactions through which learning and identity are negotiated, such as consciously asking open-ended questions (Theme 2) and translating abstract jargon into action-oriented prompts (Theme 3). In addition, meeting curriculum outcomes does not require rigid authority, but trust ing students to draw upon their cultural resources.  Students engaged most deeply with core competencies (critical thinking, collaboration) when teachers allowed student passion to drive the learning. For CSP to succeed, teachers must not impose curriculum onto  students’ culture, but create a space where students meet curricular goals through their culture.
