# Elementary Students’ Voices on Embodied, Emotional, and Lived Experiences of Learning Data

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how elementary students’ data literacy is shaped through the entanglement of embodied, emotional, and lived experiences in working with data. Drawing on post-interviews with fifth graders who participated in a five-day data curriculum, this analysis finds that students’ embodied, emotional, and lived experiences were deeply intertwined and shape how they understood, used, and valued data by assigning it personal meaning that linked their past, present, and imagined future. Students’ reflection also demonstrated their perceived value of data as a rhetorical, problem-solving, life tool that supported persuasion, decision-making, daily practices, and livelihoods. These findings highlight the importance of designing data curricula that intentionally leverage embodied, emotional, and lived experiences to help students develop personally meaningful understanding and relationships with data.

In today’s data-rich world, the ability to read, analyze, and effectively communicate data has become essential since data is manifested in many aspects of daily life, which highlight s the widespread need for all individuals to develop data skills for real-world problems (Boaler et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2022; Valtonen et al., 2019).  Yet, research on data science and data literacy has primarily focused on high school and undergraduates, leaving younger students with limited opportunities to develop these competencies (Herro et al., 2025; Pangrazio & Sefton-Green, 2020). Responding to this gap, researchers have called for expanding data literacy in elementary education (Adisa et al., 2023; Cottone et al., 2023; Lee & Delaney, 2022; Martinez & LaLonde, 2022; Podworny et al., 2025). Despite these efforts, data literacy in elementary contexts remains an understudied area (Herro et al., 2025; Podworny et al., 2025). Additionally, current instruction is often embedded in mathematics, with a narrow focus on quantitative reasoning using decontextualized data (Adisa et al., 2023), which risks reducing data literacy to technical skills, neglecting opportunities for students to assess the purposes of data collection and data application in the real world (Noushad et al., 2022), and may result in a “math of morgue” – a disembodied, detached, and unemotional view of math in data literacy education (Matuk et al, 2022), and exclude a large cohort of people who are less comfortable with math from entering this field (Bhargava & D'Ignazio, 2017). This means the field is not as broadly inviting and there are already reports that it is not diverse, for example, in the data science profession, historically marginalized groups are underrepresented (Matuk et al, 2022). Therefore, there is a growing need for innovative approaches to make data science learning  more inclusive and accessible for a broader audience (Jiang et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2022).

To address these gaps, this study designed a five-day da ta curriculum built on students’  embodied, emotional, and lived experiences of working with data to support elementary students’ development of foundational data competencies. While pre/post-test analyses demonstrated significant learning gains (Zhou, 2026), it remains important to understand what students noticed about the process of learning to inform the next iterations. Therefore, this study asks: 1) how do elementary students perceive their embodied, emotional, and lived experiences as being drawn on while they work with data? 2) how do elementary students perceive the value of data after participating in this study?

## Theoretical framework

The field of Learning Sciences has increasingly emphasized the need to foreground the humanistic aspect of students’ engagement with data (Bhargava & D'Ignazio, 2017; Lee et al., 2021a). One promising research strand highlights the role of visceral experience in data practice, such as physically creating/manipulating data objects, using coordinated movements to represent data trends in graphs, and expressing emotions about data (Kahn et al., 2022; DesPortes et al., 2022). Building on this research strand, this study hypothesizes that embodiment is such a visceral experience that can serve as a powerful resource for developing elementary students’ data literacy. In exploring this possibility, this study draws on embodied cognition (Danish et al., 2020; Nathan, 2021), emotion from data feminism (D'Ignazio & Klein, 2020), and lived experience in  the humanistic approach to data science (Lee et al., 2021a) to adopt a broad view of embodiment as an umbrella term that encompasses a range of physical experiences, including whole-body movements, gestures, crafting and manipulating physical objects, along with 397 emotions and lived experiences that are intertwined with those activities. This work aims to understand how each of these aspects mediates the others in unique ways.

Embodied cognition highlights how human thinking and learning are inseparable from the body and our sensorimotor interactions with the world (Danish et al., 2020; Kersting et al., 2021; Nathan, 2021). In data literacy education, students’ bodies can become part of the representation to anchor abstract concepts in movements and multimodal experience, for example, playing the role of both data and data representation (Weisberg & Newcombe 201 7). Studies of data dance and data theater where students use coordinated movements or performance to represent graphs and numerical values show that such design helps students notice patterns and deepen conceptual understanding (Bhargava et al., 2022; DesPortes et al., 2022; Sommer & Polman, 2017). The sociocultural perspective draws attention to moments when students coordinate their movements and interpret each other’s positions because these interactions can shift their attention from isolated data points to broader patterns (DesPortes et al., 2022; Ma, 2017).

Traditional approaches to data prioritize neutrality, minimalism, and detachment from emotion (Börner et al., 2019). In contrast, data feminism argues that emotion is not a distraction but influences what patterns students notice, how they interpret those patterns, and how they relate to the real-world problem of data (D'Ignazio & Klein, 2020). This interacts with embodied cognition as embodied activities often evoke emotional connections that make abstract concepts more meaningful and relevant. Students often report excitement when enacting data through gestures, movements, and performance, and how those emotions help make data more concretized and relatable (Bhargava et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2007). Additionally, research on data visceralization show that multisensory interactions, such as data sculptures and physicalization, enable s data to be heard, felt, ingested, rather than only seen, through which it fosters stronger emotional connection and engagement with data compared to traditional visualizations (Bhargave & D’Ignazio, 2017; Dasgupta & Hill, 2017; Lupton, 2017; van Wart et al., 2020). Despite the importance of emotion in how students engage with data, its role in education remains understudied (Nathan, 2021).

In scholarship built on phenomenological perspective, lived experience refers to how individuals engage with the world through their embodied, first-person point of view (Kersting et al., 2021). Here, the body is not only a physical structure but an experiential one that both shapes and is shaped by the world (Popova & RączaszekLeonardi, 2020). Traditional approaches to data literacy often use decontextualized datasets, which disconnect learning from students’ real-world experiences of data (Adisa et al., 202 3). In prior study on data literacy, leveraging lived experience can take the form of using data collected by or about students’ experience, thus drawing on their prior knowledge and past experience s (Drake et al., 2017). Studies show that when students analyze self-generated data, such as steps tracked during recess, they connect statistical reasoning to lived experiences. This connection brings in emotional responses, including a sense of competition, which leads them to notice discrepancies between their memory and the wearable devices’ measurement, through which  they develop critical perspectives on data tools and reflect on issues of data ownership and privacy (Lee et al., 2021b). Beyond sensor data, projects like Dear Data (Lupi & Posavec, 2016) illustrate how self-tracked experiences, such as recording of goodbyes for a week, can be transformed into creative and expressive visualizations of everyday life that carry both personal meaning and emotion.

Thus, these perspectives are interrelated and can be leveraged collectively to promote data literacy as a human-centered practice. Drawing on the intersection of these perspectives and prior work that has explored them individually or partially, this study designed four activities that combined embodied and tactile participation with emotional connections to students’ lived experiences of working with data. This analysis centers on what students noticed about their experiences rather thandirect measures of learning for insights into how embodied, emotional, and lived experiences of working with data is perceived by students. Design The present study is the first iteration of a two-cycle design-based research project aimed at developing elementary students’ data literacy through embodied, emotional, and lived experiences of working with data. The present more than 44% of students identified as minority and 75% as economically disadvantaged, 24% scored at or above the proficient level of math and reading. Twenty students participated in five sessions of approximately 35-40 minutes each. On day 4, an unplanned drill shortened the session to around 20 minutes. The unit topic was selected with the teacher to ensure it suited students’ interests. On Day 1, students were introduced to data and data science by searching for data in their classroom and comparing reading data to reading novels. They also reflected on creative ways to represent data, such as guessing the researcher’s food preference using arm length to represent a bar graph and showing fruit popularity in the U.S. using circle size. This activity aims to involve students in recognizing data as omnipresent in their daily lives and can be represented in multiple embodied and creative 398 ways. On Day 2, students collected data of their conversation, such as the number of times they clapped, laughed, or argued with each other, and then created artistic graphs to represent these numbers. They are encouraged to use familiar, personally meaningful symbols such as emoji, house, soccer ball, or hearts to design the data representations (see Figure 1).

Figure 1 The Granola Bar Commercial, Data Cards, and Data Graphing On Day 3-4, students worked with a public dataset from a local animal shelter. They were told to act as data scientists to sort animal data cards by variables such as age, size or breed, and were encouraged to connect those results to real-world problems, including medical care and food preparation at  the shelter. Then, they were assigned to a poster showing an animal and its data. They were told that they will embody a data point by role-playing the animal assigned to them in an adoption event. They were told to compare their data variables to adopters’ preferences to decide the chance of them being adopted (see Figure 2). As the researcher assigned each animal, students were asked about their preference to foster  connection. These activities were designed to evoke emotional connection with the data while grounding abstract variables in embodied experience. Figure 2 Animal Adoption Activity Day 5’s activity was inspired by a student who mentioned that data is everywhere, and you can find data in cereal in a conversation with the researcher. Thus, the researcher added an activity to involve students in examining nutrition labels on granola bars, analyz ing the data to decide which bar was the healthiest, and performing a commercial by role-playing salesmen to persuade others of their choice (see Figure 1 a). This activity aimed to help students use data from a real-world context to address real-world problems. Throughout the unit, students were introduced to digital graphs in CODAP (The Concord Consortium, n.d.) and encouraged to compare them with their physical data representations. For example, after sorting data cards or arranging granola bars, the researcher prompted them to compare their result to a digital graph. During the classroom activities, students did not have extended time using CODAP individually; instead, interacted with it mainly during individual interviews. 399

## Data and data analysis

To understand how students perceived these activities and learning of data, semi-structured interviews were conducted after the implementation. Seven consented students completed individual interviews lasting 30– 35 minutes. Each began with a brief introduction to CODAP, where the researcher demonstrated how to create graphs, replace variables, and apply filters. Students then independently explored the animal shelter datasets for 10-15 minutes before participating in an interview that covered five areas intended to highlight the role of embodied, emotional, and lived experiences: 1) first impressions of CODAP, 2) how physical activities supported understanding, 3) ranking the activities from the most to least favorite and why, 4) learning about data from eac h activity, and 5) recommendations for future activity design. This study reports on the analysis of students’ post-interviews. Interviews were first transcribed using the university’s automated transcription service for social science research and then manually cleaned. The analysis begins with structural coding (Saldaña, 2021) to organize the transcripts into six topics on students’ perception of the activities and learning: 1) how physical activities supported understanding of CODAP, 2) how physical activities supported understanding of data in general, 3) data collection of peer conversation and graphing, 4) animal data card, 5) animal adoption event, 6) granola bar commercial. Under each topic, individual student comments were compiled, which resulted in identification and categorization of large transcripts segmen ted by topic for further coding and analysis. Next, inductive open coding (Saldaña, 2021) was applied to remain close to students’ ideas. The researcher immersed themselves in the transcripts by iteratively reading and writing descriptive and analytical memos (Saldaña, 2021) about students’ recalled experiences, emotions, and perceived learning about data. During this process, prior codes from small-scale research on embodied approaches to data learning (Zhou et al., 2024; 2025), such as social interaction, complementarity of representations, were referenced to support the analysis. Following this round of coding, considerable overlap was observed across sections. For example, students expressed similar emotions during the animal adoption and granola bar com mercials and highlighted the social dimension and perceived learning of data in all activities. Thus, to answer the first research question, the codes were organized deductively based on the theoretical framework, which emphasizes embodied, emotional, and lived experiences as intertwined dimensions of embodiment. This combination of inductive and deductive coding process highlighted how the theoretical dimension of embodiment played out in students’ own words. During this process, the connections between those dimensions become apparent, for example, students’ reported embodying as an animal was tied to the feeling of empathy and future-oriented experiences. To illustrate how these dimensions interconnected with each other in a distinctive way  in each activity, the results section re-situated the dimensions within each activity along with students’ direct quotes to show how embodied, emotional, and lived experiences came together in unique ways to mediate students’ learning with data.

To understand students’ experiences of the learning through their embodied, emotional, and lived experiences of working with data, the researcher explicitly encouraged students to reflect on their learning during the interview and coded their reflections with attention to how they described what they believed they had learned about data. Following the thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2012), codes used for students’ understanding and learning of data in the inductive coding were clustered under three overarching categories and themes about: 1) data as a rhetorical tool, 2) data as a life tool, 3) data as a problem-solving tool. RQ1: How do elementary students perceive their embodied, emotional, and lived experiences as being drawn on while they work with data?

## Emotional and lived experiences in data collection and data graphing

The entanglement between emotion and lived experiences is shown in an activity where students were involved in collecting peer conversation data, transforming those data into self-designed graphs, and communicating their representational choices with others. At each stage of this direct experience of working wit h data, students foregrounded emotion as data that could be counted and represented, which shaped how they collected, represented, and shared their data-related ideas.

First, students viewed their lived experiences of social interactions with peers as data, “we can talk about like with the data, such as  the data was how many times we agreed, disagreed, laughed, and clapped.” While reflecting on this activity in the post-interview, none of them retold conversations content, but instead focused on how they quantified emotional moments such as disagreement as a whole, “you can see how many times you 400 disagreed instead of oh I disagree with this person.” Here, peer conversations were turned into data as students experienced collecting and quantifying their own social and emotional interactions. Next, students described the process of transforming their collected data into creative representations, a lived experience of graph design that deeply entangled with layered emotion, including joy, pride, and frustration. In designing these representations, they leveraged their imagination and personally joyful topics, “using my imagination, I was thinking of a different graph and a graph that I liked better or I understood more. So I was using how I can use the data to collect the data from the paper, and put it into the graph that I was thinking about, how I did was like a bigger heart, smaller heart. Bigger heat means we clap more. So I'm taking the data from the paper and putting it into technically, like my imagination of a bar graph.” Here, students demonstrated frequency thinking by mapping symbol size to quantity while connecting their imaginative design back to a familiar graph, in this case, a bar graph. The heart symbol carried dual meanings, mathematically it represented frequency through size; emotionally, it captured a joyful topic that mattered to the student, which collectively supported students’ perceived better understanding. Similarly, another student highlighted the power of using a personally joyful topic in data graph design, “I can learn with mine better because I have an understanding of soccer, and I really like soccer. So what I’m learning about something that’s exciting is that I really learned about it.” These reflections show how transforming data into representations grounded in students’ imagination and personal joy made data learning more accessible and enjoyable.

Students developed stronger ownership of their design through the lived experience of communicating and sharing self-designed representations. Students’ pride was evident when their ideas were recognized by peers, “I explained it to my older friends. They asked me with the lines around the heart, and I told them about it. They thought it was very cool and they wished they thought of that. That was really cool for me to use the graphs and stuff. This is like how to use different data”. This pride was also extended into advocacy for their designs, as one student urged the researcher to replace the researcher-designed flower graph with their own soccer ball graph when asked about suggestions for future  implementations, “my general feedback to keep all of these and then change this (flower graph) to the soccer.” This advocacy for their own representation revealed how eager students are to carry forward their representation beyond the immediate activity. However, the ownership of personal ideas could turn intonegative emotion when their idea was altered or misinterpreted by peers, “I wanted to do the hearts. That was my idea, but he was putting lines around like that doesn’t make sense. If I knew that like the first place. I've done like my idea and then explained it better.” Here, the emotional investment in representing the data made the design personally significant; thus, changes to it felt consequential. These reflections show students’ emotions were deeply woven into the lived experience of working with data and mediated how they designed, communicated, shared, defended, and advocated their data representations. Embodied and lived experiences in animal data cards The entanglement of embodied and lived experiences was evident in the animal data card activity, where students worked in groups to physically sort data cards representing newly rescued shelter animals as data scientists. Students reported the direct experience of organizing, filtering, and ranking data cards as positive because of how it supported their choices in working with the data, “The part that I liked most was getting to choose. From youngest to older, not old, rank them. That one the most. I like it the most. Cause, I feel like it was giving me a lot of data and helping me a lot.” The student further commented on how physically manipulating the cards supported their reasoning, “It’s helping me because I got to grab it and choose from the youngest to the oldest and then I got to read it. And then I was collecting all of that data in your head.” In this case, the act of physically sorting and organizing cards functioned like creating a data physicalization (Jansen et al., 201 5), through which the tactile manipulation of cards grounded the formation of mental structure of data order in an external world, where the visible and sensible arrangement of cards data (see Figure 1 b) reduces cognitive resources demands (Wilson, 2002). Not all of the students enjoyed this, though, as others reported difficulties in managing a large dataset in smaller groups, “I think this was kind of hard because it was hard to find all them because there's so much.” These comments suggested that while embodied and lived experiences played a central role in supporting cognitive processes, the activity organization can still lead different individuals to experience positive or negative emotions depending on how challenging it was for them.

Embodied, emotional, and lived experiences in acting as shelter animals for adoption Students’ reflections on embodying shelter animals for an adoption demonstrated the entanglement of embodied, emotional, and lived experiences. By embodying an animal, they mapped the data variables of the animal onto themselves, “we were pretending to be the animal s and we have to look at the cards to know what was about us, our genders, names, and stuff.” In this way, the abstract data variables became personally enacted features, “we 401 actually got to really be the animal instead of that just being a bunch of numbers, colors and stuff,” which offered students with a lived experience of reasoning with and about data as they physically embodied it. Next, participating in the adoption event provided an immersive experience that stimulated emotional responses. Students described feeling nervous while waiting to be adopted, joy when adopted, happiness when their friend enacted animals were adopted, and sadness when they were not. For example, “I felt like kind of nervous because I didn't know if I was going to get adopted or not, but in the end, some, like most of my friends, they got adopted like the three cats, and the 60 pounds dogs they got adopted and I felt really happy for them cuz they got adopted. But I was still kind of like sad that I didn't get adopted. But I know there was plenty of other chances.” Even when the adoption result was pending, some found joy in acting as their favorite animal, “ my favorite was that I was a b unny because bunnies are my favorite animals.” These emotional responses made the adoption outcome personally important, which encouraged students to actively use data to reason about whether they might be adopted by comparing their own attributes to adopters’ preferences, as one explains, “it was pretty fun. I love that. The owners were saying how they wanted animals and all that stuff and it was pretty fun. That's why I liked it. We're learning about, like what size, like how  old, how much pounds all of that. I feel like we are using data because like they told us, they give us data of how they wanted it. And if we match the data then we will like adopted.” Thus, embodying an animal triggered students’ emotional response that led them to situate their thinking in a lived experience of comparing two datasets and making predictions from their relationships. In addition to emotion in the immediate context, students reported embodying an animal fostered empathy and perspective-taking that extended to their own pet, “I could really put how my dog feels and I could understand my dog a little bit more.” Some projected this into future experiences, “when you’re picking an animal at the shelter, you can think more about how the dogs feel  and how we connect with them more and how we can use information to figure out more about the animal. I just felt really nice being adopted.” Altogether, these reflections show how embodying an animal in an adoption event intertwined emotional, embodied, and lived experiences: acting as an animal grounded data reasoning in students’ enacted features; the adoption outcome made animal data emotionally important; reasoning with data became a lived experience of evidencing qualification for adoption, which was motived by their emotion and in turn impacted their emotion; and perspective-taking through embodiment fostered empathy that students connected both to their immediate experiences with pets and to projected future experiences.

Embodied, emotional, and lived experiences in granola bar commercial Students’ reflections on granola bar commercials illustrated another example of how embodied, emotional, and lived experience are entangled. In this activity, students examined nutrition data from different granola bars and role-played as salesmen to advertise their chosen bar as the healthiest by performing a commercial that used data as evidence to convince their peers as audience.

The embodied experience was evident in students’ report of holding real granola bars, which emphasizes the experiences of manipulating actual items that was much more joyful than using symbolic representations (e.g., animal posters), “I like the most because we actually got to like hold the item and actually like look at it and see. And I think that one way is my favorite because unlike this one (animal poster), like they weren’t actual physical items. This one was so it made more fun to me.” In addition to handling the physical items, students highlighted how the broader social context shaped their embodied experience. They reported the presence of cameras for recording and peers acting as their audience provided an immersive environment for their embodied experiences, which made them feel like real salesmen, “there's cameras and people around sometime and like felt like you were actually giving a commercial about the bar and telling people information about it.” Here, the materials (e.g., real granola bars and cameras) and the social presence of peers create the cultural and social context of producing a TV commercial, which strengthened the sense of immersion. Students also reported layered emotion of this embodied experience, including excitement about using data to persuade others, “I was excited that I actually got to, like, pretend to get people to buy my item using data,” and evolving feelings of excitement, courage, nervousness, and relief while performing the commercial to the class, “I feel like exci ted and I had a bunch of courage because when I walked up there I seen everybody looking and I felt I felt kind of nervous for a little bit, but then I read the script and then yeah happy.” This reflection illustrates that the embodied and social experiences evoked a mix of feelings that made the experience emotionally memorable. Students' reflections also demonstrated how emotion became attached to their lived experience of using data to persuade peers and evaluate peers’ choice. Some described feeling confused and unconvinced when peers’ interpretations of data conflicted with th eir own, “But she was saying, like so, it's better. But when you’re comparing it, this one has more energy, this one didn’t. It had more than what, because I remember like how much you're supposed to have. And I was like actually it’s not. The other one was like in the good range.” In an opposite case, students described not understanding commercials in general in their life but  feeling persuaded by peers’ 402 comprehensive analysis of data in all granola bars, “I was convinced because they shared a lot of information of how some total fat. It was supposed to be, I think 5 to 10. I'm pretty sure. like this one was 6 and another one that they presented and remember it was like 15 or something. I was like, oh, that doesn't sound very good. So,  I was like they did the right thing to make sure that they went over all of the data and make sure they have all the correct information to go, via a commercial person.” These reflections highlight how students’ lived experiences of interpreting and evaluating data were layered with imagination afforded by their embodied roles. As they collaborate and take on roles of advertisers and audiences, their emotional responses, inclu ding confusion when peers’ claims conflicted with theirs or conviction when persuaded by peers, emerged from both what they experienced in working with data but what was imagined and embodied in a commercial scenario. Finally, students reported how their lived experiences working with data to evaluate granola bars extended beyond the classroom into both immediate and future life. Some were eager to share, without asking, how the experience immediately influenced their daily habits, “I got to learn like what I was eating and stuff with the data. So now I'm looking at the packaging of like all the food I eat and throwing it away because it's not healthy.” Others described the activity as joyful because of its impacts on imagined future experiences, “But I just really like these ones, you could actually like maybe experience  like say you want to buy a granola bar and you don't know which one's good for you. We looked at the calories and the fat, like the sodium, and we found out which ones are good for us to eat or whatever. And like if we're older, we don't know which ones to eat or do whatever with, then we would now know how to check the things that we need.” These reflections suggest that the chances to connect their lived experiences with data were emotionally significant and shaped students’ immediate life habits and helped them envision themselves in using data for making decisions for future life. RQ2: How do elementary students perceive the value of data after participating in a curriculum designed around their embodied, emotional, and lived experiences? Students reflected how they used and valued data during the activities, which can be summarized into three themes: as a rhetorical tool, a life tool, and a problem-solving tool: Data as a rhetorical tool This theme was directly connected to students’ report in the granola bar commercial where they reflected how they used data to convince others of their ideas or validating others’ ideas by treating data as evidence to support arguments used in the commercials and evaluating peers’ presentations. This reflects their perception of data as a rhetorical tool. As one student explained: “I liked it because we actually got to, like, speak up at the class and the class and tell them. Why should they buy the item wh en we were also using data to help convince or persuade them to buy. I was excited that I actually got to, like, pretend. To get people to buy my item using data.” In this case, students treated data as a rhetorical tool that could strengthen arguments and make them more credible. Their reflections suggest that learning with data was tied to imagining themselves in a persuasive role and using data strategically to influence others.

Data as a life tool Students also reported data as a life tool including sustaining livelihoods and informing daily choices of food. For example, one student treated their mother’s work of taking customer order as a waitress as collecting data, noting that how accurately data is tracked will impact the tips their parents earn, “She is a waitress at [anonymized], so she has to use some of the data on like based on their orders like. Well, I want a steak, but I don't want it with mashed potatoes or I want a burger, but no lettuce. She has to use that data based on what they want and she has to write it down and take their orders and give it to them, so she has to think about how, like, like saying if I was a customer from my mom, sometimes I can think like if I want to give her a  tip on how much like other service like it was it good? Was it not? So I can take that data based on the tip and I think that they can use that for like when they get older so they can think more about it.” This example illustrates how students perceive data as integral to life through sustaining livelihoods. More importantly, it reflects students extending their learning about data into family labor by reframing service work as requiring sophisticated intelligence of using data and how effective use of data directly shapes earnings. This reflection echoes Rose's Blue-collar Brilliance (2005), which informed that data should not be understood simply as numbers in a school context but as practices that exist in multiple forms in daily life and are important to everyone. Data as a problem-solving tool Students reported using data as a problem-solving tool including the application of some practices such as organizing data by different variables so as to make complex problems more manageable. For example, one students reflected on using data to solve an adoption problem: "Say you wanted to adopt a dog and you see all 403 this information and you're like stressing out about it cause you don't like you should pick that dog or not or pick that food or whatever that you can end up grouping it or like grouping it. Like male or female. like you can group it by pounds by the size. And then if you don't like the size or the age, then you could maybe look at a different dog. So, it's just easier." This example illustrates how students recognized data as a tool for managing a stressful problem. By grouping data based on different variables, the students unpacked a difficult choice into a more systematic process of solving a problem. This reflection shows that using data strategically could transform decision-making into more manageable and less overwhelming. This study illustrates how embodied, emotional, and lived experiences were deeply entangled in ways that shape how elementary students collect, represent, reason, and communicate data and data graphs. Across these activities, students reported emotions such as joy, pride, nervousness, doubt, and frustration that were inseparable from their embodied experiences of manipulating data objects, enacting data points, and performing data-informed commercials. These embodied experiences grounded their lived experiences of collecting personally relevant data, designing personally joyful graphs, reasoning with data through enacted roles, and interpreting, persuading, or evaluating with data in ways connected to their own lives (e.g., seeing commercials and granola bars in life). These overlapping interactions demonstrate that data learning is never neutral but inherently emotional, and that emotion can be further strengthened through embodied experience grounded in manipulating data objects and embodied actions tied to the data. The embodied nature of the activities evoked emotional actions that anchored students’ understanding of data in lived experiences; and such a combination made the role of data tangible and relevant in students’ own lives. While most activities i nvolve students’ emotional, lived, and embodied experiences, some variations across the designs highlight how different activities may foreground or background certain types of experience, which offers insights for future analysis and design. For example, in the data collection and graphing activity, students’ reports highlighted emotional and lived experiences but backgrounded the embodied experiences, possibly because those in the post-interview chose to draw graphs. Future work could examine whether embo died experiences were more evident in groups chose to create graphs using movements (e.g., squat heights) and whether the drawing inadvertently limited it. Likewise, emotion was only sparsely present in the animal data card activity, where it was tied prim arily to the enjoyment of manipulating cards and the challenge of managing a large dataset. This may be due to weaker attachment to the cards compared to embodying a single data point in the animal adoption event or using data to make personal choices in the commercial, whereas the card activity afforded forming a mental structure of the entire dataset rather than individual data points. Future work could further examine how different representations and embodiments shape connections between emotional experiences and conceptual reflection of the data.

Moreover, this analysis found that students’ entangled experiences are temporal, connecting students’ past with their present, and shaping the imagined future. Specifically, the lived experience of working with data in the present was personally meaningful because it draws from students’ past experiences, including having pets or eating granola bars for breakfasts, while also shaping their immediate lives through emotional connections with pets and new habits like checking nutrition labels on their everyday food. Meanwhile, these present experiences served as tools for reframing how students interpreted past situations where they might act differently, and for imagining how they would use data in future. Taken together, these temporal connections across past, present, and future suggest a continuity of data learning, where embodied and emotional experiences link prior knowledge, present reasoning, and imagined futures. Each is grounded in students’ lived, emotional, and embodied experiences, creating a cohere nt sense of the role of data in their lives. Therefore, temporal continuity offers a design perspective: connecting data practice to students’ past experiences, grounding them in the present, and extending them toward projected future can help students perceive the value of data in ways that, in turn, enrich their current experiences and reframe their past ones.

As demonstrated in this analysis, the three dimensions of embodied, lived, and emotional experiences were deeply entangled in students’ work with data, but also explicitly shaped their views of the value of data in their lives. Students' reflections on vie wing data as a rhetorical tool, a problem-solving tool, and a life tool demonstrate that such entangled experiences hold epistemic value in terms of shaping students’ way of knowing what data is, how data can be used, and who can work with data. In this way, these findings suggest that such embodied and emotional experiences of working with data grounded in students’ lived experience are not only fun but also central to developing meaningful epistemologies of data.
