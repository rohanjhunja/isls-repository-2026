# From Solo to Collaborative: Design Affordances in Multi-User Immersive VR Simulations for Transforming Science Learning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Immersive virtual reality (IVR) shows promise for science learning by enabling visualization of microscopic phenomena and dynamic processes. However, research has focused predominantly on individual learning experiences, overlooking the social nature of learning. This study investigates the collaborative affordances of multi-user IVR for science education through participatory design, involving six teams of undergraduate students and educators who co-created IVR learning simulations. Findings revealed seven science learning affordances: microscopic visualization, spatial relationships, scale traversal, representational connections, perspective-taking, temporal phenomena, and procedural skills—which multi-user IVR amplified through embodied communication, role-taking that grounds collaborative discourse, and distributed perspectives that require coordination. Analysis of designs highlights how narrative structures establish group-worthy joint tasks, fostering six collaborative activity types. Variations in structuring collaborative processes emerged from play-level scripts with undifferentiated roles, and scene-level scripts with defined roles enhanc ing interdependence. Results provide design insights for leveraging IVR ’s spatial and embodied features in collaborative science learning.

Immersive virtual reality (IVR) has emerged as a promising technology for supporting science learning, particularly for visualizing abstract phenomena that are difficult to observe in traditional classroom settings (Coban et al., 2022; Matovu et al., 2023). Recent research demonstrates that s ituating learners with in dynamic scientific models in  IVR simulations enables engagement with microscopic molecular structures and processes previously inaccessible through direct observation (Lui et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2025; Reen et al., 2025). However, studies have focused primarily on individual learning experiences, overlooking a fundamental theoretical insight: learning occurs within social contexts  (Stahl et al., 2022). While the effectiveness of computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) in STEM education is well-established (Jeong et al., 2019), current knowledge about collaborative learning in IVR  remains limited (Lui et al., 2026; Paulsen et al., 2024; vander Meer et al., 2023). Without understanding how to design for effective collaboration in multi-user IVR, we risk perpetuating isolated learning in an inherently social medium.

Unlike screen-based collaborative environments,  IVR offers co-presence, where learners share a three-dimensional space, use embodied interaction and spatial deixis naturally, engage in multimodal communication, and coordinate their attention across dynamic simulations. These characteristics suggest that collaborative IVR may support different forms of joint activity and distributed cognition. Moreover, current approaches to technology-rich educational design typically position  learners as end  users rather t han as design partners. Participatory design methods  center learners ’ and educators’ insights throughout the process  (DiSalvo et al., 2017), surfacing tacit knowledge about what supports effective collaboration. This study addresses the following: RQ1: What are the design affordances for learning science with IVR? RQ2: What are the design affordances of collaborative learning with scientific IVR simulations? Prior reviews identif y key rationales for adopting IVR  in science education, including visualizing abstract concepts, experiential learning, and practical skills development, with visualization as the most prevalent (Matovu et al., 2023). IVR enables learners to explore spatial relationships in molecular structures, traverse scales from microscopic to macroscopic perspectives, and experience temporal phenomena that unfold too quickly or slowly to observe in real-time (Coban et al., 2022; Matovu et al., 2023)—support ing embodied cognition through natural gestures and movements (Johnson-Glenberg, 2018). However, while existing  IVR applications incorporate sensory and actional design features, they often lack narrative and, critically, social features —reflecting a predominant focus on individual learning.  Recent reviews have expanded our understanding of how IVR can support collaborative learning, identifying key affordances such as enhancing engagement, fostering social skill 479 development, and enabling remote collaboration (vander Meer et al., 2023). However, they also note that many designs underutilize the collaborative potential of immersive environments (Hatch & Michaelis, 2024) and rely predominantly on play-and scene-level CSCL scripts (Lui et al., 2026). This study draws on Jeong and Hmelo-Silver’s (2016) seven core CSCL affordances as its analy tical lens: (1) engage in a joint task, (2) communicate,

(3) share resources, (4) engage in productive processes, (5) engage in co-construction, (6) monitor and regulate

collaborative learning, and (7) find and build groups and communities. T o structure productive engagement, technology candeploy CSCL scripts—digital supports that guide learners engaged in collaborative tasks (Fischer et al., 2013). Vogel et al.  (2017) described three different CSCL script levels  of increasing socio-cognitive scaffolding complexity: play-level scripts involve undifferentiated roles,  scene-level scripts involve role differentiation, while  scriptlet-level scaffolds provide meta-cognitive supports to fulfill specific role s and responsibilities (Vogel et al., 2017).

## Participants

Eighteen individuals (7 male, 11 female) from undergraduate life sciences programs at the University of Toronto participated: 14 students and 4 educators. Participants were organized into six teams (2-4 members each) of either mixed student-educator groups (4 teams) or student-only groups (2 teams). Most had minimal I VR experience. ## Procedure

Each design team participated in a series of three face-to-face or hybrid co-design sessions over a period of 6 weeks to 4 months. Three-hour sessions were facilitated by three researchers, using sticky notes or Miro (a webbased collaborative whiteboard platform) to capture and discuss participants’ ideas. Data sources and analysis For RQ1, we analyzed written artifacts and video recordings from Session 1 discussions of  challenging science concepts suitable for IVR. All video recordings were transcribed using text-to-speech software (Adobe Premiere Pro) and cleaned. Open codes from NVIVO 15 were transferred to Miro and examined inductively through affinity diagramming. For RQ2, finalized designs were converted into conjecture maps (Sandoval, 2014) and examined for the seven CSCL affordances (Jeong & Hmelo-Silver, 2016) and the science learning affordances identified in RQ1. The research team met weekly to discuss and stabilize interpretations, reviewing emerging codes collaboratively to build consensus and resolve discrepancies. Eleven participants identified IVR ’s capacity to situate learners within microscopic environments, making molecular-level processes visible that are inherently 3D yet typically encountered through 2D abstract representations.

Fourteen participants noted IVR’s value for learning anatomy through 3D models that can be viewed from multiple angles, resolving the depth-cue limitations of 2D representations. Traversing scales. Seven participants emphasized IVR's ability to move back and forth across micro and macro levels, allowing learners to contextualize microscopic processes within macroscopic effects—for example, how molecular-level hormones affect organismal physiology. Representation-perspective cluster in practice Connecting representational forms. Three participants identified IVR’s potential for helping learners make sense of mathematical representations by visualizing the corresponding molecular processes —bridging visual and mathematical forms of scientific representation.

Practicing perspective-taking. Five participants described how learners could embody biological entities (e.g., a cell performing its function) or adopt clinical perspectives (e.g., experiencing symptoms of a neurological condition), supported by IVR’s immersive and embodied nature. Experiencing temporal phenomena.  Thirteen participants identified two modes: allocentric (3rd person observation of processes unfolding over time) and egocentric (1st person participation in biological processes). Embodied practice Developing procedural skills. Six participants identified IVR as valuable for learning laboratory skills, with embodied interactions more closely mirroring physical lab work than mouse-and-keyboard interactions. Science learning affordances in action: The CRISPR-Cas Design Simulator (CR) The CRISPR-Cas Design Simulator (CR)  s imulation exemplifies how viewing micros copic phenomena, visualizing spatial relationships, and scale traversal operate synergistically. Players in groups of three design a treatment for cystic fibrosis  using CRISPR-Cas gene-editing technology. Players are shrunk to subcellular size and enter a cell nucleus, making genome structure and associated processes visible  (viewing microscopic phenomena). A cooperative mini-game distributes views of the genome across three organizational scales (one per player): 3D genome structure, epigenetic interactions with histone proteins, and linear nucleotide sequences. To locate the target PAM sequence (by traversing scales and visualizing spatial relationships), coordination among players is required.  Once the PAM sequence is identified, players collaborate to design the guide RNA and to determine the desired editing function of the CRISPR-Cas system. This transforms individual science learning IVR affordances (viewing microscopic phenomena, traversing scales) into meaningful collaboration that requires joint problem-solving.

RQ2: Collaborative affordances in multi-user IVR designs Finding 1: Narrative structures enable diverse collaborative activities through joint tasks All six designs established joint tasks through compelling narrative structures that created shared goals and authentic contexts. F rom competitive gameplay (IM, IB), clinical scenarios (P D), laboratory work (QP), to designing genetic therapies (CR). Within these narrative s, six distinct types of collaborative activities emerged:

(1) discussing, (2) consensus-building, (3) troubleshooting (identifying and resolving problems collaboratively),

(4) strategizing, (5) designing, and (6) coordinating interdependent tasks. Competitive game narratives (IM, IB)

naturally generated strategizing and coordination of interdependent tasks as teams planned attacks and defenses. Clinical scenarios (PD) required discussing observations and consensus-building for treatment plans. Laboratory simulations (QP) incorporated troubleshooting through error alerts and consensus-building through conceptual checkpoints. These narrative structures transform individual I VR affordances into collaborative necessities. For example, viewing microscopic phenomena becomes a shared perceptual experience that requires  joint interpretation, and traversing scales becomes distributed among team members, requiring coordination. 481 Finding 2: Role differentiation creates interdependence through scene-level scripts structuring approaches. Two designs incorporated play-level scripts (PD, QP), in which learners oscillate between individual and collective roles without role differentiation. In the Parkinson ’s Disease Patient Simulator  (PD) simulation, players engage with the same AI patient individually before being prompted to discuss and design a treatment plan for them collaboratively.  Four teams utilized scene-level scripts  (CR, IB, IM, LC), with complementary role differentiation that structured interdependent tasks. In CR, each player has a distinct main task while others offer support, with differing perceptual access to the genome during the mini-game. In IB, the Doctor/Drug and Patient/Immune System roles collaborate against the Virus role, each with  distinct abilities and information. In IM, immune cell team members have differentiated biological functions that contribute to unique actions. Notably, all designs lacked scriptlet-level scaffolds —specific prompts supporting the metacognitive processes needed to fulfill assigned roles.

Finding 3: IVR’s embodied nature enables unique forms of collaborative meaning-making Beyond standard CSCL affordances, our analysis revealed I VR-specific collaborative features that extend the affordances for communication, joint tasks, and co-construction. Embodied communication through gesture and spatial positioning.  All six designs implicitly assumed that learners would use spatial positioning, gesture s, and shared gaze—forms of non-verbal communication that mirror face-to-face collaboration. When learners point at shared virtual objects or position themselves relative to structures to indicate perspective, they engage in deictic communication that supports  joint reference and shared attention, extending the communication affordance into 3D space. Embodied role-taking for shared perspective understanding. Multiple designs (IM, IB, LC) incorporated embodied role-taking that goes beyond traditional role-playing by providing first-person sensory experiences that become objects of collaborative reflection. In IM, learners embody different biological entities —some players become immune cells (macrophages, dendritic cells, neutrophils) or  Salmonella bacteria—with e ach role constraining actions according to biological functions, creat ing a foundation for collaborative understanding. In LC, learners experience simulated stress responses (e.g., tunnel vision through field-of-view blurring), creating shared visceral experiences that ground subsequent discussions about cortisol’s effects. Distributed perspectives requiring coordination for knowledge co-construction. Three designs (CR, LC, IB) strategically distributed perceptual access or abilities across team members, requiring coordination to achieve complete understanding. In the CR, team members simultaneously view the genome at different scales with unique possible actions. Locating the PAM sequence required communicating  across perspectives and synchronizing actions.

This study investigated design opportunities and collaborative affordances of multi-user IVR simulations for science learning through participatory design with undergraduates and educators.  Our analysis of RQ1 identified seven key IVR affordances for science learning  that extend Matovu et al.’s (2023) rationales for IVR adoption, emphasizing the interconnected nature of visualization, scale, and representation in scientific understanding. Importantly, our findings demonstrate how collaboration amplifies these affordances to leverage collective cognitive resources, transforming individual affordances into opportunities for distributed cognition and joint problem-solving (Paulsen et al., 2024; vander Meer et al., 2023). For RQ2, all six designs used narrative structures to establish joint tasks and shared goals with six collaborative activity types. Both the current study and Hatch & Michaelis (2024) identify discussion and problem-solving as essential collaborative processes; this study adds consensus-building, strategizing, designing, and coordinating interdependent tasks. Using Vogel et al.’s (2017) framework, two designs (PD, QP) employed play-level scripts, whereas four designs (CR, LC, IB, IM) utilized scene-level scripts, consistent with recent findings in IVR collaboration (Lui et al., 2026). Future IVR environments may benefit from incorporating more explicit scaffolding to elicit explanation, argumentation, and reasoning—forms of dialogue known to enhance CSCL learning (Fischer et al., 2013). Our findings illuminate how IVR’s embodied and spatial nature creates distinctive opportunities beyond traditional CSCL. D esigns leverage co-presence, synchronous communication that mirrors co-located face-to-face settings, enabling joint reference through gesture  and spatial positioning that is difficult in remote, screen-based collaboration. IVR enables novel forms of embodied  role-taking and perspective-sharing—providing  first-person sensory experiences that become objects of collaborative reflection, amplifying emotional resonance and enabling learners to inhabit others’ perspectives more fully (Järvelä & Rose, 2022).
