# Bridging Gaps: Participatory Design as a Tool for Social Justice in Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium explores how participatory design (PD) candisrupt the "one-size-fits-all" approach by fostering equitable, responsive, and contextually relevant practices in the learning sciences. Leveraging PD to address systemic inequities, the symposium showcases studies that empower immigrant teens as co-designers and learners in crafting experiences for informed civic participation, employ micro-genetic analyses to examine equitable partnerships among diverse stakeholders, explore curriculum co-design in resource-constrained settings to enhance teacher agency, utilise cooperative inquiry for designing inclusive schools for LGBTQIA+ students, and use of participatory design to advance inclusiveness in an international network of informal STEM clubs. This symposium illustrates how PD can change educational spaces and advance the Learning Sciences' commitment to social justice, ethical collaboration, and inclusive practices through case studies, methodological insights, and interactive discussions. Overview and significance Participatory design (PD) is increasingly recognised for its potential to advance equity and inclusion in education. Rooted in collaboration, PD engages learners, educators, researchers, and community members in co-designing learning environments, tools, a nd systems that address diverse experiences and needs. The Learning Sciences community has long emphasised the creation of ethical, inclusive practices that disrupt systemic inequities and foster equitable learning opportunities (e.g., Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Gomez et al., 2021). However, despite the continued focus on inclusion within the learning sciences, empirical evidence and case studies that highlight how marginalised and underrepresented voices are included remain limited. This symposium aligns with the learning sciences’ commitment to social justice by showcasing how PD can challenge the "one-size-fits-all" approach to education. PD provides a pathway to address systemic challenges such as language barriers, identity affirmation, and power dynamics, creating equitable and contextually relevant educational practices (DiSalvo & DiSalvo, 2014; Hoadley, 2017; Vossoughi, 2020). PD fosters inclusive practices that support all stakeholders, particularly in diverse, resource-constrained settings, by including marginalised voices. The symposium presents five contributions that cover a wide range of educational contexts, including empowering immigrant teens as co-designers and learners in crafting civic engagement experiences, using micro-genetic analysis to explore teacher-stakeholder collaborations in curriculum co-design, designing inclusive schools for LGBTQIA+ youth through Cooperative Inquiry, and how participatory design can advance inclusiveness in an international network of informal STEM clubs. These cases illustrate how PD can adapt to real-world constraints, enhance agency, and create educational spaces aligning with the learning sciences' commitment to diversity, equity, and inclusion. Through interactive presentations, participants will engage with empirical, methodological, and theoretical insights, fostering discussions that bridge research and practice. This symposium not only advances the understanding of PD as a tool for social justice but also invites the learning 2365 sciences community to reimagine educational research and design for a more equitable future, echoing calls for ethical and inclusive practices (Gomez et al., 2021; Uttamchandani et al., 2020). Paper 1: Participatory design to promote teen civic engagement Nitzan Koren Abstract: This paper discusses the benefits of Participatory Design for empowering and engaging teens from immigrant backgrounds in informed politics. Two roles assumed by teens: co-designers, brainstorming and ideating learning activities for non-citizens to engage in informed politics, and learners, testing and participating in design activities to practice informed politics and improve political skills, knowledge, and self-efficacy. Techniques that led to meaningful civic learning and led to meaningful design recommendations are discussed. In adolescence, teens form a civic identity and establish their beliefs, values, and responsibilities. The definition of civic identity remains a matter of debate amid the tensions between citizenship, globalization, and immigration (Hart et al., 2011). Fo r this and other reasons, forming a civic identity can be challenging and confusing for all teens, especially for teens from immigrant backgrounds who have diverse cultural experiences and need to immerse themselves in a new political system. Civic educati on designed for all teens can feel exclusionary for immigrant teens, as it is often tied to citizenship and traditional political participation. More inclusive approaches to civic education, such as “civic imagination,” that focus on individuals’ roles in imagining a better future through community practices, may better serve teens from immigrant backgrounds (Jenkins et al., 2019). Focusing exclusively on immigrant teens excluded from civic education programs, this study explored how the Participatory Design (PD) methodology can enhance their political participation and learning. PD can serve as a form of participation and learning for immigrant teens to establish their societal position. Some PD techniques can serve as a beneficial learning experience for immigrant teens because they allow teen co-designers to express their cultural perspectives while participating in civic activities (Mainsah & Morrison, 2012). As a mere participation opportunity, studies engaged individuals in civic projects to design smar t cities (Gooch et al., 2018), integrate immigrants into the community (Chen, 2023), increase volunteer participation (Armouch, 2021), work with teens to contribute to their neighborhoods (Campos & Garcia, 2018), and invite teens to critique designs that can impact their communities (Sabie et al., 2022). While research used PD to promote equitable solutions by engaging community members in the design process, immigrant teens were rarely invited to participate in the design of civic programming. In this study, teens from immigrant backgrounds were invited as (1) co-designers to identify meaningful activities and practices to support non-citizen immigrant teens engage in informed politics through a learning program, and as (2) learners, to gain political knowledge, skills, and self-efficacy while participating. The design process comprised five phases (see Table 1) and involved three cohorts of teen participants. Adults and young adults are mentioned in Table 1 for context. Nineteen teens, ages 14-17, born in Nigeria, Afghanistan, Guatemala, El Salvador, Nicaragua, and the United States, participated in the study (all but one participant were either 1.5-or second-generation immigrants). Cohort 1 (N=8) was a mixed group of teens, including four immigrant teens, who met in Spring 2022. Cohort 2 (N=6), consisting solely of immigrant teens (3 returning), met in summer 2023. Paper 2: Studying interaction in participatory design processes: An analytical approach for unpacking power and relational dynamics at the micro-genetic level Emily Oswald This contribution to the symposium examines how micro-genetic studies of participatory design could assist educational researchers in developing more just and equitable partnering processes with diverse educational stakeholders (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Ethnographic studies of design processes have explored how collaborative design can be organised to support teacher agency (Severance et al., 2016) and the value of participatory design as a methodological orientation when the confirmation or denial of epistemic authority within a design consortium is at stake (Booker & Goldman, 2016). Studies of the interaction between researchers and other participants, as well as among participants in participatory design processes, can complement ethnographic 2367 studies. This interest in the potential for studies of interaction in participatory design research to contribute to the development of more just and equitable design processes is inspired by recent calls from researchers in the learning sciences who argue  that micro-genetic studies of teacher learning in professional development can contribute to both the design of better professional develop and a more nuanced understanding of teacher learning in these contexts (Walkoe & Luna, 2020).

One way studies of interaction can contribute to developing and facilitating equitable, participatory design processes is by highlighting and unpacking instances in which the process's activities fail to support participants. Hall and Stevens (2016) have argued that “one of the primary values of an IA [interaction analysis] perspective on knowledge is that it provides an ongoing check on what is persistently a danger in the analysis of ‘others’ (and especially less powerful others), namely the production of deficit accounts of their knowledge” (80). Researchers using participatory design research approaches may begin with the ambition to centre participants’ knowledge and expertise in a design process, yet still struggle to plan and facilitate it, and to cultivate relationships in ways that meaningfully accomplish this.

Booker and Goldman’s account of a participatory design research project provides a compelling example of how a design process that took countering deficit positionings of parents’ mathematical knowledge as a point of departure nevertheless involved activities in which parents deferred to the epistemic authority of classroom teachers and university-based researchers (2016: 227-29). Booker and Goldman’s analysis pinpoints one instance in which a pilot workshop for parents, organised and facilitated by a parent member of the design team, was largely successful in helping other parents feel comfortable and confident in a workshop explicitly focused on math. The pilot workshop design, however, failed to support the parent-facilitator in connecting the hands-on activity with mathematical concepts of radius and circumference. At the end of the workshop, both the parent-facilitator and the parent participants “turned to the researchers in the room” (Booker & Goldman 2016: 299) to supply this explanation: one researcher’s attempt to “reinvigorate a wider field of epistemic authority” (Booker & Goldman, 2016, p.229) by posing a question was apparently unsuccessful. Booker and Goldman’s analysis usefully shows how the outcomes of the piloted workshop were taken up in subsequent iterations of the design and supporting materials, resulting in resources that were ultimately wellaligned with the project’s ambition to support parents in advocating for their children’s math learning. A microgenetic analysis of this episode, however, could contribute to either a nuanced understanding of how parents as workshop participants ceded epistemic authority or a shift in our understanding of the deferral to researchers in terms of “what was going on” (Hall & Steven, 2016, p.79) for the parent-facilitator, the parent-participants, the researcher who responded to the parents’ orientation with a question, and the other researchers in the room. Paper 3: When co-design meets the real world of Global South schools Paulo Blikstein, Tatiana Hochgreb, Tamar Fuhrmann, Cassia Fernandez Abstract: This paper discusses the implementation of a science education reform project in a Global South municipality, in which a research team was redesigning the city’s curricula from the ground up. The team’s initial intention of a full curricular co-design with teachers faced several obstacles, informing a deep redesign of the program to fit the realities on the ground. We describe two phases of the project, the data that informed the changes between them, and conclude by addressing the limitations, compromises, and lessons learned when co-design frameworks are applied in real-world contexts, especially outside developed nations. Traditional approaches to curriculum design often position teachers as implementers rather than contributors, leading to a disconnect between prescribed curricula and classroom realities (McKenney & Reeves, 2012). This disconnect can result in educational materials that are less responsive to students’ needs and the contextual nuances of individual classrooms. Recognizing these limitations, there is a growing interest in involving teachers in the co-design process, leveraging their practical expertise to cr eate more effective and contextually relevant curricula (Voogt et al., 2015). Engaging teachers as co-designers could be a powerful professional development activity that enhances the alignment between curricula and pedagogical practices (Penuel et al., 2007). Several studies in the learning sciences have indicated that when teachers participate in curriculum co-design, they are more likely to adopt innovative practices and feel a sense of ownership (vanden Akker et al., 2006). However, co-design processes can be time-intensive and require significant support to be effective. This paper explores the dynamics of curricular co-design with teachers, focusing on schools in the Global South. Compared to institutions in developed countries, they lack the same level of government support and have significantly less availability for professional development (see, for example, Barreto & Souza, 2013; OECD, 2021), which makes co-design 2368 processes more challenging. Using data from two design phases of a curricular reform project, we examine the outcomes of co-design engagements and propose new approaches that better fit the local context.

## Context and methods

The first phase of the co-design process was systematically conducted through a series of online meetings held at regular intervals between teachers and researchers over 16 months. The second phase had a similar duration. These collaborative efforts unfolded in three iterative cycles, each lasting approximately three months. During these cycles, researchers and science teachers engaged in in-depth discussions to develop lesson plans, address pedagogical dilemmas, and collectively reflect on the experiences of teachers. This methodology served a dual purpose: to identify and address barriers, tensions, and opportunities for improvement in the local implementation model and to support the professional development of science and laboratory teachers. Within each iterative cycle, science teachers selected a curricular topic aligned with the content they were scheduled to teach during the ongoing bimester. These topics were then redesigned intonew curricular units structured as sets of detailed lesson plans. The c o-design sessions were carefully tailored to consider the teachers’ prior experiences and their confidence levels in implementing changes to their pedagogical practices, in line with the framework proposed by Severance et al. (2016). Insights and reflections derived from each cycle informed and guided subsequent iterations, consistent with the principles outlined by Penuel et al. (2011). Consequently, expectations for changes in teachers’ pedagogical practices gradually increased across cycles, fostering continuous professional growth and the refinement of instructional strategies.

The first phase of the co-design process (the first 16 months) presented significant challenges, particularly regarding the time and effort required of teachers and researchers. The labour-intensive nature of the process was compounded by the limited availability of teachers to engage deeply with the curricular redesign tasks, a common feature of the Global South context we studied. Moreover, the initial expectations for the co-design were ambitious, aiming to produce exemplary curricular units that could be used across the broader network of public schools in the project. However, while the units created by the teachers were instrumental in advancing their professional development, these outputs were not applicable to the school network. The context-specific, processdriven nature of the units rendered them valuable primarily to the teachers who had actively participated in their creation. In other words, while the co-design process proved an effective mechanism for professional development, the curricular products themselves had limited utility beyond the immediate context of their design, underscoring the need to balance professional learning goals with the practical scalability and adaptability of outputs in future initiatives.

For the project's second phase, a new strategy was needed to expand teacher training and facilitate the implementation of the new curriculum. From the first phase, we learned that, to produce lesson plans that met the established quality criteria and were rolled out across the entire school network, significant intervention from the researchers/trainers was necessary. This extensive guidance compromised the authenticity of the process and created an impression within the administration that teachers were in dependently producing high-quality instructional sequences, thereby diminishing the perceived need for further professional development. The reality was that teachers faced significant time constraints and encountered challenges in designing lesson plans, and gaps in teachers’ content knowledge were identified as a critical barrier. Our analysis showed that an entirely “teacher-led” co-design process was impractical. An alternative strategy was thus adopted to address these challenges, in which curricular units were developed prior to alignment with the desired instructional principles. These units were tested with teachers during training sessions, where teachers first engaged with the activities as “students” before reflecting on their experiences. During the reflection phase, teachers provided feedback, suggested modifications based on their classroom and student context, and discussed potential adjustments. This feedback informed the iterative refinement of the lesson plans, which were then presented back  to the teachers, allowing them to evaluate whether their earlier concerns had been effectively addressed. Post-implementation debriefing sessions were also conducted with teachers to identify further refinements needed for subsequent iterations. For example, teachers highlighted the need to adjust some instructional sequences to extend the duration of planned activities, ensuring better alignment with classroom dynamics and time constraints. While requiring much less time from teachers, this new approach needed a significant investment of time from researchers to develop exemplary instructional sequences. Testing these sequences with teachers proved an effective strategy for several reasons. First, it allowed researchers to gain insight into teachers’ perceptions of the materials and their applicability within specific classroom contexts. Second, it facilitated the adaptation of the instructional sequences 2369 to better align with the realities of teachers’ teaching environments. Third, it contributed to the development of a community of practice. By involving teachers in the iterative refinement process, the initiative fostered a sense of co-ownership over the materials, avoiding the perception of a “top-down” imposition that could have elicited resistance. This approach also optimized the time demands placed on teachers, allowing them to participate meaningfully in the process without being overburdened by the extensive time commitments typically required for curriculum design. Finally, teachers could apply these plans in the classroom and observe changes (stemming from the application of curriculum principles and the training) that might not have been possible if they had used plans created by themselves.

Many co-design studies involve experienced teachers who already incorporate reform-based practices into their classrooms. By contrast, the teachers in this study were at the early stages of their professional development, being introduced to these concepts for the first time. Consequently, they lacked an established repertoire of instructional strategies and teaching resources. One significant challenge was related to content knowledge. Many participating teachers had backgrounds in biology or chemistry and demonstrated limited familiarity with subjects such as physics. Developing exemplary instructional sequences required substantial time dedicated to research, reading, and collaboration. Teachers were not yet accustomed to such intensive planning practices  and often had limited availability, necessitating meetings on weekends or in the evenings. The second strategy adopted in this project expanded teachers’ instructional repertoires and provided opportunities to test and refine new strategies in their classrooms. Future investigations should examine how this approach influenced teachers’ planning for other lessons, potentially in simplified forms that still embed the core principles and practices introduced during the codesign process. This study highlights the potential and challenges of involving teachers in curriculum co-design, especially within resource-constrained contexts. In the first phase, teachers actively participated in curriculum creation, but the time and effort required limited the scalability of this approach. To address these limitations, the second phase adopted a more structured model in which researchers created foundational curricular units that were refined with teacher feedback. This model optimized teachers’ time while still fostering ownership and contextual adaptation. This hybrid co-design model proved effective for professional development and curriculum alignment, but still required substantially more time from the research team. Paper 4: Supporting LGBTQIA+ Youth Through the Design Partnership of a School Jason Yip, Allison Druin, Atlas Bederson Participatory design (PD) as a field of research and design ranges in the amount and type of involvement stakeholders have in the process. The work of participatory design is important to the learning sciences as a means to develop inclusive practice and t o shape collaborative involvement among learners, educators, designers, and researchers (DiSalvo et al., 2017). One of the most inclusive and intensive participation paths for stakeholders in participatory design is the Cooperative Inquiry method (Druin, 2002; Yip et al., 2017). This PD method focuses on children and adults as equitable and equal design partners. This design partnership philosophy is built on developing relationships, enabling idea elaboration, facilitating collaborative design sessions, and design-bydoing together (Yip et al., 2017). Unlike other participatory design approaches, which may focus on a couple of days (Horton et al., 2012) or weeks (Large et al., 2006), much of Cooperative Inquiry works on the scale of months to years (Druin, 2002; Hunt et al., 2023; Yip et al., 2019). Design partnership is an important process in instructional and learning design. Cooperative Inquiry has been utilised in the design of learning technologies (Ahn et al., 2018; Banerjee et al., 2018; Kang et al., 2020; Norooz et al., 2015), international children’s digital libraries (Druin et al., 2009), library digital activities (Yip et al., 2019), and family-based technologies (Yip et al., 2023). While these designs are focused on learning, we do not yet know the full potential of Cooperative Inquiry for larger educational institutions. Specifically, we do not have a clear understanding of the participatory design and Cooperative Inquiry processes required to co-design an entire school. Schools, as institutions, are notoriously difficult to co-design. Numerous stakeholders are involved in schools. Policies, laws, administration, and other institutional factors alsoneed to be considered. How can participatory design and Cooperative Inquiry be a power for good and change when schools themselves, as an object or goal for design, present a massive challenge? 2370 For this symposium talk, we propose a case study of the role of Cooperative Inquiry in the design of the J.S. Bryant School, a therapeutic high school dedicated solely to the needs of LGBTQIA+ students in grades 9-12. ## Case study design

This symposium presentation will focus on a case study of the J.S. Bryant School and the role of Cooperative Inquiry in its inception, design, evaluation, and implementation. This case study will focus on two time periods. First, we will look at the historical development of Cooperative Inquiry from the 1990s to the 2020s. It is important to note some of the history and growth of Cooperative Inquiry as a method for understanding what we have achieved through design partnership in learning, as well as what has been particularly challenging. Second, we will explore the use of Cooperative Inquiry in the co-design of the J.S. Bryant School from 2022 to 2025. This case study is an ideal case (Yin, 2009), that is, what are the optimal conditions and settings that allowed the J.S. Bryant School to be co-designed from the ground up?

Hamilton Abstract: This study examines how participatory design can advance inclusiveness within an international network of informal STEM clubs that connect adolescent youth from Brazil, Canada, Ghana, Kenya, Mexico, and the United States. By incorporating feedback loops w ith peer facilitators and teacher leaders, the program enabled iterative redesign while maintaining student autonomy and minimizing adult intervention. Participatory design has increasingly become a pathway to promote equity and inclusion within learning environments by positioning participants not just as recipients of design but as co-constructors of their own learning experiences. In the context of informal STEM learning, such approaches have been used to democratize design processes, amplify voices of student participants, and engage educators as collaborators in iterative cycles of reflection and redesign (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016; Penuel et al., 2007). This project situates participatory design within a global informal STEM learning network, where adolescent students, educators, and researchers collectively contribute to design decisions across cultural and geographical boundaries. We view social justi ce not solely as an outcome but as a process enacted through inclusive participation—particularly through the program's structure, which foregrounds cross-cultural collaboration and shared agency. This work draws on an online community of informal STEM learning clubs, composed of adolescent youth from Brazil, Canada, Ghana, Kenya, Mexico, and the United States. Students from each club connected globally through online synchronous video conference calls known as global meet-ups. These weekly gatherings provide opportunities for students to collaborate across borders and work in teams to create STEM-focused media projects addressing prompts such as sustainable solutions to global issues or how AI can improve everyday life. The project community embodied an inherently inclusive model: participants from diverse linguistic, cultural, and socioeconomic contexts working collectively toward shared goals, engaging in authentic intercultural collaboration that foregrounds mutual respect and distributed expertise (Bang et al., 2016).

## Context and methods

In earlier iterations of the project community, researchers primarily guided the design process based on observation of weekly activities and progress, with solicited input from educators. In the most recent iteration year, the research team implemented a participatory design approach that incorporated two structured feedback loops—one involving peer facilitators (experienced youth participants) and the other involving teacher leaders. The peer facilitators were selected from past program participants who demonstrated leadership in their project groups and consistent attendance. During each session, the peer facilitators supported team coordination within their groups, maintained student-centred discussions with minimal adult intervention, and participated in debrief sessions with researchers afterwards. These debriefs provided timely insights into group dynamics, challenges, and successes, with their input and suggestions directly informing adjustments to subsequent sessions. Simultaneously, teacher leaders formed a separate professional learning community that met during the global meet-up sessions. While students worked independently in breakout rooms, educators gathered in their own virtual space to exchange strategies, share contextual insights from their local club sites, and reflect on site leader challenges, offering one another advice. Afterwards, they debriefed and shared their input with the researchers—offering complementary perspectives that also shaped subsequent sessions. Together, these feedback mechanisms established a participatory ecosystem in which teachers and youth jointly influenced the evolving program design while maintaining student autonomy within project groups. Insights Embedding participatory design through educator and peer-facilitator feedback loops led to multiple positive outcomes. Teachers reported enhanced professional learning through their collective reflections, while peer facilitators experienced meaningful leadership development and ownership of the program’s trajectory. The distributed design process fostered greater student autonomy, enabling teams to pursue self-selected topics that resonated with their lived experiences and community contexts. Notably, the integration of these participatory structures corresponded with an increase in project completion rates and the emergence of more contextually 2372 relevant, creative, and sustained student collaborations. These findings suggest that participatory design can serve as a mechanism for enacting inclusivity in global, informal STEM learning—by reconfiguring who participates in design, whose voices guide iterative improvement, and how equity is experienced across boundaries.
