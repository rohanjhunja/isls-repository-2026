# Towards Teachers-AI Collaboration for Professional Development in Learning Design

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) can enhance learning design (LD) by exploring stakeholders’ preferences for five conceptual GenAI-based conversational agent functions —Authoring Assistant, Inspiration Hub, Design Feedback, Possibility Engine, and Ethics Guardian—adapted from the GenAI Speculative Functions Framework for LD. Proposed for the TRAILS teacher professional development platform, these functions target underexplored early phases of LD. Using a convergent mixed-methods design, 56 participants evaluated these functions, their support needs across design phases, and related opportunities and concerns. Findings reveal a two-pillar demand: creative inspiration, prioritized in the Investigation phase through the Inspiration Hub, and drafting efficiency, prioritized in the Authoring phase through the Authoring Assistant. The analysis revealed role-dependent value: experienced practitioners favored generative and creativity-oriented functions, while prospective teachers emp hasized diagnostic and feedback support. The Ethics Guardian emerged as an essential ambient safeguard. The study offers practical guidance for integrating human-centered GenAI into teacher professional development platforms and human-AI collaboration in LD.

The rapid development of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) offers both opportunities and challenges for teachers. Rather than replacing teachers, GenAI serves as a collaborative tool that enhances teaching practice by providing evidence, creative ideas, and refined materials for lesson planning, activity design, assessment, content generation, and timely feedback (Bond et al., 2024; Giannakos et al., 2024). This perspective of GenAI as a productive partner is strongly reinforced by conceptual frameworks such as Human-Centered AI (Shneiderman, 2020), Human–AI Collaboration (Akata et al., 2020), and Hybrid Intelligence  (Holstein et al., 2020). These perspectives emphasize GenAI’s potential to enhance creativity, inclusivity, and efficiency while keeping human judgment central. Sharples (2023) suggests that GenAI can take on social roles in learning—such as collaboration coach, co-designer, or possibility engine —shifting from simple assistance to more dialogic and exploratory interaction. However, constructive engagement of GenAI requires teachers to balance its benefits with awareness of its limitations and their responsibility as critical thinke rs and ethical practitioners (Divjak et al., 2025; Giannakos et al., 2024). Key concerns include over-reliance, reduced critical thinking, bias, and broader pedagogical and ethical risks. Scholars caution that premature or uncritical adoption could hinder rather than advance education. Therefore, effective GenAI integration requires teacher professional development that offers structured support for both tool use and pedagogical design (Giannakos et al., 2024; Hernández-Leo et al., 2025). Despite its recognized value, Learning Design (LD)  practices remains underused in both schools and higher education, due to barriers such as limited time, low confidence with design methods, insufficient digital skills, and lack of institutional and professional development  support (Dagnino et al., 2018). A line of effort to increase LD adoption is integrating supportive tools within dedicated p rofessional development environments (Beardsley et al., 2023).  The rise of GenAI intensifies this challenge while also opening new possibilities (Alkhasawneh, Lampropoulos, et al., 2025). Consequently, there is a growing need for tools that can both scaffold teachers’ design practices and promote the responsible and ethical use of GenAI in education (Albó et al., 2024; Ravi et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2025). In this context, our study addresses this wider problem by using one particular professional development platform as a methodological case to engage stakeholders in concrete reflection and prioritization of GenAI functions for teacher professional development. The insights gained may inf orm and extend to other LD and professional development contexts.

To address these combined needs, the TRAILS teacher professional development platform was developed within the Erasmus+ project “Towards the Responsible and Constructive Use of Generative AI in Language Learning at School.”  TRAILS functions as a professional development community, offering a collaborative environment for teachers to co-design GenAI-enhanced learning activities with a strong emphasis on pedagogical quality and responsible adoption. Its core novelty lies in integrating ethical reflection directly into the design workflow. Unlike tools that focus primarily on GenAI’s functional capabilities (e.g., content generation),  the platform guides teachers to critically address ethical issues 99 such as plagiarism, bias, and authorship when positioning GenAI as a learning partner in the design of learning activities. This approach promotes authentic student engagement and may contribute to improved learning outcomes (Giannakos et al., 2024). The platform combines collaborative lesson design with inquiry-driven teacher reflection and offers a shared digital space for documenting designs and exchanging resources (Saar et al., 2026). Despite the platform’s practical orientation toward ethics and community, research on how the platform can offer GenAI features to support teacher-AI collaboration when exploring and using the platform towards developing their learning design skills remains in its early stages, with clear calls for empirical studies to validate proposed functions and evaluate their relevance for teachers’ collective practices (Hernández-Leo et al., 2025). To structure GenAI support within the platform and address this gap, we drew on the GenAI Speculative Functions Framework for Learning Design (see Figure 1; Giannakos et al., 2024). This framework is central to our approach as it positions GenAI as a collaborative design partner—not a replacement for teacher expertise—and is explicitly intended to complement and extend human creativity and professional judgment. Building on this, we situate the framework within the established structure of the LD lifecycle. This design process is commonly organized into four interconnected phases: Preparation, Investigation, Conceptualization, and Authoring. In the Preparation phase, teachers define the context and learning objectives; in the Investigation phase, they analyze learner s’ needs and contextual constraints; the Conceptualization phase involves structuring pedagogical ideas into coherent strategies; and the Authoring phase translates these strategies into concrete instructional materials (Giannakos et al., 2024). While all phases are important, the early stages — Preparation, Investigation, and  Conceptualization—remain comparatively underexplored, despite their critical importance for pedagogical coherence and contextual relevance (Ch’ng, 2023; Parsons & Curry, 2024).  The GenAI Speculative Functions Framework addresses this gap by outlining potential roles for GenAI to support teachers in these crucial early phases  through scaffolding processes such as brainstorming, inquiry, structuring, and refinement. Specifically, the framework introduces five GenAI speculative functions, each conceived to complement the teacher's expertise (Alkhasawneh, Hernández-Leo, et al., 2025; Alkhasawneh, Lampropoulos, et al., 2025; Giannakos et al., 2024). These functions include the Co-Design Facilitator, which supports collaboration among stakeholders; the Enquiry Analyst, which generates insights into learner needs and contextual challenges; the Inspiration Hub, which sparks creativity by drawing on prior designs and community examples; the Design Tutor, which provides pedagogically grounded feedback; and the Prototyping Consultant, which assists in refining and formatting materials for implementatio n. Drawing on this theoretical foundation, we adapted this  framework to the platform’s context and proposed five candidate conversational agent  functions conceptually designed to make the speculative roles actionable for teachers. These functions are: the Authoring Assistant, which helps draft or refine task descriptions, learning objectives, and activity instructions; the Inspiration Hub, which suggests ideas, activity formats, or real-world examples by leveraging existing learning designs on the platform; the Design Feedback function, which reviews design drafts to strengthen their alignment, clarity, and engagement; the Possibility Engine, which proposes alternative activity structures or creative variations of learning activities; and finally, the Ethics Guardian, which monitors responsible GenAI use and addresses teacher concerns about originality and plagiarism. While some GenAI speculative functions were directly adapted into corresponding conversational agent functions—for instance, the Inspiration Hub was retained and the Design Tutor evolved into Design Feedback— others were reinterpreted to better align with the specific pedagogical and ethical orientation of the platform. This context-sensitive adaptation was informed by the platform's specific requirements and driven by the authors' 100 professional judgment, prioritizing alignment with collaborative design practices and reinforced safeguards for responsible GenAI use. Accordingly, the Co-Design Facilitator evolved into the Possibility Engine, with the latter focusing on generating alternative design options rather than merely simulating role-play. The Enquiry Analyst was fundamentally reimagined as the Ethics Guardian, shifting focus from learner profiling—which raised privacy concerns —to safeguarding responsible GenAI use by addressing i ssues of originality, plagiarism, and integrity. Finally, the Prototyping Consultant was adapted into the Authoring Assistant, emphasizing the drafting and refinement of instructional elements over technical formatting (see Table 1 below). Given these adaptations, a practitioner-oriented validation was required to assess the relevance and perceived value of the proposed conversational agent functions  for collaborative educational practice. This is especially important in emerging fields where empirical data is scarce, making practitioner evaluations indispensable for generating critical insights that strengthen research validity and applicability (Zon dervan-Zwijnenburg et al., 2017). Building on the adapted GenAI Speculative Functions F ramework, this paper presents  a conceptual validation study. It investigates how five candidate conversational agent functions —Authoring Assistant, Inspiration Hub, Design Feedback, Possibility Engine, and Ethics Guardian—can be embedded within the authentic LD environment of the TRAILS platform (used here as a case study). This process focused on assessing the conceptual relevance and perceived value of these proposed functions, rather than their fully implemented performance. Using aggregated data from 56 participants (teachers, experts, and researchers) collected between April and July 2025, the study explores: participants’ preferences and rationales for the candidate functions; their needs across LD phases; the validation of speculative-function rankings across participant groups; and the opportunities and concerns for GenAI integration in LD. These findings inform phased, responsible GenAI integration into TRAILS and contribute to the wider discourse on evidence-informed, ethical, and collaborative uses of GenAI in LD. The study is guided by the following research questions (RQs):

• RQ1. How do teachers, experts and researchers perceive and prioritize candidate conversational agent

functions aligned with the GenAI Speculative Functions Framework for LD, and what rationales underpin their preferences?

• RQ2. In which phases of the design process do teachers, experts and researchers perceive the most urgent

need for GenAI-supported assistance?

• RQ3. How do GenAI Speculative Function rankings converge or diverge across practicing teachers,

researchers and experts versus prospective teachers?

• RQ4. What perceived opportunities and risks do teachers, experts and researchers identify for

responsible GenAI integration in the platform, and what design and policy implications follow?

## Study design, participants, and procedure

This study employed a convergent mixed-methods design (Creswell & Creswell, 2017), combining quantitative and qualitative data to gain a comprehensive understanding  of teachers’, experts’, and researchers’ perspectives on GenAI support for LD. The study pursued four main aims: (a) to identify teachers ’ preferences and priorities for a GenAI assistant —operationalized as a GenAI-based conversational agent  aligned with the GenAI Speculative Functions Framework; (b) to determine in which phases of the LD process (Preparation, Investigation, Conceptualization, and  Authoring) participants perceive the greatest need for GenAI-supported assistance; (c) to validate the GenAI Speculative Functions Framework by ranking the functions according to their 101 perceived value, and examining how these rankings relate to participants’ conversational agent  functions preferences; and (d) to identify the perceived opportunities and risks associated with GenAI integration in the platform, which would then be used to generate evidence-informed design and policy implications for the platform's development.  A total of 56 participants took part between April and July 2025. Participants were organized into four distinct cohorts representing diverse professional perspectives: (1) 20 experts in GenAI, LD, and Educational Technology (EdTech); (2) 13 higher education teachers and researchers; (3) 10 prospective teachers enrolled in a Master’s program; and (4)  13 secondary school teachers. This cohort structure ensured small, interactive groups and enabled open discussions grounded in shared professional contexts while enriching the study with multidisciplinary and cross-sectoral insights. Each cohort participated in a single workshop facilitated by the same expert to maintain consistency in delivery. All workshops began with an orientation to the TRAILS platform to ensure familiarity with its features before engaging in a design task. Participants were subsequently asked to design use cases for GenAI-supported learning activit ies within the TRAILS platform, aligned with their teaching and institutional contexts. Afterward, participants were introduced to the GenAI Speculative Functions Framework for LD, mapped to five candidate conversational agent functions and their conceptual roles within the TRAILS platform (Authoring Assistant, Inspiration Hub, Design Feedback, Possibility Engine, and Ethics Guardian), as well as the four phases of the LD process (Preparation, Investigation, Conceptualization, and Authoring). Participants then completed the following tasks: (1) A 10-point Likert scale rating their current use of GenAI/LLMs for LD. (2) A conversational agent  function preferences task (multiple selection; indicating which functions participants considered useful) and a prioritization task  (single choice; identifying the one function seen as most important), accompanied by short written justifications for their selections, allowing us to assess the perceived conceptual value and rationale underpinning their choices. (3) A phase-needs task (selecting up to two LD phases where GenAI support was most needed). (4) A ranking of the five GenAI Speculative Functions on a scale from 1 to 5, where 1 represented the highest perceived value and 5 the lowest. This specific task was completed individually by two cohorts only and as a group consensus activity for one cohort, in which participants discussed and agreed on a shared ranking. (5) Open-ended reflections described participants’ perceived opportunities and concerns regarding the responsible integration of GenAI in LD, which informed subsequent design and policy implications. The cohort representing secondary school teachers did not participate in the ranking task of the GenAI Speculative Functions due to time constraints during the workshops, resulting in a smaller sample size for that component. The exact N  is reported for each analysis. Participation was voluntary, informed consent was obtained, and all responses were pseudonymized in accordance with GDPR-aligned institutional ethics protocols.

## Data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics (frequencies, means, medians, modes, and standard deviations) to summarize patterns in conversational agent  function preferences, prioritization, and perceived needs across the design phases. Given the ordinal nature of the measures and the relatively small sample size, descriptive summaries were the most appropriate analytical approach. For the GenAI Speculative Functions validation, results from the experts, teachers, and researchers were aggregated and analyzed based on their individual rankings, while the prospective teachers’ group-based ranking was analyzed using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) to measure the level of consensus.  Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006), combining deductive coding—guided by the  GenAI Speculative Functions F ramework and the phases of the design process—with inductive coding to capture emergent themes. Coding was conducted by one trained researcher and peer-checked by the co-author to ensure reliability and fidelity to participants’ perspectives. Triangulation across instruments (function preferences and prioritization, phase needs, and written justifications) further enhanced validity.  Finally, quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated  to link observable trends with participants’ rationales, providing a cohesive interpretation  of how GenAI can enhance creativity, efficiency, and pedagogical decision-making in LD.

## Participants’ context and GenAI use

Participants reported varied levels of prior engagement with GenAI and L LMs in their LD practices. On a 10-point Likert scale (where 1 represents “never” and 10 represents “very frequently”), usage clustered around the midpoint (mean = 5.3; median = 5; mode = 5), with a standard deviation of 2.8 reflecting substantial variability across participants. In total,  43% reported low use (scores of 2– 4), 29% moderate use (5 –7), and 27% high use (8–10). This distribution illustrates a continuum ranging from minimal reliance and sporadic experimentation to systematic and deeply integrated use. Qualitative accounts provided further insight into these patterns. Those with 102 low engagement tended to use GenAI sporadically, often for minor modifications, rephrasing, or occasional inspiration. Participants with moderate engagement described employing it more deliberately—for example, to co-design activities, generate assessments, explore creative alternatives, or refine instructional materials.  Those with high engagement portrayed GenAI as a daily companion, serving as both a productivity enhancer and a creative partner in lesson planning, assessment, and pedagogical innovation. Collectively, these findings suggest a continuum of adoption, with over half of participants (56%) reporting moderate to high reliance, highlighting an evolving landscape in which GenAI is steadily becoming embedded in LD practices. Participants’ preferences for conversational agent functions, prioritizations, and justifications Across the five proposed conversational agent functions, the Inspiration Hub was the most frequently selected (33/56; 58%), followed by Design Feedback and Possibility Engine  (24/56; 42% each), with the Authoring Assistant and Ethics Guardian close behind (22/56; 39% each). When participants were asked not only to indicate multiple preferences but also to identify a single highest priority (38 respondents completed this task), the Inspiration Hub again led (17 first-place votes). The Authoring Assistant—des pite fewer overall selections—was ranked first by half of those who selected  it (11/22). Design Feedback  (5 first-place votes) and  the Possibility Engine (2) were seen as valuable but secondary enhancers, while the Ethics Guardian (3) was viewed as an important safeguard rather than a core interactive feature. ## Participants’ demand for GenAI support by design phase

Across the four design phases, participants  (N = 56) most often requested Gen AI support via a conversational agent during the Investigation phase (34%), citing support for exploring pedagogical strategies, locating resources, and interpreting learner needs. The next highest need was  the Authoring phase (27%), where teachers sought assistance  in drafting and refining materials to translate ideas into implementable activities.  The Conceptualization phase (24%) followed, reflecting interest in structuring idea s and sequencing activities to overcome creative blocks. The Preparation phase (15%) was least selected, suggesting that defining goals and highlighting context remains largely grounded in teachers’ professional judgment. Justifications revealed that the Investigation phase is time-consuming and cognitively demanding, making GenAI support for resource exploration, strategy scanning, and needs analysis particularly valuable. The Authoring phase was described as tedious yet highly amenable to GenAI-enabled drafting and refinement. The Conceptualization phase was framed as one in which GenAI can act as a co-designer, helping to structure and sequence ideas. By contrast, the Preparation phase was viewed as best led by teachers’ expertise, as it requires goal-setting, curricular alignment, and ethical and contextual awareness beyond GenAI’s current capacity — positioning GenAI a as minor, supplementary aid at t his phase. Overall, the results indicate a clear hierarchy — from Investigation to Authoring to Conceptualization to Preparation—that can guide phased integration: prioritizing research/inspiration and drafting supports, positioning  creative structuring as a complementary aid, and treat ing Preparation as predominantly human-led. These phase-based findings reinforce the patterns observed in the conversational agent  function preferences and prioritization analysis. The strong demand for support in the Investigation and Authoring phases echoes the prioritization of the Inspiration Hub and Authoring Assistant as core functions, while Conceptualization aligns with complementary creativity and structuring aids such as the Possibility Engine. Preparation, by contrast, remains grounded in professional expertise, resonating with the view that pedagogical and ethical framing should be safeguarded but not foregrounded. Taken together, the convergence of function-level and phase-level analyses emphasizes a two-pillar design need for the platform:

(1) GenAI-enabled inspiration and exploration in the early stages of design, and (2) GenAI-enhanced drafting and

refinement in the later stages, with other functions serving as valuable complements rather than primary drivers.

## Participants’ validation of GenAI speculative functions

The validation of the GenAI Speculative Functions  was conducted with three different cohorts who applied different ranking methods according to their context and composition. The first two—experts  in GenAI, LD, and EdTech (n = 20) and higher education teachers and researchers (n = 13)—jointly formed a combined group that completed individual rankings of the five functions on a scale from 1 (highest perceived value) to 5 (lowest). The third group—prospective teachers enrolled in a Master’s program (n = 10) —conducted a group-based ranking, where consensus levels were calculated using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W). Among the combined group of experts, teachers, and researchers, the Inspiration Hub emerged as the top-ranked function (mean = 1.67; median = 1; mode = 1), indicating strong consensus on its importance as a 104 creative catalyst supporting idea generation and conceptual exploration. The Design Tutor  (mean = 2.00; median = 2) and Co-Design Facilitator  (mean = 2.06; median = 2) followed closely, valued for their formative and collaborative potential. The Prototyping Consultant (mean = 2.58; median = 2) and Enquiry Analyst (mean = 2.61; median = 3) were rated moderately, reflecting that while participants recognized their usefulness for refinement and contextual analysis, they were viewed as less immediately essential. The relatively low standard deviations across functions (ranging from 0.94 to 1.56) indicate moderate agreement within this group, particularly regarding the consistent prioritization of creativity-and feedback-oriented functions. The prospective teachers, in contrast, reached an almost unanimous consensus (Kendall’s W = 0.96) in their group-based rankings. They placed the Design Tutor  first across all groups, followed by the Enquiry Analyst. The Inspiration Hub  was consistently ranked in the middle (third or fourth), while the Prototyping Consultant and especially the Co-Design Facilitator were ranked  as least relevant (fourth and fifth, respectively). This pattern suggests that early-career teachers prioritize diagnostic and feedback-oriented tools that support the  refinement and evaluat ion of  designs over generative or collaborative functions.

Taken together, these findings reveal both convergence and role-specific differentiation in how participants perceive the value of GenAI Speculative Functions for LD. Across groups, there was clear agreement on the importance of creativity and feedback as core areas of GenAI support. The combined group of experts, teachers, and researchers prioritized the Inspiration Hub and Design Tutor, emphasizing GenAI’s capacity to act as a creative engine for sparking ideas, facilitating the exploration of key concepts, and strengthens pedagogical alignment. Their appreciation of the Co-Design Facilitator further reflected recognition of GenAI’s potential to scaffold collaborative reflection and joint problem-solving in professional design settings. In contrast, the prospective teachers placed the greatest value on the Design Tutor  and Enquiry Analyst, revealing a focus on diagnostic and feedback-oriented tools that provide structure, formative guidance, and support for decisionmaking during early-career practice. This progression—from inspiration and collaboration among experts to feedback and evaluation among novice teachers —suggests that GenAI functions resonate differently depending on users’ experience and design autonomy. Accordingly, a phased integration approach within the platform — beginning with inspiration and feedback features and progressively expanding to collaborative and prototyping tools—would best align with the evolving needs and capacities of diverse teacher  groups. Opportunities and concerns for GenAI integration in LD Thematic coding of open responses revealed a balance of optimism and caution. On the opportunity side, participants highlighted practical, high-leverage uses of GenAI in LD. The most frequently cited opportunities were: (a) Creativity support—rapid idea generation, alternative activity formats, and examples to overcome design blocks; (b) Efficiency—time savings in drafting  and refining objectives, task descriptions, resources, and variants for different levels; (c) Differentiation—tailoring and adapting mat erials to learner profiles; (d) Prototyping/simulation—quickly producing illustrative artefacts for lessons; and (e) Pedagogical  breadth— surfacing a wider repertoire of pedagogical approaches. Collectively, these point to GenAI’s value as both a creative catalyst and a productivity amplifier in teachers’ everyday design work.  Counterbalancing these opportunities, concerns clustered around: (a) Professional risks—over-reliance that may hinder teacher creativity, reduce critical reflection, or homogenize outp uts; (b) Quality risks—inaccurate, misleading, or decontextualized content requiring careful review; (c) E thics and integrity —plagiarism, opaque sourcing, and bias; (d) D ata and privacy—uncertainty about using learner data to personalize suggestions; and (e) P ractical barriers—limited time and training to use tools effectively  and integrate them into real classroom constraints. Several participants stressed that GenAI should assist, not replace, pedagogical judgment.

This study set out to investigate how teachers, experts and researchers perceive, prioritize, and rationalize the integration of GenAI into teacher-AI collaboration in LD, as well as the opportunities and concerns they associate with its use. By using the TRAILS Platform as a professional development context and methodological case, we were able to engage stakeholders in concrete reflection and prioritization of GenAI functions, directly contributing to the wider discourse on how GenAI can systematically enhance collaborative and reflective LD processes. Building on the GenAI Speculative Functions Framework for LD (Giannakos et al., 2024), the study adapted five candidate conversational agent functions for conceptual validation. Across analyses, a consistent pattern emerged: GenAI is valued for  saving time, enhancing  creativity, and scaffold ing drafting, while raising concerns  about pedagogical depth, professional agency, and ethics.

At the function level, the Inspiration Hub and Authoring Assistant emerged as core tools, reflecting views of GenAI as a creative collaborator that complements human expertise (Sharples, 2023; Holstein et al., 2020). Design Feedback  and the Possibility Engine  were seen as enhancers, while the Ethics Guardian—though less prioritized—was expected as an embedded safeguard aligned with Human-Centered AI principles (Shneiderman, 2020). Conversational agents further support the dialogic nature of GenAI, enabling iterative and exploratory interaction (Sharples, 2023). At the phase level, demand was strongest in the Investigation and Authoring phases, echoing literature that identifies these stages as resource-intensive and cognitively demanding (Parsons & Curry, 2024; Ch’ng, 2023). The Conceptualization phase played a secondary but useful role for GenAI collaboration, while the Preparation phase remained largely teacher-led. Role-based differences also emerged: practicing teachers prioritized creativity and inspiration, whereas prospective teachers emphasized feedback and diagnostic support—reflecting how perceptions of GenAI align with professional experience (Dagnino et al., 2018). Overall, support, particularly for differentiation, but concerns persisted around over-reliance, standardization, ethical risks, and data privacy (Divjak et al., 2025; Bond et al., 2024). This study has several limitations. The sample size (N = 56) was relatively small, limiting generalizability. The focus on the design phase of the LD lifecycle  leaves other stages —particularly Implementation and Evaluation—underexplored. Validation involved only three cohorts, reducing the diversity of perspectives. As a workshop-based study, findings reflect perceived usefulness rather than long-term adoption. Moreover, the study is based on the conceptual validation of candidate conversational agent funct ions that were not yet implemented in the platform, capturing perceived usefulness and prioritization rather than observed usability or impact. Nevertheless, this conceptual validation step remains important for ensuring relevance and aligns with strategies supporting LD adoption (Beardsley et al., 2023). Building on these insights, future work will focus on empirically evaluating the integrated conversational agent functions  and their impact on teachers’ design practices, efficiency, creativity, pedagogical decision-making, and learner outcomes. In conclusion, this study offers a human-centered prioritization of the Speculative Functions Framework for LD. Findings show that teachers value GenAI support that broadens options and improves efficiency, while emphasizing the need for ethical safeguards and preserved professional judgment. A phased rollout centered on Inspiration and Authoring, supported by transparent feedback and embedded ethics features, addresses these expectations and supports responsible GenAI integration in LD.
