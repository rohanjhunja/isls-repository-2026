# Fostering Metacognitive Awareness for Effective Human–AI Collaboration in Learning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This exploratory study investigated whether experiential learning is more effective thandirect instruction for developing students' metacognitive awareness of AI's effects on learning. Among 126 first-year engineering students, the experimental group completed hands-on exercises demonstrating productive and counterproductive human– AI collaboration, while controls received equivalent lectures. Using bespoke exercises and a novel questionnaire and construct-level analysis, the experiential group showed significant increases in metacognitive awareness and AI engagement, suggesting that direct experience with AI's cognitive impacts is essential for developing reflective human–AI partnerships.

The emergence of generative AI (GenAI) based on Large Language Models (LLMs) has fundamentally transformed educational contexts, creating new paradigms for human-AI collaboration in learning (Giannakos et al., 2025; Hernández-Leo et al., 2025). Students are already using these  tools regardless of institutional policies (Yan et al., 2024), shifting the educational question from whether to allow AI toward how to help students collaborate with it effectively. Students lack the metacognitive awareness necessary to distinguish between productive and counterproductive AI support (Fan et al., 2025). Evidence reveals starkly different outcomes depending on human-AI collaboration patterns: inappropriate use can impair memory formation, reduce problem-solving skills, and create an "illusion of competence" (Gerlich, 2025; Kosmyna et al., 2025), while strategic collaboration aligned with learning principles enhances outcomes (Orynbassarova & De Lasala Porta, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). The ISAR model proposed by Bauer et.al (2025) captures this spectrum, categorizing AI's educational effects as inversion, substitution, augmentation, or redefinition depending on how the tool is integrated into learning. The challenge is fundamentally metacognitive: students need awareness of how AI interactions affect their cognitive processes, memory encoding, and learning outcomes. Emerging research suggests many students lack this awareness, exhibiting what has been described as metacognitive laziness when using AI tools (Fan et al., 2025). Yet little research has explored how to best develop such awareness. Purely instructional approaches risk triggering psychological reactance  (Brehm, 1966) or fostering overconfidence without functional understanding (Castel et al., 2007; Dunning, 2011). Experiential learning theory suggests that concrete experience followed by reflection enables deeper understanding than instruction alone (Kolb et al., 2001). This exploratory study examines whether experiential learning approaches are more effective than purely instructional approaches for promoting metacognitive awareness on the effects of human-AI collaboration in learning:

• RQ: Is experiential learning on productive and unproductive modes of human-AI collaboration more

effective than instructional approaches for promoting metacognitive awareness of how AI partnership affects learning?

The study followed a pre-post test design conducted in two-hour sessions. Both groups completed the MAI-AI questionnaire, an original instrument based on the Metacognitive Awareness Inventory (Schraw & Dennison,

1994) and the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (Duncan & McKeachie, 2005), before and after

intervention. The questionnaire consists of 28 items on a 5-point Likert scale across six constructs: Attitudes towards AI, AI Engagement, AI Literacy, Metacognitive Awareness, Metacognitive Regulation, and Autonomy & Control. The full instrument is available at Zenodo (Benazet i Montobbio, 2026). Participants were (N=126)  first-year engineering students from a higher education institution in Spain (after data cleaning from initial N=138), assigned to groups by class schedule: G1 (control, n=61) and G2 (experimental, n=65).  The control group (G1) received a traditional lecture explaining productive and unproductive AI collaboration patterns. The experimental group (G2) received the same lecture content but additionally engaged in four structured exercises allowing them to directly experience these patterns. Inspired by the ISAR model, acting as either as an inversion, substitution, augmentation or redefinition effect  (Bauer et al., 707 2025), the exercises followed a three-phase structure: (1) perform a task with AI (5 –10 min), (2) distraction task (∼5 min), (3) similar transfer task without AI (∼10 min). A table with a complete description of the exercises can be found at Zenodo (Benazet i Montobbio, 2026).

The experiential group (G2) showed significantly greater increases in both AI Engagement (r = 0.383) and Metacognitive Awareness (r = 0.328). Notably, the control group's median change in Metacognitive Awareness was zero, indicating that lecture-based instruction alone did not shift awareness of AI's effects on learning. No other constructs differed significantly between groups, including Metacognitive Regulation (p =.910), suggesting the intervention's effects were specific to awareness and engagement rat her than a general shift across all dimensions.

The construct-level findings provide evidence that experiential engagement with both productive and counterproductive AI collaboration patterns promotes metacognitive awareness more effectively than lecture-based instruction. Students who directly experienced the consequences of different collaboration modes developed stronger awareness of AI’s cognitive effects and greater intentions to engage with AI in their learning. These findings connect to work on cognitive offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016)  and productive failure (Kapur, 2008), suggesting that firsthand experiences is what drives metacognitive development. The specificity of the effect is worth noting: the intervention increased awareness and engagement but not regulation, attitudes, literacy, or autonomy. This pattern is consistent with metacognition theory, which distinguishes knowledge of cognition from regulation of cognition and recognizes that the latter typically requires more sustained deliberate practice (Flavell, 1979; Pintrich, 2002). Whether and how increased awareness eventually translates into strategic regulation of AI use remains an open quest ion for future research.

## Limitations and future work

This exploratory study used a short intervention window (two hours), relied on self-report measures, and the MAIAI instrument awaits psychometric validation. Group assignment by class schedule limits causal inference. Future research should incorporate extended interventions, behavioral trace data, and psychometric validation of the MAI-AI across larger and more diverse samples.
