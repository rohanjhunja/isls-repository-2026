# If One Hack Week is Pedagogically Effective, Why Not Ten?

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While hackathons are established pedagogical tools, questions persist about measuring their impact when integrated longitudinally into curricula. We investigate a data science curriculum incorporating eight Kaggle-based hackathons over ten weeks with 137 participants. Using immersive ethnographic methods, we constructed narrative vignettes illuminating how longitudinal hackathon participation fosters identity development, distributed cognition, and community formation. Drawing on Communities of Practice and distributed cognition as both design principles and analytic lenses, we argue that hackathons, when embedded as recurring curricular structures, function as sites for constructing consequential learning identities.

Hackathons are valued for fostering experiential learning, collaboration, and professional skills (Towhidi, 2022; Flus, 2022; Oyetade, 2024), yet fundamental challenges persist: most research remains empirical rather than theoretical (Schulten, 2024), asse ssment relies on self-reported surveys, and studies predominantly examine hackathons as isolated events rather than longitudinal curricular structures (Chau & Gerber, 2023). We address these gaps by examining a data science curriculum in which hackathons serve as the primary pedagogical method over ten weeks with 137 participants in eight Kaggle-based competitions. Unlike Puthipiroj (2025), who quantitatively assesses outcomes through rankings and submission frequency, we adopt immersive ethnography to capture the experiential and identity-forming dimensions of sustained hackathon participation. Our research questions are: (1) How does sustained participation in a longitudinal hackathon curriculum shape participants’ learning identities and community membership? (2) How are cognitive and collaborative processes distributed across people, tools, and social structures during extended hackathon engagement? Program design During early 2025, an AI/ML program selected 137 participants from an open virtual first phase into an intensive second phase. Participants were divided into six teams of roughly 25, composed to balance experience levels — mixing high school students (ages 15–18), undergraduates, recent graduates, and working professionals (up to age 50)—with varying proportions of programming backgrounds. The program featured eight five-day Kaggle hackathons: two weeks online via Discord, four weeks onsite at a residential s ite in Thailand where participants lived alongside mentors, and two final online weeks. Each weekly cycle introduced a Kaggle challenge on Monday, provided lectures Tuesday– Wednesday, and devoted Thursday –Friday to development. Kaggle’s realtime leaderboard provided immediate feedback, and weekly pitching rounds evaluated originality and communication beyond predictive accuracy.

## Theoretical framework

Two frameworks guided both design and analysis. Communities of Practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991) frames learning as increasing participation in a community; we designed the curriculum to scaffold legitimate peripheral participation and analytically traced how  participants moved from peripheral to central roles, attending to moments when community membership became salient. Distributed cognition (Salomon, 1993) provided a lens for understanding how cognitive work was shared across team members, tools (Kaggle, L LMs, Discord), and social settings; in design this motivated the team-based, multi-platform structure, and analytically we traced how intellectual labor was distributed during critical problem-solving episodes. The first author conducted immersive ethnographic fieldwork throughout the program—residing in the same hotel during the onsite phase, visiting every team room daily, and documenting observations in fieldnotes covering team dynamics, task allocation, and interpersonal interactions. Digital ethnography extended this presence through Discord and Gather Town during online phases. Twenty-four semi-structured interviews (30–60 minutes) captured perspectives across the full demographic range (ages 15–50). Fieldnotes, transcripts, and communication 797 logs were analyzed through iterative thematic coding oriented by our two frameworks, and recurring patterns were distilled into narrative vignettes built from triangulated data sources to illuminate distinct theoretical dimensions. Vignette 1: Distributed cognition under pressure On Week 3, Jean, a 20-year-old university graduate who had participated in dozens of hackathons and sold medical algorithms to hospitals, took on unofficial leadership of his team. The task, developing forecasting models for retail SKUs, seemed straightforward, but inconsistencies accumulated: missing values without logical patterns, duplicate entries across time periods. During a meeting with the corporate sponsor, Jean asked what his teammates had all been thinking: “Why is your data so poorly kept?” The question violated unspoken protocols of deference. Organizers pulled him aside with concerns, but his teammates defended him. Their investigation revealed that the evaluation metric itself appeared rule-based—a crucial insight. With only four Kaggle submissions allowed per day, each became a costly experiment probing the hidden logic of the ground truth. Midway through the week, a COVID outbreak forced 11 team members into hotel-room isolation—the most of any team. The team’s cognitive network adapted: isolated members remained tethered through Discord, analyzing data and sharing observations. Crucially, an isolated member noticed a pattern in submission scores that the room-based subgroup had missed—reshaping their fina l strategy. Distributed cognition operated across physical and digital spaces, with the platform mediating contributions no co-located subg roup could have produced alone. Jean’s trajectory also illustrates legitimate peripheral participation in reverse: his expertise initially positioned him centrally, but the team’s validation of his confrontational approach—and the organizers’ pushback—soci alized him into the community’s norms around challenging authority constructively. Vignette 2: Identity formation through sustained participation By the time Week 6 arrived, Pine’s stress had reached a maximum. At eighteen, fresh from high school graduation, she was acutely aware of what everyone already knew: her team was fundamentally different. While other groups boasted participants with extensive hackathon experience, polished portfolios, and even a PhD researcher and an adjunct professor, Pine’s group had finished last twice and second-to-last three times. Week 6 carried the largest prize pool yet, backed by a major Thai bank actively recruiting for internships. A team gathering that was supposed to help nearly broke them. Staff and researchers were asked to leave the room, and everyone anonymously wrote down their reasons for attending. Two distinct groups emerged: one vocal faction wanted to win and double their efforts; the other, quieter but equally present, had come primarily for learning—the prize was secondary to their curiosity. This moment —what Lave and Wenger might recognize as the community negotiating its shared enterprise—proved catalytic rather thandivisive. Against all expectations, Pine’s team won. What made the victory particularly meaningful was the timing: Week 6 was the final onsite week, and organizers had planned a grand celebration where researchers, lecturers, staff, and all 137 participants gathered to mark the end of the intensive month. As the evening unfolded, each team voted for their most valuable member. Pine heard her name—recognized not for technical prowess but for holding the team together across weeks of losses. In Nasir’s (2020) terms, he r trajectory exemplifies consequential participation: she moved from peripheral uncertainty to recognized community membership, constructing a learning identity through sustained engagement rather than isolated achievement. These vignettes reveal what surveys miss: the formation of what Nasir (2020) describes as consequential participations in learning pathways. Both Jean and Pine underwent identity transformations only legible across weeks of sustained engagement. Pine moved from peripheral uncertainty to recognized community membership; Jean’s expertise was reshaped by the community’s norms around constructive challenge. These are trajectories that require longitudinal observation to document, not outcomes that post-event satisfaction surveys candetect. Jean’s story further illustrates how distributed cognition operates as an emergent property of longitudinal engagement—his team’s cognitive network reorganized across physical isolation and digital platforms over days, not hours, with Discord functioning as genuine cognitive infrastructure because the team had built shared practices over prior weeks. Critically, these processes required time—the ten-week structure provided what Nasir calls “the fodder” for constructing productive identities. The field should move beyond evaluating hackathons as isolated interventions and investigate how recurring, longitudinally embedded structures create conditions for consequential learning.
