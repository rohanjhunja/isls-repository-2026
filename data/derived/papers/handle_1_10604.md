# Specific Motivational Effects on Students after Experiencing an Imaginative VR “Molecular World”

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Virtual reality (VR) experiences are well-documented for increasing student motivation, but studies often stop short of providing important details about the link between the kinds of experiences and types of motivation. For example, do realistic VR simulations work better than VR imaginary worlds at increasing subject matter interest? Pedagogically, it is of interest to determine if there are divergent effects on students when interacting with different types of VR experiences. This study uses quantitative analysis of pre-post survey data to bring clarity to the motivational aspects of different types of chemistry-based VR experiences. Eighty 8th-12th grade students interacted with a VR chemistry lab simulation and either a traditional “ball-and-stick” physical manipulatives intervention or a VR molecular world intervention. The students who experienced the imaginative VR world had significantly greater increases in their agreement with the statements "I like my science class" and "I am interested in careers that use technology".

## Introduction

Virtual reality (VR) is known to be an exciting new  technology that can provide fascinating experience s, especially for education. The ability of VR experie nces to increase student motivation in educational contexts is well-documented (Hu-Au & Lee, 201 7), but it often neglects to  provide important details about the kinds of experiences and links to types of motivation. These nuances are difficult to parse out since research often reports simply the use of a VR experience and general motiv ational results. Specifically for chemistry education, two major categories of VR experiences are laboratory experiment simulations and immersive experiences where users interact in an  imaginative world with abstract scientific artifacts (e.g., atoms and molecules). While each experience provides different advantages for learning, i t is pedagogically useful to determine what motivational effects arise when interacting with these different kinds of VR experiences. This study aims to clarify these different learning designs and the changes in motivation or interest that may result for students.

## Background and theoretical framework

VR’s affordance of easily reifying abstract concepts into concrete experiences is one of the best reasons for its use in science education. Its ability to digitally replicate realistic lab si tuations is also a highly valuable tool. Past research has shown student interest increases due to the use of VR lab simulations (Tatli & Ayas, 2013) or a combination of simulations and abstract visual izations (Crosier et al., 2000). Also, a study invo lving carbon nanotube VR visualizations demonstrated positive motivational impacts (Ferrell et al., 2019), supporting the idea that VR experiences of microscopic, invisible objects, could be highly motivating. As VR applications in science education continue to grow, there is a need for guidance in the designs of these learning experiences. The Cognitive Affective Model of Immersive Learning  (CAMIL; Makransky & Petersen, 2021), the Control-Value Theory of Achievement Emotions (CVTAE; Pekrun, 2006), and Bandura’s Self-efficacy theory

(1977) guide this research in exploring the motivational  potential for the VR experiences being tested. CAMIL

identifies six factors that can lead to learning outcomes, including interest, motivation, self-efficacy, embodiment, cognitive load, and self-regulation. This study is specifically focused on o bserving the affective factor of situational interest and whether it differs for different types of chemistry-based VR experiences. CAMIL and this study both rely on self-efficacy theory as an explanation of positive effec ts stemming from student accomplishments. The CVTAE presents a framework for analyzing the ef fects of an agent’s control and values on their emotions in relation to educational achiev ement. Within this, the actions of the students in this study’s VR experience may be directly relatable to the feelings they report at the study’s end. This study uses quantitative analysis of survey dat a to bring clarity to the motivational aspects and benefits of different types of VR experiences. It directly compares the use of imaginative VR worlds with realistic VR lab simulations and sheds light on the unique effects on young student s’ STEM  journeys. The research question this study aims to answer is: How do diffe rent chemistry-based VR learning experiences affect student motivation and interest in STEM subjects?

## Study design

The data collection of this study took place in an urba n city in the Northeast US A. The research sample was 80 children, ages 11-18, predominantly from Latino and African-American backgrounds, randomly selected from a local community center. Forty-two students were female and 38 were male. 34% of the students rated themselves as “having tried VR numerous times” and 45% had tried VR only two times or less. There were four conditions into which students were  randomly divided. Students completed the survey prior to conducting an interactive lesson on the su b-micro foundations of chemical reactions (the  sub-micro intervention) and a laboratory exercise involving chemical reactions. They then completed the same survey again after completing the two experiences. The laborator y exercise was a VR simulation while the sub-micro intervention could have been a separate V R experience or a real-life, hands-on session using a traditional “ball-and-stick” modeling kit, depending on their condition g roup. The order of conducting the lab exercise and the intervention could also vary depending on their condition group. Materials VR laboratory The laboratory experiment involved mixing anhydrous copper (II) chloride with water and then introducing a piece of aluminum metal to the aqueous solution. This particular lab exercise was recommended for middle to high school (ages 11-18 years) chemistry students by the American Association of Chemistry Teachers (AACT, 2020). The VR lab was designed with standard safety and lab equipment (see Figure 1). Figure 1 The Design and Materials of the VR Chemistry Lab Simulation and VR Sub-micro Intervention Sub-Micro intervention The Physical Manipulatives version of the sub-micro intervention was a short introductory lesson on atoms, molecules, and atomic behavior. It utilized the “ball-and-stick” physically manipulable objects used traditionally in chemistry classes. The lesson that incorporated these manipulatives was designed by the primary inv estigator and verified as appropriate by two subject matter experts. The VR intervention was a gamified VR experience designed by the principal investigato r. The user is in the “molecular world” where atoms and molecules are at the same scale as the participant. Within the lesson, the user is guided verbally by a n outside experimenter. The script is identical to the script in the Physical Manipulatives version except for a few references to the controls in VR. A key attribute of this lesson is the user’s ability to manipulate the atoms and use physical gestures to bring them together to cre ate new molecules or break them apart into their constituen t atoms. ## Measures and data analysis

The STEM Attitude & Motivation Survey questions wer e taken from the science and technology sections of  the STEM-Career Interest Survey (Kier et al., 2014). This psychometrically sound survey was  conducted at pretest and repeated as a post-test. The survey answers were in Likert scale form and were assigned values of 1 for “Strongly Disagree” up to 5 for “Strongly Agree”. Since the data from the Likert scale are ordinal and not normally distributed, a non-parametric test, the Mann-Whitney U test, was used for analysis.

This was divided by level of embodimen t, where the high embodiment condition utilized the  VR modality and the low embodiment condition used the traditional ball-and-stick manipulatives. ## Discussion

Students who used the VR sub-micro intervention significantly increased in their  positive responses over the Traditional intervention students for the statements, “I like my science class” and “I am interested i n careers that use technology”. This supports past research that has described VR as a highly effective tool for increasing student motivation and interest (Lee & Hu-Au, 2021). However, it is also important to note th at even the students in the Traditional condition had a substantial VR experience in the VR lab simulation. Thus, the significant change s in interest and motivation are not just from using VR, but potentially from the specific “molecular world” experience only the VR condition had. The immersive experience  of being in the VR microscopic world was  a highly motivating STEM experience for students, as has been observed with other immersive learning experiences (SáezLópez et al., 2015). T he use of the VR intervention significantly increas ed students’ positive affect towards their science classes even though the research study took place outside of the traditional time and place of formal schooling. It is possible that having this experienc e with the VR intervention was interesting enough that it positively influenced their memories and outlook on their science classes. Effects like this are not unheard of; research shows that some of the benefits of high-quality afterschool science programs are improvemen ts in in-school science achievement and greater interest in pursuing scienc e careers (Newell et al., 2015). Both of these improvements can follow from and lead to an increase in enjoymen t of science classes. Similarly, informal science e ducation programs have been observed to increase middle scho ol students’ confidence in taking advanced STEM cou rses when back in school (Ricks, 2006). It is possible that through the VR intervention, students became aware of the value of understanding chemistry content, and as CVTAE presupposes, this led to grea ter levels of positive emotions for similar educational situations.

The VR condition also demonstrated strong improveme nt in student attitudes toward technology. The fact that students felt more interested in pursuing careers with technology is not surprising considering what they accomplished in the study. In the VR intervention, they successfully split apart molecules and created  new ones with different atoms. Most students had never had a n experience like that before, where they utilized VR technology to explore abstract science content. Thi s sense of accomplishment, coupled with learning to  master a new technology, seemed to contribute to the students’ stronger sense of confidence in utilizing technology. Based on Self-efficacy theory, the students experienced a mastery experience that led to a stronger sense of efficacy and motivation in the content area (Bandura, 1977).

## Conclusion

This study demonstrated in this sample population that there was a different motivational effect when experiencing a VR imaginative “molecular world” versus a VR lab simulation. All participants had an experience with VR but the ones who engaged with the “molecular world” sho wed greater motivation in certain STEM categories. One finding of note that is not included in this study is if the conditions led to greater academic outcom es, either on this study’s content or in future STEM classes. Future analyses from this study aim to explore these questions and bring clarity to the connections between VR learning experiences and learning outcomes.
