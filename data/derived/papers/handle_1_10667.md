# Women Reaching Desired Identities in Educational Contexts Through Improvisation in Shifting Figured Worlds: A Study of Memoir

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study focuses on the identity transformation of women who come from a traumatic childhood to later become a college educated through improvisation. Using the lens of Holland et al.’s figured worlds, I explore the identity negotiation in of two women who have recounted their experiences in memoir. Results indicate improvisations in early educational experiences and role models helped to transform the identity of the women in the shifting figured worlds of childhood survival to being college educated.

## Introduction

Educational attainment is a goal and focus of young people in America, but often the journey to reach that goal is difficult because of limitations in the form of par ental neglect or abuse, inadequate community and in stitutional supports, or environmental barriers. National Institute for Children’s Health Quality (2024) estimates nearly half of American children are exposed to Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) such as toxic stress and hous ehold dysfunction. Children who live in chronically dysfunctional environments exhibit struggles with learning, school attendance, and social interactions (Terrasi & Crai n de Galarce, 2017) Yet some overcome these obstacl es and achieve despite the overwhelming odds. Through prev ailing over these phenomena, identity is transforme d, and new selves emerge. Holland et al. (1998) propose: Thus persons and, to a lesser extent, groups  are caught in the tensions between past histories that have settled in them and the present discourse s and images that attract them or somehow impinge upon them. In this continuous self-fashioning, identities are hard-won standpoints that, however dependent upon social support and however v ulnerable to change, make at least a modicum of self-direction possible. They are possibilities for mediating agency (p. 4). Negotiating the space between those past histories and future identities is impossible for some and difficult for most. The emerging question is, then, how do people reconcile with their pasts in w hich they have settled and become a part of to seek a  desired identity and author themselves to reach those goals ? Through Holland et al.’s (1998) concept of figured worlds a nd the advancement of narratives through improvisat ion as agency, this article will provide a conceptualization of two women who produced identities in a figured world of survival in childhood to later enter a figured world of a high achieving college student. Viewing these challenges through shifting figured worlds aids us in understanding what is necessary t o help troubled children and teens overcome their past hardships tonegotiate a world of achievement.

## Conceptual framework

Figured worlds according to Holland et al. (1998) a re social and cultural phenomena where people weave  their identities through features distinct to the people who participate in same contexts. For example, Holland describes the cultural world of Alcoholics Anonymous where it s members transform their identities from drinking nonalcoholics to non-drinking alcoholics (Holland et al. 1998).  Individuals can enter or be recruited into figured worlds, and even at times forced into them. Shared characteristics include common narratives and modus operandi for events or activities in the world where positio ns matter. Individuals have common concerns or orie ntations and common roles they fill within a specific contex t, and they may have common artifacts that have spe cial significance.

Figured worlds have been used to describe identity production in political, social, and educational contexts where different worlds encounter each othe r (Holland et al., 1998). Urrieta (2007) describes figured worlds of Chicana/o Activists where people develop new understandings of their lives and produce new identities of being educators within the world to further thei r social justice aims. Naraian (2010) described a s pace of negotiating identity with the professional boundari es of special education and general education teach ers in an inclusive classroom.  Urrieta (2007) also introduced a hybrid world in which African American male youth were in the figured world prison but entered the learning world of a college student where they authored new identities of learners.

Improvisation Holland et al. (1998) defines identity development occurring through the constant changing of conceptualizations and artifacts through the improvisations of its actors. The woman who climbed up the house to avoid the cultural impropriety of entering a higher caste home in Nepa l is an iconic example of improvisation when faced wi th a problematic situation that requires a solution, though unconventional, tonegotiate a particular situation (Holland et al., 1998). Bateson (1989) contends that “life as an improvisatory art, about the ways we combine familiar and unfamiliar components in response tonew situations” (p. 3). Individuals constantly face perplexing situations and must use existing cultural resources to address problems and conditions in their present. Improvisations as agency are important because common conventions, such as s table family life and uninterrupted schooling are n ot available to youth who are forced to survive on their own. They must remake the conditions of their lives (Bateson, 1989), and their path to a brighter future lies in their improvisations. Achievement, then, does not follow a straight trajectory for some, but is patched together like a  quilt, exemplifying the “creative potential of interrupted and conflicted lives” (Bateson, 1989, p.9).

In this paper, I seek to explore how two women who experienced childhood trauma and instability engaged in improvisations to remake their understanding of themselves and their trajectory towards aca demic success. I employ their own writing to understand t his process through the lens of figured worlds and improvisation as agency.

## Methods

Data I draw upon two memoires as my primary data source to explore how rich themes of improvisation as agency can be developed that might guide future collaborations  with research participants. Walker (2017) indicate s how autobiographical stories and the recounting of one’s own feelings, reactions, and motives influence what they do or think in situations within context of cultural settings. I chose memoir because of the rich narrative in which the authors recount the phenomena of their lives that s hape their id entities. Data for this paper comes from the memoires of two women who overcome traumatic circumstances  in their childhoods to attend elite universities. Breaking Night by Liz Murray is a personal account of her survival in a home of parents addicted to cocaine which includes description of her time being homeless to avoid being placed in foster care as well as her later attendance at Harvard University.  Educated by Tara Westover accounts her upbringing in a famil y of survivalists isolated from mainstream s ociety where she endure d sibling abuse, inadequate medical attention, and on ly periodic schooling while later completing college at Brigham Young University then graduate school at Cambridge. Both women exist in one figured world in which they are forging identities of resilience and survival to la ter move to a figured world of a college student.

## Analytic approach

In my analysis I applied the framework of interpretive phenomenological analysis which focuses on a partic ular phenomenon understood by individuals in particular contexts (Smith et al., 2022). The steps in the analytic process include 1) engagement with the data; 2) initial notes and c omments to explain what the researcher is observing;

3) forming “experiential statements”; 4) making con nections between experiential statements; 5) naming

emerging themes and organizing them; 6) working wit h larger samples emerging from the group (for this study, a comparison is made between the two cases); and 7)  looking for patterns across cases (Smith et al., 2 022). As I show in my findings, I developed the theme improvis ation in education to prepare for college. I organi ze my presentation of the theme through discussing each participants’ evidence of these theme.

## Findings

The emerging themes illustrated that the participan ts, Tara and Liz, perceived education as a pathway to a better life. Each accessed their agency through improvisat ion of preparing themselves for college through unconventional means. In addition, they eac h had a role model who informed their life path either dire ctly or indirectly. Through this improvisation and other supports, Tara and Liz were able to shift from a figured world of survival in childhood to a figured world of a college student. Improvisation in education Neither Tara nor Liz followed a typical trajectory of attending K-12 schools for their education. They had to make, as Bateson (1989) says, “desperate improvisation” for significant achievement to attain a college education. In Educated, Tara stated: “Learning in our family was entirely s elf-directed: you could learn anything you could teach yourself, after your work was done”  (Westover, p. 46). She learned how to read from on e of her oldest brother’s, Tony, when she was four years old. Although her parents used the term “homeschooled”, she only “did school” when she acquired her own textbooks and self-taught. I studied trigonometry for a month. I sometimes dre amed about sine, cosine and tangent, about mysterious angles and concussed computations,  but for all this I made no real progress. I could not self-teach trigonometry. But I knew someone who had (Wes tover, p. 126).

Tara improvises her math learning through seeking t he help of her older brother, Tyler, who had self-taught and had escaped home to get his formal education. Later, with Tyler’s encouragement, Tara decides to apply for college without a high school education. She improvises her education through studying specifically for the ACT and eventually scores high enough to be admitted to  college. This excerpt is the defining moment when resolves to go through with the application despite not having her parents’ approval: I applied to BYU a week later. I had no idea how to  write the application, so Tyler wrote it for me. He said I’d been educated according to a rigorous program designed by my mother, who’d made sure I met all the requirements to graduate.

My feeling about the application changed from day t o day, almost from minute to minute. Sometimes I was sure God wanted me to go to college, because he’d given me that twenty-eight [ACT score of 28]. Other times I was sure I’d been rejected, and that God would punish me for applying, for trying to abandon my own family. But whatever the outcome, I knew I would leave. I would go somewhere, even if it wasn’t to school (Westover, pp. 148-149). Tara accessed her agency through reflection of her emotions during the turning point of deciding to leave home and through overcoming an inner battle to reconcile her actions with God. In Breaking Night, Liz describes learning to read initially by watching her father read books he borrowed from the public library. She looked at pictures and pieced together words she knew. She had spotty attendance at school but improvised to pass every year before reaching high school: It’s not that I never went to school, but more like I passed through it the way a net passes through water, passively snagging whatever happens to drift  inside. Any formal education I received came from the few days I spent in attendance, mixed  with knowledge I absorbed from r andom readings of my or Daddy’s ever-growing supply of unreturned library books. And as long as I still showed up steadily the last few weeks of clas ses to take the standardized tests, I kept squeaking by from grade to grade (Murray, p. 112).

When she was 17 years old and homeless, Liz decides to return school. Through a recommendation of a friend, she finds an alternative high school where she is accepted with no prior credits. She works fe verishly to excel and graduate in only two years. She learns ab out college there and decides to apply for a New York Times scholarship. She writes of this turning point: I immediately picked up the Times scholarship application again….I held my pen to the  paper, trembling. I worked in some kind of trance, pouring  out everything I had onto that page. My frustrations, my sadness, all of my grief, they pushed the pen across the page; t hey wrote the essay, or the essay wrote itself. Whatever it was, it wasn’t me writing because I wasn’t there. I was floating above looking down on myself, watching  my hand move feverishly across the page, watching everything in my life that had ever held me back, breaking (Murray, p. 303). After this pinnacle turning point, Liz wins the scholarship, she applies to Harvard Univer sity where she is accepted, and moves to the figured world of a co llege student.  This passage reveals how she mediated her agency by allowing her emotions to drive her actions to begin the shift to a new cultured world. Improvisation as agency The findings indicate that Tara and Liz worked towa rd achieving a desired identity through improvisati on as agency by looking beyond their upbringing and scant educat ional experiences in childhood to move toward change. This improvisation helped them to shift from a fi gured world of survival to that of a college student/graduate. Bateson (1989) says “the recognit ion that many people lead lives of creative makeshi ft and improvisation surely has implications for how the n ext generation is educated and what we tell our son s and daughters” (p. 16). Surely then, the lives of Tara and Liz will have a grand influence of generations of readers to come.

## Discussion and significance

Considering how Tara and Liz accessed their agency in shifting figured worlds is an important undertak ing because it informs current educational contexts. Th e findings suggest that teens who are escaping trau matic circumstances may access alternate means o f education through improvisation. This has implica tions for alternative education placements for K-12 schooling in providing opportunity for recovery credits for teens who are homeless or who have significant gaps in their education. Educators who are cog nizant of the needs and struggles of teens who come from traumatic home env ironments will be in position to effectively assist  and support their unique needs. Particular attention ne ed be given to students who seek to be first genera tion college students. Tara and Liz had family members who had entered the figured world of college before them which gave them a symbolic agency which represented their own potential. Finally, a practical but powerful influence in these narratives is that of literacy. Wedin (20 20) asserts literacy as agency for positioning ones elf in a new setting and gaining power and equal opportunity for education. Without early literacy, Tara and Liz would not have been able tonegotiate their makeshift education which eventually helped them enter college.
