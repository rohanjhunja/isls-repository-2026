# Knowing for Using: Tracking Mathematics Preservice Teachers’ Knowledge of Dialogic Teaching in a Semester-Long PD Program

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Dialogic teaching pedagogy is widely acknowledged for its effectiveness in orchestrating meaningful discussions in mathematics classrooms. However, mathematics preservice teachers (MPSTs) often lack the knowledge to structure mathematical discussions, resulting in teacher-dominated classrooms. This study seeks to monitor the process of acquiring knowledge in dialogic teaching through three knowledge tests conducted during a semester-long professional development (PD) program for MPSTs. The study involved a group of 39 Chinese MPSTs. The findings revealed a positive trend in the acquisition of dialogic teaching knowledge among participants. Those MPSTs who received targeted training and guidance in dialogic teaching demonstrated significant improvement in the knowledge tests compared to their peers. This study represents an initial attempt to examine the changes in teacher knowledge related to dialogic teaching within PD initiatives. It underscores the importance of incorporating knowledge assessments to enhance the development of initial teacher education programs.

## Introduction

In contrast to authoritative teaching, in which teachers maintain control over classroom discussions (Aguiar et al., 2010), dialogic teaching pedagogy values students’ voices as they actively engage with the teacher or their peers to co-construct knowledge. This approach has been recogni zed for its effectiveness in increasing student engagement and fostering meaningful learning experi ences in the classroom (Alexander, 2018). Drawing on sociocultural constructivism, which emphasizes the role of social interactions in individual developme nt (Vygotsky & Cole, 1978), Sfard (2007) introduced th e commognitive framework to emphasize the intertwin ed roles of “communicative” and “cognitive” aspects in mathematics education. It is essential f or mathematics teachers to structure their language systematically to orchestrate productive talk with students and support their cognitive growth (Sfard, 2007).

While the advantages of incorporating dialogic teac hing pedagogy are widely recognized, mathematics preservice teachers (MPSTs) often lack the necessar y knowledge on how to organize mathematical discussions (Bishop et al., 2022; Goos, 2004). This challenge i s particularly prevalent in East Asian countries, w here collectivist cultural norms often encourage authoritarian teaching practices (Leung, 2001). Consequently, students may be more inclined to remain silent or engage in choral responses during mathematics classes (Xu & Clarke, 2013, 2019).

Knowledge acquisition of dialogic teaching in PD programs To address this issue, Borko et al. (2014)  highlighted that offering high-quality professional development (PD) programs is a promising strategy for enhancing teachers’ understanding of dialogic teaching. However, there has been limited focus on knowledge-based training specifically related to dialogic tea ching. Current PD initiatives have predominantly adopted a practice-based approach to assess teachers ’ implementation of dialogic teaching pedagogy in their instructional practices (e.g., Ch en et al., 2020), without gauging the teachers ’ comprehension of the underlying concepts and pedagogical implicat ions. For instance, Chen et al. (2020)  conducted a series of practice-oriented workshops to support experienced in-service mathematics teachers in China in incorporat ing dialogic teaching strategies into their daily instr uction. The results indicated a significant increas e in the use of dialogic teaching strategies among teachers in the interv ention group compared to those in the control group s. Despite being encouraged to reflect on dialogic teaching, it remains unclear to what extent teachers have acquired pedagogical knowledge of dialogic teaching and grasped the relevant concepts. Acquiring knowledge is a crucial objective of PD programs to enhance effective teaching practices (Ethel & McMeniman, 2000; Stürmer et al., 2013). Similarly, gaining a deep understanding of dialogic teaching  can be viewed as a prerequisite for delivering high-quality instruction. Hauk et al. (2023)  argued that existing research on PD programs focusing on dialogic teaching has he avily relied on teachers ’ subjective reflections and self-reports, rather thandirectly evaluating their know ledge. While teachers may claim to comprehend dialo gic teaching, their inability to provide specific examp les of related concepts may indicate a limited unde rstanding of the topic. In a recent study, Zhang et al. (2024) ini tiated efforts to address the challenges associated  with quantifying differences in understanding dialogic t eaching among novice secondary mathematics teachers  in China. However, their pre-post design study focused on a single workshop with in a PD program, potentially restricting its ability to comprehensively c apture the nuanced and continuous process of knowle dge acquisition. They also acknowledged the importance of administering knowledge assessments multiple times in PD programs to monitor the changes in teacher learning (Zhang et al., 2024). The Academically Productive Talk framework A commonly observed pattern in authoritative teachi ng is the Initiation-Reply-Evaluation (IRE) sequence (Mehan, 1979). Studies have indicated that such tea cher-centered patterns can be restrictive (e.g., Chen & Chan, 2022; Howe et al., 2019). For instance, Tainio and Laine (2015) identified that IRE sequences in Finnish primary mathematics classrooms overly emphasized the correctness of responses, resulting in limited student engagement and reduced opportunities for students to share their o wn thoughts and shallow understanding. To overcome the constraints of authoritative teaching, Michaels et al. (2008) and Resnick et al. (2010) developed the Academically Productive Talk (APT) framework, among other relevant frameworks. This framework outlines four key goals— elaborating, reasoning, listening, and thinking wit h others— and includes eight talk moves: say more, revoice, press for reasoning, challenge, restate, agree/disa gree, add on, and explain others. A detailed description and examples of the goals and talk moves can be found in Table 1. Empirical studies, such as those conducted by Chen and Chan (2022) and vander Veen et al. (2017), have shown the effectiveness of APT-based interventions in fostering the use of productive talk moves in everyday teaching practices. However, these studies concentrated on practice-oriented training sessions, prompting the question of the extent to which teachers have truly acquired expertise in dialogic teaching. The current study This study aims to fill the gaps in tracking the knowledge acquisition process of APT-based dialogic teaching among MPSTs throughout a semester-long PD program. The adapted PD design is shown in Figure 1. The changes in the knowledge of dialogic teaching were examined  in the way of three coding tests.  These tests required the teachers to watch three video segments of mathematics instruction accompanied by transcriptions and to identify specific APT talk moves. Throughout the school-based teaching practicum, MPSTs had around three opportunities to teach. Each coding test was administered iteratively to them in alignment with their completion of each in-class teaching practice. This study concentrated on the three knowledge tests of dialogic teaching, i.e., coding tests 13, highlighted in blue in Figure 1. It represents one of the initial efforts to contribute  to the existing literature by investigating changes in teacher knowledge acquisition of dialogic teaching within PD initiatives, moving beyond subjective self-assessments and reflections. The primary research question addressed in this study is as follows: RQ: Do the three coding tests facilitate the knowledge acquisition of dialogic te aching among MPSTs in four different groups throughout the PD program? If so, in what ways?

## Methodology

The PD program This study is based on  a large-scale PD program grounded on a Dynamic-Visualization-scaffolded-Dialogic Teaching (DVsDT) approach. This PD program aims to track MPSTs’ knowledge acquisition of dialogic teaching and enhance  dialogic teaching skills in technology-rich environments. The participants were undergraduate students at a normal university in China. All parti cipants were in their final year of the four-year initial teacher education program when they were required to complete a semester-long teaching practicum (see Figure 1). Before school-based teaching practicum,  p articipants were randomly divided into four groups by participating in various combinations of one-week workshops: the first group received training i n APT-based dialogic teaching (introducing eight APT talk moves and their pedagogical implications with hands-on exercises) along with dynamic visualization (using dynamic geo metric technologies like GeoGebra to support dialogic teaching); the second group received training solel y in dialogic teaching; the third group received tr aining solely in dynamic visualization; and the fourth group serv ed as the control group without any specific traini ng. This study primarily examined the knowledge related to t he dialogic teaching aspect (groups 1 and 2 versus groups 3 and 4), with the effects of dynamic visualization falling outside the study’s scope. During the teaching practicum, the coordinator in e ach group distributed the three coding tests to the participating MPSTs upon submission of their teaching practice video recordings, i.e., teaching practices 1-3. The first coding test took place to coincide with the completion of most MPSTs ’ first in-class teaching practice. Subsequently, the second coding test was administered following the conclusion of their second teaching practice. The third coding test occurred to align with the conclusion of the school-based teaching practicum. Despite some MPSTs not having opportunities to complete their second and/or third in-class teaching practices, all three coding tests were distributed to them as per the establish ed schedule. Participants from groups 1 and 2 received targeted feedback on their dialogic teaching practices and were encouraged to review the concepts and incorporate diverse APT talk moves in the subsequent teaching practices, while participants from groups 3 and 4 only received general feedback, such as the lesson design and the classro om management, on their teaching practices. Though participants from groups 3 and 4  did not receive prior training and feedback on dialogic teaching, they were provided with introductory materials such as the AP T framework and examples of talk moves  when completing the three coding tests.

## Participants

Thirty-nine (n = 39) MPSTs voluntarily took part in the current st udy and completed all three coding tests. The distribution of participants across the four groups  is as follows: the first group consists of 7 MPSTs, the second group comprises 10 MPSTs, the third group includes 8 MPSTs, and the fourth group comprises 14 MPSTs. Among the participants, there are 14 males (35.9%) and 25 females (64.1%), with no statistically signi ficant difference observed between the genders  (χ2 = 39, p =.43). Informed consent was obtained from participants involved in the study.

Test design Each coding test incorporated a video segment for MPSTs to identify the APT talk moves. The first video segment was sourced from an exemplary online teaching demon stration featuring rich mathematics discourse. The subsequent two videos consisted of animations creat ed by the research team to simulate classroom inter actions between the teacher and students. Prior to the PD p rogram, the research te am had already coded all three video clips according to the APT framework.

Coding test 1  comprised 24 items. Coding test 2 consisted of 40 items, and coding test 3 included 25 items. All items across the three tests were struct ured as multiple-choice questions with a maximum of three correct responses. Scoring was carried out in a bin ary manner, where one point was awarded for accurately identifying all APT talk moves within a teacher ’s utterance. At the same time,  incorrect or partially correct responses received zero points. The correct rates were calculated for each participant. Test quality Five research team members, possessing expertise in the APT framework and dialogic teaching research, meticulously selected the first video clip for codi ng test 1 and collectively devised transcriptions f or the subsequent simulated animations for coding tests 2 and 3. These team members evaluated the transcriptions based on the wording, adherence to the talk moves, and goals outlined in the APT framework. They have undergone several months of training in the coding procedure ahead of the PD program. Thus, the content validity and the coding accuracy were ensured to the largest extent.

Additionally, the psychometric properties of the th ree coding tests  were assessed. Item difficulty and discrimination were evaluated to ensure the reliabi lity and construct validity of the tests. Item diff iculty is a measure that indicates how easy or difficult an ite m is for test-takers. Item difficulty is often calculated as the proportion or percentage of test-takers who answered the item correctly (Brennan, 2023). Item difficulty indices ranged from.077 to.718, with an average difficult y of.307 for coding test 1; from.128 to.872, with an avera ge difficulty of.369 for coding test 2; from.077 to.795, with an average difficulty of.441 for coding  test 3. These values suggest a balanced distribution of easy, moderate, and difficult items on the three tests. Item discrimination is a measure that indicates how well an item differ entiates between high and low performers on a test (Brennan, 2023). It ranges from-1 to 1, and a positive item discrimination value indicates that high performers are more likely to answer the item correctly than low perfor mers. The average discrimination index was.366 for  coding test 1,.282 for coding test 2,  and.439 for coding test 3, suggesting that most items effectively diff erentiated between MPSTs with varying levels of knowledge on dialogic teaching.

## Data collection

The data for this study include  the responses to the three coding tests and the cor rect rates. MPSTs in the four groups were instructed to utilize their mobile devices, such as laptops or mobile phones, to access the Classroom Discourse Analyzer (CDA) to view the three video segments (Chen et al., 2020). According to Chen et al. (2020), CDA can visualize the dialogic teaching practices of the three testing video segments in multiple manners; for example, the CDA is equipped with various sources, including original videos, transcripts, and the vis ualization of utterances in a bubble chart, to capture the intricate nature of classroom discourse data effectively. Participants can click the bubble chart to locate a specific discourse talk excerpt. The size of the bubble indicates the relative proportion of words spoken by the teacher in that  turn; in other words, the larger the bubble, the mo re words the teacher spoke in that turn. Once an excerpt of interest is located, participants can access the corresponding video and transcripts to gain insights into exemplary or simulated teacher  teaching, facilitating reflection and improvement. Subsequently, they were required to complete the tests simultaneously using the online platform known as Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/). An example of the testing procedure is presented  in Figure 2. ## Findings

## Discussion

Though dialogic teaching pedagogy has long been ack nowledged in daily instruction practices as an effective approach to promoting productive classroom talk (Alexander, 2018) and supporting students’ cognitive growth in communicative environments (Sfard, 2007), little attention has been paid to the knowledge acquisition perspective. As argued by Hauk et al. (2023), it remains unclear whether the teachers mastered sufficient pedagogical knowledge to appropriately implement talk moves instead of relying on subjective self-reported reflections, such as in the PD program conducted by Chen et al. (2020). High-quality PD programs that prioritize the transmission and evaluation of knowledge as a prerequisite for teachers could offer a promising solution to address these gaps (Ethel & McMeniman, 2000; Stürmer et al., 2013). Building on this, th e current  study aimed to track the knowledge acquisition process of dialogic teaching among Chinese MPSTs in a semester-long PD program. The results indicated a general upward trend in the  acquisition of dialogic teaching knowledge among MPSTs from coding tests 1 to 3, suggesting an enhanced comprehension of dialogic teaching. This contrasts with the findings of Hauk et al. (2023), which reported no discernible difference in discourse-related content knowledge before and after a one-year PD intervention. One potential explanation for this disparity is that Hauk et al. (2023) utilized self-developed knowledge tests in a pre-and-post-test format using pencil-and-paper questionnaires, primarily focusing on the recall of fundamental con cepts of dialogic teaching. In contrast, the current study expanded on their approach by developing three vide o-based assessments that required participants to ide ntify specific talk moves within a context more specific to mathem atics, thereby offering additional evidence and feasibility in assessing teacher knowledge.

Participants from groups 1 and 2, who underwent tar geted training in dialogic teaching before their school-based teaching practicum and were instructed to inc orporate APT talk moves during their three teaching practices, demonstrated significant improvement in the final  coding test compared to the initial one. In contrast, participants in groups 3 and 4, who received no specific training on dialogic teaching, did not show notable differences in knowledge acquisition. These finding s provide insights into the cultural aspects of ini tial teacher education in East Asian countries  influenced by collectivism (Leung, 2001). Rather than the traditional practice of students remaining silent or responding collectively in a typical Chinese mathematics classroom (Xu & Clarke, 2013, 2019), acquiring knowledge of dialogic teaching can offer teachers an alternative approach before utilizing them in teaching practices, to shift from a teacher-centric to a more dialogic and student-centered environment. Dialogic teaching is new to MPSTs (Hauk et al., 2023), and this study aligns with previous literature emphasizing knowledge acquisition as a critical outcome in evaluating the effectiveness of PD programs (Ethel & McMeniman, 2000; Stürmer et al., 2013). The statistical evidence on the progress of participants in groups 1 and 2 supports the idea that integrating three knowledge tests in an iterative manner can enhance the quality of PD and guide effective instruction (Borko et al., 2014). Furthermore, this research extends the work of Zhang et al. (2024) by enhancing test design and addressing the gap between theory a nd practice in PD programs. Initially, the three co ding tests were aligned with the chronological sequence of school-based teaching practices among MPSTs in the study. The iterative testing and practice cycles were structur ed to encourage the practical application of knowle dge in real teaching settings and explore the relationship between teachers’ grasp of dialogic teaching and their instructional strategies, including the integration and implementation of this knowledge in classrooms. Nevertheless, though the current study primarily focused on the comparison of the effects of knowledge training on dialogic teaching, i.e., groups 1 and 2 vs. groups 3 and 4, it should be noted that participating MPSTs from groups 1 and 3 received additional training and guidance on dynamic visualization throughout the PD program. Though it is beyond the scope of this study to explore how knowledge and practices of dialogic teaching are mediated in technology-mediated environments, as discussed in a recent work of Hennessy (2024), it is worth further investigation into the nuanced differences among the four groups. Moreover, Zhang et al. (2024) highlighted the challenges in adequately covering all APT talk moves and associated goals when using online video resources for testing, such as the video segment for coding t est 1. The unequal distribution of test items among  different talk moves posed a potential threat to th e test’s validity and reliability. To address this issue, the research te am collaboratively developed simulated animations f or coding tests 2 and 3, aiming to achieve a more balanced re presentation of all aspects to a significant extent. Unlike traditional test de sign methods that prioritize logical and coherent i tem organization, the approach taken in this study involved simulating a dynamic classroom discourse to showcase a range of teacher talk moves. Overall, by maintaining suitable item difficulty and discrimination levels, the study ensured the validity and reliability of the three coding tests to a considerable degree.

## Implications and limitations

This study holds significant implications. From a t heoretical perspective, it is among the initial end eavors to enhance existing literature by examining the process of teacher knowledge acquisition in dialogic teaching within PD initiatives, going beyond mere subjective self-reflection. In practical terms, the study offers viable methods to guide teacher educators in integrating multiple knowledge tests of dialogic teaching into initial teacher education programs to support their professional growth.

However, there are limitations to this study that need attention. Firstly, the focus of the study was on the overall correct rates of the coding tests. Further investigation is  required to delve into the subtle differences in knowledge related to each talk move and the comprehension of the four goals in the APT framework. Secondly, while the three teaching practices aligned with the  coding test process, more emphasis should be place d on how this knowledge is transferred in teaching practices to enhance pre-service teachers’ dialogic teaching abilities in orchestrating classroom discussions. Thirdly, the study involved a limited sample of pre-service teachers. Future research could scale up the testing scenarios to better understand the general learning trends in knowledge acquisition of dialogic teaching in teacher education contexts.

## Conclusion

This research aimed to address the gaps by tracking the knowledge acquisition process of dialogic teaching among Chinese MPSTs over a semester-long PD program. Through the implementation of three coding tests with a cohort of 39 participants, the study revealed a positive tr end in the understanding of dialogic teaching from the first to the third coding test. Statistical analysis demonstrated that, in contrast to their untrained counterparts, participants who received targeted training and guidance on dial ogic teaching exhibited notable advancements in the  final coding test compared to the initial one. The study contributes theoretically by expanding the existing literature on teacher knowledge acquisition in dialogic teaching within PD initiatives. From a practical standpoint, it highlights the necessity of integrating knowledge assessments to shape the development of initial teacher educati on programs. Future research could explore the nuanced and perso nalized learning trajectories of dialogic teaching for individual MPSTs, as well as investigate how th e understanding of dialogic teaching is influenced in technology-rich teaching environments.
