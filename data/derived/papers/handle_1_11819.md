# Assessing AI-Generated Feedback Quality: Instructor Perspectives on Fine-tuned GenAI for Higher Education Student Writing

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This convergent mixed-methods study investigates the integration of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) for providing formative feedback in higher education. The study explores the perspectives of eleven experienced instructors regarding GenAI-generated feedback in an online graduate program. Participants reviewed sample feedback and shared their evaluations via a numerical questionnaire, an open-ended survey, and a focus group discussion. Results indicated that instructors generally viewed GenAI feedback positively, noting its relevance, clarity, actionability, usefulness, and comprehensiveness, highlighting its effective alignment with course rubrics and comprehensive guidance. However, challenges were also noted, including redundancy and overly lengthy suggestions that could overwhelm students. The study concludes with recommendations for refining GenAI to enhance its feedback effectiveness and improve the learning experience of higher education students.

## Introduction

The advantages of formative feedback on university students' writing have been extensively documented (see reviews by Morris et al., 2021, and Pearson, 2022). Research indicates that tailored feedback from instructors can aid students in recognizing their own strengths and weaknesses, enhancing their writi ng skills, and promoting independent learning. With the increasing prevalenc e of Generative AI (GenAI) models, new methods of providing formative feedback are emerging, potentia lly changing the landscape o f writing instruction in higher education (Cope & Kalantzis, 2024; Tzirides et al., 2024; Zapata et al., 2024). However, it's crucial to thoroughly assess the advantages and drawbacks of GenAI feedba ck tools to evaluate their effectiveness compared t o traditional human feedback from a pedagogical perspective.

In this paper, we seek to address this goal by investigating experienced university instructors’ evaluation of fine-tuned, AI-generated feedback, with a focus on its pedagogical  effectiveness. First, we discuss the key characteristics of human and AI feedback based on existing literature. We then present our research questions and methodology, which includes a sample of feedback on  a university student’s work generated by a fine-tuned GenAI model, highlighting possible similarities with human feedback. This is followed by the results, discussion, and pedagogical recommendations.

Characteristics of human and AI feedback on written work Existing analyses of university instructors’ format ive feedback on written work (e.g., Hyland & Hyland, 2001; Pearson, 2022) have revealed that such feedback typ ically includes praise, criticism, and suggestions aimed at motivating students, enhancing thei r self-efficacy, and providing actionable guidance for imp rovement. These comments are grounded in specific pedagogical goals  and outcomes (Connors & Lunsford, 1993; Straub, 19 97). Furthermore, instructors often mitigate criticism t hrough strategies such a s hedging, using question forms, and employing paired-act patterns (where criticism is softened by praise, often within the same sentence) (Hyland & Hyland, 2001). These techniques have been shown to prevent negative emotional responses that could hin der students’ ability to revise their work effectively (Pearson, 2022). Additionally, studies have revealed recurring patte rns in the organization of instructor feedback. For instance, Mirador’s (2000) analysis of feedback on postgraduate students’ writing in the UK identified a common rhetorical structure. Feedback often begins with general impressions and a recapitulation of ideas, followed by an articulation of strengths and weaknesses, suggestions for improvement, and an overall judgment of the work. This structure, referred to as the clinching pattern, ensures that feedback is comprehensive and goal-oriented, fostering both reflection and action (Holmeier et al., 2018).

In recent years, AI-generated feedback has emerged as a complement to h uman feedback. However, studies (e.g., Grimes & Warschauer, 2010; Shermis & Wilson, 2024; Wilson & MacArthur, 2024) suggest that AI feedback often lacks the precision, specificity, and contextual relevance of human feedback. AI-generated comments tend to be broad, wordy, and sometimes unc lear, which can make them less effective for recipi ents (Grimes & Warschauer, 2010; Lee, 2024). One  significant limitation is that AI tools often lack the contextual understanding required to address specific assignme nts, leading to irrelevant or overly generalized co mments (Lee, 2024).

To address these challenges, integrating rubrics to  guide AI-generated feedback has been proposed as a strategy to enhance quality (Lee, 2024). This approach mirrors effective practices in human feedback, where explicit references to rubrics or assessment criteria have been shown to significantly improve r elevance and effectiveness (Black & William, 2009; Graham, 2018;  Hillocks, 1986; MacArthur, 2016; Roscoe et al., 20 13). Additionally, recent research (e.g., Steiss et al., 2024) suggests that,  while AI feedback can sometimes approximate human feedback in quality, it remains prone to errors, particularly when lacking specific fine-tuning or training.

These findings highlight the characteristics of eff ective feedback—whether human or AI —which must go beyond mere evaluation. Feedback should be perso nalized, actionable, and aligned with learning obje ctives. Its effectiveness depends not only on the quality o f the content and its organizational structure, but also on strategies that mitigate criticism and create a supportive environment for student reflection and growth. While AI feedback can exhibit some similarities to human fee dback, it continues to fall  short in terms of precision, contextual relevance, and reliability, often producing generalized and verbose comments. However, assessing the quality of feedback alone of fers only a partial view. Equally critical is understanding how students interact with that feedb ack. While this study centers on the quality and composi tion of AI-generated feedback, it also acknowledges that effec tive formative assessment hinges on how students engage with and use feedback. Recent literature (e. g., Carless & Boud, 2018) has highlighted students’ active roles in interpreting, acting on, and dialoguing with the suggestions for improvement they receive. Understanding how feedback functions within these processes is essential to evaluating its overall impact on learning. Building on this perspective, t his study contributes to the literature  on AI feedback  by exploring an underexamined area: How experienced educators perceive the quality of Ge nAI-generated feedback when assessed against a rubric designed for evaluating student work. By focusing on educators’ assessments of both the alignment of GenAI feedback with the rubric and its  effectiveness in providing actionable suggestions for improvement, this work addresses gaps in understanding the practical implications of using AI tools in educational settings. Positioned as a necessary precursor to broader inve stigations into how students engage with such feedback, this work seeks to inform how GenAI-generated feedback can be refined to more effective ly support pedagogical goals.

## Research questions

In this study, we investigate d the effectiveness of fine-tuned, AI-generated formative feedback in a n online graduate education context in the United States, focusing on two key questions:

1. How do experienced university instructors evaluate the extent to which GenAI feedback aligns with the

pedagogical objectives defined in the rubric for assessing student work?

2. What are the perceptions of experienced university instructors regarding the overall effectiveness of

GenAI feedback in facilitating meaningful revisions of student work?

## Methods

This study examined experienced instructors’ views on the quality of AI-generated feedback produced by a GenAI tool tailored for formative assessment of online graduate student work (MA, PhD, and Certificate) at a Midwestern US University.

## Participants

Eleven university instructors, with experience in h igher education ranging from 3 to over 20 years, ev aluated GenAI feedback. The participants had a thorough und erstanding of the theory behind the rubric used by the AI, as well as GenAI’s educational affor dances and limitations. The group included  tenured faculty, non-tenured lecturers, and advanced doctoral students recruited from gr aduate-level programs in education, teacher training, and interdisciplinary studies at a Midwestern U.S. university. Their ar eas of expertise spanned TESOL, educational technology, applied linguistics, curric ulum studies, and STEM education.  The sample consisted of six women and five men, representing a range of ethnic and national backgrounds, including Latinx, Black, White, and international scholars from Brazil, Greece, and India.  Participants were selected through purposive sampling based on their pedagogical experience, familiarity with AI tools in education  (specifically the GenAI formativefeedback tool used in this study), and their prior engagement with the online graduate program curriculum from which the student works were drawn.

Materials The GenAI reviews  (see example below in Figure 1)  were produced from OpenAI’s GPT4 via an application programming interface. To generate feedback on student writing, the GenAI tool was fed criteria from the rubric as prompts, by and large mimicking the way instructors and students review their own and their peers’ work using rubrics. The rubric items and GenAI prompts were drawn from the Learning by Design framework (Kalantzis & Cope, 2010; Tzirides et al., 2023). The prompts were intentionally detailed to ensure transparency, using identical prompts for both instructors and peers, even if this meant the instructions were somewhat lengthy. These prompts emphasized broad knowledge processes rather than focusing on specific facts, to reduce issues like hallucinations or unreliable sourcing, which are typical challenges in large language models (LLMs). Furthermore, these rubricbased prompts were complemented by additional syste m instructions to summarize student text, outline s ystem guidelines, and clearly assess the text based on the given criteria. Figure 1 Presence of Characteristics of Human Feedback in GenAI Review of Student Work Supplementing the prompt was a separately specified knowledge repository in the fo rm of a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) database, containing the collective works of the program’s fa culty members and graduate students since 2019 (35 million words).  RAG is a  natural language processing (NLP) technique that enhances the accuracy and relevance of feedback by adding an additional layer of domain-specific information on top of the existing LLM. It works by prioritizing the domain-specific corpus over the foundational general knowledge base of the LLM. This overlay helps ensur e that responses are tailored to the program’s spec ific academic context, effectively making the feedback m ore precise and aligned with the educational object ives. Thus, the RAG database acts to push the AI towards generating feedback that is more domain relevant. Understanding the quality of AI-generated feedback requires recognizing the layered  architecture that shapes its output, including  the following:  (1) the foundational language model and its trainin g data; (2) the specific system used (e.g., ChatGPT, DeepSeek); (3)  the prompt design and system instructions guiding the model’s response; and (4) the user’s interactions d uring inference. Our approach—using GPT-4 via OpenAI’s API, enhanced with structured prompts and RAG integ ration—was intentionally designed to  align with the program’s pedagogical goals. Still, limitations in clarity, actionability, or sp ecificity may arise from any of these layers, a point we return to in the Discussion section of this work. Generative AI feedback sample In this section, we describe the example of the fine-tuned GenAI review of a student’s work illustrated in Figure 1 above  to showcase how this plays out.  As mentioned previously, the tool that generated this feedback was calibrated via RAG to the specific topics of the educational program o n which this study focused. Thus, the tool was more advanced than typical GenAI chatbots.

The content and organization of the AI-generated review are similar to human clinching-pattern feedback discussed above (Holmeier et al., 2018). For instance, the AI starts by offering an overview of the student’s work and summarizes its main ideas. This is followed by the identification of strengths and weaknesses, the  provision of actionable suggestions, and the articulation of an overall rating. The review also includes instances (highlighted in bold and italics) of hedging, paired-act patterns, and imperative forms. All these features are common in human feedback and help establish a connection between instructor and student.

## Data collection

The participants first assessed the GenAI feedback on three randomly selected graduate-student papers. Second, they submitted their opinions via the following ways: (a) A constrained-response survey with 7 numeric-rating and 6 best-adjective items, completed once per GenAI review fo r each student paper; (b) an open-ended survey with 19 questions, completed once per participant a fter reviewing the GenAI reviews  (see Appendix); and (c) a remote, unstructured focus-group discussion reflecting on instructors’ overall impressions of the quality of the AI-generated feedback. The use of fixed-response items to assess multiple reviews may have introduced generalization challenges, as participants evaluate d feedback across varying student works. To address  this limitation, responses were triangulated with open-ended reflections and focus group data to elicit mo re nuanced judgments.

In the numeric-rating questions, participants ranked the GenAI rev iew attributes of relevance, clarity, actionability, usefulness, comprehensiveness, objectivity, and overall satisfaction  in response to the following prompt: “On a scale of 1-5, where 1 is ‘Not at all’ and 5 is ‘Extremely’, ho w do you rate the feedback provided by the AI tool for each of these attributes?” In th e best-adjective items, participants were asked to select from a short list of adjectives the one that best described the same attributes as above, minus overall satisfactio n (see Figure 2 below). These adjectives reflected those commonly associated with human feedback in previous literature and in our own work comparing GenAI and peer feedba ck from students’ perspective (e.g., Tzirides et al., 2023; Zapata et al., 2025). Finally, the open-ended survey (see Appendix) allowed instructors to elaborate on the same attributes. Due to potential ambiguity in interpretation, the item objectivity was removed from all our analyses. Following the open-ended survey, a remote, unstructured focus group  discussion was conducted via Zoom. During the focus group, participants were asked questions aimed at understanding their overall impressions of the AI feedback quality  beyond the structured categories of the survey, addressing aspects such as its consistency, clarity, depth, and appropriateness to  the rubric. They also discussed the AI’s ability t o provide nuanced comments, offer explicit examples, and conn ect theory to practice. A single focus group was conducted with all eleven participants. While relatively large in size, the group was intentionally assembled to bring together diverse disciplinary and pedagogical perspectives. The session was designed as an exploratory conversa tion, prioritizing flexibility and participant-driven dialogue. This flexible format of the focus group enabled participants to articulate their perspectives more freely, provi ding richer insights into their experiences, especi ally where the open-ended textual survey responses were constrained by participants’ busy schedules. This contributed valuable context to improve understanding of their own reflections on the strengths and limitations of the  AI-generated feedback.

## Data analysis

The numeric-rating and best-adjective items were analyzed using exact binomial  and multinomial  tests, respectively, in the statistical programming language R (R Core Team, 2024). The open-ended survey responses and the focus group discussion transcripts were analyzed thematically (Braun & Clarke, 2006) to complement and contextualize the numerical data. Specifically, we identified recurring themes that aligned with or ex panded on trends in the numeric ratings. This mixed-methods analysis provided a deeper understanding of the participants’ views.

The open-ended survey responses and the focus group discussion supported these findings. Participants expressed that the AI reviews were highly useful, particularly because they closely aligned with the course rubric by providing evaluations for each criterion, assess ing both strengths and weaknesses. As one participa nt noted: “The specificity of the feedback regarding improvem ents – highlighting what's working and what's not for each rubric criterion – was particularly beneficial.”  They also described the AI feedback as effective in offering actionable suggestions, clearly articulating what was successful and what r equired improvement, while contextualizing comments by referring to specific p arts of students’ work that could be enhanced. Another participant supported this by stating that “the feedback was very effective in providing specific suggestions.” More significantly, six instructors emphasized that the AI offered detailed and comprehensive insights that would have required significant effort from human reviewers. One participant explicitly mentioned that the feedback was “impressive” and even “surpasses what a human instructor or peer reviewer could typically offer.” The qualitative data also highlighted some limitations in the AI-generated feedback. Two participants noted that, at times, the AI’s comments lacked depth in addressing personal experi ences and motivations, which were often crucial for developing the topic. As one participant pointed out, “It could be even more useful if tailored to individual needs, particularly in addressing mor e nuanced student requirements.”  While some instructors (eight out of eleven) valued the AI’s specificity, others (two out of eleven) felt that the suggestions were not always supported by concrete, implementable example s, making the feedback feel too generic. One participant specifically mentioned that, for a rubric criterion, “issues appear[ed] to be very generic.” Moreover, the feedback was sometimes noted for being extensive and redundant, potentially overwhelming students. The combination of lengthy, repetitive suggestions and the occasional lack of c oncrete examples led some instructors to believe th at students might struggle to identify and prioritize actionable steps effectively.  This sentiment was echoed by a participant who said, “The feedback is so comprehensive and exhaustive that nothing relevant seems to have been missed. However, there is some repetition, and the amount of feedback may be overwhelming. It may not be possible to take on board all the suggestions, as relevant and perceptive as they may be.”

## Discussion

The findings from both the quantitative and qualita tive data in this study revealed that experienced i nstructors generally viewed the GenAI feedback positively. For  example, when considering the alignment of GenAI feedback with the pedagogical goals reflected in the rubric (Rese arch Question 1), the instructors seemed to believe that the AI’s comments aligned well with the rubric’s intent, emphasizing its clear and structured guidance. Most participants felt that the AI’s comment s, suggestions for improvement, and ratings of stud ents’ works reflected the rubric criteria and provided an accurate assessment of their quality. This result is consistent with the characteristics of effective human feedback highlig hted by Hyland and Hyland (2001) and Pearson (2022), who argue that formative feedback should provide clear,  structured comments, offering praise, constructive  criticism, and actionable guidance reflective of instructional goals and expected outcomes to aid student improvement. However, the results indicated more mixed perceptions regarding participants’ views on the effectiveness of GenAI feedback in supporting meaningful student revisions (Research Question 2). Although most instructors found that GenAI often provided helpful general adv ice, some felt it lacked specificity when translati ng suggestions into concrete actions, making the feedback less actionable for students needing step-by-step guidance. Additionally, instructors noted that  GenAI’s recommendations were sometimes too exhausti ve or redundant, potentially overwhelming students who might struggl e to identify specific actions within the AI’s leng thy text. These insights are consistent with the characteristics of the AI-generated feedback reported in the literature (e.g., Grimes & Warschauer, 2010; Shermis & Wilson, 2024; Wilson & MacArthur, 2024)  and are important when considering the work of Connors and Lunsford (1993), Straub (1997), and Holmeier et al. (2018), who emphasize that effective feedback should include actionable suggestions to foster meaningful revisions. Even though the sample of the fine-tuned GenAI review presented in Figure 1 clearly ex hibited characteristics and a structure similar to those fo und in human feedback, the results from this study suggest that, in experienced instructors’ views, AI feedback  remains different from that offered by humans. While the tool in this study was carefully fine-tuned and integrated with a domain-specific RAG layer, these findings prompt broader reflections about the evolving role of AI in educational contexts. As GenAI models continue to develop—often rapidly, as seen in the release of GPT-4o and comparable systems—some limitations (such as verbosity or vague suggestions) may be partially resolved by future iterations. However, the core pedagogical questions remain: Wha t happens to student learning when formative feedba ck becomes fully automated? Can one-way, non-relational feedback replace the dialogic nature of human teaching? If students receive feedback only from AI, how migh t this affect their motivation, trust, and sense of  agency as learners? These issues highlight the importance of not only improving the technical precision of AI-generated feedback, but also attending to its ethical, relational, and societal implications. With these broader considerations in mind, we now offer pedagogical recommendations a imed at refining GenAI tools to better serve educat ional goals.

Pedagogical recommendations Even though the fine-tuned GenAI tool employed in this study appeared to  have been quite effective, some limitations were still present in its feedback, as identified by the experienced instructors in this w ork. Based on our results, we offer the following pedagogical recommendations, with specific emphasis on refining prompts and fine-tuning AI models to address these limitations:

• Enhance Actionability of Feedback by Adapting Prompts for Context Sensitivity: Refine GenAI feedback

prompts to generate actionable, detailed suggestions specific to each assignment, balancing rubric criteria with the specific needs of the student work, rather than focusing solely on the rubric

• Personalize Feedback by Scaffolding Student Responses: Develop prompts that lead the AI to generate

reflective questions, helping students to think mor e deeply about their work. Instructors can also ref ine AI feedback for better educational value and relevance.

• Address Overwhelming Content  (a) by Summarizing and Prioritizing Recommendations: Fine-tune the

AI to provide concise feedback by using prompts that ask for summarization and prioritization of the key action items, helping students focus on the most cr itical improvements without feeling overwhelmed. This is especially important in online settings, where students often review feedback i ndependently and benefit from clear guidance on key priorities. Inst ructors can also use filtering tools to extract the  most relevant recommendations.

• Address Overwhelming Content  (b) by Incorporating Feedback Filtering Tools:  Develop prompts for

categorizing feedback by importance or by rubric criteria. This approach will help filter out  excessive detail and provide students with a clear focus on priority actions.

• Emphasize Emotional Considerations by Facilitating Positive Reinforcement:  Fine-tune the AI to

include hedging, paired-act patterns, imperative forms, and motivational ph rases to mitigate students’ negative responses and encourage them to stay engag ed with the revision process, especially in online environments where students might feel isolated.

• Scaffold GenAI Feedback Understanding by Developing Feedback Literacy: Refine prompts to include

examples and explanatory comments to enhance students’ understanding of feedback. This approach will enhance their feedback literacy and foster independent learning.

## Study limitations

This work exhibits the limitations often found in teaching-and-learning scholarship, such as a small number of participants resulting in low power, a narrow educational context that may not generalize to other contexts, and a heightened focus on anonymity to help ensure honest  responses among participants, which in turn preven ted any kind of analysis that accounted for correlated responses (e.g., multilevel modeling). Future research could address these limitations by involving a larger num ber of participants  in diverse higher-education contexts to investigate whether the reported results might transfer to other educational settings. A longitudi nal design might be possible, but it would require a treatment, such as expert-teacher comparisons of significantly different versions of the GenAI tool (e.g., the tool before vs. after the intr oduction of RAG).  Furthermore, the study reflects only instructors’ perspectives on feedback quality, with out capturing how students themselves perceive or u se this feedback, a limitation we have addressed in previous work (see Zapata et al., 2024, 2025).

## Conclusion

This study illustrates that incorporating well-tuned GenAI feedback in higher education has great potential to improve the quality of formative assessments, parti cularly in online environments, offering feedback t hat is comparable to, and as effective as, that provided by human instructors. However, there are still some limitations to consider. We suggest that by focusing on prompt refinement and fine-tuning, educators can ensure that AI-generated feedback on student assignments is specif ic, actionable, and  emotionally supportive. Merging AI feedback with human insights can enable personalize d and thorough support for students, promoting thei r independent learning and encouraging significant re visions. Ultimately, the strategic use of GenAI, dr iven by carefully crafted prompts and adjustments, can effectively connect automated feedback with personalized insights, leading to a richer learning experience for university students.
