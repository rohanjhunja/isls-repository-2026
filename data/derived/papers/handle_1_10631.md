# Making Listening Visible: An Exploratory Case of Mathematics Teacher Listening

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Situative studies of teaching have long examined classroom discourse to characterize pedagogy and student learning as an interactional accomplishment between teachers, students, and the institutional settings in which they work. Despite this focus, less work has explored teachers’ listening practices and how these practices are enacted spatially across classroom lessons as a component of responsive pedagogies. In this paper, we present an exploratory case of one mathematics teacher’s listening practice, drawing from a multi-year research-practice partnership in a large urban school district in the United States studying teacher learning around collaborative groupwork. Our analysis and findings integrate qualitative and spatial visualization methods to offer an initial framework to characterize teachers’ listening across spatial, embodied, and mobile dimensions. We also offer methodological insights about how to employ different qualitative, visual, and spatial methods to characterize teacher listening.

## Introduction

Situative studies of teaching have long examined cl assroom discourse to characterize pedagogy and stud ent learning as an interactional accomplishment between  teachers, students, and the institutional settings  in which they work (Greeno, 1998; Horn & Garner, 2022; Lampe rt & Cobb, 2003; Lave, 1996). However, the role of listening has often been neglected, rendering liste ning in mathematics teaching and learning under conceptualized (Erickson, 2010; Hintz & Tyson, 2015). In this paper, we build on research that explores teachers’ listening to illustrate embodied and spat ial aspects of one focal teacher’s listening practi ce in a secondary mathematics lesson. Our analysis integrat es qualitative and spatial visualization methods to  answer two research questions: 1) How do teachers use thei r bodies and classroom space to enact listening? 2)  What are the affordances of different qualitative methods to  study teacher listening? Our work also contributes  to recent calls for a spatial turn in teacher education (e.g., Weiland & Poling, 2022)  by showing how the physical design of classrooms shapes teacher listening and how teachers make places for listening across classroom lessons.

## Theoretical perspectives: Listening for learning

We draw from an emerging body of work in the learni ng sciences and teacher education highlighting how listening is an integral but often neglected aspect  of teachers’ responsive practice. In particular, w e draw from Emma Reimers’ (2021) expansion of constructs listen ing to and listening for as conceptualized as situated processes of learning. She defined listening to as “an observational stance…a holistic approach to sound” (p. 40), and listening for “indicates a seeking stance… a magnifying glass ver sion of listening” (p. 41). Similarly, we draw from Farrell (2009), Davis (1997), and Hint z & Tyson (2015) who underscore the relevance of listening for teacher education. For example, Davis  delineates three kinds of listening as a framework  for (re)interpreting teachers’ practice, highlighting d ifferent instructional purposes of listening: evaluative listening, where teachers evaluate student thinking against so me preconceived standard of correctness; interpretive listening, or listening as a means of making sense of talk; and hermeneutic listening  where the teacher participates in the learning process with students.  Hintz and Tyson (2015) expand Davis’ framework to detail complex listening – when multiple and different kinds of listening occu r in mathematical discussion. Similar to Davis, complex listening requires mathematical cont ent that makes such interactions possible. These st udies point to the social and interactional complexity of  listening. However, notably absent from this work is how listening occurs spatially and through the use of b odies and movement across classrooms. Accordingly, we advance concepts and methods to study listening in ways that attend to the spatial, embodied, and mobi le dimensions of listening.

## Methods and data analysis

We draw from a larger research-practice partnership between university researchers, teachers, and a well-regarded professional development organization (PDO) in a large urban school district in the Western U nited States. Here, we focus on our work with one of 12 f ocal teachers we worked closely with this past year, whom we call Lizette McLoughlin. Lizette had been teachi ng for over ten years, and taught Algebra 1 and AP Calculus. Prior work (Ehrenfeld & Horn, 2020; Horn & Garner, 2022) along with Lizette’s own statements identified Lizette’s potentially significant use of  listening as a part of her responsive teaching pra ctice. Thus, we selected Lizette as an exploratory case (Yin, 2017) to better understand how teachers enact intentional  listening practices. Our analysis incorporates several data sources including interviews with Lizette across the year, multiperspective classroom video  of a complete classroom lesson, and screen recordings of Lizette using a dynamic visualization tool called the Interaction Geography  Slicer or IGS, which we introduce later in this pa per (Shapiro et al., 2017; 2024). Our analysis process followed a grounded theory approach (Charmaz, 2014). After creating transcripts of interviews and classroom recordings, we content logged each transcript to identify initial themes and categories for closer interaction analys is (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). We met weekly for a period of six months to iteratively refine emergent themes  and categories and focus on three primary themes a bout Lizette’s listening practices here.

## Findings

Spatial aspects of listening One category pertains to spatial aspects of listeni ng — aspects of listening involving the physical design of classroom space. During interviews across the year,  Lizette shared her pedagogical reasoning around ar ranging her classroom space to enable her listening practic es. We summarize her reasoning in Figure 1 by layer ing excerpts from her interviews over a floor plan of h er classroom. Particularly, Lizette arranged tables  into eight student groups with additional tables and shelves a round the perimeter that hold instructional supplie s (e.g. calculators), files, books, and a self care station  (e.g. tea, masks). She used a table (the same as w here students sit) at the front of the room for her desk, with he r computer and projector, and the whiteboard direct ly behind. She explained that she wanted to open up as much sp ace as possible, for students “to be as comfortable  as possible.” She also opened her space so that she co uld move and circulate throughout her room, primari ly in the center space: “if I'm not doing something specifica lly with the projector or the whiteboard, I'm just tracking along this center area and stepping in or listening to each group [...] I can kind of just wander loops more.” Figure 1 Floor Plan of Lizette’s Classrooms Annotated with Highlighted Spaces and Statements from her Interview Describing her Perspective on Different Parts of her Classroom. Embodied and mobile aspects of listening A second theme that emerged from our analysis is ho w Lizette used her body to listen at different scal es or listening zones, which we define as an  attentive space, extending work that has begun to c onsider how people attend to one another and their environment as they  move (see Marin et al., 2020). We observed three listeni ng zones: 1) observational listening or eavesdropping, 2) concentrated listening (deciding if and how to intervene), and 3) conversational listening (engaging in a conversation with a single group). Lizette enacted these listening zones through her b ody and movement. Notably, the Interaction Geography Slicer or IGS provided crucial ways for u s to study such mobile aspects of listening and pro vided ways for Lizette to reflect on such aspects of list ening during her debrief. Figure 2 illustrates the kinds of visualizations that supported our interpretive spat ial analysis (also see: https://youtu.be/wIyVYCuPxl0). Of particular importance is the significant amount of listening that occurs while Lizette is moving. Furthermore, the figure shows how her  listening shifted across the lesson, with the first half of the lesson consisting of m ore observational listening while later stages having m ore conversation with groups or the class — often linking ideas from what she heard emerging at student groups over the course of the lesson. Closer interaction analysis of her classroom video illustrated how Lizette would slowly walk around and scan her room. Her listening zone was open, wit h her attention distributed across many groups (dep icted by the blue annotated sections in the images of Figure 2B). She seemed to be working to get a general sense of the class’s and groups’ progress – attending to ideas that were bubbling across the cl assroom and “reading the room.” During this kind of listening, she would move quite  a bit, stopping only momentarily, and her gaze constantly swiveling about the classroom. During ea vesdropping, Lizette tried to listen “in terms of t he conversations, and observing what’s going down on p aper, and then making choices about when to step in.” Her goal was to listen (and respond) to “the needs of the room” – to “[hear] something collectively.” Lizette also used her body to contract her listening zone for different purposes. For exam ple, she narrowed her attention to a singular group by leani ng in (see Figure 2 C, where her attention moves from the blue to the orange highlighted region). While eaves dropping allowed Lizette’s attention to be distribu ted across multiple groups of students, giving her a sort of z oomed out understanding of students, leaning in sig nified a contracted listening zone, as if zooming in, and concentrated where she was now listening. Lizette used another physical move, crouching, to f urther contract her listening zone. For example, as Lizette left one group interaction, she quietly app roached the next group, at first engaging in observ ational listening (Figure 2D, blue highlighted region). Stopping next to their table, she stood above them, with her back towards the middle space, and a student looked up a t Lizette, bashfully. The student then initiated a conversation – as she suggested that maybe the group did not know something, despite it being on their paper. Lizette moved closer, contracting her listening zon e. Lizette then crouched down to engage in a convers ation with the group, suggesting that she was not convinced of students’ understanding. As Lizette physically moved closer to the group, she gradually contracted her l istening zone, further concentrating her attention on the group. Lizette directed the group’s conversation, promptin g them with questions that would elicit their under standing (e.g. “tell me in words what you are thinking”). Li zette’s interactional work here not only prompted s tudent conversation, but also created opportunities to hear (Hintz & Tyson, 2015). ## Discussion

We can see from Lizette’s intentional design of cla ssroom space that it is possible for classroom spac es to be designed for listening. That is, the design of classroom space i mpacts not only students’ and teachers’ interactions with one another, but also determines what is hearable. Furthermore, we highlighted the ways t hat space impacts possibilities for complex listening (Hintz & Tyson, 2015). Moreover, our notion of liste ning zones and use of the IGS demonstrated how embodied forms of listening such as observational, concentra ted, and conversational occur across different scales and evolve across a lesson as teachers move. Methodol ogically, we illustrated strengths and weaknesses of differen t methods to make aspects of listening visible, and  the value of triangulating different methods to qualitatively  characterize different dimensions of listening. We  suggest findings from this study further theoretical and me thodological discussions about teacher listening, l eading to future work that centers spatial and embodied dimen sions. Likewise, this work has important implicatio ns for those who design physical classroom spaces and enco urages alternative ways to support teachers’ reflec tive practice about listening. At the same time, this st udy has several important limitations. Additional o bservations of different lessons may reveal themes we did not u ncover. Likewise, other teachers working in differe nt settings may use space and movement quite different ly, further emphasizing the need to expand upon thi s work in other classroom settings in different contexts. Moreover, while we employed different methods to characterize listening, these methods have particul ar limitations. For example, the IGS uses represent ational techniques that are highly reductive, do not charac terize many aspects of everyday life and experience, and are not neutral with respect to dimensions of hierarchy and power.
