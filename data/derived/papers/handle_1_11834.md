# Quantitative Evaluation of Collaborative Discussions Based on the Abstractness of Words in Utterance

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Quantitative Evaluation of Collaborative Discussions Based on the

Abstractness of Words in Utterance Ryunosuke Nishimura, Hironori Egi r.nishimura@uec.ac.jp, hiro.egi@uec.ac.jp The University of Electro-Communications, Japan Abstract: The evaluation of collaborative discussions often r elies on qualitative approaches, limiting insights into the speed and degree of know ledge convergence. This study proposes a quantitative method for the evaluation of collaborative discussions by analyzing the abstractness of words in utterances. Six groups engaged in 10-min discussions and their utterances were numerically represented as abstractness sequences, analyzed using a moving average. Results revealed that concretization and abstraction during  knowledge convergence promoted the use of concrete and abstract words, leading to variatio ns in the moving average of abstractness. Most groups showed a rise in abstractness toward the end of the discussions, reflecting a gradual shift from concrete examples to abstract conclusion s. This method captures iterative shifts between concretization and abstraction, providing d eeper insights into communication dynamics in collaborative learning. This innovative method bridges qualitative and quantitative perspectives, enabling precise analysis of knowledg e convergence and offering practical applications in the area of CSCL.

## Introduction

Collaborative discussions enable individuals to integrate knowledge they do not originally possess with their prior understanding, thereby expanding their own knowledg e and creating new insights (Sa wyer, 2014; C hen et al., 2018). Collaborative discussions  have been widely adopted in the context of CSCL, such as peer interactions (Shin et al., 2020) and self-regulated learning utilizing holographic agents (Nishimura et al., 2023; Mochizuki et al., 2023). The framework of knowledge convergence is  often assumed in the analysis of such discussions (e.g., Weinberger et al., 2007). Knowledge convergence is the process of sharing and integrating each other's knowledge within social interactions; it involves swinging between concrete experiences and abstract concepts (Roschelle, 1992). Thus, analyzing collaborative discussions based on know ledge convergence increases the understanding of knowledge construction  such as “the ladder of abstraction,” elaborating concrete experiences into abstract theories and reexamining abstract theories using concrete examples (Hayakawa, 1978). In particular, there is a need for research that quantifies insights on learning sciences such as knowledge convergence and connects them to the computer systems (Jeong, 2014; Wise, 2017). However, knowledge convergence is often analyzed qualitatively, using coding or by examining state transitions between th e concrete experiences and the abstract concepts (Schwartz, 1995; Wilmont et al., 2012). Therefore, there is a lack of knowledge on the degree and speed of knowledge convergence, resulting in difficulties in comparing the extent of knowledge convergence among different groups or conversations.

In this study, we propose an approach for the  evaluation of  collaborative discussions based on the abstractness of words contained in utterances.  Our objective is the quantitative evaluation  of knowledge convergence by transforming collaborative discussio ns into numerical sequences that represent the abstraction levels of words in utterances. For this purpose, we conduct a comparative analysis between the quantitative results obtained by our proposed method and the available qualitative results of knowledge convergence  to verify the effectiveness of our method in representing the process of knowledge convergence process.

## Knowledge convergence in collaborative discussions

Knowledge convergence is the process of deepening perspectives by abstracting concrete opinions and examples (Roschelle 1992). Through this abstraction, learners in collaborative  discussions reach consensus by integrating individual and subjective knowledge into universally agreeable knowledge  (Jorczak, 2011). In this framework, knowledge convergence progresses over time: participants share and examine their prior knowledge, leading to a convergence which is different  from their initial understanding.  Thus, research on knowledge convergence has been divided into two perspectives: one on the convergence proce ss and the other on its outcomes (Fischer & Mandl, 2005).

In the process-oriented perspective, the exchange and transformation of knowledge are examined during a discussion. The relationship between knowledge convergence and abstraction has been reported in some studies. Wilmont (2012) pointed out that knowledge convergence consists of three levels of abstraction: concrete, first-order abstractions and second-order abstractions.  Additionally, th e convergence progresses bidirectionally via reciprocal interactions  rather than unidirectionally.  In other words, knowledge convergence  involves both abstracting concrete ideas and de lving into  them using concrete examples.  Similarly, Suthers (2007) and Gnesdilow (2010) demonstrated that creating abstrac t representations promotes knowledge convergence. Additionally, Sangin (2007) reported that abstracting and modeling others’ intentions facilitate this process. While abstraction is beneficial to the knowledge convergence processes, the process quality does not always align with the outcome quality  in the results-oriented perspective  (Barron, 2003).  In other words, even if high-quality abstraction occurs within a group, the knowledge  is not necessarily transfer red to individuals.  However, the conceptual validity of abstractness remains uncertain because these studies rely on coding. For example, Mondoux

(2004) highlighted the issues in measuring abstractness within knowledge convergence. Assessing the amplitude

of the abstractness shifts is difficult  when the distinction between low and high abstractness is unclear. Consequently, insufficient abstraction can affect the outcomes of knowledge convergence. To summarize, although abstraction occurs in the knowledge convergence process, quantitative insights into the scale of abstractness shift s remain unclear, lea ding to uncertain  outcomes. The current limited understanding of knowledge convergence can benefit from quantifying the abstractness in utterances during the knowledge convergence process.

## Quantifying collaborative discussions

The advances in computer sciences have made it feasible to quantify the behavior of learners in the collaborative discussions. Particularly in the quantification of collaborative  discussions, high emphasis is placed on the utterances and actions of individual learners  rather than on their understanding during the discu ssion. The emphasis on utterances and behaviors stems from the ir clarity and observability, making them easy to h andle using digital platforms. The i nterpretations of these utterances and actions may vary among individuals, yet utterances and actions themselves remain objective.  Conversely, the level of understanding in collaborative discussions is highly individualized, and spoken knowledge does not necessarily reflect each participant’s internal knowledge (Shirouzu & Miyake, 2002; Cang et al., 2024). Thus, the quantification analysis of these discussions primarily focuses on externalized knowledge  (Okada et al., 2019)  or actions (Zottmann et al., 2013)  aimed at knowledge-sharing among learners.  For example, knowledge convergence can be  evaluated by analyzing the alignment rate among learners (Jeong & Chi, 2007) or by quantifying noun usage across collaborative discussions (Magnanini, 2022). These measurements express the perspectives of learners’ shared understanding and their level of attachment to the nouns. Representing coded utterances and behaviors as knowledge graphs has also provided insights into the aspects of collaborative discussions. The analysis of knowledge graphs has shown a correlation between the quantity of shared knowledge and improv ed learning outcomes (Zheng  et al., 2014). Additionally, the analysis of multiple discussions represented as  networks has shown  that the first discussion in volves active knowledge exchanges among learners (Xiang et al., 2024). In summary, instead of analyzing utterances and behaviors in full detail, the existing studies have focused on knowledge convergence quantification by summarizing it at a specific granularity.  However, these methods often simplify collaborative discussions into transitions among a few states, thus limiting the details. For example, knowledge alignment metrics use pre-and post-alignment percentages, and coding studies categorize discussions into few states, focusing on transitions. This approach l eads to  information loss on the transition speed of abstractness shifts. Furthermore, although behavior-focused studies provide valuable insights, they rel y on observing specific actions (e.g., annotations) that are necessary for quantification; thus,  their applicability to discussions without such these behaviors is limited. We have investigated on visualizing quantified abstractness (Nishimura et al., 2024); however, we have not thoroughly investigated its relationship with knowledge convergence. Therefore, a quantification method is needed to capture the pace of abstractness shifts per utterance using only spoken content.

## Research questions

In summary, integrating knowledge convergence theor ies with quantitative methods provides valuable ins ights into collaborative discussions. Although the  knowledge convergence process involves shifts between concrete and abstract ideas, the granularity and pace of the se shifts have not been fully investigated. In this study, we propose a method to quantify the abstractness of words in collaborative discussions, resulting in the representation of utterances as numerical sequences.  By analyzing these sequences, we aim to deepen our understanding of the knowledge convergence  process. The research question is: “How are the characteristics of knowledge convergence reflected in the quantified sequences of the abstractness of words in utterances?”

## Methods

Quantifying the abstractness of words in utterances The abstract ness of words in  each utterance is calculated using AWD-J EX  (Nara Institute of Science and Technology, 2019) because the participants were Japanese.  The AWD-J EX was developed to support research on human cognitive processes and contains 437,299 words. Each word assigned an abstractness level on the 1-5 scale; values close to 1 indicate concrete words, whereas values close to 5 indicate abstract words. For example, in this dictionary, the abstractness of “Tokyo” and “city” were 1.3 and 2.0 respectively.  Using this dictionary, utterances are represented as numerical sequences of abstractness. These abstractness sequences differ from typical time-series data because they lack local periodicity. This is attributed to the variation in the abstractness of words within a single utterance. In this study, we used a moving average to capture the overall trends in abstractness. Compared with exist ing methods, this approach reduces the influence of short-term fluctuations and enables fine-grained observation of the degree and speed of the abstractness.

## Discussion content and data collection

This study was conducted at a University in Japan, where undergraduate and graduate science and engineering students participated. To collect discussion audio data, we organized six groups of three participants each, where the participants engaged in a 10-minute collaborative discussion on “What is happiness?” This topic was selected for its accessibility and to encourage individual reflection  (Nishimura et al., 2023). To promote knowledge convergence, the participants were instructed to reach a final concl usion by the end  of the discussion. Figure 1 shows the discussion environment.

The audio data collected from each participant were recorded using a microphone connected to a singleboard computer. The audio  data ware transcribed using Whisper  API (OpenAI, 2024)  and analyzed morphologically to extract nouns. The abstractness calculated using AWD-J EX w as then ordered by the word occurrence to create a dataset. ## Discussion

Quantifying the abstractness of words in utterances The iterative process of concretization and abstract ion facilitates the convergence of the learners’ knowledge (Roschelle, 1992). As previously reported, these processes are not unidirectional; instead, they form a bidirectional cycle (Antoniou, 2019). Based on the quantified abstractness of words in utterances, we examined how the knowledge convergence characteristics appear in the time series of abstractness. The results in Figure 2 show that the sequences tend to fluctuate, and concrete and abstract nouns rarely occur consecutively. This can be attributed to the combination of examples and conclusions in utterances or subtle shifts in abstractness. These findings are similar to those reported in the literature, indicating that abstract concepts are grounded internally and prompt experience-based utterances (Villani et al., 2022). For example, discussing “happiness”, probably led to statements that link it with personal experiences, such as “I felt a happiness when I did ….” Additionally, as shown in Table 1, the means and SDs in all groups were close to each other. Thus, it is not easy to discern trends directly from the plotted time-series data on abstractness. However, Figure 3 shows that the moving average of abstractness increase s in all groups except Group 1 at the end of the discussion.  In this experiment, the participants were instructe d to summarize their final conclusions in order to con verge their knowledge by the end of the discussion.  Thus, the increase in the abstractness reflects the convergence process.  A possible reason for this trend is the increased use of abstract nouns at the end of the discussion to unify individual concrete experiences into a general conclusion. For example, the participants in Group 3, which had the highest moving average, shifted their focus from discussing specific examples of happiness to a more abstract perspective. In this group, the moving average of abstractness decreased between the 225th and 275th nouns. During this phas e, the participants shared specific examples of happiness, such as satisfying hunger with a “meal”, impulsively “buying” a high-performance “PC”, and receiving “New Year’s gift money ” (which called “otoshidama” in Japanese). However, from the 275th noun to the end of the discussion, they began to abstract these points, ul timately concluding that  the "fulfillment” of “desires" is what defines happiness.  A similar trend was observed in Group 5. This group  exhibited the lowest moving average among those that showed an upward trend toward the end of the discussion. In Group 5, the moving average of abstractness decreased between the 150th and 250th nouns. During this phase, the participants shared examples such as achieving the “top four” in a “club activity” and feeling no “regret” after trying their best. They abstracted this happiness into “future-oriented” happiness. Toward the end, they discussed “stress” that was linked to experiences, such as club practice, and concluded that if “satisfaction” outweighs the “stress”, one might feel a “happiness”. T hese examples show the transition from specific  and tangible examples to more abstract  and generalized conclusions as the  participants aimed to consolidate their knowledge.  However, the abstractness decreased the end of the discussion, implying a shift to toward concrete language in Group 1. This may have been affected by the participants ’ awareness of the discussion time. In Group 1, the participants viewed happiness as “self-actualization,” referencing Maslow ’s hierarchy of needs (Maslow, 1943). However, due t o their early conclusion, they made remarks such as “Do we have about ‘two’ ‘minutes’ left?” and “Is it ‘two’ ‘minutes’ before now?” showing concern about the remaining time. The use of these concrete nouns  probably contributed to the decrease in the moving average of the abstractness in the remarks of Group 1 toward the end of the discussion. These examples indicate that the processes of concretization and abstractio n during knowledge convergence may increase the frequency of concrete or abstract nouns; this is reflected as a decrease or increases in the moving average  of the abstractness. Thus, in response to the research question, knowl edge convergence may appear as variations in the moving average of the abstractness of words in utterances.

## Limitations

This study ha s some limitations. The focus on a single topic means that the findings may not be generalized to other topics, especially those requiring more concrete conclusions, because the level of explanation may vary with the concept of the abstractness (Bechlivanidis, 2017). Additionally, some transcription errors were noted, such as the words like “movie” in Group 5, which was  transcribed without being spoken. Some errors may affect the moving average of the abstractness. However, as these errors were relatively few, they do not undermine the value of this study. Another limitation is the fixed window size used for calculating the mo ving average. E mploying different window sizes in future studies could reve al more localized characteristics and provide deepe r insights. Additionally, future research should also explore multiple topics and reduce transcription inaccuracies to validate and extend these findings.

## Conclusion

We proposed a method for analyzing word abstractness i n utterances to quantify the process of  knowledge convergence in collaborative discussions. Specifica lly, we conducted a time-series analysis to measure the differences in this process across  the discussion groups. We collected data from six groups, each eng aging in a 20-minute collaborative discussion involving three participants. The results showed that concretization and abstraction during knowledge convergence  may reflect the decreases or increases in the moving average  of the abstractness. This finding allowed us to quantify the trend of each group regarding abstractness and the rate of change in word abstractness. For the overall trend analysis, we set the moving average w indow size to approximately one-fifth of the total number of nouns; adjusting this size could allow for a more localized analysis. Our method provides a way to quantify changes in abstra ctness during the knowledge convergence process, which conventional methods have difficulties capturing. The obtained results bridge the gap between qualitative and quantitative research on knowledge convergence and contribute to the generation of new insights in the CSCL context.
