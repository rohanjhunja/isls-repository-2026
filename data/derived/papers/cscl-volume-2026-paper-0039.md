# Integrating Augmented Reality and Product Design in Makerspaces

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study aims to explore the affordances of augmented reality technologies (AR) to facilitate studentsʼ product design processes in a business course. We analyze the affordances of AR in the context of a product design activity in an undergraduate classroom. The course is held in a Makerspace lab at a university campus, and it requires students to conceptualize, design, prototype, manufacture, and market new products by using 3D computer-aided design (CAD) software and 3D printing. Our study illuminates that although CAD allows students to visualize and manipulate digitally manufactured 3D objects on a 2D computer monitor surface, it does not allow them to visualize and manipulate those 3D objects in 3D space. However, AR does afford visualization and mani pulation of digitally manufactured 3D objects in 3D space which is why we investigated how do interactions with 3D models in AR impact product design processes for students.

Developing students’ ability to visualize and manipulate three-dimensional (3D) objects is essential for design and engineering education, yet traditional computer-aided design (CAD) tools constrain learners to 2D screens. These constraints can limit stude nts’ spatial reasoning and make it difficult for teams to achieve shared understanding of designs when collaborating. Recent advances in augmented reality (AR) provide new possibilities for supporting these processes by enabling learners to view and intera ct with 3D models in real physical space (Garzon & Acevedo, 2019). Due to its  immersive visualization and shared spatial reference capabilities, AR can make abstract design concepts more tangible and promote visualization. Prior research  has documented the pedagogical potential of AR for enhancing spatial cognition and engagement in science learning (Cheng & Tsai, 2013). However, studies examining AR-supported product design, particularly within Makerspace learning environments, remain limited. Existing work has primarily emphasized technical implementation or learner motivation, with less attention on how students experience and interpret AR’s affordances for understanding and collaboration during authentic product design tasks.

To address this gap, we conducted an exploratory study that investigates how college students engage with 3D modeling tasks supported by AR technology. This study took place in a Makerspace course that integrates CAD and 3D printing. After completing a CAD-based design task, students engaged in a similar activity using AR headsets to interact with their 3D models in an immersive manner. T he researchers then  examined how students described their individual and team-based experiences with AR and what affordances they perceived as supporting spatial understanding and collaboration during product design. Grounded in spatial cognition and social constructivist perspectives, we view AR as a tool that can make spatial relationships perceptually accessible and provide a shared visual field for the co-construction of ideas. Our goal was not to compare learning outcomes between CAD and AR, but to explore students’ perceptions of how AR-mediated interaction might enhance visualization and teamwork in an actual design-based learning context. Specifically, we sought to answer two key research questions: RQ1: How do students describe their experiences interacting with 3D models using AR during individual and team-based product design activities?

RQ2: What affordances of AR-supported interaction do students perceive as supporting their spatial understanding and team collaboration during product design? Spatial cognition AR is the technology that overlays virtual elements in the real world (Akcayir & Akcayir, 2017; Garzon & Acevedo, 2019). This technology has been shown to have potential for pedagogical applications for over a decade. In a literature review of empirical research on affordances of augmented reality in science learning, Cheng & Tsai

(2013) suggest that AR affords students’ spatial ability, particularly by supporting spatial cognition, which refers

to how individuals perceive, represent, and reason about spati al properties like size, shape, location or direction 283 of objects or events in the world. AR affordances are features or properties of elements that present cues on what can be done with them and provide input on how students can interact with them (Shin, 2021). In this sense, AR can reduce reliance on internal mental visualization by externalizing spatial representations, allowing learners to directly perceive and manipulate 3D forms. The mix of physical and virtual elements in the coexisting physical and virtual learning environments create a new type of real ity for students, a mixed reality (MR). The MR and AR definitions regularly adopted by empirical research are described as variations of Virtual Reality (VR) publicized by Azuma as shown in Figure 1 (Azuma, 1997; Cheng & Tsai, 2013). Situated along the vir tuality continuum (Milgram & Kishino, 1994), these environments enable iterative interactions between perception and action, which are central to developing spatial understanding in design contexts. AR allows students to interact both with  their peers and the virtual environment, constructing a new understanding of the underlying data as the virtual objects respond to user input (Lan, C., et al, 2013; Taber, K.S., 2018). Through shared interaction with these representations, learners can co ordinate attention, negotiate meaning, and build joint understanding in real time. In this way, AR environments function as mediating space where collaborative sensemaking is anchored in shared 3D artifacts. Framing the learning problem and design approach To study the potential pedagogical application of AR technology for product design in Makerspaces, our research team analyzed how students’ product design processes are impacted when they are interacting with 3D objects in these immersive environments as individual learners and when they are socially interacting with other members of the team. The multimodal AR-aided experimental intervention that we designed utilizes hardware such as AR headsets, laptops, and desktops to facilitate students’ product design processes while they are designing physical objects which will be 3D printed.

Teaching students (our future workforce) how to manufacture physical products can be a challenging experience for instructors. Often instructors don’t have the desired manufacturing equipment required to teach the students the necessary skills they need to learn about how to manufacture physical products. Tools for milling, drilling, grinding, and finishing are vital artifacts that teachers use. However, manufacturing equipment can be costly, timely to use or set up, difficult to use or set up, and sometimes inadequate to achieve learning goals due to its physical nature. Therefore, it is important that instructors take advantage of all available resources to them. One available resource that helps instructors teach students how to manufacture physical products is by using 3D printers to enable quick prototyping. However, creating models for 3D printing requires knowledge of CAD tools, which have become easier to use over time but still require you to work in 2D. Due to the limitations of working in 2D while trying to design a 3D model, students struggle with spatial reasoning in CAD because it requires them to imagine and approximate how their 3D model would look like in the real world rather than just seeing the actual 3D model in the real world. Designing in 2D becomes increasingly more difficult when working in teams due to lack of shared 3D understanding amongst team members.  This creates a dual challenge: students must internally translate between 2D representations and 3D outcomes while also attempting to align these interpretations across team members. From a spatial cognition perspective, this increases cognitive load by re quiring internal visualization rather thandirect interaction with 3D forms. From a social constructivist perspective, it constrains collaboration by limiting access to shared, manipulable representations that support joint attention and coordinated action. But AR does afford interactions with 3D 284 objects in 3D space in the context of Makerspaces which can afford students the opportunity to measure, identify, understand, and practice seeing general principles at work while using their spatial abilities in the real world, thus creating a joined understanding of the problems that students are trying to solve (NCTM, 2022). By externalizing design artifacts into the physical environment, AR enables learners to directly perceive and manipulate 3D models, supporting both individual spatial reasoning and shared interpretation within teams. Extended reality (XR) technology, such as augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR), has been listed as critical and emerging technology by the National Science Foundation (NSF) and it has been highlighted as one of the most innovative pedagogies in recent empirical reports (National Science and Technology Council, 2022; Kukulska-Hulme et al., 2021). Social interaction among students who are immersed in environments with interactive 3D objects in 3D space could allow for AR-aided learning to occur. Uniting the affordances of Makerspaces 3D printing technology with AR technology could not only enhance the learning experience, but it also has potential for transformational pedagogical applications in science learning (Cheng & Tsai, 2013; McQuiggan, 2015). To explore this potential, we designed an intervention to test the effects of 3D modeling with CAD in AR-facilitated product design activities situated in a Makerspace lab with easy access to 3D printers. The intervention was designed to allow students to view and manipulate their CAD-created 3D model in a 3D space with an AR headset as a form of iteration before they physically print them on a 3D printer and spend physical materials. This study therefore examines how AR-mediated interaction with 3D models shapes students’ spatial reasoning and collaborative product design processes in this context. Approach Utilizing 3D printers as a resource for teaching students how to design, manufacture, and market physical objects could serve as a substitute for costly physical manufacturing equipment. Makerspaces can serve as catalyzers for innovation where students turn ideas to objects using 3D computer-aided design (CAD) software. Traditional 3D modeling with CAD only allows for visualizing 3D objects on a 2D plane which means that the users’ spatial abilities are distorted since they can’t visualize the third dimension, the dimension of depth (or height). Further, these technologies limit the viewpoint to a small 2D computer screen rather than a large 3D space we typically embody while we communicate and interact in person. That means that our human sense of vision is  limited by the capabilities of technology. However, those 3D CAD objects could be viewed and manipulated in AR. AR technology will allow students to visualize 3D objects in 3D space.

## Participants and context

The participants in the study’s intervention were from an undergraduate business course that is a hands-on course in which interdisciplinary teams of business, design, and engineering students conceptualize, design, prototype, manufacture, and market new products. To create these products, they use 3D software and hardware. By enrolling in this course, students develop collaboration, design, manufacturing, and marketing skills. This course is held in a Makerspace at a university campus, and it typically has an enrollment of 21 students that are split into 7 teams of 3 members each. Of the 21 students that were enrolled in the course, 16 students participated in our study. For this study we designed an experimental intervention that incorporated AR into the existing Makerspace learning environment while students are learning how to do 3D modeling with CAD. The intervention was designed to allow students to view and manipulat e their CAD-created 3D model in a 3D space with a Microsoft HoloLens2 AR headset as a form of iteration before they physically print them on a 3D printer and spend physical materials. The HoloLens2 headset is a lightweight mixed reality device that users w ould wear on their head like a pair of glasses. This device allows users to not only see the world around them but also see digital augments in their field of view. Students wore the HoloLens2 AR headset and interacted with the virtual space with simple manipulations such as buttons on the headset or with hand gestures. Each team had one AR headset that was set up to cast a live view of the AR headset to a nearby computer monitor. That meant that, regardless of who was wearing the AR headset, every member of the team had a live view of the same 3D object in that same 3D space. The intervention enabled students to interact with their 3D model in 3D space by moving it, rotating it, and scaling it, giving them the opportunity to construct products that had been previously unrealized by traditional tools. Manipulating 3D objects in 3D space afforded students the opportunity to measure, identify, understand, and practice seeing general principles at work while using their spatial abilities and socially interacting in the real world (NCTM, 2000; Topraklikoglu & Ozturk, 2021).

## Procedure and data sources

285 Data was collected from students who filled out paper questionnaires forms after day two of the study. Participants answered two open-ended questions to describe how interactions with 3D models in AR impacted their product design process as an individual and as a team: “How did interactions with 3D models in AR impact your product design process as an individual?” “How did interactions with 3D models in AR impact your product design process as a team?” The intervention took place during the students’ regularly scheduled class instruction. Our intention was to make minimal disruption and as small changes as possible to the natural learning environment. It is standard practice in this course for students to complete 3D modeling with CAD activities and questionnaires about their education. Thus, integrating this intervention into the classroom activities aligned well with the regular curriculum because it comprised of 3D modeling with CAD + AR and a questionnaire. Figure 4 3D Models in CAD, 3D Models in AR, and Printed 3D Models We conducted an inductive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006) of students' open-ended responses to explore how they experienced and described their interactions with 3D models in AR. While our coding process was datadriven and exploratory, we drew on frameworks from spatial cognition (Hegarty & Waller, 2004) and social constructivism (Vygotsky, 1978; Dillenbourg, 1999) to inform our interpretation of emerging patterns. These theories guided how we grouped and named themes, particularly in relation to how students described their spatial reasoning and collaborative practices during the design process. Two researchers independently coded the questionnaire responses, identifying patterns in how students described their individual and team experiences with AR. Initial codes were compared and refined through interaction. Each theme was defined based on recurring patterns in student language and the types of affordances students perceived as supporting their design work.

Spatial visualization Students described AR as enabling them to perceive their designs as tangible objects in physical space, rather than as abstract representations on screens. This theme captures how AR supported students' ability to assess scale, proportion, and spatial relationships without requiring mental rotation or imagination. Many students described how seeing their 3D models in AR  supported their ability to visualize the product's shape, scale, and structure in physical space. Unlike working in CAD alone, which required mental rotation or imagining proportions, students reported that AR helped them "see" the model as if it were alr eady built: “We were all able to visualize the product before printing it and seeing where we candevelop on our design and next actions to be taken in regards to our product and design.” “Seeing our model using the AR technology helped us get a feel of what our product would look like in real life and helped us understand what areas we should change.” These comments suggest that AR helped bridge the gap between digital modeling and embodied spatial reasoning. Rather than relying on mental transformation of 2D views into 3D understanding, students could directly perceive spatial relationships, making des ign evaluation more concrete and action-oriented. The shift from "imagining" to "seeing" represents a reduction in cognitive demand—students no longer had to mentally construct the third dimension.

287 Prior research in spatial cognition highlights the importance of external visualizations in supporting mental manipulation and design planning (Hegarty & Waller, 2004; Johnson-Glenberg et al., 2016). Our findings suggest that AR may offer similar benefits by providing situated, scale-aware representations that reduce the cognitive demand of imagining 3D transformations. By making spatial properties perceptually accessible rather than inferential, AR may support more direct spatial reasoning during design it eration. Collaborative awareness This theme captures how AR enabled shared perception of design objects, allowing team members to reference the same spatial features simultaneously and coordinate their attention during design discussions. Students described AR as supporting both individual spatial understanding and collective decision-making. Eight students explained how AR enabled team collaboration and communication during the design process: “As a team AR facilitated collaborative decision-making by providing a shared immersive experience where we could all examine the 3D model from different angles. It improved communication, as we could point out issues directly on the model, making discussions more efficient.” “The interactions improved visualization and realism. They enhanced collaboration. They helped with design accuracy and functional products.” The emphasis on "shared immersive experience" and the ability to "point out issues directly on the model" suggests that AR provided a common spatial reference field that grounded collaborative discourse. Rather than each team member maintaining their own m ental model and attempting to coordinate through verbal description alone, AR created a shared perceptual space where spatial features could be directly indicated and jointly attended to. Considering that 50% of our participants’ responses supported decades of empirical research,  social interaction itself enables learning and has a positive effect especially for young learners. Along the same lines, positive effects of collaborative learning where students work together toward a common learning goal instead of a solo learning environment have been known for a very long time in the Learning Sciences  (Hersh-Pasek K., et al, 2015; Johnson, D.W., et al, 1981). Thus, it was a pleasure to see that this type of collaboration is also supported in AR environments  where students reported that they enjoyed the  shared immersive experiences and also be aware of the physical outside environment.

Immersive interaction This theme captures how students experienced AR's interactive capabilities  – particularly the ability to manipulate, navigate around, and physically engage with virtual objects in 3D space. Students described these interactions as more intuitive and effective than traditional CAD interfaces, emphasizing how direct manipulation in immersive space supported their design evaluation. Eight  students highlighted the importance of interacting with 3D objects in 3D space through the AR interface: “Interacting with 3D models in AR helped me visualize the product in a real world environment, allowing me to assess its scale, properties, and functionalities more effectively than with traditional sketches or digital models.” “As an individual, the interactions  with the 3D model allowed me to navigate and “ feel” our prototype in an easier and more intuitive manner than the 3D viewing possible when using a model in Fusion 360.” These responses reveal a distinction between passive viewing and active interaction. The emphasis on being able to "navigate" and "feel" the prototype suggests that AR's value extends beyond visualization alone—it includes embodied interaction with virtual objects. The comparison to Fusion 360 (a CAD program) is particularly telling: while CAD allows rotation and zoom through mouse and keyboard controls, AR enables manipulation through natural gestures and physical movement around the object. This shift f rom interface-mediated control to direct manipulation may make the design evaluation process "easier and more intuitive." The student's use of "feel" in quotation marks is noteworthy. While AR does not provide haptic feedback, students described a sense of tangibility or material presence when interacting with virtual objects in physical 288 space. This suggests that spatial co-location and gestural interaction can create a psychological sense of physical engagement even without touch, supporting more concrete design reasoning. Research on embodied cognition suggests that physical interaction with objects supports deeper conceptual understanding than passive observation (Johnson-Glenberg et al., 2016). By enabling gestural manipulation and navigation in 3D space, AR may activate sensorimotor schemas that support spatial reasoning. The ability to walk around objects, rotate them with hand gestures, and view them from multiple perspectives mirrors how we naturally explore physical objects in the world. This alignment between AR inte raction and everyday spatial behavior may explain why students found AR "more intuitive" than screen-based CAD tools that require learning arbitrary interface conventions.

Furthermore, the immersive quality of AR —where virtual objects appear embedded in the physical environment—may reduce the psychological distance between design representation and fabricated product. This could support what cognitive scientists call near transfer, where learning in one context (AR visualization) transfers more readily to another context (physical product evaluation) because the two contexts share perceptual and spatial features.

This study explored how undergraduate students experienced AR technology during product design activities in a Makerspace course. We analyzed students' descriptions of their experiences and identified three primary themes: spatial visualization, collaborative awareness, and immersive interaction. Our findings contribute to understanding how AR can support both spatial reasoning and collaborative work in design-based learning environments.

AR and spatial cognition Our findings show that AR reduced the cognitive demands students faced when working with 3D designs. Students repeatedly described AR as enabling them to "see" their products "in real life" rather than having to imagine what their 2D CAD designs would look like in 3D space. This matters because traditional CAD tools require students to mentally rotate, scale, and imagine depth—cognitive operations that can be difficult and error-prone. AR externalizes these spatial relationships by making them directly visible in physical space. This finding extends prior work on AR in science education (Cheng & Tsai, 2013; Garzón & Acevedo,

2019) by showing a specific way AR supports learning: students perceive reductions in cognitive demands with

mental transformations. Instead of viewing AR simply as a motivational tool, our results suggest AR's core value is making spatial properties directly perceivable. This is particularly important for design education, where spatial reasoning is central and where learners often struggle with the transition from 2D sketching to 3D fabrication. Students emphasized being able to assess "scale" and "proportions" in physical space. This highlights how spatial understanding is grounded in our bodily experience of navigating and interacting with objects (Johnson-Glenberg et al., 2016). By placing virtual objects in physical space, AR taps into students' natural abilities for perceiving depth, distance, and scale more effectively than screen-based tools. Supporting collaborative design Our second major finding was that students valued AR for supporting teamwork. Eight students described AR as facilitating collaborative decision-making through "shared immersive experiences" where team members could "examine the 3D model from different angles" and "point out issues directly on the model." This shows how AR functions as a mediating tool that supports joint attention and coordinated activity (Vygotsky, 1978; Roschelle, 1992).

What stands out is how AR creates a shared visual space that grounds team discussions. In traditional CAD workflows, each team member views the model on their own screen, and coordination requires verbal description or screen-sharing. AR allows team member s to view and manipulate a single virtual object together in shared physical space. This shared visual reference supports the development of common understanding through joint activity (Roschelle, 1992).

This finding adds to existing research on AR and collaboration (Lan et al., 2013) by identifying a specific collaborative affordance: the creation of shared spatial reference. AR enables pointing, joint attention, and simultaneous manipulation—all central to effective design collaboration (Dillenbourg, 1999). This suggests AR may be particularly valuable in learning contexts that emphasize collaborative problem-solving around spatial or physical artifacts.

## Design implications for learning

Our findings suggest several practical applications for educators integrating AR into Makerspace s. 289 AR as an intermediate design tool Rather than replacing CAD or 3D printing, AR works best as an intermediate visualization and evaluation tool. Students in our study used AR after creating CAD models but before 3D printing, allowing them to iterate on designs without wasting physical materials. This suggests a design workflow where AR serves as a spatial preview stage that reduces costly fabrication errors.

Supporting collaborative design reviews The collaborative benefits of AR suggest its value for design critique and peer feedback activities. Educators can structure design review sessions where teams use AR to present their work, with peers providing feedback while viewing the AR model together.  This formalizes the collaborative benefits students described and supports peer learning.

Scaffolding spatial reasoning For students with less developed spatial abilities, AR provides essential support during the transition from conceptual sketches to fabricated objects. Instructors might provide additional AR visualization opportunities for students who struggle with mental rotation or spatial transformation tasks. Design for shared attention When implementing AR in collaborative contexts, prioritize features that support joint attention —for example, multi-user AR experiences where team members see the same virtual object simultaneously, or annotation tools that allow collaborative marking and discussion.

## Limitations and future research

Our study has several limitations. First, our sample was small (sixteen students) from a single course, limiting generalizability. Second, we relied on self-report data through open-ended questionnaires. While this provided insight into students' experiences, we cannot assess actual learning outcomes or compare AR-supported learning with traditional instruction. Students may have valued AR for novelty rather than learning benefits. Third, we did not include a comparison condition or measure spatial reasoning abilities directly. We cannot make claims about AR's causal effects on learning. Fourth, our thematic analysis was interpretive, and other researchers might identify different themes.

Future research should address these limitations in several directions. We plan to iterate on this intervention across multiple course implementations using design-based research methods (Brown, 1992), gathering both qualitative and quantitative data on learning outcomes. This includes pre-post assessments of spatial ability, comparative analysis of design artifacts, and video analysis of collaborative design sessions. Future studies should include comparison conditions (CAD only vs. CAD + AR) and measure learning outcomes such as design quality, spatial reasoning test scores, and team communication effectiveness. Longer-term implementation would show whether AR's benefi ts continue as novelty fades and whether students internalize spatial reasoning strategies. Research should also investigate whether AR supports equitable learning across diverse student populations and examines differential effects by prior spatial ability or domain expertise. This study explores how AR can support both spatial reasoning and collaborative work in design-based learning contexts. Our findings suggest AR's value lies in transforming the nature of spatial reasoning and collaborative work during design activities. By making spatial relationships directly perceivable and creating shared visual fields for collaboration, AR addresses two challenges in design education: the cognitive demands of working across 2D and 3D representations, and the coordination challenges of collaborative design work. As AR hardware becomes more affordable and accessible, and as educators prepare students for futures where mixed reality technologies are commonplace, understanding AR's pedagogical affordances becomes increasingly important. Our analysis of this AR-integrated product design activity offers a model for incorporating AR into Makerspace curricula, grounded in spatial cognition and social constructivist theories. We hope this model will guide other educators and researchers in designing and studying AR-supported learning experiences. The future of work increasingly involves designing, prototyping, and collaborating in mixed reality environments. Similar to how personal computers, laptops, and smartphones evolved from emerging technologies to essential tools, we anticipate that today's immersive head-mounted displays will become essential in the future.
