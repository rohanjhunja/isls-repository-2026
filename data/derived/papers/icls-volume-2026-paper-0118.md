# The Impact of Anthropomorphic Digital Human Teachers on Learners’ Cognitive Engagement in Online Learning: An Eye-Tracking Study

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigated how digital human teachers with different levels of anthropomorphism affect learners’ cognitive engagement in online learning. Seventeen university students participated in an eye-tracking experiment using a Tobii Pro eye tracker. Three teaching conditions were compared: real teacher, high-anthropomorphism digital teacher, and low-anthropomorphism digital teacher.Results from one-way repeated-measures ANOVA showed significant effects of teacher type on fixation duration (F(2,32)=18.59, p<.001) and fixation count (F(2,32)=30.95, p<.001) within the teacher area. Learners showed higher attention and deeper cognitive processing when viewing the real or high-anthropomorphism teacher than the low-anthropomorphism one. Only a slight difference was found in slide attention ratio (F=3.84, p=.028).These findings suggest that higher anthropomorphism enhances learners’ visual attention and cognitive engagement by increasing social presence and realism, providing practical insights for AI-based instructional design.

With the advancement of digital technology and the acceleration of educational digital transformation—especially following the COVID-19 pandemic —online learning has become a key component of higher education and lifelong learning systems (Aristovnik et al., 2023). Compared with traditional face-to-face teaching, online learning offers advantages of flexibility, openness, and scalability, yet continues to face long-standing challenges such as low learner engagement, limited interaction, and delayed instructional feedback (Kebritchi et al., 2017). Moreover, the production of high-quality instructional videos remains highly labor-intensive, typically requiring collaboration among directors, videographers, and post-production specialists, resulting in long production cycles and high costs (Guo, Kim, & Rubin, 2014; Brame, 2017). When curricula change, course re-recording further compounds these resource demands. There is therefore an urgent need for more efficient, flexible, and sustainable approaches to online course production and delivery.

Against this backdrop, the integration of artificial intelligence and education (AIED) has given rise tonew instructional forms such as intelligent tutoring systems, virtual teaching agents, and digital human teachers (Ifenthaler et al., 2024). Among these, digital human teachers—virtual instructors powered by AI speech synthesis, motion capture, and visual generation—can simulate human teaching behavior with advantages in cost efficiency, scalability, and content renewability (Ryzheva et al., 2024). A core pedagogical value of digital human teachers lies in their capacity to simulate "social presence," which refers to the perceived sense of "being with another" (Short et al., 1976). Through computer graphics and natural language processing, digital human t eachers can reproduce realistic facial expressions, gestures, and vocal features, thereby constructing quasi-authentic teaching interactions in online contexts (Gao et al., 2025). In this process, anthropomorphism —the attribution of humanlike characteristics to non-human entities (Epley, Waytz, & Cacioppo, 2007)—becomes a crucial design factor influencing learners' perceptions and engagement (Cai, 2023). Research indicates that anthropomorphic features can positively influence learners' attitudes, satisfaction, and learning outcomes (Zhao et al., 2025). For instance, higher human-likeness in digital teachers has been shown to improve satisfaction and perceived quality of the learning experience (Chu, 2025). However, excessive realism may trigger discomfort and distraction—a phenomenon known as the "uncanny valley effect" (Ackermann et al., 2025). Anthropomorphism must therefore achieve an appropriate balanc e, emphasizing social cues without overloading learners' cognitive resources or inducing perceptual dissonance. Despite the growing use of digital human teachers, systematic comparisons of different anthropomorphism levels and their effects on online learning remain scarce.

To provide a more integrated theoretical framework, the present study draws upon the Community of Inquiry (CoI) model (Garrison, Anderson, & Archer, 2000) as a complement to Social Presence Theory. The CoI framework identifies three interdependent dimensions of meaningful learning: social presence, teaching presence, and cognitive presence. In the context of this study, the anthropomorphism level of digital human teachers can be understood as simultaneously operating on social presence —by enhancing learners ' perceived sense of co-presence through human-like appearance and expressions —and teaching presence—by improving instructional 1058 delivery through naturally coordinated gestures, speech, and facial cues (Anderson et al., 2001). These two dimensions work in concert to facilitate deeper cognitive processing, i.e., cognitive presence. This multi-dimensional perspective provides a robust theoretical rationale for using gaze behavior as an indicator of cognitive engagement: learners' fixations on the teacher region reflect responses to social and teaching presence, while fixations on the slide region more directly capture information extraction and meaning construction at the cognitive presence level.

Learning engagement is recognized as an essential variable for evaluating learning effectiveness (Fredricks et al., 2004), typically comprising behavioral, cognitive, and emotional dimensions. Among these, cognitive engagement —the mental resources learners  invest in comprehension, analysis, reasoning, and self-regulation—is considered a core predictor of academic performance (Sinatra et al., 2015; Shi et al., 2021). The non-face-to-face nature of online learning makes it difficult for instructors to assess engagement through traditional observation, rendering cognitive engagement a particularly important indicator of online learning quality (Henrie et al., 2015). Based on this understanding, the present study collected eye-tracking data to objectively capture learners' attention distribution and cognitive processing characteristics during video-based learning. These process-level indicators were used to assess cognitive engagement, aiming to explore how different levels of anthropomorphism in digital human teachers influence learners' cognitive engagement in online learning.

RQ1: How does the use of digital human teachers, compared with real human teachers, influence learners' cognitive engagement in online learning?

RQ2: How do different levels of anthropomorphism in digital human teachers (high vs. low anthropomorphism) affect learners' cognitive engagement during online learning?

## Measures

Experimental Design To investigate the effects of different levels of anthropomorphism in digital human teachers on learners' cognitive engagement, this study adopted a single-factor between-subjects design with three experimental conditions: (1) a Real Human Teacher group, f eaturing videos recorded with a real instructor; (2) a High-Anthropomorphism Digital Human Teacher group, featuring a digital instructor with a highly human-like appearance, natural facial expressions, and smooth body movements; and (3) a Low-Anthropomorphism Digital Human Teacher group, featuring a digital instructor with a more cartoon-like appearance. All three conditions shared the same course topic, slides, and teaching script, differing only in the teacher's visual appearance  and voice. Instructional Materials The lesson topic across all three conditions was "Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning," which introduced the definition, principles, and applications of machine learning. The course slides and supporting materials were prepared by the instructor in advance, and a word-for-word teaching script was developed to ensure content consistency across conditions. Each video lasted approximately 10–12 minutes and comprised three instructional segments (approximately 3– 4 minutes, 5 minutes, and 2– 3 minutes, r espectively), corresponding to the three subtopics of the lesson. These segments were analyzed separately to enable fine-grained examination of learners' cognitive and emotional engagement across different learning stages. The real human teacher video was recorded in a professional studio at a university in Shanghai, equipped with high-quality lighting and audio systems. The instructor was a doctoral candidate from the same institution with extensive experience in course del ivery and video recording, ensuring fluent and confident on-camera performance. Post-production editing was conducted by professional video editors to refine transitions, synchronize visuals, and enhance overall quality.

The two digital human teacher videos were produced using the Chanjing Digital Human Platform, a widely used AI-based digital human generation system in China. The same PowerPoint slides and teaching scripts were uploaded to the platform, and digital teache r avatars and voices were selected according to the target anthropomorphism level. The platform automatically synthesized complete course videos with speech synchronized to lip movements. The generated videos did not undergo additional manual editing, ensuring standardization and reproducibility of experimental conditions while authentically reflecting the actual output quality of current AI digital human platforms.

Operationalization of anthropomorphism levels The distinction between high-and low-anthropomorphism digital human teachers was operationally defined along four dimensions. First, regarding facial appearance, the high-anthropomorphism digital teacher exhibited facial 1059 features closely resembling a real human, with realistic skin texture, natural proportions, and authentic lighting effects, whereas the low-anthropomorphism digital teacher displayed a distinctly cartoon-like style with simplified features and non-photorealistic rendering. Second, regarding facial expressions, the highanthropomorphism condition featured rich and natural expression changes including eyebrow movements, lip synchronization, and micro-expressions, while the low-anthropomorphism condition exhibited more limited and mechanical expression variation. Third, regarding body movements, the high-anthropomorphism digital teacher demonstrated diverse and fluid gestures with natural coordination to speech content, whereas the low-anthropomorphism digital teacher's movements were relatively stiff and repetitive. Fourth, regarding voice characteristics, the high-anthropomorphism condition used AI-synthesized speech that more closely approximated a real human voice with natural intonation and rhythm, while the low-anthropomorphism condition had relatively flat vocal delivery. These dimensional differences were primarily achieved through the selection of different avatar types on the Chanjing platform —the high-anthropomorphism condition used the platform's most photorealistic hyper-realistic avatar, while the low-anthropomorphism condition used a cartoon-style avatar. These design decisions were collectively determined by the research team based on relevant literature (Epley et al., 2007; Cai et al., 2023) and the available options on the platform. The online course designs for the three experimental groups are illustrated in the examples below. Regarding production efficiency, the real teacher course required a professional studio, lighting and audio equipment, and post-production editing, with an overall production cycle of approximately 2– 3 days. In contrast, each digital human course required only the upload of slides and scripts followed by automated video synthesis, with a generation time of approximately 30 –60 minutes per video. This difference in production processes itself illustrates the significant efficiency advantages of digital human teachers over traditional video recording approaches.

## Participants

A total of 17 undergraduate students (6 males and 11 females; M = 20.1 years, SD = 1.2) from a university in Shanghai participated in this study. All participants were non-computer science majors to minimize the potential influence of prior knowledge on course comprehension. They possessed basic computer literacy and prior experience with online learning. Participation was voluntary, and all students provided informed consent prior to the experiment.

## Experimental procedure

The experiment was conducted in a quiet and evenly lit laboratory to minimize external distractions. Each participant completed the task individually. A 23-inch monitor (1920×1080 resolution) equipped with a Tobii Pro eye tracker (sampling rate: 60 Hz) was used, with the device mounted below the screen for stable gaze data collection.Prior to the formal session, all participants underwent a 9-point calibration to ensure high tracking accuracy (average error < 0.5° of visual angle).

The study included three instructional conditions: (1) a real teacher, (2) a high-anthropomorphism digital teacher, and (3) a low-anthropomorphism digital teacher. The course topic was “ Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning”, covering three modules —definition, principles, and applications of machine learning. Each participant watched three video segments (total duration ≈ 10 –12 minutes).The course content and narration were identical across all conditions, differing only in the teacher ’ s appearance, expressions, and gestures. During the session, participants were instructed to maintain a natural sitting posture, keep their eyes on the screen, and minimize head movements or gaze shifts.

## Eye-tracking measures

In this study, a Tobii Pro eye tracker (sampling rate: 60 Hz) was used to record learners’ gaze behaviors during the online learning task. To assess learners’ cognitive engagement and attention allocation, six key eye-tracking indicators were selected based on established literature (Holmqvist et al., 2011; Van Gog & Scheiter, 2010; Alemdag & Cagiltay, 2018; Hyönä, 2010; Kizilcec et al., 2017), as shown in Table 1. Fixation Duration – reflects the depth of information processing and the degree of cognitive investment (Holmqvist et al., 2011; Alemdag & Cagiltay, 2018). Number of Fixations – indicates the intensity of v isual attention and focus (Hyönä, 2010; Van Gog & Scheiter, 2010). Mean Glance Duration – measures sustained attention and processing stability (Holmqvist et al., 2011). AOI Attention Ratio – represents learners’ attention distribution between the teacher and courseware regions (Van Gog & Scheiter, 2010; Kizilcec et al., 2017). Saccade Length – reflects information-search behavior and cognitive load variations (Hyönä, 2010; Van Gog & Scheiter, 2010). Eye Activity (pixels/s) – indicates visual processing effort and mental workload (Holmqvist et al., 2011; Van Gog & Scheiter, 2010). Fixation-based measures were used to assess information-processing depth and sustained attention, while saccade and movementbased measures were sensitive to changes in cognitive load. All gaze data were preprocessed in Tobii Pro Lab, with fixations shorter than 60 ms excluded, and participants with more than 20% missing data removed from Areas of Interest (AOIs) are central analytical units in eye-tracking research, referring to specific regions predefined by researchers on the visual stimulus display for extracting and analyzing learners' gaze behavior data within those regions (Holmqvist et al., 2011). In this study, based on the visual layout of the instructional videos, each video frame was divided into two primary AOIs: the Teacher AOI and the Slide AOI. The Teacher AOI encompassed the body region where the teacher (or digital human teacher) appeared on screen, including the face, upper body, and gesture range. The Slide AOI encompassed the region where the PowerPoint slides were displayed, including text, diagrams, and other instructional content. AOI boundaries were delineated by manually drawing rectangular regions in Tobii Pro Lab software, with consistent delineation criteria maintained across all experimental conditions. By comparing learners' gaze behaviors between the Teacher AOI and the Slide AOI, learners' attention allocation patterns and cognitive processing characteristics can be objectively captured, thereby inferring their level of cognitive engagement. Longer fixation durations and higher fixation counts within the Teacher AOI are interpreted as indicators of deeper social-cognitive processing directed toward the teacher's image, while gaze behaviors in the Slide AOI reflect learners' information extraction and comprehension processes related to the instructional content itself.

## Data analysis

The data analysis consisted of three main stages. First, all eye-tracking data were preprocessed by removing missing and abnormal samples (e.g., fixation durations < 60 ms). The mean values for each indicator were calculated separately for the teacher AOI and slide AOI. Descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation) were computed for all indicators under each teacher anthropomorphism condition. A 2 (AOI type: teacher vs. slide) × 3 (teacher type: real, high-anthropomorphism digital, low-anthropomorphism digital) repeated-measures ANOVA was then conducted to examine the effects of teacher anthropomorphism level on learners’ visual attention and 1061 cognitive engagement. The significance level was set at p < 0.05, and Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc tests were used for pairwise comparisons where significant effects were found.All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0.

Descriptive statistics and inferential analysis To comprehensively present learners ’ visual attention patterns under different instructional conditions, this study calculated key eye-tracking indicators within both the Teacher Area of Interest (Teacher AOI) and the Slide Area of Interest (Slide AOI). These indicators included Fixation Duration, Number of Fixations, AOI Attention Ratio, Saccade Length, and Eye Activity. Additionally, a one-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted to examine the statistical significance of differences across the three instructional conditions. Highly anthropomorphic teachers enhance learners’ sense of social presence and sustained focus, facilitating deeper cognitive engagement, whereas low-anthropomorphism teachers—characterized by rigid movements and limited expressiveness —tend to evoke superficial attention and reduced processing depth. Effects of digital human teachers on cognitive engagement (RQ1) learners’ cognitive engagement, as reflected by shorter fixation durations and fewer fixations within the teacher AOI. This finding suggests that although digital human teachers can visually simulate real instructors, the lack of authentic social cues and natural micro-expressions still limits their ability to sustain learners’ deep attention. According to the Social Presence Theory (Short et al., 1976), learners respond socially to media representations when they perceive human-like characteristics. However, in the case of digital human teachers, learners may still perceive a subtle “distance” from the instructor due to reduced nonverbal cues, leading to lower social presence and cognitive engagement.

From the perspective of the Community of Inquiry (CoI) framework, the present findings can be interpreted in greater depth through a multi-dimensional presence lens. The highest gaze engagement toward the teacher region under the real teacher condition sug gests that real teachers possess the strongest effects on both social presence and teaching presence dimensions —authentic facial micro-expressions, natural eye contact, and fluid body language collectively construct the richest social cue environment and the most natural presentation of teaching behaviors. Although the high-anthropomorphism digital teacher was slightly less effective than the real teacher on these two dimensions, its more natural facial expressions and body movements effectively enhanced the visual delivery of teaching presence compared with the low-anthropomorphism condition, enabling learners to perceive a stronger sense of "instructor presence" and thereby facilitating deeper cognitive engagement. Under the low-anthropomorphism condition, stiff and repetitive movements not only diminished social presence but may also have compromised teaching presence —learners may have experienced cognitive dissonance due to the unnaturalness of the teacher's image, finding it difficult to integrate the di gital teacher's instructional behaviors into their existing social-cognitive schemas, thereby reducing attentional investment in the teacher region and overall depth of cognitive processing.

Nevertheless, the gap between digital human teachers and real teachers was not absolute. The results indicate that high-anthropomorphism teachers significantly improved learners’ visual attention and sustained focus compared to low-anthropomorphism teacher s. This implies that well-designed digital teachers can approximatereal teachers’ effectiveness in capturing learners’ attention, especially when equipped with natural movements, facial synchrony, and coherent speech gestures. Effects of anthropomorphism level on cognitive engagement (RQ2) The comparison between high-and low-anthropomorphism digital teachers revealed that anthropomorphism plays a crucial role in shaping learners’ cognitive engagement. Learners watching high-anthropomorphism teachers displayed longer fixation durations, more fixations, and higher attention ratios in the teacher AOI, indicating deeper cognitive processing and stronger social orientation. This finding aligns with prior research showing that human-like visual and behavioral cues enhance learners’ perceived realism and trust (Zhao et al., 2025; Epley et al., 2007), which in turn increases social presence and cognitive effort. High anthropomorphism may activate empathy and perceived immediacy, enabling learners to sustain mental effort during online learning.

Conversely, the low-anthropomorphism condition—characterized by repetitive and stiff gestures —may induce mild cognitive dissonance or a decrease in realism, leading to reduced attention allocation. Overly mechanical visual cues disrupt the learner’s social  schema, increasing extraneous cognitive load and thereby weakening overall engagement.

These results underscore the importance of achieving a balance between realism and comfort in designing digital human teachers. While increasing anthropomorphism enhances engagement, excessively lifelike representations may risk triggering the Uncanny Vall ey Effect (Ackermann et al., 2025), potentially distracting learners instead of fostering focus.

The findings have several implications for the design of AI-based instructional systems. First, digital human teachers with moderate to high anthropomorphism can serve as effective substitutes for real instructors in noninteractive online courses, maintaining learners’ visual focus and cognitive engagement. Second, the 1064 integration of multimodal behavioral data (e.g., eye-tracking, emotion recognition) provides an objective method to monitor learners’ engagement dynamically. Third, educators and designers should consider adaptive anthropomorphism — adjusting teacher reali sm according to learners’ preferences, course type, and content difficulty.

This study examined the effects of digital human teachers with varying levels of anthropomorphism on learners' cognitive engagement during online learning, using eye-tracking methodology to capture fine-grained indicators of visual attention and cognitive processing. The results revealed that digital human teachers generally elicited lower cognitive engagement than a real human teacher; however, higher anthropomorphism significantly improved learners' visual attention and cognitive processing compared with the low-anthropomorphism condition. These findings collectively indicate that anthropomorphic design is a key determinant of digital human teachers' instructional effectiveness.

The findings carry important practical implications, particularly regarding the trade-off between production investment and instructional outcomes. As documented in this study, producing a real teacher course requires professional studio facilities, specialized filming crews, and post-production editing, with an overall cycle of approximately two to three days; content updates typically entail repeating the entire process. In contrast, a digital human course can be generated within 30 to 60 minutes by uploading slides and a teaching script to an AI platform, and content revisions require only script modification and regeneration. Although real teachers still hold advantages in eliciting cognitive engagement, the gap between high-anthropomorphism digital teachers and real teachers was substantially smaller than that between low-anthropomorphism digital teachers and real teachers. When production efficiency and resource constraints are taken into account, high-anthropomorphism digital teachers may offer particu larly compelling value in scenarios such as rapid deployment of large-scale online courses, subject areas requiring frequent content updates, settings where instructors lack professional recording conditions, and multilingual or internationalized teaching contexts. This cost-effectiveness trade-off provides educational institutions with a practical basis for selecting instructional video production approaches that balance pedagogical quality with resource sustainability.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged, which also point to directions for future research. First, the present study relied primarily on eye-tracking indicators to measure cognitive engagement; future work should incorporate complementary behavioral and affective measures, such as physiological data and emotion recognition, to construct a more comprehensive picture of learner engagement. Second, the instructional videos used in this study were delivered in a one-way, non-interactive format; subsequent research should explore interactive learning contexts to examine how anthropomorphism influences engagement when learners can actively communicate with digital human teachers. Third, with the rapid iterative development of AI technology, the realism and expressiveness of digital human avatars are continuously improving. Longitudinal and follow-up studies are therefore warranted to track how advances in digital human technology progressively narrow the engagement gap between AI-generated and real human instructors across diverse instructional scenarios.
