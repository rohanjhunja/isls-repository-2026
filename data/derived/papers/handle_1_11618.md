# Discourse and Disability: Understanding Special Education Talk in Science

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Engaging in discourse is central to students developing scientific knowledge. However, how students with disabilities engage in productive talk remains understudied. We analyze a case of two middle school special education students as they participate in an online plate tectonics curriculum. Analysis suggests explicit discussion-based instruction sparks more productive science talk and sensemaking than side-by-side and contrary to deficit stereotypes, students with disabilities can engage in meaningful discourse around scientific ideas.

## Introduction

Students with disabilities often experience poor and inequitable learning opportunities in science leading to dismal achievement (Juergensen et al., 2023; Taylor et al., 2011). Recent attention to supporting science learning for all students brings forth considerations of how to support students with disabilities. Most scholarship around students with disabilities in science focus on the teacher, including teaching strategies, interventions, attit udes, and even preparedness. In this study, we attend to the ways students with disabilities agentively take part in their knowledge building through talk. We focus on leveraging disco urse as a means of building scientific knowledge (K elly, 2010). Building on work around progressive discours e (Bereiter, 1994) as a r epresentative of productive sensemaking talk, we seek to understand how two spe cial education students’ talk influences their scie ntific sensemaking. Specifically, we ask: how does the discourse of two special education students develop and impact their understanding of plate tectonics?

## Methods

The context of this study occurs in an inclusive middle school science classroom in the northeastern United States. Students in this class were engaging with an online plate tectonics curriculum (blinded for submission). The focal students of our study, Jimmy and Oliver (all names are pseudonyms), are two special education students working collaboratively through the curriculum. Their class  engaged in the curriculum using pairs of laptops f or approximately nine periods of eighty minutes each. We collected audio and video data using embedded la ptop cameras to capture the students’ gestures, expressi ons, and talk. We then aligned this to screen capt ure that recorded students’ actions and observations on scre en. Using case study methods (Dyson & Genishi, 2005), Authors 3 & 4 analyzed data through two rounds of coding. First, instructional time was coded when either Jimmy and Oliver were working individually and when they were working with: each other, other peers, a paraprofessional, and the teacher. We also captured  times in  which these students participated in whole-class discussion. Once we had all of these instances, we coded their discourse for sensemaking talk and the entailments of progressive discourse (openness, expansion, mutu al understanding, and empirical testability), while also examining how their ideas evolved throughout their discussions.

## Analysis and findings

Through our analysis of Jimmy and Oliver’s work, we  identified three types of discourse: side-by-side, peer-topeer, and student-teacher. For the purpose of this proposal, we highlight exam ples of side-by-side talk and peerpeer talk to represent how their talk exists and evolves. Side-by-side talk Students’ scientific conceptions were less robust before students had engaged in either peer-to-peer or teacher-topeer discourse. The students’ answers to curriculum  questions when working individually, were typicall y short and fragmented. For example, on the first day of module one, Jimmy and Oliver were instructed to work together but chose to engage independently with the activity. This resulted in Jimmy and Oliver each moving in parallel at their own pace, working on different questions. Whi le writing initial ideas to the very first question, “What will the Earth look like in 500 million years?” Jimmy realized Oliver had already moved on which led to the following dialogue with Oliver: J:  Oliver, what did you type in here? I said, ‘I think  the Earth would be more spread out than right now.’ What did you put?

O:  I thought it would have more jostling and more wate r because remember global warming.

J:  Oh.

O:  Yeah, global warming will bring the water level up and maybe the earthquakes will transfer into a new thing J:  *types* and more water because of global warming.

In this example, the both students present their ideas but do not expand on o r interrogate them. Jimmy simply adds what Oliver said to his written response, combining the individual ideas. Jimmy and Oliver do not make the connection between Jimmy’s idea of plates moving apart and Oliver’s idea about jostling. Peer-to-peer talk Continuing from the talk above, the teacher prompted the class to discuss their ideas with their tablemates. Jimmy and Oliver had previously attempted to ask their ta blemates for feedback but they were ignored. Howeve r, the teacher's prompt encouraged the tablemates to include Jimmy and Oliver and the following discussion takes place. O:  Do you guys have the thing written down?

Sophie: Yeah, it might just be like Pangaea again?

O:  Pangaea, Pangaea was like... *smushes hands together* O:  Europe is moving to it. Look at this, this is Europ e. Europe is moving 2 inches every year. If you think about it, it will be moving this  way and crashing right into Africa. So, in 500 million years from now, Africa could be here and Europe could be here so the spreading could be less.

J:  I see what you’re saying.

Prior to hearing their peer’s idea, Jimmy and Oliver agreed the continents were jostling and moving but assumed this movement was away from each other, resulting i n the continents spreading apart further. However, Oliver was able to use GPS data provided in the module sho wing the direction and speed of continental movement to support the idea presented by his tablemate that in  500 million years Earth may look like Pangaea agai n. At this point, we argue that their idea has moved towards a more sophisticated explanation of plate tectonics.

## Discussion and conclusion

The case study of Jimmy and Oliver reveals that stu dents with disabilities engage in progressive disco urse when provided opportunities to participate in the broade r classroom community. The side-by-side talk they engaged in with one another can fall short of more sophisticated forms of progressive discourse, but provide space for students to gather their thoughts and prepare for interactio ns with larger groups. In peer-to-peer talk, significantly more sensemaking and progressive discourse emerge d, facilitating more in-depth understanding. During peer-to-peer conversations, we saw the students critique each other's ideas, combine ideas, and provide evidence to support or refute their peers’ ideas (Bereiter, 1994).  Though not included in this proposal, we saw that teacher-student discussion produced the most sensemaking and progre ssive discourse from these students. This type of talk focused on one-on-one support from the teacher. Our findings look across all types of talk to suggest a continuum that as special education students engage with othe r actors in the classroom, they begin to develop mo re sophisticated or normative scientific explanations.

Our findings indicate that special education students benefit from active discourse with peers, which runs counter to the typical assumption they are unable t o participate in discussion with their peers. Our r esearch aims to interrogate and trouble deficit perspectives and myths around students with disabilities that they are limited or incapable of learning with low academic expectation s (May & Stone, 2010). Jimmy and Oliver serve as an example of how students with disabilities experience different types of talk as they make sense of plate tectonics. They provide meaningful ideas and can build knowledge by engaging in progressive discourse with one another. Teachers need to know how to best serve all of thei r students. They must seek to overcome the historically poor achievement in science, in particular for special education students (Taylor et al., 2011). In many respects the support of special education students' participation in discussion-based science classrooms is the same as it should be for all students-engaging them in conversations with peers and teach ers, which leads to sensemaking and progressive discourse in the classr oom. However, better understanding the ways in whic h students with disabilities talk with each other to build knowledge can provide insight into thoughtful  inclusion into the classroom community.
