# Renewing Socio-Cultural Theory in a Networked, Decentralized Supercapitalist Society

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Socio-cultural theory, with its emphasis on identity, culture, critique, and power, offers both an explanatory toolkit given the rise in extremism, authoritarianism, and tribalism, but also faces significant challenges, given the increasingly fractured and dynamically-changing nature of life in online worlds. Traditional metaphors of enculturating students into communities of scientific practice face external threats from a public suspicious toward social institutions. This session asks what socio-cultural theory might have to offer for understanding the current moment, and how this current state of the world may be pushing back on our understanding of socio-cultural theory. This session offers three papers and a robust discussion session by four leading researcher / theorist / practitioners from socio-cultural traditions. Each paper offers a look at how learning is occurring in a networked, digital age, with community partners that include Homeboy Industries, the Network of Academic Esports Federations, and with Indigenous Education Partners. This session is particularly focused on how macro trends across culture, specifically occurring through supercapitalist economic and online cultural structures contribute to cultural logics operating upon us. Learning from this perspective is a process of mediation, whereby people internalize the language, tools, and concepts of a culture (Daniels, Cole, & Wertsch, 2007). Appropriation occurs as people make such tools their own, expanding their application tonew situations (Wertsch, 1997). Play, the creation of imagined, liminal contexts in which learners can engage within a zone of proximal development, is crucial for understanding rule systems, appropriating representational systems, and employing imagination toward expanding one’s experiences beyond that which has been directly experienced (Vygotsky, 1967). Schools may be important locations of academic learning, but experiences outside of school largely form the experiential foundation for future understanding, provide social identification and belonging, shape the identities and dispositions we bring toward school, and govern how we mobilize such identities outside of school (Cole, 1999; Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003; Wertsch, 1997). Cultural historical learning theorists look beyond individual or familial interactions and focus on how learning occurs in complex settings, including workplaces, classrooms, and after school settings (Cole, 1999). Cultural Historical Activity Theorists (CHAT) are particularly interested in studying learning as it occurs in complex activity, stretched across people, their tools, and social institutions (including their formal and informal rules and divisions of labor, (Engeström, 2014; Hutchins, 1995). Such analyses reveal the complex social achievement embodied by such professional performances, and outcomes (learning) is shaped by social context. Values, identity, and culture co-constitute emergent understandings; competent action in the world involves identifying resources, employing tools, navigating social structures and institutions, appropriating formal and informal rules, and coordinating with complex networks of divisions of labor (Gee, 1990; Lave & Wenger, 1991). A defining feature of socio-cultural work is learning through participation as a social achievement, recruiting identities from learners as “certain kinds of people” with dispositions, values, and ways of being in the world (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Nowhere is such identity formation clearer than through language, where such work meaning-making work is coordinated, negotiated, and manifested into action (Gee, 1990; Wertsch, 1997). Learning scientists within the socio-cultural tradition have turned toward informal learning spaces, where complex learning configurations can be designed, according to specific values, which might become habits of mind for those engaged in such programs. The 5 th Dimension project (Cole, 2006) uses a game-based metaphor 2504 to engage kids in game-based learning activities, under the mentorship of college students, who themselves gained learning experiences toward their development as teachers, driven by values of diversity. The Migrant Student Leadership Institute created a collective Third Space in which every day and institutional literacy practices are mobilized for critical social thought, using literacy practices to link historical practices, current situations, and possible futures (Gutiérrez, 2008). FUSE Studio creates paths for engaging youth in STEAM practices in after-school settings by challenges based designed in response to youth interests (Stevens et al., 2016). These are but a few design-based research programs that build on socio-cultural learning theory but propose learning environments based on specific underlying social commitments or values that are orthogonal, if not contradictory to those of school, which are often based on sorting. Underlying such socio-cultural approaches to learning is a commitment to defining and articulating underlying values, although recently, values driving such efforts (diversity, equality of opportunity, social justice) are under assault. As Nasir (2006) and colleagues write, “The dominant uptake of sociocultural theory in the learning sciences has treated social interaction primarily as a means to cognitive ends

— even where the theory itself contains resources that point elsewhere.” (p.489) Collaboration scaffolds thinking;

dialogue mediates development; joint activity builds  knowledge. And while these broad frameworks have been enormously productive over the last fifty years, they may well have left us theoretically underprepared for the present moment.

My previous work reflects this predilection, with social interaction figuring into analyses of multiplayer games as a potent means through which deeper engagement, richer diversity of content, and more complex cognition become possible. What that framing r enders invisible, however, is sociability itself — fellowship, civility, connectedness, trust, kinship — as an outcome worth theorizing and designing for explicitly. In today’s post-pandemic, digitally mediated society where platform capitalism (Srnicek, 2016) has systematically hollowed out the conditions for human connection, this gap has become increasingly urgent. We need a steadier eye focused on sociability not as a means toward some greater cognitive end but as the fundamental substrate of democratic civil life, one requiring careful cultivation and conservation. In this talk, I detail my recent work that begins in investigation into the rise of toxicity, hate, and extremism among adolescent and young adult players in commercial online multiplayer games and shifts to a counterpoint example of a game-based program in San Quentin Prison showing markedly opposite outcomes. In one of the oldest and most notorious prisons in the US, an incarcerated-led gaming event appears to be increasing safety and mental health and reducing hostility and hate. Here, I sketch out mechanisms by which games spontaneously produce such contrasting outcomes, drawing on Goffman’s (1961) analysis of social encounters, Tronick's (1989) rupture-and-repair model of dyadic affect regulation, Collins’ (2004) notion of interaction ritual chains, and Allport’s (1954) contact theory.

This strange counterpoint — fellowship emerging under conditions of maximum constraint — foregrounds what standard sociocultural accounts of gameplay in the learning sciences tend to overlook: that sociability itself, not cognition, can be theorized as a p rimary outcome in its own right, and that doing so may yield insights of considerable consequence for today's technologized and deeply atomized social landscape. Survivance: Indigenous thought for restorying relationships with technology Rose Bigheart O’Leary In the current socio-political atmosphere in the United States and Canada, many area a ware of the toxicity and misinformation that permeate spheres facilitated by internet and computerized communications: social media, proliferation of news and information sources (some reputable and others not), fictional entertainment, such as video games and television and, sadly, some educational spaces as well  perpetuate harmful stories. Critiques of the narrowing of diversity of thought and calls for a genuine exchange of ideas foreclosed upon by these phenomena have been called for, but the majority of stories perpetuated within digital culture are not authored with indigenous values (de Ridder, 2021; Muhammad & Mathew, 2022). H ow do we move beyond just critique and critical evaluation  onto redesigning of futures we want to have?  I argue that to place this burden solely on indigenous peoples is mistaken, and that the project of desettling media narratives can be undertaken by everyone. Survivance is a concept originally coined by the philosopher Jaques Derrida  (2011) has since been repurposed by Indigenous scholars such as Gerald Vizenor (2008) or Michelle Brown (2021) to mean something akin to not mere survival but a connection to ancestors and descendants that insists also on enjoying life and making or remaking media to perpetuate worldviews (ontologies, epistemologies, and axiologies) grounded in Indigenous thought (O’Leary, 2018). Indigenous Peoples of Turtle Island  have been navigating settler-colonial impositions via survivance in spite of, and sometimes through deft management of, the channels of colonial-capitalism for over 500 years. This research is  grounded in the Turtle Island context, but particularly the Osage (Washazhe), Cherokee (Tsalagi), Quapaw, and Micmac (Mi’kmaq) background of the author. By examining these proven survivance strategies that stand in opposition to, or sometimes just apart from, colonialism, particularly as they function with story, we might remake relationships with technology to better serve a decolonized future. This critical examination of digitally-based media environments begins with desettling embedded colonial assumptions  (implicit colonialism) and regenerating narratives of non-colonialism or decolonizing (regenarrativising). The goal of this examination is to suggest a survivance strategy for reshaping our relationships with technology, which is then articulated into a curricular model. This survivance model has been applied to HCI 2509 (O’Leary & Turner, 2020) and curricular design in LatinX informal learning communities (Anderson-Coto & O’Leary, 2021). Early versions of this model emphasize the importance of enacting care as a central driving pedagogical concern. Similar to findings reported by Martinez (this proposal), maintaining a primacy for communal and individual care, specifically through elevating feeding, emotional care, personal advice, or professional check-ins. Using counterstorying to desettle common media framings, youth developed unique digital tools and increased a sense of agency and authorship across sessions held during the COVID-19 pademic (Anderson-Coto & O’Leary, 2021).

Working with indigenious youth, more explicit desettling practices are essential for  examining assumptions that are normative to Western colonial conceptions but may not be to others and then desettling them to make room for other ontoepistemaxiologies (Bang et al., 2013). The primary example for desettling comes from early grade science classrooms when the concept of living vs non-living was critically questioned by learners who have cultural concepts of energy transfer as indicating life rather than the presence of functioning respiratory systems (Bang et al., 2013). Desettling can also involve  questioning the framing of non-western preindustrial societies as necessarily “primitive” simply because their focus of technological development was different from Western colonizing cultures (Bang et al., 2013; Graeber & Wengrow, 2021). Regenerative narratives  or “ regenarratives” are the creation or revitalization of non-colonial or decolonial stories, epistemologies, and frameworks that can replace or bolster previously singular colonial-capitalist perspectives (Archibald, 2008; Brown, 2021; hooks, 1994; Mthoko et al., 2022; O’Leary, 2024). These critical reflections and repositionings are necessary when attempting to adapt systems and technologies that were built in the context of colonialcapitalism to shift to meet the needs of decolonial futures, otherwise the replication of oppressions, unintentional though they may be, will be continued (Benjamin, 2019; Bhabha, 1990; hooks, 1994). Games, social media and television are some of the primary sites of technology engagement, and as sites of leisure and joy, can be function  as survivance-oriented media, making an ideal site for culture work  (Dienlin & Johannes, 2020).  Structurally, there is an obvious connection to survival  written into many common game dynamics; in minoritized communities we are so often characterized through deficit frames, and in our urgency to describe the inequity we are so often told by agents of authority does not exist, we often ourselves reinforce these same frames (Medina 2021; Patton et al, 2019). We, as minoritized persons, also often fall into the trap of crowing about resilience and grit or other buzzwords for how surviving something can “make” one strong (Mahdiani & Ungar, 2021). Narratives not centered around being made strong by the hardship, but rather, surviving because they were already Strong suggest new possibilities. Perhaps when we consider our children and the future generations we don’t have to repeat these bootstrapping mythologies and instead can give them coping and survival skills along with permission to have joy and rest. T he crucible is not the only way to build resilience. Perhaps we can fortify them with mind, body, and spirit nutrients that make them feel confident and whole and loved and they will learn survivance not just survival (Brown, 2021; Brocato et al, 2021; Vizenor, 2008).  This paper closes with a model for survivance stories through games.

Socio-cultural theory and the crisis of contested meaning James Paul Gee At its heart, Socio-Cultural Theory (SCT) argues that meanings are not inherent in objects but are social constructions. Words like "tomato" or "democracy" do not point to a single, mind-independent essence; instead, their definitions shift based on the goals and contexts of the people using them. Even a physical object like a tomato is subject to "contextual truth." To a cook, a tomato is a vegetable. To the U.S. Supreme Court in 1893 (Nix v. Hedden), it was also a vegetable, for trade and tariff purposes. To a scientist, it is a fruit. Yet even within botany, classification is fluid. One researcher might define "tomato" narrowly (the cultivated species), while another uses an evolutionary cluster. A plant breeder, meanwhile, defines it by "crossability." The linguistic boundaries change too. Spanish speakers often categorize tomatillos as "tomatoes" (tomate verde), whereas English speakers treat them as a separate category entirely, reflecting a difference in genus (Physalis vs. Solanum) that one culture prioritizes and another ignores. When we move from botany to politics, the stakes rise. "Democracy" is what philosophers call an essentially contested concept. For thinkers like Milton Friedman or Peter Thiel, democracy is often viewed through the lens of individual liberty; a system that  places heavy constraints on private wealth cannot, for them, be seen as a democracy. Conversely, for many others, a core feature of democracy is the ability of the collective to determine how economic resources are shared. Where one person sees "money in politics" as a total negation of democracy, the Supreme Court has seen it as a protected form of speech. These differing meanings exist within Discourses —integrated ways of being, acting, and valuing that define specific social groups. Law, botany, and cooking are all Discourses, and they each have a different viewpoint on tomatoes. As Donald Schön has sugges ted, these Discourses rely on "appreciative systems"— the internal filters that tell a group what is worth valuing and how to evaluate "truth." Ultimately, Discourses provide more than just definitions; they provide social belonging. Our need to belong to a  tribe often dictates which definitions we adopt, making meaning a badge of identity rather than a neutral observation. The challenge for SCT is its alignment with Nominalism, the philosophical view that "universals" or "essences" do not exist, and that only individual objects and the names we give them are real. Since the 17th century, critics have viewed Nominalism as a civilizational "wrong turn." To many today, the idea that meaning is purely a social construct is terrifying. They argue that it denies a mind-independent reality accessible to reason. 2512 If "better" and "worse" are just social constructions of a specific Discourse, our capacity for moral judgment is paralyzed.

For those who crave a "fixed" world, SCT feels like a degradation of society. Because their sense of identity and belonging is tied to the idea of fixed essences ("kinds" —see Genesis 1:24 –25) and fixed truths, any evidence provided by SCT is viewed not as a "theory," but as an attack on their existence. Traditional SCT has a blind spot. In its focus on how we construct reality through language and social interaction, it often overvalues academic evidence. It assumes people should or would prioritize "truth" over "tribe." It fails to account for the psycho logical distress caused by a world where everything is "fluid." From an evolutionary standpoint, a shared (even if "incorrect") belief that binds a group together is often more effective for survival than a nuanced, academic understanding of social construction. To speak to the current moment, SCT must move beyond simply explaining how we construct meaning. It must address the deep-seated human need for the very "certainty" that the theory seeks to deconstruct. Large Language Models (LLMs) occupy a strange, almost ghostly position in this debate. They are, in many ways, the ultimate Nominalist machine. They do not possess an "appreciative system" rooted in a biological need for belonging. Instead, they function a s cross-Discourse translators. Because they have been trained on the vast breadth of the human record—from botanical journals to Supreme Court briefings to political manifestos — they can inhabit these different "worlds" of meaning simultaneously. However, the existence of LLMs heightens "Nominalist anxiety." To a critic of SCT, LLMs are proof of their worst fears: a system that treats "democracy" or "value" as nothing more than a statistical probability of tokens, devoid of a soul or an objective moral anchor. By reflecting the social construction of meaning so efficiently, LLMs risk making the world feel even more "fluid" and untethered. Their role, therefore, is double-edged: they are a tool that can help humans navigate and understand the boundaries of their different Discourses, but they also serve as a mirror that forces society to confront just how much of its "Objective Truth" is actually built on the shifting sands of collective agreement.

The pressing task for contemporary SCT, then, is to shift its focus toward social alignment in conversation—not alignment as agreement or the convergence on "truth," but as the capacity to live together in a shared society and on an imperiled planet. This means studying how conversation can bridge the value differences that divide Discourses without collapsing them into one another. LLMs offer a remarkable laboratory for exactly this work. Because they can move fluidly across Discourse boundaries, they are uniquely suited to testing how conversational strategies can foster genuine mutual understanding—without tipping into echo chambers —while opening space for new learning on all sides.
