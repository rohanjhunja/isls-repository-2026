# How Teachers Learn by Designing AI-Ethics Scenarios Using an LLM-Supported Authoring Tool

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how teachers learn by designing AI ethics scenarios using a customized LLM-supported authoring tool. Seventeen pre-and in-service teachers engaged in dilemma-based scenario design within the Learning by Design framework through int eractive dialogue with the LLM. Data from prompt logs, think-aloud sessions, surveys, interviews, and reflections revealed diverse prompting patterns. Teachers alternated between seeking guidance and generating, evaluating, and refining ideas. Through this process, they developed a deeper understanding of AI ethics, greater confidence, and more student-centered perspectives, viewing AI as a collaborative design partner. The findings suggest that iterative prompting and scenario design provide an effective pathway for teacher learning and highlight the potential of LLM-supported environments for professional growth in AI-integrated education.

As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes increasingly integrated into everyday life, educators need to prepare students not only to understand how AI works but also to critically examine its ethical and social implications (Long & Magerko, 2020; Touretzky et al., 2019). Issues such as accuracy, fairness, explainability, privacy, and transparency require students to reason through competing values. However, teachers face persistent challenges in designing such learning experiences, including limited confidenc e and pedagogical knowledge, as well as difficulties adapting abstract resources to diverse classroom contexts (Lin & Van Brummelen, 2021; Walsh et al., 2023). To address this challenge, we draw on the Learning by Design (LBD) framework, which conceptualizes design not as a mere preparatory stage for teaching but as a process of learning that unfolds through iterative cycles of planning, enactment, and reflection (Cope & Kalantzis, 2015). Through this process, teachers generate ideas, deepen their understanding of subject matter, and develop design capacity by selecting contextually appropriate pedagogical strategies, while also enhancing their motivation, confidence, and sense of professional competence (Drits-Esser & Stark, 2015; Mor & Mogilevsky, 2013). Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) suggest new possibilities for supporting such design processes. Teachers can use LLMs to broaden idea generation and reduce time for instructional design, while also receiving adaptive feedback aligned with their experience, suggesting the potential to support instructional design competence (Choi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). However, prior work has largely emphasized efficiency rather than how LLMs may cultivate teachers’ design capacity, highlighting the need to examine their role as catalysts for professional growth. Building on these perspectives, we designed an LLM-supported authoring environment to scaffold teachers in designing AI ethics scenarios centered on value tensions and multiple perspectives. Given the limited understanding of how teachers interact with such tools and how teacher learning occurs through these processes, this study aims to provide insights into how teachers engage in LLM-supported learning for designing dilemma scenarios in AI ethics education. The present study is guided by the following research questions:

• RQ1: What patterns characterize teachers’ prompting and rationales when designing dilemma scenarios

on AI ethics?

• RQ2: How do teachers develop instructional design capacities and AI ethics awareness through the

design process using the LLM-supported authoring tool?

## Research context and participants

This study is part of a larger project on designing an AI ethics dialogue game. A total of 17 teachers (8 in-service, 9 pre-service; 5 males, 12 females) participated in this study. Participating teachers specialized in diverse subject areas, including Korean, English, social studies, history, computer science, music, and science.  All had prior experience using generative AI tools for lesson preparation, but most had no experience teaching AI ethics. 1557 Chatbot-based scenario design tool We developed an LLM-supported authoring tool to scaffold teachers in designing AI ethics dilemma scenarios. The tool design was informed by five principles  (see Figure 1): (1) a dilemma-based structure emphasizing conflicting ethical values (Sanderson et al., 2023), (2) story-based gamification to support envisioning AIintegrated futures and design ing “flipping” narratives that expose the unintended consequences of different choices (Long & Magerko, 2020), (3) role-based design to incorporate multiple stakeholders’ perspectives, (4) an AI chatbot facilitating Socratic questioning and reflective human –AI co-construction (Paul & Elder, 2007; Xu et al., 2025), and (5) a five-step modular design process guiding scenario construction. ## Procedure and data analysis

Participants used the chatbot-based tool to design an AI ethics dilemma scenario through step-by-step prompting and applied it within a game interface. Data included prompt logs, designed scenarios, and semi-structured interviews with retrospective think-aloud (RT A; vanden Haak et al., 2003), which captured participants’ reasoning and decision-making during their interactions with the chatbot. In addition, five in-service teachers implemented their scenarios in classrooms and provided follow-up reflections through interviews For RQ1 on prompting patterns and rationale, teachers’ prompts were analyzed using a codebook adapted from Carl and Dignam (2025). A hybrid approach combining deductive and inductive coding was employed to allow new utterance types to emerge (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006). The final codebook included categories such as idea proposal  (IP), request for idea  (RI), information seeking  (IN), revision  (RV), critique  (CR), acceptance (AT), and choice (CH). Interrater reliability was established between two coders on 20% of the data (Cohen’s κ = 0.88), and the remaining data were coded using the finalized coding scheme. A hierarchical cluster data were qualitatively analyzed to explain teachers’ rationales and decision-making processes underlying these patterns. For RQ2  on teachers’ instructional design capacities and AI ethics awareness, all utterances were analyzed thematically following Braun & Clarke  (2006), using LBD as the theoretical framework. This analysis explored how teachers strengthened their instructional design c apacities and AI ethics understanding through scenario design and implementation. Data included interviews and post-surveys, along with reflections and follow-up interviews from teachers who implemented their scenarios in classrooms.  Triangulation across these data sources was used to enhance trustworthiness.

Code frequencies and cluster analysis of prompting patterns Five prompting patterns were identified through clustering analysis, ordered as B–A–E–D–C to reflect increasing diversity and complexity in prompting behaviors  (see Figure 2). Cluster B (I3 –I5, P1) represented the simplest pattern, combining idea proposal (IP) with acceptance (AT). Teachers proposed ideas, often based on personal experience, and accepted the chatbot ’s elaborations with minimal revision, as the chatbot ’s responses aligned closely with their intentions: “It did exactly what I meant, so I thought there was noneed to change it.” (I4) Cluster 1558 A (I1–I2) extended this pattern by adding requests for ideas (RI) and choices  (CH). Teachers initially relied on the chatbot  for idea generation, but gradually took a more active role, proposing ideas themselves: “I’d been passive earlier just choosing among options. For the detailed story, I wanted to write it myself.” (I1) Cluster E (P4–P9, I7–I8) advanced further through critiques  (CR) and revision (RV). Teachers critically reconstructed the chatbot’s responses by simplifying wording, adjusting details, and clarifying reasoning. They also sought the chatbot’s ideas to deepen or verify thinking (“I’d already thought about accuracy and bias but wanted to see what else might emerge,” P6) and selected topics to challenge both themselves and their students (“I wanted this to be something not only students but I myself could think about,” I7). Cluster D (P2– P3) resembled E but included more information-seeking (IN) to clarify concepts, such as asking whether “fairness” was an ethical issue (P2) or checking the chatbot’s suggestions. (P3) Cluster C (I6) formed a distinct, less integrated pattern dominated by request for ideas (RI) with minimal elaboration. ## Expanded understanding of AI ethics and educational significance

Many participants, especially pre-service teachers, reported that interacting with the chatbot broadened their understanding of AI ethics by introducing unfamiliar concepts and clarifying abstract ideas. The chatbot helped them move beyond superficial knowledge to a more concrete understanding of the field (P2, I2, interview). As one participant noted, AI ethics had previously felt “abstract and distant” (I2). Some teachers encountered new value conflicts and expressed a desire to explore them further, while others shifted their perspectives through dialogue, for example, reconsidering previously held views on AI-generated works (I1, I8, P8, interviews). Beyond content learning, constructing and revising scenarios prompted teachers to engage more deliberately in moral reasoning, including considering consequences and trade-offs. One teacher emphasized that the process required them to “consider counter-effects” rather than passively accept ideas (I7, post-survey). This reflective engagement also led teachers to recognize that designing dilemmas could support deeper learning for students compared to simply discussing given cases (I1, P5, P8, interview). At the same time, teachers began to view AI ethics as an urgent, real-world issue rather than a distant topic (I2, interview). This awareness was further strengthened during classroom implementation, where the level of student engagement was unexpectedly high (I1, follow-up interview). Observing this engagement increased teachers’ motivation to continue such practices, as students were seen to “engage and take it meaningfully” (I7, interview). Enhanced confidence and pedagogical shift toward student-centered practices Through scenario design, participants, mostly in-service teachers, moved from viewing AI ethics education as abstract to how it could be applied in a meaningful way.  Rather than simple debates or lectures, they identified the value of narrative, branching outcomes, and “flip” elements in supporting ethical reasoning (I1, P5, interview; I6, reflection). During classroom implementation, their students showed higher engagement, more active participation, and greater openness to diverse perspectives than expected, leading teachers to recognize the importance of giving students space to think and share ideas (I4, I6,  I8 follow-up interviews). As one teacher noted, the approach helped students “engage” while supporting critical thinking (I7, follow-up interview). Teachers also recognized possible curricular connections that made AI ethics more teachable within their disciplines. Science teachers, for instance, linked this activity to socio-scientific issues or research ethics that had previously felt difficult to address (I1, I8, interviews). Some also proposed concrete ways for application, such as having students design dilemmas with C hatGPT (I1, follow-up interview), using ChatGPT to compare multiple perspectives (I7, interview), or linking scenarios to authentic ethical cases in science (I8, interview). Importantly, this growing confidence extended beyond AI ethics to broader pedagogical change. Teachers reported that moving 1559 away from  lecture-centered routines toward more participatory approaches, reflecting on the need to apply “different instructional approaches” in their teaching (I5, interview) Improved design capacity and perspective for generative AI-assisted lesson design Through scenario design with the chatbot, teachers shifted from using generative AI for limited tasks (e.g., generating examples) to recognizing it as a design partner that supports richer learning experiences (P7, I5, interviews). Chatbots’ scaffolding helped transform abstract ideas into concrete scenarios and introduced perspectives beyond teachers’ own thinking, while the structured, step-by-step process supported idea development, confidence, and cross-subject application (I3, I5, interview). Some also highlighted that access to multiple pedagogical chatbots could enable them to experiment with a wider range of instructional approaches that are often difficult to implement due to time and preparation constraints (I7, follow-up interview). This optimism, however, raises questions about overreliance: Is it acceptable for teachers to rely  on chatbot support in lesson design? Closer observation suggested otherwise. Some teachers at first relied heavily on the chatbot’s prompts. But as they continued the design process, many began to understand the logic behind the step-by-step structure and gained confidence to adapt or even create their own ideas within ChatGPT (I1, followup interview; P3, P9, interviews). This shift suggests that chatbot s’ scaffolding not only supports initial lesson design but also helps teachers internalize the design process and develop sustainable design capacity. Teachers also emphasized that working with chatbots differs from using pre-made materials, framing it as a collaborative process in which the chatbot provides structure and ideas while the teacher ultimately shapes and implements them (I1, I7, follow-up interview. At the same time, they acknowledged the ongoing risk of uncritical acceptance, cautioning against simply “copy-pasting” AI-generated outputs (I7, P7, P9; interviews). Envisioning community-based collaboration to extend Learning by Design Additionally, teachers proposed extending Learning by Design (LBD) through community-based collaboration, emphasizing that peer interaction can strengthen instructional design capacity (Huizinga et al., 2014; So & Kim, 2009). They highlighted the value of accessing exemplary scenarios, exchanging feedback, and engaging in mutual critique to refine their lesson design (I7, I8, P4, interview).  Beyond feedback and exemplary sharing, teachers envisioned technology-supported collaboration, such as real-time co-design spaces and communities for sharing prompting strategies, to support collective growth and sustained professional learning (P2, P9, interview). Some also noted that awareness of peers implementing similar lessons could motivate continued practice (P7, interview).

Teachers’ prompting interactions suggest that the LLM-supported environment functioned as a Learning by Design space, where iterative cycles of idea generation, critique, and revision supported the articulation and refinement of ethical reasoning, consistent with prior works (Cope & Kalantzis, 2015; Drits-Esser & Stark, 2015; Mor & Mogilevsky, 2013). At the same time, Request for Ideas and Information Seeking enabled teachers to explore diverse ideas and information in AI ethics, while Acceptance and Choice reflected practical considerations, such as decision-making processes and the efficiency benefits of LLMs (Choi et al., 2024). However, these patterns also revealed moments of uncritical acceptance, in line with concerns in prior studies about overreliance on AI-generated outputs (Yang & Stefaniak, 2025).  As teachers progressed through design and implementation, they reported that designing such scenarios prompted them to reconsider their instructional approaches  (Hood,

2015) and adopt more student-centered practices, while also enhancing their motivation and confidence (Drits-Esser & Stark, 2015). Theoretically, this study contributes to expanding the framework of Learning by Design (LBD) into LLM-supported teacher learning contexts. Whereas previous LBD research emphasized collaborative, human-centered reflection and iterative cycles of design, enactment, and reflection, the present study demonstrates that similar reflective cycles can also emerge through teachers’ dialogic interactions with LLMs. At the same time, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the study’s context-specific setting, small and relatively novice sample, limited subject diversity, and single-iteration classroom implementation, which constrain generalizability.  Future research should examine more diverse participants and extended design cycles, while refining scaffolding that balances AI-led and teacher-led prompting to support both idea expansion and critical reasoning (Xu et al., 2025).
