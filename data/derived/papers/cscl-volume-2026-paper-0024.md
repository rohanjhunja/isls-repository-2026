# Bridging Curriculum and Creativity: NLP-Powered Semantic Analysis for Knowledge Building

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Knowledge Building is a collaborative approach that treats ideas as improvable knowledge artifacts and engages learners in advancing collective understanding. To support this, educators need analytics that reveal deep discourse patterns. This paper introduces a semantic analysis tool for Knowledge Building that uses natural language processing to illuminate conceptual dynamics in student-generated content. The tool takes a glossary of curriculum terms and student notes, preprocesses text with spaCy, and computes semantic similarities using a transformer model. By analyzing word-to-term relationships, it identifies curriculum coverage, related concepts beyond official terms, and evidence of conceptual growth. We outline the tool’s design, demonstrate its outputs through network visualizations, and discuss implications for formative assessment, curriculum design, and longitudinal tracking, positioning it as a flexible analytic for studying and fostering knowledge-creating discourse.

Knowledge Building (KB) is an educational theory and pedagogy developed by Scardamalia and Bereiter (2006) that emphasizes the creation and continual improvement of ideas within a community of knowledge builders. In a KB community, students collectively ta ke responsibility for advancing shared knowledge —treating ideas as communal objects to refine, rather than simply as personal opinions (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). This approach, going beyond traditional knowledge acquisition, emphasizes on engaging lea rners in practices akin to those of scientific and creative communities (e.g. theory-building, questioning, explaining) (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). Over decades of research, twelve KB principles (e.g. Community Knowledge, Idea Improvement, Epistemic Agency, Embedded and Transformative Assessment) have been articulated to guide classroom practice (Scardamalia, 2002). These principles underscore that students should not only learn content but also engage in the higher-order process of creating new knowledge and assessing their collective progress. For example, embedded and transformative assessment  calls for assessment that is woven into the learning activities, helping the community reflect and improve in real time, rather than external, summative evaluation. Such theoretical foundations highlight the need for tools that make the often invisible dynamics of idea development visible and actionable to learners and educators.

Central to Knowledge Building is Knowledge Forum (KF), an online environment purpose-built to support knowledge creation through engaging students in asynchronous discourse where they post “notes” to share ideas, build on peers’ contributions, and co-develop theories (Scardamalia, 2004). Knowledge Forum itself has an evolving suite of built-in analytic tools aimed at “making thinking visible”  for participants (Burtis, 1998; Zhang et al., 2007). These include basic measures of online activity (e.g. notes written, read, and built-upon) as well as more content-oriented tools. However, standard Knowledge Forum analytics (e.g. post counts, reply counts, word cloud) offer only very limited insight into the quality of discourse. Teachers cannot easily discern, for instance, which key concepts have been grasped or how creatively students are connecting ideas, simply from quantity-based metrics. Prior research in Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) and Knowledge Building communities has therefore stressed the need for more advanced analytics attuned to discourse quality and knowledge dynamics (e.g., Jung et al., 2025). In line with this need, a variety of analytic tools have been developed to support embedded assessment in Knowledge Building environments (Zhu & Kim, 2017).  Several analytic and visualization tools have contributed to this goal. However, each existing tool has specific focuses and limitations. For example, Comparative Word clouds (Resendes et al., 2015) provide quick overviews of frequently used terms and can highlight alignment between student and expert vocabulary, but they rely on exact matches and ignore context or synonyms. KBDeX  (Oshima et al., 2012) is another analysis tool that uses socio-semantic network analysis to visualize collaboration patterns and conceptual clusters but connects words only by co-occurrence rather than meaning. Similarly, Vocabulary Analyzer  and Lexical Analyzer  (Hong et al., 2015; Laferrière et al., 2016) track vocabulary growth using curriculum-based lists, offering valuable but limited insights since they miss emergent or rephrased ideas.

Overall, none of the tools directly measures semantic similarity  or the presence of conceptually related terms that are not exact matches. In a KB context, where students are encouraged to use their own words and even coin new terms or analogies, this is a crucial gap. Our work responds to this call by introducing a semantic analysis 139 tool that can flexibly detect conceptual connections. By leveraging modern Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques, the tool complements existing analytics: it goes beyond counting specific words to identifying when different words mean similar things, thereby revealing latent thematic threads and conceptual progress in the discourse.

Description of the semantic tool The proposed semantic analysis tool is designed to examine word-to-term similarities within Knowledge Building discourse. By focusing on words and their semantic relationships, the tool captures the subtle ways students express key ideas —even when they do not use the exact curriculum terms. This makes it possible to recognize both direct alignment with disciplinary vocabulary and creative extensions that signal the emergence of new ideas. Indeed, this tool goes beyond identifying exact matches: it uses a transformer-based model to compute semantic similarity scores, revealing how closely student language relates to glossary terms and how alternative expressions may still connect to core concepts. In this way, the analysis provides insight into both the depth of curriculum engagement and the diversity of student contributions that drive collective knowledge advancement. In the sections that follow, we describe the tool’s inputs, the natural language processing pipeline, the semantic similarity computations via transformers, and the overall conceptual architecture that supports these analyses.

Data input The tool begins with two inputs: a glossary of  terms and the set of student notes written in Knowledge Forum. The glossary represents the target  conceptual space —for example, a set of curriculum keywords, scientific concepts, teacher-defined “big ideas” for the unit, or a collaboratively built class glossary. The student notes are the raw texts contributed by students over some period, representing the community’s collective discourse. Each note typically contains a title and a body of text written by a student, possibly with quotes from other notes (buildons) and scaffold supports. Preprocessing with spaCy Both datasets are cleaned and prepared using spaCy (Honnibal, 2017), a widely used language analysis toolkit for cleaning and lemmatization. During the lemmatization step, words are normalized into their base forms (e.g., “recycling” → “recycle”,  “trees” → “tree”) so that different grammatical variations are count ed together. This step is crucial because students may use different grammatical forms of the same word. Lemmatization reduces words to their base form, ensuring that our analysis counts them as the same concept when appropriate. Semantic modeling with transformers At the core of the tool’s capability is its ability to compute semantic similarity scores between words/terms in student notes and terms in the glossary. Rather than relying on simple keyword or root matches, the tool employs a modern transformer-based model (Wang et. al., 2017) named “ all-MiniLM”  that captures deeper conceptual relationships.

Transformer-based models represent a major advance in natural language processing, enabling systems to capture the contextual meaning of words and phrases rather than treating them as isolated tokens. At the core of transformers is the self-attention mechanism  (Vaswani, et al., 2017), which allows the model to weigh the importance of each word in a sequence relative to others. This means that the representation of a word like cell will differ depending on whether it appears in a biology or prison context. By stacking multiple layers of attention and feed-forward networks, transformers learn rich, high-dimensional embeddings that capture semantic relationships between terms.

The tool described in this paper employs all-MiniLM, a lightweight but powerful transformer from the Sentence-BERT family (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019). MiniLM achieves strong performance in semantic similarity tasks while being computationally efficient. It maps words, phrases, or sentences into dense vector spaces where semantically related terms are placed close together. For example, “habitat”  and “home” appear near each other in the embedding space, even though they are different words. This property make s all-MiniLM particularly suited for analyzing student discourse in Knowledge Building: it enables the detection of conceptual alignment with glossary terms even when students use paraphrases, synonyms, or creative expressions. In this way, transformer-based embeddings go beyond exact word matching to reveal the deeper conceptual dynamics of discourse.

Leveraging transformers will enable the tool to not only identify when students use glossary terms directly but also detect when they are discussing related or conceptually adjacent ideas. For example, even if a student does not use the glossary term photosynthesis directly, the tool can still capture their engagement with the 140 concept. If their notes include related terms such as chlorophyll  or carbon dioxide, these words will appear close to photosynthesis in the embedding space. This proximity reveals that the student is implicitly addressing the glossary concept, even without explicitly naming it. From an educational perspective, this semantic approach makes visible both curriculum-aligned understanding and emergent connections beyond the glossary, helping instructors and students see how discourse reflects not only formal knowledge but also the creative and exploratory aspects of knowledge building.

Similarity computation The tool compares every glossary term with student words and terms in two ways:

1. Exact Matches – If a student uses a glossary term directly, the connection is marked as a perfect match.

2. Semantic Matches – If students use their own words that are close in meaning to glossary terms (e.g.,

“bugs” instead of “insects”), the tool computes a similarity score indicating how close these words are conceptually.

Cosine similarity is a standard similarity score that ranges from-1 to 1, where 1 means the vectors point in the exact same direction (identical meaning in this context) and 0 means orthogonal (unrelated). For example, suppose one glossary term is “habitat”  and a student note contains the word “home”  in the context of animals. The transformer  model might place “habitat”  and “home” reasonably close, yielding a similarity score-sayaround 0.7. Filtering and visualization To avoid overwhelming detail, the tool filters results using a similarity threshold so that only the most meaningful matches are displayed. Crucially, this threshold is customizable, allowing educators to adapt the analysis to different knowledge building goals. A higher threshold foregrounds only the strongest concept-to-concept links, helping instructors and students identify the core ideas that anchor collective inquiry. It also highlights the extent to which students are working within curriculum expectations, offering a focused view of how discourse aligns with established learning goals. By contrast, a lower threshold opens the analysis to a broader range of connections, capturing tentative, exploratory, and peripheral ideas that may not yet align tightly with the curriculum language. From a knowledge building perspective, this looser view is valuable because it makes visible the creative extensions, emergent concepts, diverse ways of expressing understanding, and even ideas that go beyond curriculum expectations —all of which can fuel deeper discourse and collective progress. In this way, adjusting the threshold functions as a pedagogical choice: “zooming in” to highlight consolidation, or “zooming out” to encourage exploration and idea diversity.

The retained connections are displayed in a network graph with an intuitive color system: ● � Red nodes  represent glossary terms (i.e., curriculum concepts) that were not explicitly used by students in their written notes. These terms are present in the curriculum or conceptual framework provided by the instructor but do not appear verbatim in the students' discourse. Red nodes help identify potential conceptual gaps in students' engagement with core ideas. ● � Green nodes  represent glossary terms that students explicitly used in their notes. This indicates direct alignment between the curriculum and student contributions. The shift from red to green occurs dynamically in the graph when a glossary term is found in the stude nt-generated content. These green nodes make it easy to visually track coverage of key curriculum terms. ● � Blue nodes represent student-generated words that were not in the glossary but are semantically similar to one or more glossary terms, with a similarity score above a given threshold. These reflect students’ own language, paraphrasing, or expansion beyond the provided curriculum terms. Blue nodes are particularly valuable for identifying emergent ideas, student agency, and conceptual extensions that go beyond the original terminology.

This color-coding approach allows researchers and educators to easily identify not only which curriculum concepts were addressed directly, but also how students may have rephrased or extended those concepts using their own vocabulary. The visualization thus provides insight into both curriculum coverage  and conceptual innovation within a Knowledge Building environment.

Edge color coding based on semantic nearness In addition to node colors, the graph employs color-coded edges to represent the strength of semantic similarity between glossary concepts and student-generated terms. Each edge connects a glossary term to a word or phrase from the student notes that the transformer model identifies as semantically related. 141 The intensity and hue of each edge color correspond directly to the similarity score, as indicated by the legend on the right. Lighter cyan edges represent lower similarity values (closer to the defined threshold), while deeper magenta edges represent stronger similarities (approaching 1.0). By consulting the legend, viewers can translate any edge color into its approximate similarity score, making it possible to distinguish strong conceptual alignments from weaker, more tentative connections. This design allows the visualization to go beyond a simple binary match/no-match view: it presents a semantic gradient that highlights not only whether a student’s word relates to a glossary concept, but also how strongly it does so: ● A nearness score approaching 1 (indicating very high similarity) will be represented by an edge in a dark, vivid shade, signaling strong alignment with a glossary concept. ● An edge with a score close to the threshold (moderate similarity) will appear in a lighter hue, reflecting a looser, more exploratory link.

From a Knowledge Building perspective, darker edges indicate where student discourse remains tightly anchored to curriculum expectations, while lighter edges reveal emergent or exploratory ideas that may signal creative extensions, tentative reasoning, or steps toward moving beyond the formal curriculum. Together, these gradients provide educators and researchers with a nuanced picture of student thinking—ranging from the consolidation of established concepts to the exploration of new conceptual territory. This allows for a more precise assessment of how closely student discourse aligns with expected curriculum while also illuminating moments where students push the boundaries of the curriculum to generate novel insights. Novel capabilities of the semantic tool By applying the above methodology, our tool unlocks several novel analytic capabilities that extend beyond what prior Knowledge Building tools offer. In this section, we discuss these capabilities in turn, explaining how each addresses a specific need in Knowledge Building analytics. The key affordances include: (1) gauging curriculum coverage in discourse, (2) identifying the use of related concepts beyond the given curriculum, and (3) illuminating cross-topic bridges in discourse. We describe each capabil ity and provide examples of the kinds of insights the tool can reveal, emphasizing how they can inform teachers’ and researchers’ understanding of the community’s knowledge progress.

Curriculum coverage via semantic matching The most immediate use of the tool is to determine to what extent students have covered the intended curriculum concepts in their discussions. Traditional keyword checks only flag when specific terms are mentioned; our tool goes further by flagging when concepts are discussed in any terms. For each term in the teacher’s glossary, the tool finds any semantically related words that students used. This means that even if a concept’s official name was never mentioned, we can recognize its presence in the conversation. For instance, suppose “photosynthesis” is a key term in a biology class. If students never wrote “photosynthesis” explicitly, a simple keyword search would wrongly conclude they ignored it. But our tool might find that students frequently discussed plants producing food with sunlight, using words like “energy”, “sunlight”, “make food”, “chlorophyll”. These words would show high similarity to “photosynthesis”, and thus the tool would indicate that the concept was indeed covered implicitly. In a real scenario, one could imagine the output:

- Term: Photosynthesis

143-Related student words:  sunlight (similarity score: 0.7), food  (similarity score: 0.75), energy

(similarity score: 0.75), chlorophyll (similarity score: 0.85) Such a report tells the teacher that students are engaging with aspects of photosynthesis, even if they never use the exact term. In this case, the teacher might conclude that students have developed a working understanding of the concept and simply need t o connect it to the formal scientific terms. Teachers can then strategically introduce authoritative terms and resources at moments when they will extend and deepen rather than replace student ideas. Conversely, if no related terms are detected, the teacher can recognize that the concept has not yet been addressed and may require deliberate attention.

This kind of semantic curriculum coverage mapping directly supports the Knowledge Building principle of constructive use of authoritative sources (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). Rather than treating authoritative terms as rigid checklists, the tool makes visible how student-driven inquiry is already converging toward canonical scientific ideas, while still valuing the diverse, informal language stu dents bring to the discussion. Unlike earlier tools such as the Vocabulary Analyzer, which offered only a binary view (term present or absent), this approach provides a gradient that captures degrees of relatedness and partial understanding. It also reduces false negatives— cases where conceptual understanding is emerging but phrased differently—thus helping teacher s recognize when students are ready to engage productively with authoritative knowledge sources in ways that advance collective inquiry.

Detection of related concepts beyond the curriculum Knowledge Building emphasizes not just covering the official curriculum, but also going beyond—bringing in outside knowledge, making interdisciplinary connections, and pursuing unforeseen ideas (Scardamalia, 2002). Our semantic tool is particularly adept at detecting when students introduce related concepts that were not in the provided glossary. Because we analyze all content words, we inevitably encounter terms that are not on the teacher’s original list. Some of these will be irrelevant or off-task, but others could be highly relevant to the topic and even extend it. The tool can cross-check the semantic similarity of these “extra” words to the entire set of curriculum terms to gauge their relevance. If a student-used word is semantically distant from all glossary terms, it might be an off-topic tangent (or something completely new). But if it is moderately close to one or more glossary terms, it could represent a valuable extension or enrichment of the topic. For example, in a unit on ecosystems, students might bring up “climate change” or “pollution” even if those terms were not in the original list of concepts for that unit. Our tool would identify these as prominent words in the discourse and might show they have some semantic proximity to terms like “ecosystem.” The teacher’s glossary did not include them because perhaps the curriculum did not explicitly address human environmental impact, but the students made that leap. The tool thus can flag such emergent topics for the teacher. This serves as evidence of student-driven knowledge advancement,  connecting to KB principles of idea diversity  and knowledge building discourse (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006) that crosses boundaries. It provides teachers and researchers a way to quantify and catalog how the community knowledge is extending beyond given parameters. Illuminating cross-topic bridges in discourse Another interesting analysis is how cross-topic bridges form. In many knowledge-building classrooms, students cycle through different investigation topics (e.g. one month on plants, another on ecology, another on human body). Ideally, ideas from earlier topics permeate into later ones, creating a continuous knowledge-building trajectory rather than isolated units. Our tool candetect when vocabulary from topic A reappears in topic B. Suppose a class studied light in physics and later plant growth in biology. If students make a connection (e.g., they mention “light” and “energy” in the plant discussion, relating to their physics knowledge), our semantic similarity measure might connect those uses to the earlier glossary terms from the light unit. We can produce an (from Unit 1 on Light) was referenced in Unit 2 on Plants (via words: light, sun, power). This shows cross-topic knowledge transfer. If one were to visualize the entire year’s discourse as a network, each unit’s glossary could be a cluster of nodes, and cross-topic bridges are edges between nodes of different clusters. A healthy knowledge building environment might show many bridges (students leveraging prior knowledge to inform new inquiries), whereas a traditional unit-by-unit approach might show none. The tool thus supports longitudinal tracking, helping educators ensure continuity of learning. It also helps to document one of the promises of Knowledge Building pedagogy: that students will “rise above” and connect ideas across domains for deeper understanding.

For research, these analyses could be correlated with assessments of transfer or integrated understanding. This capability is somewhat analogous to what Authors (2019) described as “crisscrossing topics”  – tracking student use of concepts beyond their grade-level expectations. Their analytic looked at how students incorporate 144 concepts across disciplinary boundaries and beyond curriculum guidelines, finding that KB students often exceed the bounds of the given content areas. Our tool automates the detection of such occurrences by spotlighting vocabulary that originates from outside the provided knowledge space but is relevant. Collectively, these capabilities illustrate how a semantic analysis approach can yield a richer picture of discourse. It preserves much of what earlier analytics offered, but adds layers of meaning: recognizing paraphrases as evidence of understanding, surfacing student-introduced content, and detecting the evolution of the community’s knowledge.

Classroom and research applications The semantic analysis tool has broad applications in classroom practice and research. For teachers, it offers a new form of formative assessment, showing which key concepts have appeared in class discourse and which remain unexplored. By running analyses periodically, teachers can identify gaps and adapt instruction accordingly— supporting data-informed scaffolding consistent with Knowledge Building principles. The tool can also visualize community discourse to prompt collective reflection, helping students notice idea progress, missing concepts, and opportunities for deeper understanding.

For students, the tool provides formative feedback that fosters epistemic agency. Visualizations of concept networks make visible which ideas they have explored and where connections are lacking. This helps students take greater ownership of their learning, intentionally extend discussions, and contribute new perspectives. Rather than treating analytics as a checklist, students use them to guide inquiry and strengthen collaboration.

For researchers, the tool enables large-scale analysis of Knowledge Building discourse. Its semantic outputs quantify conceptual growth and integration, supporting studies on how scaffolds, teacher moves, or contexts influence idea development. It can also compare classes or correlate discourse patterns with learning outcomes, offering new ways to understand how semantic connectivity reflects and supports knowledge advancement.

In this paper, we introduced a novel semantic analysis tool tailored for Knowledge Building (KB) environments and demonstrated its potential to enrich both research and practice in collaborative learning.  The semantic making idea improvement visible through vocabulary and concept development. It operationalizes epistemic agency, allowing students to see how their contributions shape collective understanding and guiding teachers with ongoing semantic feedback that supports open-ended inquiry while ensuring core goals are met. The tool also embodies concurrent, embedded, and transformative assessment, turning evaluation into a collaborative process that informs further inquiry rather than judging it externally. By revealing idea diversity and highlighting connections among concepts, it promotes “Rise Above” synthesis, where communities integrate ideas into higherorder understanding. Ultimately, the tool bridges qualitative and quantitative approaches to discourse analysis, providing empirical evidence that students —when engaged in true knowledge building—can extend beyond the given curriculum, connect domains, and participate in progressive, expansive knowledge creation. The successful integration of this tool would exemplify how learning analytics can be married with a strong pedagogical framework to produce actionable insights. Often, learning analytics suffer from focusing on what’s easy to measure (clicks, counts) rath er than what’s educationally meaningful. Here, we take on the challenge of analyzing meaning – a complex thing to measure – because Knowledge Building demands it. We hope this inspires more “pedagogy-first” analytics development. Additionally, our approach demonstrates the value of combining quantitative and qualitative methods. The tool quantifies similarities, but its outputs are best interpreted with qualitative context (reading the notes, understanding the classroom narrative). It does not replace qualitative content analysis; rather, it guides where to look, making qualitative analysis more focused (akin to how digital humanities scholars use text mining to find themes in literature before close reading specific passages). Several limitations should be noted. The tool analyzes only textual data, so ideas expressed non-verbally (e.g., drawings or gestures) remain outside its scope, though future multimodal extensions could address this. Its performance also depends on the language model’s quality—challenges may arise with highly specialized jargon, small datasets, or messy student text (e.g., spelling errors). While fine-tuning and preprocessing help, accuracy can vary. Nonetheless, these constraints are manageable through technical improvements and teacher awareness, and the tool’s overall benefits outweigh its current limitations. Despite these limitations, the work reported here is a step toward a theory-aligned analytic innovation that exemplifies the synergy between learning sciences and AI technology. The semantic analysis tool empowers educators and learners in Knowledge Building communities to better understand and guide their collective inquiry. It provides a new form of feedback that is content-aware, going beyond structural metrics to the level of meanings 145 and ideas. By integrating this tool into Knowledge Forum or similar platforms, we move towards a new generation of CSCL environments that not only connect learners but also illuminate the knowledge they are building together. We envision that as teachers and students use these semantic insights to reflect and adapt, classroom discourse will become more inclusive of important concepts, more connected across diverse ideas, and more visibly progressive toward deeper understanding. Such an outcome aligns deeply with Scardamalia and Bereiter’s original vision for Knowledge Building – a community collectively improving its ideas with the help of supportive technology.

Future research will focus on evaluating, extending, and enhancing the tool. Empirical studies should assess its impact on classroom processes, concept coverage, and teacher practices through mixed-methods or quasi-experimental designs. Further, integrating automated feedback could help teachers respond more effectively to analytic insights by suggesting prompts or highlighting overlooked concepts, while maintaining teacher control and professional judgment. Finally, a student-facing dashboard could foster self-reflection by showing which concepts learners have (or have not) engaged with, encouraging more intentional contributions. Such developments would refine the tool’s pedagogical value and broaden its applicability across disciplines. As we continue to refine the tool and study its impact, we anticipate new insights into how students learn together and how technology can respectfully and powerfully support that process. The journey of integrating advanced analytics with human-centric educational theory is just beginning, and the work presented here is a promising step on that path.
