# Illustrating Four Synergistic Approaches to Equity Among Preservice Elementary Teachers of Science: “If This is How We Do Science, Then The Future Could Be a Lot Better”

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning to engage in justice-oriented science teaching as a new elementary teacher is challenging. We explore how six focal preservice elementary teachers demonstrate beginning versions of equity-oriented science teaching. We focus on these teachers’ assets and strengths, using these to highlight what is possible and, at the same time, to identify where teacher educators may need to provide additional support. Drawing on interviews, lesson plans, and videorecordings of lesson enactments, we use these six teachers to provide examples of novice engagement in four approaches to equity: increasing opportunity and access to science, increasing representation and identification in science, expanding what counts as science, and seeing science as a part of justice movements. Our findings show how committed teachers who are supported in learning to engage in justice-oriented teaching are able to provide early versions of equitable experiences in their classrooms. We close with implications for practice and theory.

## Four approaches to equity and theoretical framework for teacher learning

Given the inequities in the US and globally,  antiracism and justice must be addressed in all lea rning-to-teach experiences, not bounded in foundations courses in teacher education. We use a framework (Davis & Bautista,

2024) informed by two recent National Academies of Scienc es, Engineering, and Medicine reports (2022, 2024)

to emphasize four “approaches to equity” (NASEM, 2022) in our science methods course and in our research that explores how novice elementary teachers learn to en gage in justice-oriented science teaching, similar to other frameworks for secondary teachers (e.g., Kang & Nation, 2022; Luehmann et al., 2024). The four approaches to equity include (1) increasing opportunity and access to science, (2) increasing representation and identification in science, (3) expanding what counts as science, and (4) seeing science as a part of justice movements. First, access to science education for many children globally remains minimal. To increase children’s opportunity and access to science, teachers can, for example, use language scaffolds  to support emergent multilingual learners (e.g., Suárez & Otero, 2023) and use talk moves to enhance  access to science discourse (Michaels & O’Connor, 2012). Time for science is limited at the elementary gr ades (Banilower et al., 2018), so teachers can also work simply to ensure that science is taught. Second, children need to see themselves and others like them  as people who do science (Carlone et al., 2014; Wright et al., 2018). To increase children’s representation and identificati on in science, teachers can, for example, privilege investigation and science practi ces while supporting children’s epistemic agency (e.g., Manz, 2015). Teachers could use examples in lessons to feature women scientists, Black, Latine, and  Indigenous scientists, and others, to provide a broad perspect ive on who does science. They also can position chi ldren as knowledge builders, thinkers, doers, and even critics of science (Carlone et al., 2021). Yet third, science is often viewed as the realm of white, cisgender men (Harding, 1986). To push against this dominant narrative and expand what counts as science involves uplifting multiple ways of knowing and being in science (Wright et al., 2018), watching for ways of knowing that can contribute to knowledge-building (Solomon et al., 2022), ‘hearing the science’ in what children say, write, draw, or do (Carlone et al., 2021; Suárez & Otero, 2023), and connecting to children’s families’ and communities’ knowledge and practices (Barajas-López & Bang, 2018). Focusing only on representation, without a more exp ansive perspective on science, may serve to reify dominant perspectives of science.

Finally, science can be used to create a more equitable and j ust world (NASEM, 2024). Seeing science as a part of justice movements can involve connecting science to justice issues in children’s lives or in a classroom community or the broader neighborhood, town, or region (e.g., Morales-Doyle, 2017). Davis and Schaeffer (2019) described how elementary children took up issues of  water justice when learning about the nearby Flint  water crisis. Teachers can position children as agents of  change, for example through writing to their mayor about ecological effects of a proposed development  or designing an engineering solution  to a classroom problem (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020; Morales-Doyle, 2017).

No single approach is sufficient on its own for addressing the inequit ies in the teaching and learning of science within educational systems (NASEM, 2022), and each provide s an important lever for working toward a more just world. We are interested in how novice elementary teachers learn to use these four approaches to equity. Our theoretical framing is that l earning to teach is sociocultural and situated in e xperiences, and over time what is learned can be generalized across contexts (Esmonde, 2017). Learning to teach requires orchestrating skill, knowledge, and identity to work toward a communal goal (Berland, 2011; Grossman, 2018), such as teaching science well and equitably. We have developed a jus tice framework  (Davis & Bautista, 2024; see elementarysciencejustice.marsal.umich.edu) organized around the four approaches to equity desc ribed above, intended to serve as a cognitive and cultural tool for learning (Wertsch, 1998). Long term, we are interested in how this framework supports beginning elementary te achers in learning to engage in more just science t eaching. Toward that end, we want to know what beginning ver sions of equity-oriented science teaching, using these approaches to equity as guideposts, can look like.

Thus, our research question s are, What do preservice teachers’ approaches to equity look like  as they plan, enact, and reflect on science teaching? Specifically, what does it look like when they focus on: (1) increasing students’ opportunity and access in science, (2) increasing students’ representation and identification in science,

(3) expanding what counts as science, and (4) seeing sci ence as a part of justice movements? We focus on these

teachers’ strengths to help us identify building blocks that can serve as a foundation for their future growth.

## Methods

This study, which is a part of a larger multiyear and multi-wave longitudinal study,  took place in the third and fourth semester s of a four-semester undergraduate elementary teacher education  program. The program is a practice-based, justice-oriented teacher education program situated in a large public university in the midwestern US. Preservice elementary teachers  took their science methods course during the third semester and did their student teaching during the fourth and final semest er of the program. They had a ~9 hour/week field experience during the science methods course and  most completed this in the same classroom as their stude nt teaching experience. Participants taught one science lesson for their  science methods course and were asked to teach an additional science lesson during student teaching, for this project. As authors, we all identify as cisgender women  and mothers—the first author identifies as white, the second author identifies as white and Hispanic, and  the third author identifies as a Black Latina. The  first author taught a class the preservice teachers had earlier in their program and, as faculty lead for elementary science, also participated in the ir science methods class; the second author taught the science methods class in w hich the participants were enrolled; and the third author ob served in a different earlier education class. Thus, the three of us had complementary viewpoints on these participants from both an instructional and a research standpoint. We extended invitations to participate in this research project to the full cohort of 16 preservice teachers. Eight preservice teachers  agreed to participate and became the focal particip ants with whom we conducted interviews and whose data we analyzed in depth. The  8 focal participants are representative of the larger cohort in some ways (e.g., most of the focal participants identify as white women, which was also the case in  the larger cohort), but were different in other ways. Most notably, the research project explicitly focuses on justice-oriented science teaching, and these participants volunteered. Thus, there is likely some selection bias in the sample. Because of our focus on illustrating teachers’ stre ngths (and not, e.g., evaluating a teacher educatio n program), in this paper, we focus on  6 of these 8: Elise, Lana, Leo, Mia, Skye, and Teri. We focus on these 6 because each, in their own way, demonstrated partic ular strengths, grounded in science teaching, aroun d one or more of the four approaches to equity framing the ASSETS project. Their use of the moves, and their engagement with the justice framework more generally, seemed (based on their talk, plans, and enactments) to be sincere and genuine. (Our other two focal participants also demonstrated  important strengths, but less clearly allowed us to address our research interests in what is possible among early career elementary teachers vis-à-vis justice-oriented teaching.) ## Results: Illustrating the four approaches to equity

Approach 1 was the most frequently used or discusse d across all participants, followed by Approach 2. Leo focused more on Approach 3 than other participants did, and this seemed a clear interest of Leo’s; thu s, we use Leo to illustrate Approach 3. Similarl y, Lana focused more on Approach 4 than other parti cipants did, and this seemed a clear interest of Lana’s; thus, we use Lan a to illustrate Approach 4. We use Mia and Elise to illustrate Approach 1 and Skye and Teri to illustrate Approach  2. In this section, we draw on interviews, annotated lesson plans, and enactment logs – that is, participants’ verbal reflections as well a s their plans and enactments – to illustrate what it can look like when preservice elementary teachers take up these four approaches to equity. Elise and Mia: Illustrating increasing opportunity and access to science Elise and Mia both bemoaned how infrequently they saw science in their field placements. Mia, for example, said, “I’ve seen probably like 2 or 3 science lessons in t otal [across three semesters of field placements], conducted in an elementary classroom” (Mia, fall interview). Elise and Mia both enjoyed science themselves and, perhaps as a result of that confidence and enthusiasm, worked to increase children’s opportunity and access to science in their field placement classrooms.  They did so  similarly, in several ways, by: making time for science, doing more science, and not taking away science  as a form of behavioral management; carefully allocating resources, using readily available  materials, and setting expectations for their use; p roviding opportunities for children to talk science and engage in science discourse; and leveling the playing field by providing shared experiences. Elise provides an example of how the teachers  reflected on, plan ned for, and enact ed increasing opportunity and access by making time for science a nd not taking away science. For example, Elise expressed concern about the kindergartners in her room who typically opted out of learning experiences, writing: There are two/three students who don’t come to the rug for lessons (90% of the time) when me or my [mentor teacher] is teaching. I feel like even though I’m not explic itly excluding them, they are being excluded. I want to find ways to inc lude them or support them in feeling safe to learn on the carpet. (Elise, fall lesson plan) In her enactment of that lesson, Elise made moves to try to encourage these and other children to engage with the lesson. For example, Elise said to a child who had moved away from the group: “I know you have some really cool scientific thinking, [student name], can you come back? ” (Elise, fall science lesson enactment log). She continued  to calmly (re)invite children to engage with each o ther and share their thinking,  offering her kindergartners ongoing access to  learning opportunities in science. Elise and Mia both  used moves like this to encourage student participation and engagement.

Elise and Mia both also worked to increase children ’s opportunity and access in their lessons by payin g careful attention to how they were using and distributing materials to children. For example, Elise planned to tell children that she had clipboards with “noticing pap er” and pencils for each child, for their nature walk outdoors. Similarly, Mia wrote, in her plan: I will clearly state how to use the tools before al lowing students to interact with them. Having clear expectations lessens the possibility of misuse of the items and students being punished or excluded from the lesson. This is especially import ant for Black and brown students who are disproportionately punished and excluded from instruction. (Mia, fall lesson plan) Elise and Mia both also carefully and purposefully supported children’s science talk, which served as another way that they increased children’s opportun ity and access in science. For example, Elise had s tudents “whisper to a friend what they think plants might need” (Elise, spring enactment log) and discussed having science observations as the focus of conversations during morning meetings (Elise, spring interview). Mia, in particular, also increased children’s oppor tunity and access in science by providing shared experiences that served to level the playing field to make her science lessons more accessible to chil dren. The following extended excerpt from Mia’s lesson plan illustrates how she did this in a lesson on properties of matter (which used sorting the objects in a junk drawer as a focus). Today, we are going to [do] a science investigation. We will investigate what p roperties or characteristics objects have. The objects we are lo oking at today are from a junk drawer. Have you ever heard the phrase “junk drawer”? … Does anyone have one at home? What is in yours? I have a junk drawer at home. We put things in there that don’t have any other place to go. Mine has tape, birthday candles, string, batteries, screwdriver, and more random items. Let’s look at some pictures of junk drawers. Are they organized? How are they organized? Turn and talk to your partner about what you think. Make  sure you listen to your partner’s ideas so that we can talk about them as a class. (Mia, fall lesson plan) Mia was concerned, in her planning, about whether all children would know what a junk drawer was, so in her plan (as well as in her enactment)  she asked if children had one, she mentioned her ow n junk drawer, and then showed photos of several junk drawers.

Skye and Teri: Illustrating increasing representation and identification in science Skye and Teri both worked to increase representatio n and identification in science. As illustrated in the text that follows, they did so in several ways, including connecting to  students’ interests; positioning children as knowers and doers in science (i.e., as people who can and d o engage in meaningful science with epistemic agenc y); supporting children’s science, linguistic, and racial identities; and connecting to children’s families and communities. While there were some similarities acr oss Skye and Teri, there were also some differences  in how they approached this work, with Skye focusing more on supporting students’ identification in science and Teri focusing on both representation and identification.

Skye discussed at length how STEM-focused Spruce Elementary (and Skye’s mentor teacher) provided a context where she could connect science lessons to students’ interests. For example, in an interview, Skye said: It’s all project-based learning too, so they are doing—they raise chickens, they just got salmon eggs, they’ll be raising salmon, and that’s … how they [explore life cycles]. [T] hey have a whole garden that they have to plant, raise, grow. They, each year harvest stuff from the year before and they make a little farm stand, and they make salsa and soup and whatever. … I notice that they love doing it, and that’s … where I have found a lot of joy in watching [my mentor teacher] teach it too. It’s just like, whatever comes up for them, he really wants to expl ore and have their interests in mind. (Skye, fall interview) Skye made many moves to connect informally to children’s interests (e.g., in rock hunting). In her student teaching semester, Skye asked children about their specific interests in astronomy, and then extended her original unit to develop a sequence of lessons based directly on their stated interests (Skye, spring interview). Skye supported children in identifying with science  through engaging them in science practice and thus positioning them as knowers and doers in science. For example, in the enactment log from one of Skye’s lessons, she highlighted students’ particular strengths with making and recording observations: Guys, look at this really quick. Pause. [S tudent name] has some really good labels that he’s starting to put on here. I see a lot of you guys have ‘inside’ or ‘ outside’ or diagram 1, diagram

2. That’s awesome. … (Skye, fall science lesson enactment log)

Skye discussed this with us in an interview, saying  that she saw this as a way to “highlight a ton of different competencies of different students” (Skye, fall interview). She named the kinds of observation practices she noticed her children engaging in (e.g., drawing  detailed depictions of the ridges of the seed, tracing the seed for size accuracy), and then went on to say, “ That was my biggest thing was just letting them exp lore and seeing themselves as students that cando really cool scientific work” (Skye, fall interview). Skye saw engaging in science practices as a direct way to support their development of science identities. Race was a more prominent focus in Teri’s talk, and she linked identification in science to representation. Discussing how she worked to build students’ science identities, she focused on her own experiences as a Black girl and emphasized how she wanted the Black children in her classroom to develop identities as science people: [I]t goes back to them developing a sense of self and their identities as learners. … I don’t know how accurate it is now, but for me growing up, I di dn’t really see many Black scientists. And when you don’t see representation in something that ’s difficult, you might not think that’s for you or that it’s fitting for you. So just helping t hem to see that they are doing the work and they’re capable of it, I think it’s just really beneficial for them. (Teri, spring interview) Similar to Skye, Teri also prioritized positioning children  as science people through engaging them in science practice and talk. The following quotes, from Teri’s science methods l esson plan and enactment log, illustrate some of how she did this: … [B]ased on what you just saw happen to the paper clip,  what are some of your predictions? You can start your prediction like this: “I think t hat objects move without touching by ____.” (Teri, fall lesson plan) How did we decide that some metals are magnetic? We  need evidence. What from our data showed that some metals are magnetic, not all of them? (Teri, fall science lesson enactment log) Amazing job guys! You’re such great scientists! You started the investigation by making predictions, conducting an investigation on your own, collecting evidence, and creating a claim about magnets. (Teri, fall lesson plan) Teri referred to her students as “scientists,” which is a typical move among novice elementary t eachers, but went deeper than that, ensuring that she was actually engaging them in meaningful science practices. Leo: Illustrating expanding what counts as science As noted above, Leo talked about Approach 3, expanding what counts as science, far more than other participants. Leo connected science to the everyday (including children’s families and communities), but Leo mainly expanded what counts as science  by attending to multiple ways of knowing in science, and in particular, Indigenous ways of knowing. Leo discussed their interest in Indigenous ways of knowing with us in their spring interview: Before the [teacher education] program and before reading the book [Braiding Sweetgrass, by Robin Wall Kimmerer], I think when I thought about science, it was like, ‘This is a scientific method. These types of practices go into how you do  science.’ … [T]hat was the biggest shift for me was … thinking about the reciprocity and respect and almost like there’s the spirituality in doing science and it’s very revolutionary too. And connecting to it is a movement too, because then it’s like--it made me very hopeful too because if this is how we do science and this is how we do things, [then] the future could be a lot better. (Leo, spring interview) This interest was particularly clear in the lesson Leo developed for the science methods co urse. Leo decided to lead their children on a wondering walk in the woods beside their school, and to explicitly  connect dominant Eurocentric science practices and Indigenous science practices on equal footing. In the overview of the lesson, Leo wrote about how they would first develo p a list as a class of how children would make observations on their nature walk, and then read a book focused on Indigenous practices. Leo wrote that the class would add ideas from that book to their discussion of making observations, then go on the nature walk. The class would then “discuss what we observed, thinking about reciprocity and what plants can teach us” (Leo, fall lesson plan). In the instructional sequence, the plan continued  with careful attention to identity and pronunciation, illustrating Leo’s intentional respect and care with this expansive work: So now we have a list of what we are thinking [abou t making observations] before we begin our exploration. Now we are going to read a book together, it is about Native American science practices. The book is called Walking Together and it is written by Elder Albert D. Marshall (Mi’kmaq, pronounced “meeg mahh”) and Louise Zimany i (French-Canadian, Hungarian), as well as the illustrator Emily Kewageshig  (KEE-WAH-GEE-SHIG) (Anishnaabe). The book is called Walking Together because one way to practice observation making is by going on nature walks. After we finish reading, we are going to try  this ourselves. As we read, pay attention to other ways these scientists make observations. If y ou notice something you want to add to our list, raise your hand while I read. (Leo, fall lesson plan) The lesson plan went on to name specific spots to pause, questions to ask, and ideas students might bring up that warrant highlighting, and then Leo wrote that they would say, “From the book, we learned that we should treat nature like family. What are some ways we cando this and show respect to nature?” (Leo, fall lesson plan). Leo’s enactment followed their plan fairly closely.

Throughout this lesson, Leo made respectful, meaningful connections to Indigenous practices (including “two-eyed seeing,” or using both dominant Eurocentric and Indigenous lenses  for observing and sensemaking) and supported children in recognizing the value of these practices and ways of knowing and being. Lana: Illustrating seeing science as a part of justice movements Lana discussed and used social justice science issu es more extensively thandid other participants in the study; this was the main form of how Lana approached seein g science as a part of justice movements, though sh e also occasionally referenced activism a nd the idea of justice for whom. With regard to soc ial justice science issues, Lana’s main foci were issues of environmentalism, sustainability, climate change, and climate justice. Across our interviews with her, Lana talked about h ow her focus on sustainability drove her and gave her confidence in working on science as a part of justice issues. For example, in our fall interview, she said: Science and math have always been my favorite subje cts when I was in elementary school, middle school, high school. In college, I have been  the president of a sustainability, environmental and social justice focused organizati on, so I’m around science all the time. Climate science, like all, the whole shebang, so I have been very excited to looking into ways how I can kind of bring this into the classroom. (Lana, fall interview) When asked directly about where she thought her strengths were as a teacher, Lana noted: I feel like that’s one [approach 4, seeing science as a part of justice movements] that I’ve always been pretty good at. I mean, we’ve talked about, like I said, Flint, Line Five [a controversial oil pipeline in the state], things that are going on in their own community, environmental racism. I feel like that’s one that comes so naturally to me. (Lana, spring interview) This comfort with Approach 4 was highly atypical among participants. As part of the spring interview, we asked Lana to look at a set of news articles that might be used to support social justice science issues. Lana told us: My students are extremely passionate about Line Fiv e, and we had lots of discussions, and whether or not [our state] should be donating their water… to California. And we had debates about that. The wildfire smoke, we’ve talked a lot about things like that. So I feel like so many of these, but I am totally the type of teacher wher e if a student brings something up in sc ience, I will open a tab onto my computer and type in some thing. … So I really try to tie in current events …. (Lana, spring interview) In her student teaching placement, Lana taught a unit on human impacts to the environment – but it was a writing unit, not a science unit. So, while she  did not have the opportunity  to engage students in issues of environmental and climate justice in the context of a science unit, she leveraged her writing unit as an opportunity to continue to engage students in these kinds of is sues – concomitantly working to break down some of the sil os between subject areas and thoughtfully working across disciplines.

## Discussion, implications, and conclusion

Describing these beginning teachers’ engagement wit h the four approaches to equity, as we have done ab ove, informs some implications for practice and for theo ry. First, being able to name these teachers’ strengths in thei r ideas, plans, and enactments with regard to these a pproaches to equity could help teacher educators take responsibility to conceive of developing supportive instruction (Gray et al., 2021). These  novice teachers consistently brought strength to the work of suppor ting children to have opportunities to talk about s cience (Approach 1) and positioning children as knowers and doers in science  (Approach 2); we illustrated this with examples from Mia, Elise, Teri, and Skye, but all six focal participants demonstrated similar strengths across these two approaches. Teacher educators can build on thes e strengths. For example, teacher educators can focus preservice teachers’ attention on the importance of  meaningful sensemaking talk (going beyond “talk to your neighbor”), as we saw our participants doing (Michaels & O’Connor, 2012). Similarly, when preservice teachers refer to children as “scientists” (as both Skye and  Teri did), teacher educators can encourage teacher s to also engage children in authentic science practices (as Skye and Teri also both did) (e.g., Berland, 2011). We also, however, saw less consistent engagement wi th Approaches 3 and 4. This suggests that teacher educators and curriculum developers who hope to help teachers  expand what counts as science or help children connect science to justice movements may need more concrete examples of what these can look like in elementary classrooms. Such examples might help to support more effective work toward justice, as is needed for a more just and sustainable world (NASEM, 2024).

Theoretically and conceptually, the four approaches to equity do not appear to be mutually exclusive. These novice teachers all drew on all of the approa ches, suggesting an interlocking constellation of synergistic pieces. Better understanding the relations, synergies, and tensions among these four approaches will help to support investigation of a trajectory of developmen t over time, contributing to a learning progression  for equityoriented teaching (Kang & Zinger, 2019; see also Morales-Doyle et al., 2021). Furthermore, this research suggests two additional factors that appear to matter in how a teacher engages in this kind of work: identity and context (Esmonde, 2017). For example, both Elise and Mia expressed strong science identities, and both worked hard to increase children’s oppor tunity and access to science. On the other hand, Skye and Teri both did not see themselves (initially) as having strength in science. Perhaps as a result, both set an explicit goal of helping their students to have strong science identities (Carlone et al., 2014). They each worked to connect to children’s interests and, in Teri’s c ase, to their  racial (and linguistic) identities. Skye was able to engage in strongly child-oriented science instruction in part because of her  school setting where such connections were valued. Leo felt that expanding what counts as science, par ticularly by connecting to certain values often embodied in Indigenous ways of knowing, helped them feel more hopeful for the future. Thi s hopefulness may have served as a guide for their wo rk toward enacting social justice science teaching (Rivera Maulucci, 2013). Lana wanted to engage her children in social justic e science issues and was able to do so to an extent – but in the context of her student teaching placemen t, was not able to do so as much as she wanted to. These findings remind us of the importance of the resources of a teacher’s context (Allen & Heredia, 2020). We have focused, intentionally, on these beginning teachers’ strengths. Taking an asset orientation is crucial in crediting elementary teachers with the many resources they bring to the work of teaching (Zembal-Saul et al., 2020) and furthermore, such an orientation helps us to identify the foundations on which teacher educators can build (Gray et al., 2021; Haverly & Davis, 2024). That said, these teachers also experienced struggles. Being able to name these areas for further growth is impo rtant, too, even when espousing a n asset orientation.  Our ongoing work follows these beginning teachers into their early years of teaching. We are interested in seeing how their justice commitments shift and are realized ov er time, where they face challenges, and how these commitments are realized within their enactments compared with their lesson plans and interviews. These participants saw teaching  as a way to work toward a more equitable and just world. As noted above, Leo said that “the future could be a lot better” if we taught science in more expansive ways. Each, in their own ways, took seriously increasing children’s opportunities to learn science, supporting children’s identification in science and broadening representation of who does science, expanding what counts as science to incorporate a wider range of scientific ways of knowing and being, and using science to sup port a more just world. In keeping with the ICLS 2025 theme, these teachers are contributing, in nascent ways, to world-making with their students. Learning to teach is complex and challenging – and this is compounded as beginning elementary teachers work toward equitable and just science teaching. Beginning elementary teachers bring many strengths, assets, and resources to this work. These six teachers shared rich examples of what justice-oriented science teaching could look like very early in a teacher’s career, supporting knowledge development in the fields of the learning sciences, science education, and teacher education. It is our job to notice such strengths and to help beginning teachers to build on their strengths, by designing supportive tools and experiences for teachers and children.
