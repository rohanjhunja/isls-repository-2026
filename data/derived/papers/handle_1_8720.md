# Novice Versus Advanced Undergraduate Computing Students’ Engagement in Collaboration in an Online Flipped Classroom

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores students' engagement in collaborative learning activities within two data science courses (one introductory and one advanced) taught synchronously online during the 2020-2021 academic year. Here, we draw on a multidimensional perspective of student engagement to understand novice and advanced computing students' collaborative learning practices and propose instructional design elements informed by students’ unique needs and the limitations of the virtual format.

## Introduction & Rationale

Considering the mass shift to online learning due to the Covid-19 pandemic, instructors of undergraduate students have been tasked with adapting their course materia l and instructional practice to be appropriate for remote instruction. For disciplines in which peer and instructor interaction are paramount in the development of industryauthentic interpersonal ‘soft’ skills, the constraints of online learning formats have proven to be a barrier to student engagement. Computing is one such discipline that experiences these tensions. There is furthermore an increasingly widespread view that flipped classroom approaches engage students in developing these interpersonal skills and practi ces through active student participation and intera ction (e.g., Bishop & Verleger). The flipped classroom model allows students to use class time to engage in iterative problemsolving, collective sense-making, and critical thin king through collaboration with classmates and inst ructor support. In our prior work, we identified a set of instructional design factors which the literature s howed to be effective for flipped classroom implementations (Mithun & Luo, 2020; Mithun et al., 2021). In this research, we consider how computing student s engage in online small group collaborative learni ng activities in an introductory data science fundamentals course and an advanced database design and management course. We use a multidimensional (Fredericks et al., 2004; Sinatra et al., 2015) and sociocultural (D anish & Gresalfi, 2018) framework of student engagement to understand the behavioral, cognitive, and emotional aspects of students' engagement intersected with their social engagement in collaborative learning settings. We then use these insights in conjunction with instructor observations to identify how the current instructional design for each course can be revised to better suit the unique needs of each group of students. We pose the following questions:

1. How do novice and advanced undergraduate students behaviorally, cognitively, and emotionally engage

during peer collaborative activities in an online data science course?

2. How does understanding students’ engagement enable instructional design modification?

## Methods

A variety of data sources were used to analyze undergraduate students' experiences in the introductory database fundamentals course (n = 21) and the advanced datab ase design and management course (n=12). Students completed an end-of-semester (EoS) survey designed by the authors and a collaborator with expertise in student engagement as multidimensional (see Humburg, 2020).  Students also completed university-mandated course/instructor evaluations and participated in a n informal focus group facilitated by a third-party  evaluator. Novice students additionally responded to weekly ex it-ticket check-ins; each week’s survey featured a new question about students’ experiences within the context of the course, school more broadly, and/or their personal lives.

Quantitative data from the EoS engagement survey wa s segmented based on the target dimension of engagement, individual versus collaborative setting, and course enrollment. Basic statistical descript ives and comparisons were calculated across all segments in addition to student course/instructor evaluation data. We then ran significance tests across courses for each dime nsion of engagement and the individual/collective ownership items. We additionally coded all qualitative data (i.e., focus group reports, exit-ticket survey responses, instructor observations, and responses to open-ended text-based survey questions) using two coding schemes.

## Findings & Discussion

Our analysis revealed that novice students were con fronted with several barriers to engagement in smal l group collaborative settings which we seek to address in the next implementation of this course. First, we recognize that ICLS2022 Proceedings 2072 © ISLS students' lack of behavioral engagement could be attributed to a lack of instructional mechanisms for maintaining individual accountability in group settings. Gaps in cognitive engagement were frequently linked to students not understanding the value of collaborations for their  learning and thus, a lack of motivation to partici pate. Furthermore, novice students struggled to develop m eaningful relationships with their peers throughout  the semester – a phenomenon students attributed to the inconsistent and random group assignment and lead to a lack of group regulatory interactions.

Thus, we propose the following tweaks to novice students’ instruction: (1) negotiating longer-term group assignments during class rather than relying on random weekly assignment, (2) introducing low-stakes ‘peer/group evaluations' to more effectively identify and respond to moments of labor imbalance in gro ups, (3) requiring that in-class work is submitted to the in structor to allow for monitoring of individual stud ents contributions, and (4) increasing transparency around the ‘point’ and value of collaborative learning activities for each content area.

For seniors, small group collaborative settings enabled the sharing of diverse ideas and perspectives; they recognized that collaborative activities aided in developing interpersonal skills authentic to those they will need after graduation. However, students cited frustrations around the lack of connection between concepts addressed in individual versus group settings. Thus, we take a similar approach as described above and plan to e xplicitly mention target concepts and practices for each given activity to draw connections between the multiple learning objectives. We also plan to review the current curriculum to better align the core concepts and learning objectives between individual and group activities.

We additionally suggest that student groups could b enefit from the integration of mechanisms during large group discussions to make participation be pe rceived as less ‘high stakes.’ To do this, we hope to design discussion spaces where contribution is both more a ccessible and less intimidating for students. We an ticipate better incorporating multimodal means of participat ion (the chat, reactions, polls, asynchronous feedb ack, collaborative whiteboards etc.) into group discussions to enable students to choose the mode of participation that is most comfortable for them as opposed to not enga ging entirely. These approaches to increasing multi modal participation would prove particularly valuable for  identifying students’ misconceptions as they arise, opening interactional opportunities for group regulation and negotiation, leverage synchronous online tools in instruction as an asset rather than a limitation.

To conclude, we affirm that there is no ‘one size f its all’ instructional design solution for collaborative learning activities in computing and data science e ducation, but that the students’ unique barriers, p erceived objectives, and learning preferences be considered in conjunction with affordances of the learning env ironment to inform theoretically grounded yet context and audience-appropriate instruction.
