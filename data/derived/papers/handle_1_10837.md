# Designing Participatory Teacher Learning Environments: Inquiries, Methods, and Perspectives from Learning Sciences

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teacher educators seek approaches to design teacher learning environments that empower teachers to address situated problems of teaching by shaping their own learning. This symposium provides a learning sciences perspective on these efforts, sharing insights from six studies for designing and examining participatory teacher learning environments (PTLEs). The lessons derived provide an initial framework for designing PTLEs, highlighting three shared features: creating alternative forms of relationality, drawing on histories for transformation, and engaging in collective future-making for agentic teacher learning.

Symposium objective Teacher educators have increasingly recognized that  teaching problems are socially embedded, deeply si tuated, and complex (Horn & Garner, 2022). This points to t he need to design more participatory and flexible t eacher learning environments, offering teachers space, tim e, and support to reflect on and make sense of thei r problems of practices, while agentically engaging their prof essional learning and problem solving processes thr ough multiple resources, such as scientific knowledge in  their field, their pr ofessional experiences, and relationships. Designing such teacher learning environments requir es a dialogic approach between research-based knowledge and practice-based knowledge on teaching rather than the usual h ierarchy between these, which is not an ea sy endeavor for teacher educators (Cochran-Smith & Lytle, 1999). However, our field, the learn ing sciences, provides theoretically robust perspectives and meth ods to contribute to these efforts and to study the  learning processes within what we call participatory teacher learning environments (PTLEs). To illustrate with just a few of these precedents, we look to co-design partnerships (Penuel et al., 2007) and parti cipatory design research (Gutiérrez et al., 2016), where the latter refers t o a diverse range of approaches, all drawing on Fre irean critical pedagogy and/or Vygotskian socio-cultural theories of learning (Freire, 2000; Vygotsky, 1997). Independently of the learning sciences, some teacher education proje cts have developed their own methods, having many intersections with these approaches.

To consolidate these approaches, this symposium bri ngs together research conducted by learning scientists who have designed PTLEs. Each paper disc usses an aspect of their designs that invites teach ers to participate in their own learning. During the sympo sium, we seek to generate insights for the design a nd implementation of PTLEs more broadly, as well as approaches to analysis of the learning processes within them. Issues highlighted in this collection of papers help us to derive some initial lessons in the design of PTLEs. First, the fundamental element of a PTLE is relationality that counters traditional hierarchical role distributions in teacher learning.  All papers provide us insights about this aspect, w hile Papers 3, 5, and 6 specifically shed light on this issue. Esboldt (Pap er 3) brings an important concept, politicized trust (Vakil & McKinney de Royston, 2019), into our attention in coach-teacher relations, uncovering its influence in giving rise to very different enactments of standardized progra ms. Aldemir et al. (Paper 5) extend the meaning of "participation" in PTLEs by focusing on how student participation in co-design partnerships helps teachers reflect on their traditional roles in teacher-student relationships and experience transformative  teacher learning toward imagining new teacher roles for more democratic cla ssrooms. Chen (Paper 6) makes the case that researc hers’ deep reflection on their own emotions, experiences,  and perspectives is valuable in transforming teach erresearcher relations to achieve PTLEs. Second, PTLEs provide teachers with tools, space, and time to reflect on their current problems, questions, and extant understandings and use their own history as a resource for transformative and ag entic learning. Three papers specifically highlight this aspect of their designs. Shapiro et al. (Paper 1) focus on ho w a dynamic visualization tool supports teachers in unr aveling and bringing their own questions to the PTL E space. In Harmon (Paper 4), we see how the personal and co llective histories of Black women become a tool for  them to reflect on their current teaching ideologies. Günal-Aggül and Öner (Paper 2) discuss how teachers found space to draw on their histories in collectively constructing teaching concepts in novel ways. Any learning activity is future-oriented, aiming for change in perspectives and practices. However, who designs and owns this future is mostly ignored in c ontrolled learning spaces. Alternatively, as the th ird feature, PTLEs are spaces in which teachers are agents in de signing the future of their practices in dialogue w ith their colleagues, researchers/teacher educators, and stud ents while collectively working toward creating mor e equitable and meaningful learning environments for students. To become a PTLE, teachers need to experience the second and third features together in achieving  praxis (Freire, 2000). In Shapiro et al., along with refle ction on their histories, we see how the PTLE design supported teachers to transform their classroom space and control their own learning process. Günal -Aggül and Öner show how teacher educators candraw on the method of formative interventions  (Engeström, 2011) and the concept of third space  (Gutiérrez, 2014) while supporting teachers’ collective future-making. Harmon exemplifies how Black woman pre-service teachers found space to be agents of transformation for equity instead of objects of ‘diversity’ projects designed by others. During the 60-minute symposium, each author will give a ~7 min in dividual presentations. For the last 20-minute, we will facilitate a group discussion.

Paper 1: Supporting teachers’ agentic sensemaking and video-based formative feedback: An interaction geography perspective Ben Rydal Shapiro, Elizabeth Metts, Yeliz Günal Aggül and Ilana Horn Objectives As teacher educators have increasingly acknowledged the situated and complex nature of teaching, teacher agency gained more attention as a major outcome in meaning-centered teacher learning environments (Horn & Garn er, 2022). Whereas traditional teacher learning environ ments constrain teachers’ agency through static and predetermined learning processes and outcomes, recent work is exploring environments that use new methods to empower teachers’ agentic sensemaking. Informed by this work, we have been developing a vi deo-based formative feedback (VFF) cycle in a participatory design research project. In this paper, we explore how a method called interaction geography supports this process and teachers’ agentic sensema king by providing ways to dynamically visualize classroom video data (Shapiro et al., 2024 https://www.interactiongeography.org)

## Theoretical framework

To operationalize agency, we draw on the notion of mediated praxis (Freire, 2000; Gutiérrez & Vossoughi, 2010; Vygotsky, 1997). We define teacher agency as teache rs’ mediated reflection and action to control their professional learning and problem-solving processes by employing social, material, and conceptual resources. To support teachers’ agentic sensemaking, learning environments need to provide space and support so that teachers can connect the past to the future of their activities, empowering them to become historical actors in shaping their future practices (Gutiérrez et al., 2016). Based on this perspective, we explore how a dynamic visualization tool called the interaction geography slicer (IGS) mediated teachers’ praxis within the VFF.

## Data sources and methods

As a part of a larger research practice partnership  with a professional development organization suppo rting experienced mathematics teachers’ learning around c ollaborative student groupwork, we conducted VFF cy cles with 12 participating teachers focusing o n groupwork monitoring practices (VFF-M). VFF-M cycles centered teachers’ questions of interest around their monito ring work. We collected and analyzed detailed video  lesson data with the teacher’s question in mind. Finally, we facilitated a collaborative, reflective debrief with the teacher and colleagues using the IGS to view and discuss the teacher’s questions alongside their movement, conversation, and video clips (see IGS visualization at: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wIyVYCuPxl0).

## Results

Through a grounded, thematic analysis (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) of VFF-M debriefs across the year, we identified several themes illustrating ways the IGS supported teachers’ agentic sensemaking. First, the IGS serve d as a mediating tool for teachers’ praxis in reflecting o n their use of classroom space, making sense of the ir current problems and dilemmas, envisioning alternative ways of designing space, and transforming their classroom space to more closely align with their  pedagogical commitments. Second, the IGS encouraged  teachers’ exploratory analysis and questioning to support co-inquiry into teachers’ identified questions, while triggering them to ask more questions. Finally, teachers were not only ent husiastic about the possibilities of incorporating this tool into their reflections on practice, but actively co-designed new features they desired, including ways of coding movement, visualizing conversation, and describing patterns of movement and space.

## Significance

Our findings emphasize how integrating interaction geography within the VFF-M process offered new ways not only for teachers to reflect on spatial and mobile dimensions of their teaching practice, but empowere d them to critically discuss and advance ways t hey designed their classroom spaces and used their movement and physical space to support student learning. More generally, this work highlights the value and limitations of d ynamic visualization tools such as the IGS when integrated with video-based reflection processes to develop more agentic participatory teacher learning environments in the future. Paper 2: Putting everyday and scientific knowledge into dialogue: Teachers’ concept construction in a participatory teacher learning environment Yeliz Günal Aggül and Diler Öner Objectives Research on teacher communities has revealed that t eachers’ concept development is complex, reflecting  the tensions between the teachers’ situated needs and scientific knowledge of teaching, especially when the latter are presented as fixed (Horn & Kane, 2 019). This study examines how the learning practice s in a PTLE designed to support a group of teachers in designing their professional learning community help to overcome this problem.

## Design and theoretical framework

To support teachers in creating their community, we  designed a PTLE following the method of formative interventions (Engeström, 2011). In formative interventions, researchers support practitioners in producing novel solutions to their situated problems thr ough a series of sequential activities organized ac cording to Engeström’s

(2016) theory of expansive learning. The seven expansive learning actions are questioning and analyzing problems

in an activity, modeling, examining, and implementing a novel solution, reflecting on the process, and consolidating the solution. We designed the PTLE accordingly and supported the participants in modeling their community as a solution to the problems they identified. Here, focusing on the modeling action, we will examine how teachers’ everyday experience and scientific knowledge interacted with each other in third spaces (Gutiérrez,

2014) as they were discussing and defining the concepts of teaching as a part of the community building process.

## Methods

Nine participants teach at different schools and ar e active in a non-profit called Teachers Network (TN) which provides various support for teachers in working in  professional communities. Throughout the 1.5-year partnership with the participant teachers a nd TN, 30 community meetings were held. For analysi s, initially the expansive learning actions were coded in the transc ripts of community meetings and then, for further a nalysis of concept formation during modeling action, we followed conversation analysis methods (Goodwin, 2018).

## Results

As they questioned and analyzed their problems, com munity teachers identified the most pressing common problem as the lack of a uniting discourse in Turke y around which mathematics educators could collabor ate for transformation. In response to this, they decided t o define their community's pedagogical perspective by leveraging scientific resources and curriculum fram eworks. This marked an intermediate step in modelin g their community as a solution. They read the resources se lected through mutual negotiation and discussed them in the co-designed meetings where they created their own defi nitions for the fundamental teaching concepts (e.g., tool use in mathematics education or the equity principle). Analysis of these conversations revealed a common pattern of collective construction of teaching concepts in this PTLE. Across cases, teachers took the official scripts as the point of departure and rearticulated utilizing their conceptual resources and drawing on representations from their teaching practice. Importantly, teachers did not initially tend to challenge the official sc ripts. As they became comfortable within a safe space free from a hierarchical mindset, they engaged with  authentic discussions and asked challenging and productive questions  mobilizing their collective reasoning  and various resources. This happened specifically when the official resources tended to present teaching concepts as prescriptive, overlooking the situated and complex nature of teaching practic e and the scientific discussions and controversies in the field. The tensions between official and counter scripts gave rise to third spaces in which teachers expanded discourse and practices at both spaces and formed hybrid concepts in developing ownership over new teaching concepts.

## Significance

This study reveals that PTLEs can be productive sit es of meaningful teacher learning, where scientific  and everyday knowledge can expand rather thandistort e ach other, allowing teachers to take control of the ir professional learning and become agents in envisioning and designing their future practices. Paper 3: Epistemologies of knowledge, learning, and authority in teacher equity learning Joy Esboldt Objectives Teacher mentoring and coaching, given their ongoing  and inquiry-driven nature, offer promising professional development (PD) design to foster teacher learning around equity and justice. However, such PD programs often must juggle competing demands and potentially hiera rchical structures. Furthermore, studies on mechani sms, processes, and possibilities of mentoring and coaching to support teachers’ equity learning are relatively thin. This paper examines a  teacher coaching program aimed at fostering equity learning. It asks  how epistemologies of knowledge, learning, and authority shape learning r elationships and trajectories and what implications  for re-design these findings might offer.

## Theoretical framework

To understand how epistemologies of knowledge, learning, and authority enabled and constrained equity learning within the program design, this paper builds from the focus on teacher learning in communities through interaction rather than hierarchy (Cochran-Smith & Lytle, 1999) and Vakil and McKinney de Royston’s (2019) work on how politicized trust, a form of understanding, respect, and solidarity with specific attention to relations of race and power, shapes learning.

## Data sources and methods

This paper draws from a research-practice-partnership with a teacher coaching program collected as preliminary research following a program re-design intended to center equity. The program provided ongoing PD focused on core mentoring/coaching practices, tools designed t o comply with state requirements, and discursive ap proaches for centering discussions of equity. Coaches met with teachers weekly, focusing on a teacher-selected goal. In this paper, I draw on critical interactive and discourse analysis from two teacher-coach cases.

## Results

Both teacher-coach pairs demonstrated moments of politicized trust. Ho wever, their varying values, regarding types of knowledge, learning processes, and authori ty opened and foreclosed opportunities for equity l earning through fostering respect and solidarity.

Case one: Two white women educators at the same school shared  content expertise representing high-fidelity to program design. The coach provided ongoing, drop-in help and curriculum planning. The pair focused on breaking down core practices, walking through step-by-step explanations, and planning for future classes to cover the required curriculum. The coach’s implicit authority alongside the authority of offic ial resources influenced the direction of their conversations, even when discussing areas outside of the coach’s expertise. Case two: A non-binary teacher of color and a retired white man too k a more flexible orientation to program design. They broadly shared content area expertise (science), but the coach was less familiar with the school-site structures. However, the pair adopted a collaborati ve learning orientation  guided more by teacher agency and praxis (Freire, 2000). In their time together, they centered students’ perspectives, took an exploratory approach to what worked in the classroom, and found a balance b etween complying with the program requirements and prioritizing the teachers’ sense of what was meaningful.

## Significance

This study extends understanding of the importance of trust in mentoring and coaching relationships to  attend to politicized trust as educators engage around equity. In doing so, it sheds light on the relational and  political dynamics within empirical tensions resulting from competing demands. Furthermore, it has implications for how educators and PD design might navigate and negotiate these tensions to support transformative equity learning. Paper 4 Centering black women to reimagine teacher education Mariah Harmon Objectives For years, educators have championed numerous strategies for supporting marginalized students in U.S. schools. Recently, researchers have identified racial matchi ng—ensuring teachers of color work with students of si milar racial backgrounds—to improve stud ents’ achievement. However, calls to increase teach er diversity emphasize benefits to students without adequate consideration  of minoritized teachers’ developmental needs or hi stories. Furthermore, while extensive literature describes the rich experiential knowledge that Black teachers bring to the teaching profession, the process of developing it i nto pedagogy is undertheorized—particularly in ways that will sustain them in the classroom. In this paper, I inv estigate Caresha and Sade’s, two Black women pre-service teachers’ (BWPSTs), teaching ideologies by tracing their conversations in a teacher education counters pace. As with teachers from other marginalized groups, even when teacher education centers issues of equity and  justice, it seldom considers their particular developmental needs. Rather than positioning BWPSTs as agentic le arners, policymakers too often position them as objects to solve “diversity” issues. By uncovering Black women’s unique development as teachers, this study offers one possible design for teacher education to invest in their development as culturally responsive educators.

## Theoretical framework

To understand the complexities of Caresha and Sade’ s learning development, I use three key concepts: (1) teaching ideologies, (2) Black girlhood/Black femin ism/intersectionality, and (3) ghosts and hauntings. By looking at the development of teaching ideologies from an intersectional, Black feminist perspective, hauntology helps explain how the afterlives of slavery (Shange, 2019) permeate Black women’s experiences of becom ing teachers in U.S. schools. Specifically, Black women  are haunted by different aspe cts of their schooling experiences, and their teaching ideologies are shaped by their negotiations with the ghosts of those experiences.

## Data sources and methods

This study is a part of a larger research study called (B)lack+(W)oman+(T)eachr (BWT) where ten undergraduate and graduate BWPSTs participated in eight virtual c ounterspace sessions occurring monthly. A countersp ace refers to a “safe space” that lies in th e margins outside of mainstream educational spaces (Ong. et al., 2018). In the tradition of social design research (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016), I used ethnographic methods to understand the meanings participants derived from their experiences in BWT (Emerson et al., 2011). I invited each participant to three interviews over the course of the year to understand these individuals’ histories and experiences in becoming a teacher, with a particular eye on their experiences as K-16 students and in their teacher education programs.

## Results

To investigate each research question, I combined theoretical tenets from Black Feminist Epistemologies (Collins,

1990) and practical tenets from the Listening Guide  (Gilligan et. al, 2006). By grounding my work in B lack

Feminism, I found The Listening Guide to be a helpful analytical tool to help illuminate the voices of the BWPSTs in this study. I found that each woman was haunted by ghosts from her Black girlhood. By unpacking their ghosts from their schooling experiences, I found that thes e ghosts shaped their teaching ideologies and exper iences in their teacher education programs.

## Significance

This study offers new ways to consider the learning needs of BWPSTs. The design principles in this study provide a model for supporting the developmental needs of  minoritized teachers through shared inquiry and socioemotional connection.  By denying the assumption that BWPSTs are saviors for Black students by virtue of their shared race, this study provides a foundation for teacher education programs to support minoritized teachers in understanding their ghosts and histories in an effort to build more liberatory teaching ideologies. Paper 5: Teachers as co-thinkers and facilitators: Unpacking teacher-student dynamics in co-design partnerships Tugce Aldemir, Jooeun Shim and Susan Yoon Objectives Collaborative design (co-design) partnership aims to enhance educational inn ovations and teacher development through sustained collaborations (Penuel et al., 20 07). However, the frequent underrepresentation of s tudents in co-design may jeopardize innovation sustainability (Owston, 2007). Integrating students presents challenges due to complex power dynamics (Irgens et al., 2023), especially for teachers who face the challenge of balancing these dynamics for inclusive partnership (Higgins et al., 2019). This study focuses on a three-year STEM project (Yoon et al., 2023) and explores how to design collaborat ive spaces that support teachers in navigating thes e changes. We ask, how did teacher and student partners perceive teachers’ role evolut ion in the co-design process? What challenges and opportunities emerged, as reflected by the partners? Co-design partnership Co-design partnerships combine diverse expertise to in novate in education, emphasizing participatory lear ning (Irgens et al., 2023). As students join co-design, teachers’ roles shift, involving task redistribution and managing changing power dynamics, evolving from traditional participants to collaborative knowledge creators (Vossoughi et al., 2021). Here, we examine teachers’ role evol ution to enhance inclusive co-design partnerships as participatory teacher learning spaces.

## Methods

This study, part of a broader initiative to enhance  a 20-lesson bioinformatics curriculum (Yoon et al., 2023), involved a 2-day workshop in 2023. Day one focused on reviewing classroom data for potential improvements, and the second day aimed to establish design objectives and make revisions accordingly. The workshop included two experienced science teachers, two junior and se nior students nominated for active class participat ion, and three researchers who facilitated the event. We con ducted post-workshop interviews with teacher and student participants to gather their perceptions of the wor kshop (∼40 minutes each). Our thematic analysis centered on reflections on role evolution, navigation strategies, and the challenges and opportunities that arose. Findings were refined using inductive analysis and consensus-building discussions (Saldaña, 2016).

## Results

Teachers and students as co-thinkers. Participants emphasized the importance of student i nvolvement in the codesign process and observed cooperative dynamics em erging as they identified problems and solutions. T hese contributions were noted to draw from each actor's unique experiential lens, allowing for comparing teachers’ and students’ perspectives. However, due to the teacher s' greater curriculum design expertise, they tended  to overshadow the students, leaving little room for student reflection. To address this, participants sug gested separating teachers and students into two groups: one f or teachers and researchers to co-design and another for students to assess and critique teacher decisions. Even in separate Zoom rooms, teachers recognized th e value of co-thinking, as student feedback on their decisions spurred meaningful reflections on their instructional strategies and design practices.

Teachers as facilitators of student empowerment. All participants acknowledged the power dynamic bet ween students and teachers due to their traditional clas sroom roles. Nevertheless, teachers made efforts to  empower student voices, promoting a diverse environment and  integrating student suggestions into the design pr ocess. Despite these efforts, students hesitated to critique teachers' designs, a sentiment also shared by the teachers. This prompted the suggestions of separating student-teacher partners or pairing them with individuals f rom different schools. The sustained unbalanced power dynamics between  teachers and students also led teachers to further contemplate facilitation tactics aimed at bolstering student empowerment.

## Significance

The field seeks research on establishing sustainabl e research-practice partnerships with diverse expertise and equitable representation (Farrell et al., 2023). Ho wever, incorporating students into partnerships inv olves managing role changes and power dynamics. This stud y explores how teachers' roles evolve when students  join our co-design partnership, providing insights for designin g collaborative spaces that foster student-inclusive codesign partnerships and support participatory teacher learning. Paper 6: Mathematical inheritances as participants in collaborative learning Grace A. Chen Objectives Research on collaborative designs for teacher learning often explores how co-design can position teachers as fuller participants in shaping the goals and trajectory of  professional development (e.g., this symposium). S uch work aims to honor teachers' agency in their own learnin g and to establish more egalitarian relationships b etween researchers and teachers. This paper examines how a researcher's participant-observation in teachers' learning environments shapes the learning for everyone.

## Theoretical framework

I draw on Vea's (2020) construct of guided emotion participation, which suggests that emotion is a tar get of learning as well as being  a contributor to learning; in other words, as they participate in collaborative conversations, teachers learn how to feel about the subjects of conversation, even when emotion is never explicitly named. In addition, I draw on affective inheritance s and mathematical inheritances, to illuminate how histories are carried in our bodies and propagated in our interactions (Chen, 2021).

## Data sources and methods

This data is part of a larger  ethnographically-informed study in which I followed Algebra 1 teachers across  a school year, acting as an observer and/or participant-observer in their classrooms and collaborative meet ings. Here, I analyze the audiorecording of a nearly two-hour collaborative meeting between three teachers, which was ostensibly about planning for an upcoming unit on sequences and series but detoured into a discussion of a teacher their students had previously had; the discussion w as unusual among these teachers' meetings for its e xplicit articulation of emotion and for the nature of my participation. Supplemented by reflective and integrative memos about my own experiences as a math student and teacher (Emerson et al., 2011), the current paper uses inductive coding and microanalytic methods (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to examine how I participated in constructing this previous teacher and how my participation shaped teachers' and my learning.

## Results

Findings from this analysis include how I legitimat ed particular ideas by assigning academic language to them and how I reflected the emotional terrain made avai lable to me as a woman of color (e.g., Jagger, 1989) by disclosing information I only had access to as a re searcher in a way that had the effect of guiding em otion participation for the group. Additionally, findings show how a researcher's ma thematical affective inheritances participate in collaborative learning. For example,  the analysis illustrates my fear of mathematical v iolence becoming normalized, such as when the consequences of dehumanizing pedagogical strategies are conflated with "productive struggle." This analysis underscores se veral themes about power in researcher-teacher relationships, such as the researcher's own professional vision sh aping what is considered worth discussing in  collaborative conversations; dilemmas about whether and how to di vulge information from student interviews to studen ts' teachers; and, the researcher's presumed omniscienc e in even an allegedly egalitarian conversation  where the researcher seemed to be following teachers’ leads.

## Significance

This paper builds on work “naming the histories and dynamics behind some of th e embodied and affective experiences” (Yoon, 2019, p. 448) of researchers working with te achers to expand “the domain of the ‘researched’… to include the relational, pedagogical, and design-based activity of researchers themselves” (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016, p.174). It  emphasizes how researchers design without designing, so to spe ak, as their participation co-creates learning environments regardless of -- and  perhaps despite-- their intent. Furthermore, it argues for attention to how mathematical inheritances and other histories participate in PTLEs.
