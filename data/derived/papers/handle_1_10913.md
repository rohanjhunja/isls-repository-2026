# Teacher Candidates Leveraging Children’s Picture Books to Plan Equity-Oriented Instruction

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how teacher candidates utilize children’s science picture books to design equity and social justice-oriented lessons. Teachers drew on references to diversity, equity, and social justice represented in the books, indicating the importance of high-quality resources for beginning teachers and demonstrating the teachers’ potential for criticality.

Our objective in this study was to investigate how teacher candidates (TCs) can become critical consumers of children’s science picture books and leverage these texts to develop an equity-orientation in their lesson planning. Focusing on how TCs enact anti-deficit practices will help us trace their trajectory towards enacting social justice in the classroom. Participants critiqued children’s picture books then wrote a les son plan that built on one of the books they critiqued. We focused this work on picture books because  of the key role they play in elementary science instruction  (e.g., Pringle & Lamme, 2005). Picture books have the potential to facilitate teacher s in addressing issues of race and racial justice in the ir classrooms (Husband, 2018).  Yet, little is known about how early career teachers begin this work. Thus, we asked, in what ways do TCs recognize equity-oriented themes in children’s picture books and highlight those themes in elementary science lesson plans? Framework for equity and social justice in science education Our work takes an anti-deficit perspective by switching the focus from wha t learners are missing to identifying what they bring with them. In doing so, we support new teachers in broadening their understand ing of what it means to “do science” and “be a science person” (Carlone, 2004). Stereotypes concerning who cando science and what counts as science persist in K-12 education, influencing what science taught and how it is taught in schools. Thus, we frame our work with TC s to engage their thinking about e quity and social justice (E&SJ). All students have the right to access to high quality science learning opportunities, thus we work with TCs to examine equity from the perspective of meeting students’ needs and  avoiding deficit perspectives through culturally sustaining pedagogies, such as funds of knowledge (NRC, 2012). We apply a social justice perspective with TCs as we take a critical lens to  the societal structures in place that control the distribution of resources and actions towards students manifesting in equitable or inequitable opportunities for scien ce education. Thus, we collaborate with TC to explore how achieving equity in science education will occur through transformative changes within unjust contexts and not through isolated or technical means (Morales-Doyle, 2017).  Mills & Ballantyne ’s (2016) systematic literature review found that teacher education programs, especially those that f ocus on E&SJ, can change TCs’ equity and social justice perspectives. However, barriers to change exist, such as  when TCs understandings of social justice may conflict with teaching practices or when these E&SJ perspectives were not supported in their field placements. Our study expl ores these issues by considering the ways an E&SJ o riented tool could facilitate TCs’ integration of E&SJ into their pedagogy through lesson plan writing.

## Methodology

Using qualitative methodology, we applied thematic analysis to investigate how TCs recognized and utilized E&SJ themes in children’s picture books  (mostly science but a few featured engineering or mathematics) and associated lesson plans. Participants in this study were  TCs (N=67) taking a science methods course during the semester prior to student teaching  at a large public research university in the northeastern Unit ed States. TCs studied E&SJ through guided readings and discussions, using a tool developed by the authors to critically analyze texts and media using multiple lenses  informed by the literature. After critiquing two picture books, one with a partner and one individually, the TCs wrote an elementary science lesson plan based on one of books. One of the books was from our collection of E&SJ picture books while the second was based on the TC’s own selection. We began by identifying lesson plans that included E&SJ themes, selecting a subsample of 11  lesson plans. Next, both authors collaboratively and iteratively read and suggested codes for each lesson plan, base d on concepts of E&SJ discussed in our course or manifesting through the lesson plan text. Next, we collaboratively coded ea ch picture book critique, using the same set of E&SJ codes, and adding new codes as they emerged in the analy sis. Finally, we created a chart (Figure 1) that allowed us to compare the TCs’ analyses of E&SJ in the book critiques with how they utilized these ideas in their lesson plans.

## Findings

We organized our findings around four themes constructed after comparing E&SJ codes for the picture book-based lesson plan to the coding for TCs’ critiques of the same book and a separate book  (Figure 1). First, TCs used ideas drawn from the picture books as the basis of the E&SJ elements of their lesson plan. Nine out of eleven lesson plans included the same 1 to 3 E&SJ elements as they identified in their critique of the  picture book used in writing the lesson. In addition, six lesson plans used the same E&SJ el ement(s) as the TCs identified in their critique of the secondary picture book, not used in  their lesson plan.  Second, the code representation was most frequently discussed in the critiques, but environmental justice and activism were most frequently represented in the lesson plans. Identifying how picture books  invoke issues of representation in science could be  a strength of TCs, or this could be an E&SJ element most readily conve yed by the picture books themselves, as we coded multiple ways that TCs discussed representation in science (e.g., race, gender, disability, bias, stereotypes). Third, while many TCs used elements of E&SJ directly from their LP’s pict ure book, five lesson plans introduced additional E&SJ ideas  not previously identified in the TC’s critique. For examp le, TC #9 utilized environmental justice and representation as key elements of her lesson plan, based on “Rosie Revere, Engineer.” However, when critiquing the book, she only identified representation as an element of E&SJ in the book; environmental engineering is not a theme of this bo ok but was utilized in her lesson planning.  Fourth, TCs were also able to recognize the absence of or opposite o f the E&SJ elements. For example, TC #10 critiqued a book, “Pirate, Viking, Scientists,” identifying the lack of representation, lack of community-oriented science, and that the book was not showing science practice as influenced by culture. These codes appeared in picture book critiques for books the TCs did not use as the basis of a lesson plan, showing the TCs’ potential for criticality. ## Discussion

This study shows TCs’ potential for recognizing and applying elements of E&SJ in their lesson plans. Our findings indicate the important role children’s science picture books can play in supporting new teachers recognizing and implementing E&SJ in their lesson plans. It may be that with appropriate modeling, practice, and scaffolds in place, picture books can be an accessible way for TCs to bring potentially controversial and complex idea s into lessons for young students. The tool we provided sc affolded TCs’ identification of E&SJ themes in the picture book may have provided some leverage to bring those themes into their lesson plans.
