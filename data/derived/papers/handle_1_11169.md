# River Crossings of our Childhood: Nezuko and Yasmine Dis/orient Computing Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We present a case of two youth who are sisters and newcomer immigrants of color in Canada as they work towards re-designing and re-imagining computational models entrenched in white, colonial, and masculine ideologies. Through their representational and epistemic work, we show how centering their desires, personal and sensory-affective histories candis/orient colonial and techno-centric approaches to computational modeling. We also advance a varied range of scholarship that critiques the deficit positioning of newcomer immigrant and refugee youth of color by centring the youths’ proleptic world-making.

## Introduction

Our work advances the call to reorient computing ed ucation in a contrapuntal manner (Said, 1991; Philip & Sengupta, 2021) by making visible and re-orienting the geopolitical contexts (Takeuchi, 2021), imperialist and colonial ideologies (Vakil, 2018; Philip & Sengupta, 2021) underlying computing and STEM education. Migration scholars have noted that racist and colonial ideologies further a top-down imposition of at-risk identity on immigrant and refugee youth of color in the Glob al North (Banerjee, 2022; Ray, 2017), and systemic racism, and racial stereotyping and microaggressions may further marginalize them in computing education (Margolis et al., 2012; Ryoo & Margolis, 2022). We seek to advan ce scholarship in the learning sciences that critiq ue such deficit positionings of learners from non-dominant communities in the context of informal computing education, and argue that by centring historically marginalized learners’ desires (Leander & Boldt, 2018) and their personal, sensory-affective histories can off er a necessary dis/orientation (Ahmed, 2006) of col onial and technocentric approaches (Ames, 2019; Sengupta et al., 2021) to computational modeling, while also supporting their affective engagement with coding.

## Theoretical background

Critical migration and youth studies  have criticiz ed the imposition of a deficit framework on immigra nt and refugee youth of color in North America (Banerjee, 2022; Bernal, 2002; Ray, 2017, 2022) According to t his scholarship, the top-down deficit framewo rk imposed on these youth frames them as a populati on that requires appropriate intervention to become productive membe rs of the host society in North America. This frame work typically involves representing these youth as lack ing various capabilities like language skills, expe riencing dissonance in cultural identity and socio-economic status, that makes them at-risk of “maladaptive behaviour and negative participation in the society” (Kilbride and Anisef, 2001, p. i). Such critical scholarship instead argues for making visible and explicit the colonial, racialized, and gendered violence that are at the core of these institutional processes.

Synergistic scholarship in the Learning Sciences have adapted the framework of prolepsis (Rommetveit, 1974; Cole, 2013) for centering marginalized learners and their cou nter-imaginations of their futures that are rooted in their cultural histories while resisting dominant narratives of deficit imposed on racialized and gendered youth in North America (Gutiérrez, 2016; Sengupta e t al., 2022). For example, Shaw et al. (2023) shows  how Black womanist storytelling can counter white misogynoir imaginations in comput ing education. Vakil et al. (2023)’s participatory research work also shows how  Black youth experiencing racial profiling through various technological surveillance systems in the city of C hicago engaged in developing critical technological  literacies to counter the policing faced by their communities.  Sengupta et al. (2022) offer a critical phenomenol ogical framework for dis/orienting dominant computational models of racial segregation rooted in White Innocence (Gotanda, 1994; Gutiérrez, 2006) through centring experiences of immigrants of color in North America and Dalit phenomenological perspectives at the intersections of caste and class in India. Collectively, this body of work suggests that both computational representations and the epistemic and representational work involved in creating computat ional models may need to be dis/oriented (Ahmed, 20 06) away from the White, male gaze that underpins h egemonic approaches in computing education (Ames, 2 019; Sengupta et al., 2021) in which bodies of marginali zed learners of color are positioned as a spectacle  of performances” by White and privileged researchers, who in turn “are fixated on ‘saving’ the disenfranc hised...” (Harrington et al., 2019, as cited in Rankin et al., 2020, p. 69). In this paper, we expand and deepen  this body of scholarship by offering a computational simulation of a popular Arcade game rooted in a White colonist imaginary-the Lunar Lander (Atari, 1979) - as the site of a proleptic dis/orientation through centering voices of refugee,

women youth of color. In doing so we ask the follow ing research question:  what are the various affective, embodied, and relational histories that come to fore in a proleptic space that offer critiques to white, techno-centric and colonial ideologies of technoscientific learning spaces? We also draw from extant scholarship that offer var ious axiological orientations of designing learning spaces that support women, youth, and migrant learn ers of color in such proleptic world-making. Leander and Boldt (2018) remind us of the importance of c entering learners’ affect and desires-as opposed to the designers’ intentions-in our learning environments. Vossoughi et al. (202 0) and Espinoza et al. (2020) orient us to the importance of centering educational dignity of marg inalized learners through  recognizing and centering ethical trails of dignity-affirming micro-interactions in learning spaces. Vea (2020) and Jab er & Hammer (2016) orient our attention to the importance of recognizing the role that emotions play in learning. Collectively, these papers remind us of attending to participants’ affect-which we positioned in Ahmed’s words as “visceral forces beneath, alongside, or generally other than conscious knowin g that can serve to drive us toward movement, thoug ht and ever-changing forms of rela tion” (Ahmed, 2010, p 29). Takeuchi (2015) and Kayumova  & Sengupta (2022) further orient us to the importance of attending to  heterogeneity in the discourse and representationa l work of multilingual learners, noting that is not merely an epistemic or representational concern, rather an axiological one. Critical gender and migration scholars orient our a ttention to listening contrapuntally (Gilligan, 2015, p. 72) to the voices of women that reveal their ethic of care, rather than reifying institutional “mora l language of justice and rights” (p. 72). For immigrant women of color, this ethic of care may be rooted in feelings of sol idarity, as well as feelings of loss of dignity, suffering and hope that underlie forced migration, that may also be racialized and gendered at once (Banerjee, 2022; Banerjee and Sengupta, 2023).

## Study context, methods, and analytical approach

We present a case of co-designing a computational model with two female youth of color (Nezuko and Yasmine), who are recent immigrants to Canada from a politica lly disputed region in South Asia. The workshops we re conducted as part of a larger, ongoing research stu dy that seeks to develop multi-modal, proleptic anti-racist visions of resettlement experiences in Canada with newly arrived immigrant and refugee youth of color. Working with the researcher s over a period of eight months, the youth led the co-design of multi-modal, storied artifacts such as animation films, documentaries, musical pieces, murals, and computational simulations that centered their stories of migration and experiences around being racialized and gendered newcomer youth in Canada. The computational simulation workshops, facilitated  by the first author, aimed at redesigning and re-imagining the Lunar Lander  model (Wilensky, 2005) that  simulates a spaceship landing on the lunar surface (see Figure 1(a)). The youth (twin sisters) noted that they have bee n routinely excluded from meaningful STEM and computing learning opportunities in their school, and chose to work with the Lunar Lander simulation in order to learn coding. They participated in 5 sessions each of which were approximately 4-5 hours in duration. Data includes participants’ constructed artifacts (code,  simulations, and drawings), and video and audio-recording of conversations and participants’ interactions with each other and with the researchers. We adopted a constructivist grounded theoretical approach (Charmaz, 2014) as our goal was to focus on how and why “participants construct meanings in specific situations” (p. 239). We used the constant comparative method (Charmaz, 2014) to establish and maintain a dialogical relationship between theoretical perspective s outlined earlier and the data. Using this process, we identified ethical-affective design turns as the key emergent category that helped us understand how the epistemic and representational work of creating and modifying computational representations was progressively dis/oriented by the participants. These “turns” represent a chain of conversations, embodied interactions and represe ntational work of the participants that eventually resulted in changes in the code and the simulation interface. E ach of these turns represent both ethical and affec tive reorientations of the original computational mode, as  shown in Table l (see Table 1). For example, in De sign Turn 1, as fans of BTS (Korean pop music band), the sist ers transformed the lunar terrain into a purple-hued BTS concert space. This led to an ethical-affective turn where the objective of the simulation was shifted away from a colonial imaginary of landing. Instead, listening to BTS music and buying their mer chandise, which were also rooted in the desires of the participants,  were positioned as essential to gain access to the new land. Design Turn 2 illustrates a further reorientation of the objectiv e of the simulation from understanding the Newtonia n physics of the landing to a personal exploration of a memor y of river-crossing. Design Turn 3 illustrates further, multimodal deepening of the grounding of the computational representations in the memories of the participants. ## Findings

We present a summary of each of the design turns ma de by Nezuko and Yasmine that highlights the variou s ethical and affective dimensions of the turns and t he entailing representational and computational abs tractions. We then present some significant discur sive and embodied micro-interactions for each of the Design Turns that reveal the entanglement of Nezuko and Yasmine’s code with various ethical, affective, and relational dimensions of their lives as immigrant young Muslim women in Canada. Design Turn 1: Reorientation of lunar lander to create BTS world Nezuko and Yasmine’s first iteration centered their admiration for the Korean pop music group BTS. They created a BTS world that shifted the lunar terrain into a BTS concert space where fans were landing to attend the concert (See Figure 1(b)).

First they changed the color of the terrain to purp le, which represented the color of the BTS fandom. Next, they added two more turtles and changed their  shapes to create a BTS house, where BTS concerts w ere going on, and the other into a second moon (other than the existing moon in the model) that they called the “BTS Army moon”. Finally, they changed the shape of the existing lander to create their own rocket-shaped lander, and created a second lander in the shape of a flower, that when landing on the territory changed into a butterfly, instead of a white skull that w as part of the seed model that denoted an unsuccess ful landing of the spaceship. Towards the end of the session, Nezuko and Yasmine also dec ided on a landing experience for the people who arrived at their BTS land. It would include purchasing BTS mer chandise that would be duplicates of clothes that m embers of the BTS music group wear; listening and purchasing BTS music; and watching their music videos. In this Design Turn, Nezuko and Yasmine’s playful engagement with code (Sengupta & Shanahan, 2017) that centered their desire (Leander & Bolt, 2018) o f building a computational world that centers their  favorite music band. At the same time, this is also evidence  of  dis/orientations along two dimensions. Along t he first dimension, Nezuko and Yasmine eliminated the gamified design of the original Lunar Lander simulation in which the focus was on using mechanistic reasoning and predictive logic for landing successfully on the terrain, and this can be seen as a dis/orientation of a colonist and masc uline image computing in which learning to control the mechanics of landing is inextricably intertwined wi th the act of colonizing a planetary body. Along th e second dimension, they also offered a proleptic dis/orientation (Ahmed, 2006) of the procedurality of immigration by restorying the requirements of landing and immigratin g to their BTS world: Rather than any arbitrary cri teria of capabilities, pedigree, and wealth, people landing in their world must be(come) BTS fans. Design Turn 2: Reorientation of lunar terrain to model walk across a river The second iteration of the redesigning process foc used on shifting the setting of the model to memories of their home country from their childhood. While designing how they would want to alter the setting of the mod el, the sisters shared a memory of a journey to their aunt’ s home as children in their home country. They were accompanied by their family and crossed through a valley on foot. In the journey, they crossed a river which was surrounded by mountains and forests. They remembere d that the flowing water felt really cold to their bare feet, and that the river had rocks in it which they had to walk through. As the first author conversed with her about what kind of search terms can possib ly show her the results, she scrolled through multi ple photos provided by the search and was able to identify the  trees she was trying to remember and include in he r sketch. The photo was of a forest of Chinaar trees surrounding a valley. Nezuko:  She said if you sleep you’re going to go there Megha:  If you sleep you are going to go there?

In the above exchange, as Nezuko identified the tre es from her childhood memory, she uttered in a low voice that she wanted to go there now [line 2]. She giggled, and went silent, for a few seconds after which she said, “Okay yeah,” in a resigned tone while bringin g her focus back to her  work. During this entire exchange, Yasmine maintained her attention on her work of designing a dandelion-lander that would repurpose the code of the lander from the seed model. After Nezuko utter ed, “Okay yeah,” there was silence  in the room for a few seconds. Yasmine then sa id, again in a lowered voice, “If you sleep you are g oing to go there”, a respite that Yasmine meant to offer her sister. While the silence after Nezuko’s resigned tone made it apparent that there was nothing else to do about Nezuko’s wish to be able t o go back to their homeland, except for her to focu s on her work, Yasmine’s words offered dreaming as a way out. Nezuko instantly started giggling after that and clarified to Megha what Yasmine had said. Yasmine further emphasized h er hope in response tonezuko’s yearning by saying, “.. one day we will go, one day” The yearning expressed by Nezuko and the respite th at Yasmine offers to her must be considered in the context of their transnational lives: they haven’t been able to visit their home  country since they left it 6 years ago. In a conversation at one of the later workshop  sessions, they had mentioned that their family was  making plans for a visit in the summer of 2023, however, e ventually the plandidn’t come to fruition due to f inancial reasons, and because the sisters had to attend summer scho ol to cover up for school credits. Furthermore, in a follow up interview, the sisters shared how difficu lt it is to contact their aunt whose home they were  visiting as part of the walk through the valley, due to various  infrastructural issues in the area  owing to its status as a politically disputed region. In the backdrop of all these barriers, which makes avenues to connect to their homeland few and far between, Yasmine’s offer of dreaming to  relive their childhood and be back in their home c ountry is a way to hold out hope for her sister and herself. With this exchange, Nezuko and Yasmine imbued the simulation and the co-designing space with emotions and an ethic of care rooted in their personal memories. As they delved deeper into modeling the various det ails of what they could remember of that journey, Nezuko and Yasmine also carried forward certain ele ments from their first iteration of the BTS world, for example, coloring the river purple, to continue rep resenting their admiration for the music group in t heir second iteration of the model. They created a gradient in the shading of the purple color in the river to represent its flow: as the river flows from the upper part to the lower part of the screen, there is a shift in the shade of its color from darker to lighter to darker again (see Figure 2). Nezuko:  So it gets lighter and lighter. Okay, so like [Pauses] Let me talk in Urdu now. Megha:  Oh haan, aur voh din ka time bhi hai, toh voh yahaan reflect bhi ho jaayga – (Oh yeah, and since its daytime, there will be reflections on it too - -) Yasmine:  Haan reflect bhi ho raha hoga – (Yeah, it would be reflecting too --) Yasmine:  [turns tonezuko] Waah ji Waah! (an exclamation of praise and appreciation) The ethical-affective nature of this turn reveals itself in a few different ways here. Firstly, we must note that this conversation represents a turn away from an unspoken norm of speaking in English in institutional spaces in Canada, and toward using Urdu  (one of their primary languages at home).  As shown in the above transcript, Nezuko started to describe her design ideas for the shading of the river in her first language (“Let me talk in Urdu now”). At a later meeting, they shared wi th the facilitator (who also understood and spoke U rdu) that it was important for them to be in an educational space wh ere their language was understood and valued, so that their ideas and intentions regarding the design of the si mulation are not misrepresented. Secondly, they mod ified computational representations within the NetLogo pr ogramming language by altering shapes of computatio nal agents to represent  long and short ripples in the water, and placed the m alternatively to represent movement in the water of the river. These ripples were also positioned in accordance with the direction of the movement of the river: the upper and lower sections of the settled portions of the river were represented through horizontal ripples, and the middle portion flowing downwards was reflec ted through vertical ripples. A technocentric analysis here would focus on computational abstractions in the fo rm of the code and the algorithm (Sengupta et al., 2021), but what we seek to center instead is that the computat ional work was simply taking the shape of their des ire to recreate a sense-memory of feeling ripples around their feet. In the above exchange, Yasmine pointed at a specific frame in the animated clip which showed the sisters walking in the river, with ripples created as they wade through the waters. She expresse d that she wanted to include a similar  image of them walking in the river to be featured i n their representation o f the river in the simulation. Yasmine so wished to include this particular image of them because that frame from the animated clip closely follows the description of the river as previously detailed by them in Design Turn 2 (“Because me and her we are walking. So, we can add that in the river”). It became important for Yasmine to see their embodied presence in the simulation that had until now lacked any presence of human form. They downloaded and imported a stock image of two girls walking together  and positioned the image in the middle of the river in the simulation (See Figure 3). They chose the particular image since the two figures had their arms around each other, similar to their own style of walking with each other.

However, for Nezuko, the mere placement of the imag e did not fully capture their  embodied presence that she wanted to have in their simulation. Thus, with the guidance of a facilitator, they decided to  sketch some waves around the feet of the two figures.  As Nezuko was illustrating the flow of water by ske tching those wavy lines, she uttered, “The water is [now] moving.” Th is utterance was indicative of their continued desire to model the ripples of the river, in a way that would get c loser to their sense-memory of the river water flowing at their feet. Till Design Turn 2, the flow of the water had  been modeled through the code and parameters on Ne tlogo, that replicated the gradient in its shade and the m ovement and direction of the ripples. However, in D esign Turn 3, the inclusion of their embodied presence and the  sketching of the waves brought them closer to repr esenting the experience that they envisioned.

Reflective summary Nezuko and Yasmine’s redesign of a colonist simulation was not a politically and eth ically neutral act, as their work challenged and dis/oriented the white, colonia l and technocentric gaze that underlies many normat ive computer modeling activities (e.g., Ames, 2019; Sengupta et al., 2022). In Design Turn 1, reorienting a simulation away from masculine and colonist ideologies of control and exploration, toward a wor ld with music concerts of the participants’ favourite music group, is an exam ple of centering desires of learners (Leander & Bol dt, 2018) and valuing relational and affective dimensions of interactions wi th marginalized learners as central to the representational work (Kayumova & Sengupta, 2022). Secondly, the proleptic (Guti érrez, 2016) transformation of landing also offers a dis/orientation (Ahmed, 20 06) of visa regimes that are founded upon racist and gendered hierarchy of labour and resulting devaluation and dehumanisation of immigrants of color (Banerjee, 2022). Design Turns 2 and 3 offers further dis/orientations of the e xperience of landing from being a futuristic, techn ocentric colonial expedition to recreation of nostalgia in Azad Kashmir, that centers their recollections of sensory-affective details of crossing a river  in the Himalayan valleys. Design Turn 2 also reveal s the sisters’ affective space of yearning and hope towards their connection to their  homeland undergirding their computational producti on that further dis/orients the ahistorical and atemporal d ynamics of Lunar Lander. Design Turn 3 went beyond the superficial representation of bodies as avatars, and instead took the youth deeper into modeling their  sensory memory of water ripples flowing over their feet-a memory that could only be awakened with their eye s closed, in their mother tongue (Urdu).

So here is Nezuko and Yasmine’s gift for us: A visi on of coding that stands in contrast to  “a devicecentered discourse of control ” (Sengupta et al., 202 1, p. 24), and instead  is a journey back in time to their homeland, in a minoritized language that is often s ilenced and othered (Said, 1993) in their new land, enlivening sensory-affective memories of water flowing over their feet  on a crisp Fall morning. And in doing so, the technocentric, colonial id of computing (Philip & Seng upta, 2021; Vakil, 2018) crumbles, and instead what emerges is a vision of coding that is heteroglossic (Warren et al, 2020; Sengupta et al., 2021), affective (Vossoughi et al., 2020) and contrapuntal (Philip & Sengupta, 2021).
