# Extending Immersive Learning with Smell: An Olfactory-Enhanced Virtual Reality System

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We present VR-O, a virtual reality (VR) system that integrates olfaction into immersive learning environments. The project explores how adding scent to VR experiences candeepen engagement, support learning, and make simulations more authentic, particularly in domains where olfactory perception is central to expertise (e.g., water treatment, wine making). During this Interactive Tools and Demos session, participants will experience the olfactory VR setup firsthand and discuss pedagogical and technical implications for multisensory learning environments.

Virtual reality (VR) has become a powerful medium for experiential learning, allowing learners to explore complex systems, perform procedural tasks, and engage with abstract concepts through embodied interaction (Asad et al., 2021). Yet most VR learning environments remain limited to visual and auditory modalities, omitting senses such as smell that are integral to how humans perceive and remember experiences (Melo et al., 2020). Olfaction plays a particularly important role in many professional and everyday learning contexts. Domains such as wine making (Tempere et al., 2019), cheese production (Delahunty & Drake, 2004), and water treatment (Fox & Abbaszadegan, 2023; Spackman, 2024) depend on the ability to discriminate, describe, and identify odors. Teaching these skills is challenging: in some cases, physical materials may be costly, perishable, or difficult to standardize, while in other cases the opportunity to experience certain olfactory cues on the jobsite may happen only sporadically. These difficulties present an opportunity to create VR environments that integrate olfaction for use in educational settings. Specifically, integrating olfactory cues into VR learning environments can address physical, material, and temporal challenges by enabling: (1) lifelike practice in safe, reproducible conditions; (2) deeper engagement and motivation, as olfactory stimuli evoke strong emotional and mnemonic associations; and (3) richer multimodal learning, connecting smell with visual and linguistic representations. However, adding smell to digital environments presents technical hurdles such as accurate scent control, synchronization, and user comfort.

To explore these opportunities and challenges with a relevant test case, we designed our system to mimic a municipal water treatment facility’s laboratory, wherein water quality is tested not only through chemical means, but also through quick sensory assessment. This design choice aligns with coursework currently offered at our university, in which students study water quality through the lenses of environmental chemistry and public health. Incorporating olfaction into this context is especially valuable because smell is a crucial indicator of water quality: odors can signal the presence of organic contaminants, disinfection byproducts, or algal blooms, as well as issues with filtration and distribution infrastructure (Fox & Abbaszadegan, 2023). In professional practice, operators rely on visual inspection and sensory evaluation to detect anomalies that may not be apparent through measurement instruments alone. By recreating a water laboratory in VR, our system provides opportunities for learners to practice identifying and interpreting odor cues in a controlled, repeatable environment, preparing them for realworld scenarios that demand both technical understanding and olfactory acuity. System overview The VR-O system comprises two tightly coupled subsystems: the wearable hardware that delivers odors to the user and the software stack that controls odor timing and intensity. Hardware The wearable apparatus is a neck/shoulder rig modeled after a harmonica holder; the odor output is positioned directly beneath the user’s nose. The device houses four channels: clean air, low-geosmin, high-geosmin, and h igh-d-limonene. Each channel contains an odor chamber, a piezoelectric microblower, and a centrally mounted valve. Piezoelectric microblowers were chosen for their directional airflow and ability to act as a unidirectional seal, minimizing cross-contamination between channels. Odorants (liquid geosmin and d-limonene diluted in distilled water) are loaded into cut-to-size sponges positioned below the blower outlet to create headspace volatilization. Sponges rest atop self-healing injection ports so that odorant liquid can be added directly without opening the chamber or risking damage to the microblowers. For study sessions, we injected 15 mL of the prepared solution into each chamber prior to use; sponge size was chosen to provide headspace for steady volatilization during the session while limiting leakage.

Software and communications Peripherals (microblowers, valves/servos, and digital potentiometers) are controlled by an ESP32 development board. The ESP32 connects wirelessly to a local hotspot hosted on the experiment laptop, permitting serial commands to be transmitted over Wi-Fi. To facilitate the Wizard of Oz control flow we used in our user study, we implemented a simple web page that exposes an “On/Off” control per channel. When the wizard clicks “On,” the web page issues serial commands that actuate the servo to open the valve and modulate the microblower speed; clicking “Off” closes the valve and stops the blower. The scent delivery device included four independent channels. To avoid participants developing an association between the sound of the motor and the delivery of an odorant, one channel was reserved for clean, filtered air with no added odorants. The remaining three channels were used to deliver commonly encountered odors in water treatment contexts. Geosmin was included because a residual earthy odor is often encountered in treated drinking water in arid regions and is safe at the dilutions used in this study, while d-limonene was selected as a contrasting odor representing atypical, potentially anthropogenic contamination. To further evaluate the sensitivity and controllability of the system, we incorporated two different concentrations of geosmin, prepared by diluting the liquid odorants in distilled water: d-limonene high = 1:10; geosmin high = 1:10; geosmin low = 1:100. Wizard of Oz study The VR-O system and study design were informed by authentic water treatment workflows. We developed the virtual environment in collaboration with Gary Burlingame, former director of the Philadelphia Water Bureau of Laboratory Services, whose professional experience in water-quality management shaped the scenarios and decision points used in the learning tasks. In municipal treatment facilities, water typically passes through three principal stages: (1) intake (raw water drawn from a natural source); (2) mid-treatment (filtration, sedimentation, and chemical treatment); and (3) final treatment (post-treatment processing prior to distribution). Odors commonly vary across these stages and can serve as useful cues for contamination, treatment efficacy, or process failure. By anchoring the VR-O experience in these three stages, the study simulated decision-making situations that reflect operational judgments relevant to water-quality assessment.

## Method

We implemented a Wizard of Oz methodology (Martin & Hanington, 2012) to evaluate whether the VR-O system could support learners in detecting, identifying, and making treatment decisions based on odors encountered in a simulated water-quality laboratory. In this approach, participants interacted with the system as if it were fully autonomous, but a researcher managed key functions. Unlike in a traditional Wizard of Oz study where participants are not aware of the human in the loop, in our study, participants knew that a human researcher was operating the system and guiding the interaction. Specifically, during the study, the researcher narrated the scenarios and manually controlled scent delivery and timing. This method allowed us to assess the feasibility of the VR-O concept early in the design process without investing the time and resources required to build a fully autonomous system, and it enabled rapid iteration on non-technical elements such as scenario structure and the design of water-treatment tasks (figure 2).

Participants saw a virtual workspace with three water samples, labeled “In-take,” “Mid-treatment,” and “Final water.” Scenarios and odor delivery Participants engaged in three scripted VR scenarios, each including three virtual water samples (intake, midtreatment, and final water). Each scenario featured a unique combination of odor events and was based on information from expert interviews and real-world water-quality practices. Since one of the goals of the evaluation was to determine if participants could detect the presence of odors, some samples intentionally contained no odorant. In these cases, the system released clean, filtered air rather than remaining silent. As mentioned above, this was a critical design choice because it prevented participants from forming an unintended association between the sound of the device’s fan and the presence of an odor, thereby preserving the ecological validity of the task. During each trial, participants were asked to smell the sample, state whether an odor was present or not, and if applicable, describe and then label the odor. After all three samples had been assessed, participants were asked to indicate what actions they would take as water-treatment technicians (e.g., whether additional treatment steps were required). Behind the scenes, the researcher/wizard triggered odor cartridges or clean-air release in real time to align with VR interactions.

## Participants

A total of 23 students from Arizona State University, a large public university in the southwestern United States were recruited through mailing lists, online postings, and campus flyers. All participants were 18 years of age or older and completed a pre-screening questionnaire. Individuals with known olfactory impairments (e.g., allergies, nasal illness, recent head trauma) or susceptibility to motion sickness were excluded. Due to minor changes in the testing instrument after the first session, data from 22 participants were included in the final analyses. Among the 22 usable participants (17 male, 5 female), the mean age was 23.5 years (SD = 2.8; range = 19–29). All were currently enrolled students, except for one who had recently completed a master’s degree. Participants had varied prior experience with VR: 22.73% reported no prior use, 27.27% had used VR once, 27.27% had used VR 2–3 times, 13.64% were occasional users, 4.55% were daily users, and 4.55% identified as power users.

To assess physical comfort and possible simulator-sickness effects, participants completed a 13-item symptom checklist before and after using the VR-O system. Items (e.g., nausea, dizziness, blurred vision) were rated on a 10-point Likert scale. A comparison of pre-exposure (M = 15.61, SD = 2.20) and post-exposure (M = 14.72, SD = 1.49) scores showed no significant change in symptoms, indicating that the system did not negatively affect participants’ wellness.

A full paper containing detailed analyses is currently in preparation; here we report a brief summary of the core 8-item pretest followed by an isomorphic 8-item posttest. Items assessed recognition of common water-treatment odorants and appropriate decision-making actions. A paired-samples t-test revealed a statistically significant improvement from pre-test (M = 1.23, SD = 1.74) to post-test (M = 5.68, SD = 1.39), t(21) = –9.32, p <.001, d = –1.99, indicating that participants performed substantially better after the VR-O intervention. Interactive demo at ISLS During the ISLS Interactive Tools and Demos session, attendees will experience a short olfactory-enhanced VR scenario and explore the system’s scent-delivery mechanisms. They will have the opportunity to examine the newest prototype of the VR-O device, which consolidates key design ideas from earlier iterations into a compact, portable unit. In this third-generation version, odorants are stored in subdivisions of a cylindrical chamber which is rotated to precise positions by a stepper motor; when a chamber is turned to the “open” position, its ports align to allow air to flow from an internal fan, over odorous sponges, and out through a TPU tube to deliver scent precisely to the user’s nose. An “open” position candeliver scent or a steady stream of clean, filtered air, which facilitates purging prior odorants and breaking the sensory association between airflow and scent delivery. In a “closed” position, the system seals off odorant chambers to prevent passive leakage and cross-contamination. Mounted via a backpack with types wrapping around the shoulder, the device routes the delivery tube along its straps to sit just beneath the user’s nose, enabling controlled and well-timed olfactory cues during the VR experience. Participants will be invited to discuss design considerations, technical choices, and pedagogical opportunities for using multisensory VR in instruction and research. This demo directly reflects the conference theme, Partnering with Purpose, by demonstrating how educational technologies can be developed through close collaboration with domain experts to address authentic learning needs. The VR-O system was created in partnership with water-quality professionals and aligns with concepts taught in current courses—particularly content that is difficult to convey in traditional classroom settings. By integrating expert knowledge with intentional technological design, the system exemplifies how research–practice partnerships can produce tools that enable meaningful, practice-grounded learning experiences. This work demonstrates the potential of integrating olfaction into virtual reality to support more authentic, embodied, and practical learning experiences. By combining multisensory interaction with a portable, mechanically controlled scent-delivery device, the VR-O system offers a new avenue for teaching odor-based reasoning in domains such as water quality. The project addresses persistent classroom challenges (e.g., the difficulty of providing consistent, safe, and reusable odor samples) by enabling standardized, repeatable exposure to scents that are otherwise hard to access. At the same time, it tackles key engineering hurdles, including precise odor control, synchronization with VR events, and user comfort in portable systems. As VR continues to evolve, incorporating underutilized sensory modalities like smell can broaden the scope of experiential learning and create new opportunities for instruction, assessment, and research in complex, sensory-rich domains.
