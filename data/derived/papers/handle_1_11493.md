# Race as Substrate: Interaction Analyses of the Microgenetic Construction of Racialized Identities

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium brings together four research projects that investigate how people construct and contest racialized identities in interaction. We use Goodwin’s (2018) construct of substrate to examine how “kinds of people” (Hacking, 2007) are constructed in relation to racialized identities (Nasir, 2011). First, we introduce a particular methodological approach for racial interactional analysis and attend to the representational, theoretical and ethical complexities entailed. Second, we unpack the role of race-explicit and color-evasive language in constructing “kinds of students” in an elementary science teacher PD. Third, we examine how shared substrates of “social justice” discourses can be unintentionally leveraged to racialize students. Fourth, we examine how pre-service teachers constructed racialized categories by combing demographic data patterns with societal narratives. These analyses are valuable not only for understanding how we construct “races” in discourse, but also for thinking through learning objectives and instructional designs with respect to racialized identity construction.

Symposium overview: Race and substrate This symposium brings together four research projects that are investigating the ways people construct and contest racialized identities in discursive interaction. Ou r interest is in the ways racial-ideological systems of meaning and power, usually concept ualized at the level of societal discourses and str uctures, are invoked, negotiated, contested, and otherwise made meaningful in the mic rogenetic interactions of everyday discourse. Follo wing Philip, Olivares-Pasillas & Rocha (2016), we hope to spark discussio ns of promising approaches and units of analysis for studying the microgenesis of racial-ideological meanings and processes. Charles Goodwin (2018) provides a framework for ana lyzing the ways people construct meanings together in interaction, centered on the key idea o f substrate – the materials people use, assemble, repurpose and re-represent as they seek to achieve shared meanings. We find this concept to be useful both for developi ng new approaches to interaction analysis, and for designi ng learning environments. In this symposium we expl ore the usefulness of substrate for understanding the ways people construct the meanings of racialized identities. This topic is hinted at in Goodwin’s seminal analys is of the courtroom arguments in the trial of four police officers accused in the beating of Rodney Ki ng (Goodwin, 1994). In the course of illustrating t he ways professional vision is invoked in testimony, and ways-of-seeing are entrained in arguments presented to the jury (laying the groundwork for Goodwin’s later theoriza tion of substrate), Goodwin suggests that Mr. King’s racialized identity is part of what is assembled in the interaction between the defense attorney and the jury: Here the defense attorney uses similar procedures f or enhancing objects in the domain of scrutiny to call forth from the murky pixels on the video screen the discursive object that is the point of his argument, a large, violent, charging African-American man who was so dangerous that hitting him 47 times with metal clubs was reas onable and justified. (Goodwin, 1994, p. 622) Implicit in this description is an assumption that the descriptor “African American” is indicative of a person being seen as dangerous (along with “large, violent” and “charging”) – i.e., it is a discursive construction of a racialized identity as a partic ular kind of person. Goodwin’s analysis is not focu sed on race or racism specifically, and the forms of racism evidenced by the police violence against Mr. King, the arguments presented by the defense (and adopted by the jury), and extan t societal narratives about Black people being dang erous are hinted at, but not examined.

We believe that constructing people as particular “ kinds of people” (Hacking, 2007) and interactively assembling particular racialized identities (Nasir,  2011) in discourse, are processes that can be prod uctively examined through the lens of substrate. Gholson and Ma (under review) argue “for a radical rethinking of existing practices in interaction analysis due to their inad equacy to coherently address anti-Black violence” (p. 8). They suggest that attending to substrate offers a useful foundation for a “radical  relational” approach to exploring racial semiotics: As semiotic fields are brought into relation, they mutually elaborate each other. These relationships between semiotic fields constitute newly formed substrates that potentiate a “next action” within the analytical work. An important aspect of a radical relational approach involves the treatment of racial semiotic theory as a requisite field for analytical consideration. (Gholson & Ma, under review, p. 8) Through interaction analysis we can examine the way s particular racialized identities and meanings are brought into a space of shared attention in discour se, and are invoked, negotiated, transformed, propa gated and contested. Of interest are the semiotic fields used for these negotiations of meaning; the d iscursive materials that are developed as substrate for constructing raciali zed meanings; and the ways interlocutors seek to in struct, correct, or otherwise influence one another in shaping the identities that are produced in these interactions. These things are valuable not only for understanding how we construct “races” in discourse, but also for thinking through learning objectives and instructio nal designs with particular goals with respect to r acialized identity construction. Following Bang, Faber, Gurne au, Marin & Soto’s  (2016) arguments for “axiological innovations,” we argue that pursuing particular lea rning goals with regards to racialized identities s hould guide our study of the processes by which such identities come to be constructed in discourse. Valuing particular modes of sensemaking about race, rather than merely obser ving processes of racialization as they occur, is a  necessary step for planning instruction, assessing learning, and otherwise developing a racially-aware pedagogy. We aim to analyze the discursive construction of particular “ kinds of people” in order not only to understand th at process, but in order to change it – e.g., to reveal and confront white-supremacist assumptions; to interrupt racist violence; to cultivate nurturing and agentic racialized identities; and to problematize contradictions inherent in conceptions of race.

In this symposium we share substrate analyses of discursive processes through which racialized identities are constructed and negotiated. Rather than all fol lowing a single approach, each paper uses Goodwin’s  framing in different ways, in hopes of seeding a larger con versation that might encourage new improvisations a nd innovations in these directions.

Race as substrate through a radical relational approach: Answering complexities of racial microanalysis Maisie Gholson, Jasmine Y. Ma In this paper, we contemplate the representational, theoretical, and ethical complexities in forwarding a methodological approach for analyzing racialized social phenomena, which we call a radical relational approach. We seek to advance an approach for interaction analysis that accounts for the accomplishments of racialized social life in learning interactions and educational contexts. A radical relational approach combines relational ontologies (i.e., assemblages) wi th the substrates and semiotic fields provided by G oodwin (2007). This approach extends substrates and semiotic field s through three analytical dimensions, namely (1) t he relational histories between humans (from the local to sociohistorical), (2) rel ational ties and relationships to the more than hum an, and (3) embodied racial semiotics (Gholson & Ma, under revi ew). For this paper, we assume that the exclusion o f race critical analysis at the microgenetic level is a th eoretical and methodological tension, particularly related to sufficiently establishing the “demonstrable relevance” (e.g., Jordan & Henderson, 1995) of race as a salient factor. The relevance of race within the United States func tions as a matter of fact in everyday life. Still t he demonstrability of race in any given social interac tion (from a race-neutral stance) is a tenuous claim given the reliance on color-evasive discourses within social interaction (e.g., Bonilla-Silva, 2006). We note further that simply taking a race-critical stance (assuming the omniscience of race) does not remove the analytical burden at the microgenetic level. The salience of race and processes of racialization do not function routinely. That is, race and processes of racialization are not deterministi c; they reconfigure themselves  – in unusual and at times extremely violent ways – to maintain a material and discursive social order, i.e., existing racial hierarchies whose apex is white supremacy. All of this to say, we tak e “demonstrable relevance” as a key tenet that medi ates the racialization of substrates and semiotic fields. In  a radical relational approach we “play” inside of the rules of demonstrable relevance to foreground rigor – seeking first to establishing the specific ways race and racialization are functioning within a moment and subsequently hi ghlighting the constitution of racialized semiotic fields and substrates as more or less likely but unfolding in indeterminate ways. Our work illustrates how bodies tethered to the more-than-human and emplaced within geopolitical contexts pot entiate next-actions (imbricated by patterns of privilege or oppression).

This radical relational approach, however, raises i nteresting complexities, which is the object of thi s paper. First, in what ways can a radical relational  approach be rigorously applied and inscribed to co mmunicate the racialized assemblages interacting i n and accomplishing social life? Second, what theoretical cornerstones does this approach unsettle and are there unintende d consequences to disrupting well-established albeit infrastructural theory in interaction analysis. Fin ally, third, what ethical dile mmas emerge within racialized microgenetic analysis as advanced by a radical rela tional approach? To address these questions, we have applied the radical relational approach to moments of state-sanctioned violence exacted upon Black children. We  argue that our ability to theorize and analyze various forms of violence, such as physical, symbolic, and epistemological (Martin, Price, & Moore, 2019), are fundamental to accounting for Black children’s experiences as lear ners in formal and informal contexts shaped by anti-Black, white supremacy. If we cannot account for the worst of Black learners’ experiences, then what, in fact, have we been studying? And, if we cannot account for Black learners’ experiences at the microgenetic level, what sense c an we make of mesogenetic and macrogenetic patterns  or theories of change that interrupt systemic anti-Black racism? Constructing “Kinds of Students” through racialized discourse in science PD Ashlyn Pierson, Bethany Daniel, D. Teo Keifert Research demonstrates racial ideologies shape teach ing and learning (e.g., Philip, 2011; Philip et al., 2018; Sengupta-Irving et al., 2021), yet fewer studies unpack the role of color-evasive and race-explicit language in teacher learning (e.g., Shah & Coles, 2020). In thi s paper, we analyze a moment from an elementary sci ence teacher professional development (PD) to demonstrate how participants' discourse constructed children’s stances as epistemic (and valid) or as affective (and problematic) in relation to children’s interse ctional identities (e.g., race, gender). By calling attention to how particip ants' racialized discourse constructed “kinds of students,” we aim to identify color-evasive discourse and understand how it perpetuates  dominant ideologies. This knowledge can inform the facilitation of PD that challenges these harmful discursive norms. Education in the United States is interwoven with processes of racialization. Yet ideologies of whiteness look past existing racial inequity, leading to colo r-evasiveness. Color-evasive language deliberately avoids race to maintain White norms through discursive moves such as centering the self and claiming White racial innocence (Wilt et al., 2022). By contrast, language that exp licitly names race and racism as realities may work  to contest local relationships of power that disrupt whiteness  (Philip et al., 2016; Radinsky & Tabak, 2023). Racialization processes in the United States position students wi th certain identities as problematic. Black males, for example, are often framed as troublemakers when their behavi or and classroom contributions differ from White, middleclass norms (e.g., Warren & Rosebery, 2011)—a frame that starts from the harmful assumption that Black males are problematic rather than brilliant (Gholson et al., 2012). Racialization also shapes disciplinary learning: science knowledge has been constructed as White property (t hose who are racialized as White have greater acces s to science than those with marginalized identities, e. g., Black males; Mensah & Jackson, 2018). In this p aper, we explore how color-evasive language and  science-as-White-property ideologies influence PD participants’ discussion of students’ contributions.

We collected data during a PD workshop for elementa ry science teachers in a large metropolitan school district in the southeastern United States. We focu sed on a discussion including 2 facilitators and 4 teachers, all of White European-heritage. We analyzed 15 minutes of an hour-long session in which teachers discussed Warren et al. (2020, p. 287-289). This excerpt described how an African America n boy, Jonathan, challenged settled science knowledge (the sun is living) in a way that  his teacher interpreted as problematic. The text was intended to foster a discussion that validated multiple ways of knowing in science, and the activity was designed to respond to researchers’ prior concerns about deficit charac terizations of multilingual students (Daniel et al., 2023). To analyze our data, we drew on Philip et al.’s (2016) interpretation of Goodwin’s (2007) participation frameworks. Specifically, we focused on how PD participants con structed students’ stances as epistemic (knowledge claims) or affective (emotions) in relation to students’ identities (e.g., race, gender), as well as their use of color-evasive language. We also attended to dominant ideologies about science learning that position epistemic stances as valid in science and affective stances as problematic. Together, these micro-genetic stances and broader ideologies influenced participants’ sensemaking around the sha red substrate of the text and their individual expe riences as teachers.

In our data, participants constructed “kinds of stu dents” in terms of their perceived intent. Though n ot explicitly, participants associated White/race-unspecified and female students with inquisitive intent and African American and male students with problematic intent.  For instance, Christine (teacher) shared an exampl e of “incorrect” scientific thinking from her childhood (wind came from trees). Kwin (teacher) contrasted Christine’s stance (White female) with Jonathan’s stance (African American male) from the text: But. So, if she ((gesturing to Christine)) was the problem child in the classroom that’s always making this, and maybe he ((gesturing towards text)) is the kid in the classroom that’s always causing trouble and that’s why he’s b eing problematic to her, but if she was the problem child that’s one of those things that you can like, put on your boa:::rd and give them voice where maybe that will solve some of the problems. Above, Kwin contrasted two potential “problem” stud ents. Christine (White female) was characterized as  a solvable “problem”—a student that could be corrected if a teacher put her ideas “on your board and give them voice.” Kwin thus responded to Christine’s ideas as  epistemic contributions worthy of discussion, even  if perceived as incorrect (a judgment entwined with settled, European-heritage understandings of science as White property). Kwin gestured toward the text and used the pronoun “he” to refere nce Jonathan (African American male), who she characterized as a different kind of  problem—“a student that is always causing trouble.” Kwin thus constructed Jonathan’s contributions as affect ive rather tha n epistemic (problematic, later described as intended to “get under your skin”). Though Kwin did not explicitly or categorically compare White girls/women to African American boys/men, gesturing to Jonathan invoked social narratives about certain “kinds of students” in classrooms. Her perception of these “kinds of students” shaped her imagined response to their contr ibutions. In the full presentation, we will examine additiona l utterances from Kwin, other teachers, and facilit ators to highlight how categori es of problematic/inquisitive, boys/girls, desettle d/settled, and Black/White cultural repertoires appeared in their sensemaking about the text and about their experiences. Echoing Radinsky & Tabak (2023), we do not see our PD as an ideal or as a failure, but rather as a source of insight about the importance of attending to and challenging color-evasive discursive norms in PD. Specifically, we demonstrate how introducing a substrate that challenged ideologies of settled knowledge fostered a discussion that envisioned open-minded responses to students’ ideas (“give them voice”). However, in this case, offering a race-explicit example without unpacking ideologies around race was harmful; it resulted in participants defaulting to color-evasive language that reified dominant narratives (framing Black male students as troublemakers and their contributions as affective/problematic rather than epistemic/inquisitive). This example speaks to the need to critically and explicitly consider race as a part of STEM teacher professional learning. Moreover, while the focus of this analysis was race, we also recognize the ne ed for facilitation that attends to intersectionali ty in order to help teachers to resist harmful disciplinary norms and v alidate students’ multiple identities in STEM (e.g., race, gender, language, culture, disability; Radke et al., 2022). Making data mean “kinds of people”: Animating and laminating Mexican immigrants with demographic data Josh Radinsky, Iris Tabak In this paper we examine issues that arise when tea ching and learning with data that represent people,  such as demographic data generated by censuses, news report s, or social-scientific research. We see these teaching/learning contexts as special cases for  developing data literacy, requiring competencies th at include but go beyond the mathematical, statistical and spatial  abilities that are often associated with data anal ysis and visualization. In particular, we see demographic da tasets as one of the primary text genres with which  racialized identities are imposed and propagated (Benjamin, 20 19), and thus a key text genre with which to develo p racial data literacy (Philip et al, 2016).

We approach the study of this pedagogical phenomeno n through an analysis of the kinds of data-mediated talk that occurred in a lesson taught by the first author, Josh, with two classes of pre-service elementary teachers in a social studies teaching methods  course. The focal lesson was one of a series of les sons examining the concept of migration, and students engaged with the content both as learn ers about migrations shaping their lives, their city and their country’s history, and also as teachers-in-training engaged in exploring ways to teach with data visualizations. The analysis identifies modes of sensemaking-with-data that emerged during the lesson, in order to theorize the more general phenomenon of making data mean people. Goodwin’s concept of substrate is useful in this context for its framing of the wa ys people arrive at shared understandings. He defines substrate as “the  public structure that constitutes the point of dep arture for subsequent transformative operations” (Goodwin, 201 8, p. 12). We attend in our analysis to the ways categories of people surface in classroom talk; the kinds of n arrative elements that serve as “point[s] of depart ure” for racialized conceptions of data-as-people; and the ways the “subsequent transformative  operations” of classroom talk take up and transform the racialized identitie s that are invoked. This approach de-emphasizes individual, inthe-head understandings and competencies, and foregroun ds the public, shared, audible and/or visible resou rces for racialized sensemaking that people co-produce and use in data talk.

Using a GIS census data visualization tool on a New  York Times website, students narrated migrations of United States residents born in other countries, as documented by the US census across the decades 1880-2000. In our analysis we tracked the ways the data were discursively constructed as representations of historical people, and more specifically, as kinds of people. The GIS database and interface offered human categories inc luding “foreign-born” and “Born in Mexico,” “Born in Vietnam,” Born in Ireland,” etc. As student groups negotiated the co-construction of observations and explanations of pa rticular historical migrations using the data, in a ddition to verbal and gestural characterizations of quantities and distributions changing over time, nuanced categories were developed in substrate for representing historical people. We analyzed a variety of ways students investigatin g the Born-in-Mexico dataset developed and used discursive resources in substrate to construct hist orical Mexican-born people, living in the United States. Their data narratives included quantitative and spatial characterizations of patterns in the mapped data; historical actions that could explain or problematize those patterns; references to a variety of societally-shared narratives about Mexican immigration; and re-representations of ideas shared by other students i n previous groupings. These different sensemaking resources were combined, lami nated, and animated as students negotiated historic al and racialized meanings together.

For example, referencing a history text being read in class (Takaki, 2012), one student, Rogelio, narrates a disappearance of data in the year 1930 as being a result of mass deportations of Mexican immigrants during the Great Depression. Groupmate Jesse debates whether deportation could account for the extent of missing data: B306. Jesse:  Well I think that's like also just because the information is all not there [points to map]. Because you really think that they like completely cleared out of the whole United States [waves open hand across map] of every single Mexican, other than like the few states or cities {…}?

Groupmate Yesenia combines Rogelio and Jesse’s argu ments, suggesting that some Mexican-born people may have remained in the United States, but may have ch osen not to report themselves to census takers for fear of being deported. In substrate, multiple resources are laminated in this negotiation of meaning, including: ● animations of spatio-temporal, quantitative patterns in the dataset; ● the Takaki text’s account of deportations, interpreted by Rogelio; and ● an imagined world of Mexican-born people and census takers with conflicting moti vations, each with agency in the construction of the dataset the students are studying. Jesse, reporting these discussions to another group  later in the lesson, narrates his group’s explanat ions of the historical data in this way: B563. Jesse: So what we were talking about was, in the book [touches Takaki book] he talks about, like, there's like a migration -- not migration, um, deportation. Like a mass deportation of Mexicans, um, whether or not they were citizens.  Yeah. Um, and they [waving gestures] took ‘em all home, right around the time of the G reat Depression. So we were thinking like, that's, like -- that's a lot of Mexicans to ship home. Um, so most likely a lot of them did get shipped home, uh, as R onald [Takaki] says, but, ah, we were saying maybe a lot of them didn't document themselves on t he census, because they were scared of being deported...

Jesse here re-represents the mass deportation, previously narrate d by Rogelio (re-mediating Takaki), as a possible explanation for the data patterns he is showing, layering it with his earlier doubts (“we w ere thinking … that’s a lot of Mexicans to ship home”), as well as Yesenia’s accounting for the possible discrepancy (“maybe a lot of them didn’t document themselves on the cen sus”). Unlike Yesenia and Rogelio’s previous narrat ives, Jesse’s refers to deportation as being “ship[ped] h ome,” positioning Mexican-born people’s “home” as Mexico, and not the USA – a point that conflicts with his own statement “whether or not they were citizens.” Jesse’s brief narrative embodies multiple racial-ideological stances, laminated in substrate as stud ents negotiate the meanings of the data. Observations of  data patterns are combined with bits and pieces of  extant societal narratives about Mexican immigrants. For example, narratives of a “Mexican problem” which have been woven through media accounts for over a century (Flores, 2003) are present in substrate, and come into discursive conflict with narratives of immigrant pride and accounts of racism and anti-racism (Radinsky & Tabak, 2023). The kind of racial-ideological tension visible when these narratives a nd data interpretations come in contact (as seen above) is a resource for teaching and learning racial data literacy with demographic data. This pedagogy requires seeding classroom substrate with resources that afford opportunities to surface and engage with this tension, not simply as identifying bias or error to be avoided, but as a central part of the practice of conducting demographic data analysis.
