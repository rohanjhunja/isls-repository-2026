# Enhancing Learning and Design Processes through Online Collaborative Environments: Integrating the 4C/ID Model in a Learning by Design Approach

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines the integration of the Four-Component Instructional Design Model (4C/ID) and Peer-Assisted Learning (PAL) within a collaborative e-learning environment to enhance the feasibility of interactive online tutorials development by future learning designers. The 4C/ID model is known for facilitating complex learning challenges by aligning technological tools with pedagogical theories, while PAL provides a framework for the collaborative acquisition of knowledge and skills. Employing a qualitative approach, we explored how these models enhance learning designers' understanding of instructional processes and the development of engaging educational materials. Preliminary findings suggest that the combined use of PAL and 4C/ID improves the quality and engagement of the design process and learning content, highlighting the potential of collaborative strategies in instructional design. This research contributes insights into the learning-by-designing perspective, proposing a model that enhances the professional development of learning designers and could be beneficial for future educational practices.

## Introduction

Learning designers play a crucial role in bridging technology and pedagogy within e-learning environments (Bichsel, 2013). Their task involves shaping educat ional experiences by blending pedagogical expertise  with innovative technology to create effective, engaging, and accessible learning environments (Alexander et al., 2019). Beyond merely selecting appropriate tools and tec hnologies, learning designers must understand instructional design theories and apply these to meet specific educational needs (Bisset, 2018). Consequently, the ability to solve complex problems while adapting to unexpected changes is essential, marking it as one of the most critical skills for learning designers.

In educational settings, design thinking is fundame ntal to developing effective learning experiences. It involves understanding how to solve complex and amb iguous problems and adapting to unexpected changes (Razzouk & Shute, 2012). This process includes the systematic planning, development, evaluation, and i teration of educational interventions (Micheli et al., 2019). A thorough grasp of design thinking enables learning designers to strategically apply pedagogical theories and int egrate technological tools that enhance learning ou tcomes. Additionally, it helps in aligning educational content with learning objectives, ensuring that instructional activities are relevant and effective.

Building on the essential skills required for learning designers, the Four-Component Instructional Design Model (4C/ID) developed by van Merriënboer stands out a cognitive instructional framework designed to support complex learning by integrating knowledge, skills, and attitudes in real-world contexts (Van Merriënboer & Paas, 2003). Grounded in the cognitive theory of multimedia learning (Mayer, 2014) and cognitive load theory (Sweller et al., 2011, 2019), the 4C/ID model addresses the limitations of working memory to enhance knowledge acquisition and transfer (Kester et al., 2004). It provides a structured, task-centred approach that guides learning designers in creating effective educational environ ments across disciplines  (Frerejean et al., 2019; Van Merriënboer & Paas, 2003). The model comprises four interrelated components essential for instructional design:

(a) learning tasks, (b) supportive information, (c)  procedural information, and (d) part-task practice (Van

Merriënboer & Kirschner, 2018). By breaking down complex skills into manageable c omponents, the 4C/ID model enables instructional designers to develop au thentic, competency-based learning experiences that align with professional practice.

Integrating a collaborative design process with the  4C/ID model enhances the development of effective educational programs by incorporating diverse exper tise from content experts, instructional designers,  and practitioners, ensuring authenticity and alignm ent with professional practice (Frerejean et al., 2019). Rooted in Vygotsky’s social constructivism, Peer-Assisted Learning (PAL) emphasises learning through social interactions, with two specific approaches: peer tutoring, where a peer guides anot her (Topping, 2020), and collaborative learning, where all participants engage in shared p roblem-solving (O’Donnell & Hmelo-Silver, 2013). While Reciprocal Peer Tutoring (RPT) facilitates role-switching to deepen understanding (Topping et al., 2017), collaborative learning fosters critical thinking and teamwork, particularly when integrated with probl em-based learning (Y. H. Chang et al., 2022). Recogni sing the strengths of both models, MultiPAL  where multiple peer tutors working with a single tutee,  was developed to address challenges faced by novice  learning designers, blending structured peer tutoring with collaborativ e learning to create a flexible, interactive approa ch to instructional design.

Considering the dynamic and complex nature of educational desig n, this study aims to empirically examine the potential of the 4C/ID model and its influence on the capabil ities of future learning designers. Specifically, the research assesses how the 4C/ID model aids these designers in grasp ing the instructional design process and in developing learning materials. Additionally, the study explores the role of MultiPAL in the testing phase of the design process, investigating how coll aborative work within groups can enhance the development and refinement of the final educational products. B y addressing these questions, the study seeks to co ntribute insights into the learning-by-designing perspective, with the supports for profes sional development of  learning designers. To achieve these objectives, the study addresses tw o key research questions:  1. How feasible is the 4C/ID model in supporting future learning designers ’ understanding of the instructional design process  and the development of learning materials? And 2. How does MultiPAL enhance the testing process and contribute to the refinement of the final educational products?  By exploring these questions, this study aims to de epen the understanding of how structured instructional design models and collaborative peer-assisted learning approaches can enhance the competencies of future learning designers.

## Methodology

This study employs design-based research (DBR) methodology, focusing on the second iteration to evaluate the feasibility and quality of integrating the 4C/ID model with Mul tiPAL in the instructional design process  (Tan & Reimann, 2024). The DBR approach follows a structured cycle of prob lem analysis, solution development, implementation, and iterative refinement. In this second iteration, the study aims to address drawbacks identified in the previous iteration by shifting from a broad range of learning content to a specific focus on Single-case Research Design (SCRD) learning and teaching. Additionally, participants w ere required to maintain a design journal to document both their design process and learning reflections. The research was conducted over 6 weeks, emphasising iterative refinement through qualitative method s. Participants developed online tutorials, documenting their design choices and reflections in  their journals. Furthermore, two recorded sessions  captured team interactions and progress, providing insights into collaborative problem-solving and instructional design processes.

A purposive sample of 11 master’s students in Learning Science and Technology participated. They were divided into two groups (5 and 6 students) and rota ted weekly among roles: facilitator, designer, and tutee. Each participant took on both facilitator and  tutee roles at least once, ensuring exposure to bot h design and evaluation processes.

Participants collaboratively designed interactive tutorials on SCRD. Two development tools were chosen: RStudio (Team Alpha) for computational content and Google Slides (Team Beta) for traditional formats. The study sessions followed a structured cycle:  1. Collaborative design phase: Teams co-developed instructional materials based on 4C/ID principles. 2. Peer tutoring sessions (aka. testing phase): Tutorials were presented to a peer tutee who provided feedback.  3. Feedback integration: Designers analysed feedback to refine materials. And 4. Iterative refinement: Weekly cycles ensured progressive improvements. The process was also illustrated in the Figure 1.

Participants provided informed consent, ensuring vo luntary participation. Data confidentiality was maintained throughout, and ethical approval was obtained from the university’s Ethics Committee.

## Discussion & Implications

These findings suggest that incorporating the 4C/ID  model with the MultiPAL testing phase supports learning designers in developing structured, engagi ng, and adaptive instructional materials. The itera tive nature of the design process fostered problem-solving, while collaboration and external feedback play ed a critical role in refining instructional content. The 4C/ID framew ork enabled a scaffolded learning approach, where t asks progressed in complexity, while the MultiPAL framew ork enhanced peer-driven fee dback and adaptability in assessments. Additionally, content learning in SCRD  improved as participants transitioned from foundational understanding to practical application. However, participants also reported challenges duri ng session facilitation and material development, citing time constraints, uneven participation, and misunderstandings as key difficulties. The preliminary analysis of recorded sessions highl ighted key communication patterns among learning designers. During the preparation phase, the facilitator led discussions, setting the direction, a role that continued during feedback sessions. In the testing phase, interactions primarily occurred between the facilitator and the tutee, especially in think-aloud sessions where the tutee engaged with  the tutorial content while receiving facilitator support. These interactions demonstrated the application of the 4C/ID model within the MultiPAL framework, as both facilitator and tutee engaged in content learn ing. Across all phases, collaboration was evi dent, with team members working together to refine instructional materials and support the tutee. The evaluation of process artifacts, including week ly tutorial updates and the final interactive tutor ial, was conducted using a rubric scoring engagement, al ignment with learning objectives, and user interact ion on a 1–5 scale per criterion. Over seven iterations, a cle ar developmental trajectory emerged. Team Alpha’s f inal product scored 12 points (3 for engagement, 5 for a lignment, and 4 for user interaction), indicating h igh instructional quality, while Team Beta received 10 points (3 for engagement, 4 for alignment, and 5 fo r user interaction).

These findings suggest that the 4C/ID model and Mul tiPAL framework effectively support learning designers in developing interactive tutorials. Desp ite time constraints, the study offers initial insi ghts into how these models facilitate instructional design. The thematic analysis of design journals revealed  participants’ adaptive and reflective capabilities in addressing complex design tasks. Ongoing analysis of recorded sessions and process artifacts will further refine our understanding of the impact of these instructional approaches.

## Discussion and Implications

Feasibility of the 4C/ID model in instructional design This study examines how the 4C/ID model supports le arning designers in developing problem-solving skills, creativity, and collaboration through learning-by-design activities. Preliminary findings from design  journals, recorded sessions, and process artifacts indicate that the model effectively structures c omplex learning tasks and integrates diverse educational content. Thematic an alysis highlights improvements in problem-solving and content mastery, with iterative refinements address ing challenges like learner confusion and unclear instructions (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). For instance, Team Alpha’s use of R Studio enhanc ed engagement and skill application, demonstrating the value of creative in structional strategies (Mayer, 2014). The model’s w hole-task learning and gradual scaffolding approach ensured t asks increased in complexity while reducing support  as learners gained proficiency (van Merriënboer, 1997). Observations of tutorial versions confirmed impro vements in task difficulty and exercise diversity. From a l earning-by-design perspective, the 4C/ID model deepened participants’ instructional design skills, fostering an engaging and structured learning process. Contribution of MultiPAL to the design and learning process The MultiPAL approach integrates peer tutoring and collaborative learning, creating a supportive envir onment for novice learning designers by promoting knowledge sharing, task delegation, and stress reduction (Y. K. Chang & Kuwata, 2020; Y.H. Chang et al., 2022; Davey et a l., 2019). Analysis of design journals and video recordings identified three phases: preparation, testing, and feedback, where collaborative learning dominated pr eparation and feedback, while peer tutoring was central to te sting. Participants highlighted structured  roles and weekly meetings as key to effective collaboration, with th ink-aloud protocols providing valuable external feedbac k for iterative improvements.

Additionally, MultiPAL supports content learning th rough a learning-by-teaching effect (Duran, 2017), helping learners transition from foundational SCRD concepts to complex applications like graph customi sation. Video recordings confirmed that designers actively clarified tutee confusion, reinforcing continuous l earning progression. Overall, integrating MultiPAL with ins tructional design enhanced both teaching and learni ng outcomes, fostering deeper engagement and skill development.

## Implications for further implementation

This study provides initial insights into applying the 4C/ID and MultiPAL frameworks in designing an S CRD learning tutorial. Further research should include a detailed thematic analysis, and iterative refinements based on the design-based research cycle. Structured training for learning designers before the design phase and improved collaborative discussions during testing could enha nce the process. Additionally, future studies shoul d assess learning gains to evaluate the long-term impact of these instructional strategies.
