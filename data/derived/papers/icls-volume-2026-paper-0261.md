# The Art of Not Fixing: Embrace Self Multiplicity Through Non-Judgmental Art Pedagogy

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Mental wellness is seen as central to learning. Critics argue that some SEL programs reduce emotional complexity to cognitive skillsets and behavioral control, taking a deficit mindset towards students. This qualitative study examines non-judgmental art pedagogy as an alternative. Through observing a six-week workshop with six participants and two facilitators with follow-up interviews, we presented: 1) how facilitators practiced non-judgmental art pedagogy, and 2) effects on participants' acceptance of multiplicity. Interview analysis revealed facilitators invited norm disruption and trusted participants' inherent wisdom. Participants engaged artwork as autonomous entities to dialogue with internal conflicts, ultimately reaching reconciliation and identity expansion. Rather than eliminating difficult parts of self, participants learned to coexist with multiplicity through presence-based acceptance. This approach addresses SEL's limitation by fostering non-judgmental self-exploration, bridging art therapy with learning sciences to offer educators new perspectives on how to honor emotional complexity.

The more we explore, the closer we come to understanding the multiplicity of being. We can begin to understand others who may be stuck in a particular and limiting role. Our empathy and compassion for ourselves and others grow. When we see all possibilities, kindness, cruelty, achievement, lethargy, generosity, greed, exist in everyone, we can let go of the need to label others in order to keep our images of self wholly positive. (Allen, 1995, P105) Addressing student stress and educator burnout (Jennings & Greenberg, 2009), learning Sciences scholars positioned mental wellbeing as central to education. While Social-Emotional Learning (SEL) emerged to improve student wellbeing, critics argue certain programs reduce emotional complexity to cognitive skillsets and behavioral control, treating children as "feral creatures" requiring regulation rather than wh ole beings capable of self-discovery (Stearns, 2017; Hoffman, 2009). This deficit orientation promotes "hegemonic positivity" that evaluates emotional and behavior conformity using singular standards, hindering genuine self-exploration and acceptance (Zembylas, 2007).

Art therapy offers an alternative by accessing conscious and unconscious identity aspects, bypassing “censors” (Freud, 1899) to explore internal tensions between “who we are and who we're expected to be ” (Birtchnell, 1984). Non-judgmental pedagogy, rooted in Rogerian person-centered art therapy, amplifies this potential by positioning learners as "experts in their life," trusting their inherent capacity for self-understanding rather than conforming to imposed interpretations (Yao & Kabir, 2023). Recent learning sciences research recognizes therapeutic properties of art and identifies "Artistic Ways of Knowing" as necessary complements to cognitive theories, achieving SEL's goals through engaging mind, body, and emotion (Miller, 2020). This study examines a six-week "Art and Wellness" workshop embedding non-judgmental art pedagogy. We investigate: 1) How non-judgmental pedagogy was facilitated, and 2) Its effects on accepting multiplicity of self. Findings demonstrate how facilitators c reated judgment-free conditions, enabling participants to navigate internal conflicts and cultivated self-acceptance. By bridging art therapy with learning sciences, we offer new perspectives into processing difficult emotions and behaviors with compassion and curiosity, contributing to ongoing discussion around supporting learner wellbeing through art.

## SEL's limitations with multiplicity

SEL has been gaining traction in education. Coined in 1994 to address root causes of “problematic behaviors” while supporting academic performance ((Elias et al., 1997), the SEL movement contributed to education by 1) 1787 legitimizing emotions in learning environments (Durlak et al., 2016); 2) recognizing non-academic outcomes such as emotional regulation as essential for student success (Scott, 2017; Cross, 2005; Gatto-Walden, 2016); and 3) providing curricula that educators can implement in classrooms (Durlak et al., 2016). However, while acknowledging emotions' importance, SEL's implementation can constrain authentic identity development (Hoffman, 2009). Some programs treated emotional complexity as problems requiring cognitive management rather than natural human experiences deserving acceptance (Gillies, 2011). For instance, "undesirable" aspects such as anger or nonproductivity are positioned as deficits requiring remediation. As Stearns

(2017) observed, children's innate behaviors are "mistrusted, warded off, and taught away from," obscuring whose

emotions are legitimized (Zembylas, 2007). By suggesting cookie-cut remedies, it both affects whose identities can fully emerge, especially impacting BIPOC students, and diverts attention from transforming the systems that established and emphasized deficit-oriented mindset. (McDermott & Varenne, 2006). In summary, both critiques and iterations  (Garner et al., 2014) share one common ground: SEL sometimes uses overly cognitive frameworks for complex emotional experiences (Stearns, 2017), calling for more creative approaches to self-discovery and acceptance (McMain & Edwards-Schuth, 2024, Hoffman, 2009). Self-exploration through art Art serves as a powerful therapeutic medium for self-understanding. Art, defined by Berlyne (1960) as "harmony in disorder or unity in diversity" allows individuals to express both conscious and unconscious aspects of themselves through creative work. Art and psychotherapy have a longstanding relationship. Jung and Freud both applied therapeutic use of imagery in their self-exploration (Allen, 1995, P 66). Jung, an artist himself, began using patients' artwork therapeutically, recognizing art's power to access the subconscious mind that everyday “censors” (Freud, 1899) typically suppress (Birtchnell, 1984). Learning sciences has looked into art’s therapeutic potential in learning (Peppler et al., 2010). This approach addresses critical limitations of overly cognitive pedagogies, recognizing that effective learning requires balancing efficient routines with opportunities for innovation involving play and social interaction (Catterall, 2007; Miller, 2020). The effects correspond with what SEL aims to achieve: facilitate self-expression and provide creative ways to work with strong emotions, while destabilizing taken-for-granted assumptions and fostering empathy for others (Miller, 2020; Block et al., 2005). Through creative engagement, participants develop enhanced powers of understanding themselves and others, leading to improved self-esteem, and a reconnection with their inner selves (Pizzolante et al., 2024). These outcomes demonstrate how arts-integrated learning serves as “art as transformative experience” (Peppler et al., 2010), offering deep, lasting transformation that engages mind, body, and emotion, achieving what traditional cognitive-focused methods rarely accomplish alone (Gouthro, 2018).

Non-judgmental pedagogy Central to art-based approaches is participant-driven meaning-making, where individuals attach their own significance to creative work rather than receiving top-down interpretations and evaluation, creating environments of "dialectic democracy" characterized by authenticity, freedom of expression, and acceptance of diversity (Poulimenakou & Sarris, 2023). This requires integrating critical and creative arts not as add-ons, but as central pedagogical strategies to build capacities for navigating uncertainty and social transformation (Gouthro, 2018). Non-judgmental pedagogy, rooted in Rogerian person-centered art therapy (Hogan, 2015), positions clients as "experts in their life" rather than subjects of authority (Yao & Kabir, 2023). Acknowledging humanistic psychology's critique of behaviorism and psychoanalysis, it challenges patient-expert dichotomies, emphasizing mutuality and mutual recovery (Junge et al., 1993).

In learning sciences, there has been limited research looking at the intersection of art therapy and pedagogical design to foster self-exploration in ways that traditional SEL programs may lack. However, several classic learning sciences pedagogies mostly applied in STEM share some similarities. Papert's constructionism advocates bottom-up knowledge construction over top-down instruction (Papert & Harel, 1991). diSessa's Knowledge-in-Pieces reframes "misconceptions" as neutral p-prims applicable across cont exts (diSessa, 1993). Pedagogies of care and asset-based frameworks prioritize being over doing, becoming over performing (Noddings, 2005). Similarly to non-judgmental pedagogy, these theories also challenge teacher-student dichotomies and deficit views of students, emphasizing mutual learning. Our work aims  to further examine nonjudgmental art pedagogy’s facilitation and effects on participants’ learning about self-exploration. We conducted a six-week qualitative study with Open Studio Project, a nonprofit founded by art therapists offering community "Art and Wellness" sessions. The first two authors participated in weekly two-hour sessions 1788 alongside 5-6 participants and 2 co-participating facilitators (all with 1+ years’ experience). Each session included: intention writing (present-focused journaling), artmaking with varied mediums, witnessing (nonjudgmental dialogue with artwork), sharing writings/artwork, and open discussion. During the process, there is no commentary on self or others art and sharing. Block et al. (2005) described this process in detail in their paper. We collected ethnographic field notes, post-session voice memos, and semi-structured interviews (main data source) with six participants (45-60 minutes) exploring motivations, approaches, and impacts using their artwork as reflection prompts. Using qualita tive analysis (Miles et al., 2014), two researchers established coding consensus after open coding, then collaboratively synthesized open codes into 11 categories (e.g., process-oriented vs. product-oriented approach, shifts in artistic expression). The team re-coded all transcripts, refining definitions and emerging findings through regular discussion, and developing the findings presented. Facilitating self-exploration by creating judgement-free conditions Facilitators cultivated self-exploration by 1) removing judgements to give creativity space; 2) Disrupting internalized norms; and 3) trusting participants’ self-paced discovery. Facilitators mentioned removing both external and internal judgments for creativity to emerge. Jonathan Ayal (a participant but also a facilitator for other sessions) recognized that people’s critical selves, which "only know how to edit and evaluate," have become "overemphasized" at creativity's expense. The workshop aimed to "throw out the product to give the creative part, the spontaneous part, a chance to be." Knowing the difficulty of overriding internal constraints, facilitators actively invited norm disruption, such as “make the ugliest thing you've ever made” or “make a mess of these walls, and we're happy about it." Facilitators extended the challenge to productivity itself. Rowan said: "I release myself from the obligation of making anything." These invitations to fail, to mess, and to do nothing directly challenging societal constraints that participants may have internalized. In addition, instead of directing this creative emergence, facilitators trusted participants' inherent wisdom for self-discovery. Rowan emphasized he wasn't "healing or fixing anyone but rather simply holding space where their bodies fix themselves," trusting that participants possess "everything you need within you…all the answers, all the wisdom." Jonathan Ayal explained, "The point is, you follow each moment. It's more like improvisation in music." The emphasis is on immediate awareness and response, as  Rowan said to "get in touch with what you might need at this moment" rather than pursuing predetermined outcomes. This process-focused approach not only allows needs to emerge organically but also honors individual pacing. Jonathan Ayal gave an example of experiencing invasiveness when someone offered premature insights: "They could be spot on…but I need to get there in my own time." Their pedagogy challenges people’s internalized constraints by creating conditions where creative risk-taking receives support rather than judgment and accepting what the present self needs and may have to offer.

## Participants' journey: struggling, dialoguing, and reconciling with Self

Encountering and struggling with multiple parts of self The process revealed participants' internal conflicts between different aspects of identity they typically tried to control or avoid. Ruby struggled between her artistic self and spiritual self while trying to make a tree that became a cross. By keeping adding leaves and branches, her artistic self “wants to have control at least over that.” However, those branches were repeatedly turned into clouds. Dm encountered his perfectionist parts: "Should I draw something really special? If I don't, what's that mean? If it's just squiggles on the paper." Jason J. heard his mother's critical voice: "I'm hearing this voice going. It sounds like my mom." During this process, participants were confronted with different parts: desire to control as an artist, self-judgement from self, and critical voices from a parent.

Novel interactions through art as agentic beings Facing internal conflicts, participants engaged artwork as autonomous entities to explore multiplicity. Ruby developed a "question and answer game," posing inquiries: "I have this on my mind. What do you think? …or I felt this… I don't understand why." and wait ed with patience: "I set it up so that it can speak, then I just give it time." This externalization separated conscious intention from unconscious knowledge, allowing access to hidden meanings. Rowan trusted his artwork's guidance even when it counte red his usual preferences. Despite typically creating "small, detailed, intricate" work, he followed unconventional intuition: "My eyes went to the garbage…It just flowed…No questions, no thought really." By surrendering to the artwork's direction, Rowan e xplored creative possibilities beyond his controlling self's limitations. Jason J. integrated real-time dialogue: "I'm choosing 1789 blue…what it means to me right now…whatever comes up, I just write it down." This non-judgmental documentation created ongoing conversation with internal voices and decisions, allowing him to witness rather than judge his creative process.

Participants’ methods: questioning, following guidance, and documenting thoughts, transformed artwork into mediating entities that participants could work with, rather than against, their different parts. By externalizing internal conflicts through art, participants explored multiplicity without forcing resolution or conformity. Reconciliation and identity expansion through intuition When letting intuition lead, participants reached reconciliation with previously rejected parts of self. Ruby acknowledged "what was going to prevail was what the artwork wanted to be and not what I wanted it to be," describing this as spiritual surrender: "you need to let me [the art] sort of take the lead." By relinquishing artistic control, Ruby received her spiritual message about letting go. Rowan's breakthrough came through following intuition: "The fact that I genuinely didn't care what this ended up being. And it is now one of the most favorite things I've ever made...It was very transformative." This release from outcome-focused creation allowed Rowan to access authentic expression beyond his standards. Dm found resolution with his perfectionist parts through presence-based acceptance: "I'm here, I'm present…Even though it's not perfect, it's okay." Rather than eliminating self-judgment, Dm learned to coexist with it and retain agency, changing his relationship with self-criticism. Jason J.'s gradual integration over years of practice showed sustainable change: "I've been doing it long enough that…I don't really hear that [critical voice] so much anymore." This demonstrated how sustained nonjudgmental practice gradually leads to integration and acceptance. These reconciliations enabled identity expansion beyond previous limitations. K discovered "I think I can make art" despite previously rejecting that. Ruby escaped constraining proficiency to "reclaim my process as an artist." Jason J. acknowledged this process  as "a stamp of your soul…It's whole, it's completed, needs no comment", expressing how participants expanded their identities while also viewing their authentic self-expressions as enough without external validation or evaluation.
