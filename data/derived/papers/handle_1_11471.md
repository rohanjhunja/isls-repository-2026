# Expansive Learning for Enacting Utopias: Methodological Challenges

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium brings together leading scholars from four continents to discuss the methodological challenges of fostering and analyzing expansive learning for enacting utopian visions. By drawing on the theoretical framework of cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT), the symposium highlights the new possibilities for interventionist studies of learning opened up when expansive learning examined in activities facing critical societal contradictions and transformations. The contributions presented in this symposium showcase a diverse range of case studies, including community-based initiatives, formative interventions, and social movements. These studies demonstrate how expansive learning can be used to challenge the status quo, enact alternative futures, and create more just and equitable societies.

## Introduction

In line with the theme of the conference, this symposium presents cutting-edge research on expansive learning for enacting utopias. The theory of expansive learning (Engeström, 2015; Engeström & Sannino, 2010) is a unifying conceptual framework of the contributions. This theory proposes that learning collectively ‘what is not yet there’ can play a critical role in just societal transform ations. Expansive learning is triggered by contradi ctions that require qualitative change in the involved activity  systems. The process begins with actions of questi oning the existing state of the activity and proceeds through  the actions of analysis, modeling, examining the m odel, implementation, and consolidation. In an expansive learning cycle, the object of the activity is expanded along socio-spatial, temporal and ethical-political dimensions, resulting in a new concept of the activity. Utopias are traditionally defined as images of a perfect society in which  people work well with each other and are happy. In contrast, in line with Eric Olin Wright (2020), we understand utopias as ways to build and sustain forms of activity that serve the common good and offer alternatives to the logic of profit and exploitation. Such utopias are not isolated islands or perfect wo rlds of happiness. They are inherently contrad ictory as they struggle to exist and flourish in the midst of a wo rld dominated by global capitalism. The verb ‘to en act’ means to put something into practice or to put something into action. It is not sufficient to talk about env isioning or dreaming about  alternatives. Our symposium focuses on learning pro cesses that involve practical actions for material transformations (Sannino, 2020).

The four papers of the symposium examine methodolog ical challenges that emerge when expansive learning is fostered and analyzed in activities facing critical societal contradictions and transformations. These methodological challenges range from inter-generational succession and sustainability to bound ary crossing between the learning sciences and public affairs, t o tracing the interplay between multiple expansive cycles over time, and to the uses of history in formative inter ventions aimed at supporting expansive learning. A twin symposium will focus on theoretical and practical a dvances made in recent studies on expansive learnin g for enacting utopias.

In the first paper of the symposium, Ge Wei will an alyze how a people’s commune in China has been sustained over generations in spite of the official  abandonment of the commune system in 1978. In the second paper, José Meléndez presents a personal narrative on the challenges and  possibilities opened up by crossing the disciplinary boundary between the learning sciences  and public affairs. In the third paper, Mattia Favarettto develops a diagnostic instrument to assess the interconnected expansive learning cycles of four civic movements working to save the city of Venice. The fourth paper by Yrjö Engeström presents and tests a method for analyzing the uses of history in an expansive learning proces s within a Change Laboratory intervention conducted  in a housing unit for homeless youth. The presenters come from three continents and inclu de both senior and junior scholars. The discussants, Kris Gutiérrez and Marco  Querol, bring to the symposium their extensive exp erience and insights from the United States and Brazil.

The last people’s commune in China? Enacting a socialist utopia through education in Zhoujiazhuang Ge Wei The people's commune is a grass-roots unit that combines working and living under t he political-economic structure of China's socialism. The essence of the people's commune is that completely eliminate the p rivate ownership, and the labour process accepts organizational unified assignment (Lippit, 1981). S ince the national policy of reforming and opening in 1978, China abandoned to support the commune system. However, Zhoujiazhuang （周家庄）, a small village in Hebei Province, retains the people's commune system until today, based on the residents’ internal consensus. We can’t help as king: What is the historical and realistic situatio n of Zhoujiazhuang? Does Zhoujiazhuang successfully enact a socialist utopia? If so, how could it possible, and what lessons should capitalism learn from it?

This paper, based on an ethnographic study in Zhouj iazhuang since the December of 2021, focuses on the role of education system contributing to the  sustainability of the people’s commune system. In-depth interviews were conducted with the residents in Zhoujiazhuang. Specifically, Wang’s family (pseudonym), whose three generations have lived in Zhoujiazhuang for m ore than 70 years, was selected as a fo cal of data analysis. The three-generation family members of Wang, alongside their leaderships, neighbors, and schoolteachers in Zhoujiazhuang, were interviewed as much as possible. Their lived experiences witness the changes and the unchanged of Zhoujiazhuang over 70 years. Besides, the author lived in Zhoujiazhuang for a week, for collecting participatory observation data to enrich Wang’s fam ily’s stories.  All data was transcribed in verbatim, then translated into English, under the funding support of the GINTL project co-directed by Annalisa Sannino and Ge Wei.

The method of narrative inquiry  (Clandinin & Connelly, 2000) was used to analyze th e data across temporality, spatiality, and sociality. By telling and re-telling Wang’s family’s experiences in Zhoujiazhuang from a cultural-historical perspective, it is found that the case o f Wang’s family formed a coherent contribution to enacting utopia through their three-generation efforts. The reason why Zhoujiazhuang sustained over generations in spite of the official abandonment of the commune system in 1978, is that a so-called “intra-vention” embedded in the local residents’ collective activities (see Sannino, Engeström, & Lemos, 2016). Furthermore, the process of enacting a socialist utopia (i.e., people’s commune; c.f. Lippit, 1981) was realized mainly by education, crossing the boundaries among school, family, and the commune. The sustainability of Zhoujiazhuang could be identified as the outcome of “ intra-vention” which negotiated with the outside macro-political change, and then sustained their own collective activity by inside grass-root consensus. This finding also illuminates how t he residents in Zhoujiazhuang expanded their ideal of socialism fro m “the abstract” Marxism to “the concrete” enactmen t in Chinese rural areas (Davydov, 1990). Although it was not designed as specific intervention methods, such  as the Change Laboratory, intra-ventions happen when local people try to transform activities through their agency, in a longitudinal, open-ended and generative manner.

This study has significant implications for enactin g utopias, from the tradition of cultural-historical activity theory. The western media regards Zhoujiazhuang as the last people’s commune in China, but the activity of educating people to enact a socialist utopia in Zhoujiazhuang has actionable resonance with other r egions in China and beyond.

Re-imagining disciplinary boundaries to further the potential of learning sciences research in public affairs contexts José W. Meléndez This paper can be read as a representation of my “r eflection-in-action” (Schön, 1983, p. 50), or a testimonio (Denicolo & González, 2015) that weaves my personal  narrative as a learning scientist in a school of P ublic Affairs, as I attempt to connect my experience with research as a call for enacting new possibilities. The ideals of democracy have been a long constant in U.S. educational philosophy, with debates arising along the way on how best to teach future voting ad ults the skills and dispositions needed in a delibe rative democracy (Dewey, 2004; Liggett, 2014; De La  Vega, 2014). In this vast brush of history, the spa ces in which democracy is practice —deliberation— become a focus of what educators teach in classroom s. In more recent times, the Learning Sciences has extended this focus, with the last decade seeing a growth of interest by learning scientists collaborating and studying a range of pu blic affairs contexts. Public affairs as an umbrell a term relates to the ‘“eclectic”’ and ‘“interdisciplinary”’ approach to the study of organizations and individuals that operate in what is considered the public interest and the social issues and problems with which they are faced” (Empire State University, N.D.). Key to public affairs is the rel ation between the public engaged and an attempt to influence policy or policy outcomes.  It is indeed in collaboration with public affairs disciplines such as city and community planning, public administration, and organizational  management of third sector organization such as ch arities, social enterprises, NGOs and Foundations that fourth generation activity theory needs to converse and collaborate with if it is to be successful in helping bring about alternative futures that might be considered utopias. A trail of the potential of this research has alrea dy been materializing over the last decade as more learning scientists take on the various strands of social cultural historical research framework to engage in research across a variety of public affairs contexts. From the work of non-profits in food access and justice (Shea, Jurow, & Teeters, 2016) to differentiating between collect ive and system level expansive learning in a Partic ipatory Budgeting Process (Meléndez, 2021), eradication of homelessness in  Finland (Sannino, 2020) or the role of transformative agency in a community planning effor t (Lund, 2021). Appropriately, this line of interdi sciplinary research has pushed our disciplinary imaginaries of where learning happens and to recognize how planning plays a role in the global, environmental, and social cri ses we are facing, while also emphasizing the optim istic view for envisioning new solutions. However, the potenti al for this line of research to yield transformativ e results has been limited by the inability of current and future planning students to access the theories and tools that the abovementioned researchers use to look at public affairs context through new disciplinary lenses. Part of this challenge is systemic, given how rarely we will find public affairs students taking LS related courses or public affairs professors assigning LS r elated readings. Yet, I have seen first-hand the transformative potential for expansive learning that exposing plan ning affairs students to concepts and methods typic ally associated with the learning sciences holds. More explicitly, to influence Ma ster level students who are the bulk of planning and administration professionals across  jurisdictions in the U.S. to imagine a new way for  engaging underserved communities, whom are the most impacted  by today’s pressing issues. Here, I am making the argument for bringing the learning sciences and ass ociated research fields into planning classrooms. F or the last six year, I have been teaching a graduate level course that I re-designed using an activity-centered design (ACD) approach (Gifford & Enyedy, 1999). The class functions as both a seminar on theories of public engagement and a workshop in which students analyzed case studies and developed their own design ideas for engaging underserved communities in decision making across a  range of disciplinary fields and contexts. The bul k of my students will be future city/community planners, pu blic administrators, and third sector organizationa l management. From this classroom experience emerged a conceptualization of how students can become acti ve transformative agents or world-makers of the future as they design social-change processes. For example, by introducing planning students the u tility of the humandevelopment focus of educational/cultural psychology —central to the learning sciences (Sommerhoff, 2018) —they can begin to conceptualize how planning contexts are places where individual and collective identity development take place, akin to communities of practice. This approach asks future public affairs professionals to view the contexts when they engage the public as malleable, with the potential to inhibit or expand the identity development of individuals and communities. Subsequently, it introduces future  public affairs students to a design-based epistemology that connect the above malleability to a future professi onal practice that they could learn over iteration to design and improve.

Indeed, it is why I have been developing over the last five years what I call a System Level Community Engagement: Designing Across Three Planes approach that introduces students to Activity Systems (Figure 1). In this approach students practice over iterations designing a process: a time series of events that has objectives and outcomes, working backwards in their design; A bureaucracy: an accounting of all the resources needed to make the designed process a r eality. Although a bureaucracy also includes po licies and bureaucratic or organizational structures to carry out the work of a process, at t his introductory level of designing students first focus on the resources needed. Finally, they are introduced to t he concept of learning environments and how to desi gn for them. Distinguishing the learning environments from  the process is key, knowing full well that face-to-face interactions are moments of teaching and learning, but are not in fact when the bulk of the work happe ns, since research, site visits, communications, and many other tasks that take place in-between face-to-face meetings. Recent events, such as cruise ship ac cidents, unprecedented high tides, and th e pandemic lockdowns, have spotlighted these threats, promptin g Venetian civic networks to mobilise more dynamica lly. However, historical divisions within the civil soci ety and notable governance issues still hinder the development of unified long-term resolutions (Borelli & Busacca, 2020). Therefore, this project constitutes the diagnostic phase of one or more city-wide formative interventions (Engeström, 2011) aime d at fostering the collective learning processes enacted by four civic movements: High Residential Tension (HRT), Coordination No Incinerator (CNI), Poveglia for Everybody (PFE), and Let’s Reclaim the City (LRC). Respectively they tackle entangled critical challenges, from short-term tourist rentals, and environmental pollution, to public propert y mismanagement and neighbourhood fragmentation.

As the diagnosis aims to assess the developmental n eeds of these movements, it builds on the basic concepts of CHAT – the expansive learning cycle, its object orientatio n, the collective zone of proximal development – (Dinh & Sannino, 2024) and tries to ad apt them to a fourth-generation study (Engeström & Sannino, 2021). It consists of gradually partaking in the civil society initiatives and iteratively analysing the data collected through archival research, participant observations in orga nizational meetings, and expert interviews with Venetian activists (Favaretto, 2024). The diag nostic procedure first highlights how the objects o f localized activities can expand into critical challenges acknowledged by actors operating across social sectors and levels of governance. It becomes clear that to achieve this shared understanding, civic movements, institutional bodies, and private entities need to adopt a collaborative stan ce, despite their previous conflicts. Nevertheless,  to orient the process of coalition formation towards consequential social change, activist networks must often initiate and direct the expansive learning cycles while building new partnerships, as in three of the  cases examined: CNI co-developed a country-wide biomonitoring study of contaminated sites, inc luding Venice’s harbour, by working with research centres, the regional Departments of Health and Environment, and the National Health Ins titute; HRT activated the Italian network on touristification as well as key municipal administrations and parliamentary members to promote a national bill that restricts s hort-term tourist rentals; PFE helped design an action-research project to regenerate abandoned state-owned areas, such as the Poveglia island, in collaboration with two regional universities and the State Property Office (SPO).

The latter case well exemplifies the steps of coali tion building as interconnected learning actions ta ken by heterogeneous actors. PFE, a civic organization aiming to transform the Poveglia island from an aba ndoned site at risk of privatization into a community space, struggled for a decade to secure permission from the Regional SPO. In 2023, seeking public support, PFE partnered  with two research groups, the APsyM Lab (Universit y of Verona) and the ProPart Team (IUAV), which question ed the limited scope of  the project. Therefore, after analyzing PFE's conflicts with the SPO, they prepar ed a first model of an action-research project for all state-owned islands. While examining the proposal, the SP O made clear that the city administration would not  allow PFE to manage any island directly for political reasons. Yet, it suggested to remodel the activity as a re search coalition on the regeneration of abandoned lagoon i slands and its impact on the community’s well-being. This coalition would first implement a doctoral project sponsored by the SPO, co-supervised by the APsyM Lab and the ProPart Team, and technically assisted by PFE. Management of the island would formally pass to the APsyM Lab, with PFE acting as the local partner. This cri tical step was initially resisted by some members o f the civic organization and the National SPO. However, despite  a letter of endorsement from the Regional SPO, it was the Rectorate of Verona that rejected the concession ci ting concerns about overstepping the universities o f Venice. Ultimately, the doctoral study proceeded but was limited to a preparatory phase of the envisioned action-research project.

Amid the current stalemate, PFE and its partners could benefit from a 4G formative intervention both to expand the capabilities and goals of each activity and to better align their coalescing efforts. There fore, to effectively support such coalitions it is crucial t o trace how the civic initiatives launched by Venet ian activists have shaped their development and which key contrad ictions must now be tackled. Overall, the project a ims to learn with and from them how to move away from Veni ce's recent history of economic exploitation and political neglect and toward a future of viable co-existence between its community and the lagoon ecosystem. Historicity in expansive learning: Change Laboratory in a supported housing unit for formerly homeless youth Yrjö Engeström Historicity is a built-in principle in expansive learning. To enable well-grounded and far-reaching redesign of their activities, the participants in an expansive learning process analyze the historical development  of their activity systems and their inner  contradictions. Practical and conceptual instrument s have been developed to facilitate such analysis. These include schemes of broad historical types of work (e.g., Virkkunen & N ewnham, 2013, p. 55-59, p. 95) and techniques such as collective rememb ering and building a “history wall” based on the participants’ experiences and available documents (Engeström & Spante, 2021). However, very little sys tematic research has been done on how historicity is actual ly played out in real expansive learning processes (see, however, Paniza & Cassandre, 2018) and there is a l ack of methodological frameworks for conducting suc h analyses.

In this paper, I present a tentative methodological framework for empirical analyses of the uses of history in Change Laboratory interventions. I test the fram ework by analyzing the entire set of recorded and t ranscribed discourse data from a Change Laboratory conducted w ith the workers and managers of a supported housing  unit for formerly homeless youth in Helsinki, Finland (s ee Sannino, 2020; 2022; Prokopis, Sannino & Mykkäne n, 2022).

The proposed methodological framework offers a codi ng scheme for identifying and classifying discursive expressions of uses of history by means of four broad types, as depicted in Figure 2. Figure 2 A Framework for Analyzing Historicity in Expansive Learning In Figure 2, the lower left-hand field represents personal accounts of the past, whether in the form of memories of specific events or more general reflections. Typical to these accounts is the use of the first person (I, me) perspective, often accompanied by highly person al evaluations of past events or circumstances. The  lower right-hand field represents personal reflections on and p rojections toward future events and circumstances. The first person perspective and personal evaluations are present here, too, but the focus is on things to come or things that can be made to happen. The upper left-hand field represents systemic accounts of the past. Typical to these is the use of references to structures and systems larger than an individual. These references  range from general allusions to specific identification of for example  administrative, economic or political forces. The upper righthand field represents systemic reflections on and proposals for the future. The analysis shows that in an expansive learning pr ocess, historicity is a crucial resource for findin g ways out of seemingly hopeless conundrums – a source of generative power enacted by means of spiraling across the fields depicted in Figure 2. By engaging in personally and collectively charge d analyses of the historical evolution and contradictions of their practice, the  workers of the housing unit constructed and implem ented a future-oriented zone of proximal development for their uni t, moving toward a radically expanded object and concept of homelessness work (Sannino, 2022). The m ethod developed in this study will enrich the methodological toolkit of learning scientists, espe cially those engaged in formative interventions for  expansive learning.
