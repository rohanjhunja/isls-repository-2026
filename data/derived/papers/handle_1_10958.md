# Scaffolding to Build AI Competences: Transforming Student Presentations to Meaningful Learning Moments

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI) has become integrated into students’ daily lives. It is crucial to expose young students to AI knowledge early on to build core competencies. In this poster, we showcase how a facilitator scaffolded student groups to connect their design ideas with learned AI concepts in their final presentations of a design problem solving project and how those interactions were turned into meaningful learning moments for the entire class at an AI summer camp.

## Introduction

Introducing Artificial intelligence (AI) concepts e arly is crucial for building young students' core c ompetencies in understanding the meaning and impacts of AI technologies  (Kim et al., 2023). Despite daily interactions with AI technologies, such as Siri or Alexa, students often lack understanding of  fundamental concepts and ethical concerns of AI. Teaching AI in middle school is challenging due t o its intricate sub-components, such as natural language processing, computer vision,  and machine learning. To address this, providing practical examples that are suitable for their age and personally relevant is essential to promote a deeper understanding and application of complex AI concepts (Kim et al., 2023). In problem-based learning (PBL), students can collaboratively engage in problem solving by posing authentic problems that students can relate to their own lives (Hmelo-Silver, 2006). However, student groups may struggle with understanding and applying the target concepts in the problem context, and scaffolding can support the groups’ needs by  structuring a complex task to maintain forward direction and problematizing the inquiry process to draw students’ attention to the  overlooked ideas (Ertmer & Glazewski, 2019). Scaffolding is referred to as contingent support that requires a teacher to continuously adapt to the current status of student learning (Bae et al., 2021). It is tricky to achieve the balance  between structuring and problematizing scaffolding because it should be supportive enough to engage students in unfamiliar ideas, but also overly structuring scaffolding could strip away complexity of the problems. In this poster, we showcase how a facilitator scaffolded student groups to connect their design ideas with learned AI conce pts during their final project presentations and how those interactions  were turned into meaningful learning moments for the enti re class at an AI summer camp.

## Methods

The primary goal of the summer camp was to enhance students' understanding of AI concepts and their practical applications in solving everyday problems. During the first four days, students engaged in a series of activities of un/plugged exercises to grasp fundamental AI concepts  (e.g., sending emergency text message s with voice recognition) and daily mini-design projects. On the last day, students collaboratively engaged in a design problem solving project. Students were challenged to d esign AI-powered smart home solutions to address issues such  as temperature, water, entertainment, and chores (See Figure 1). As a culminating activity, each group presented their design ideas to the whole class, and a facilitator and AI experts participated in feedback sessions. A total of 17 students with 12 boys and 5 girls from 6 th to 9th grades participated in the Midwestern US. The primary data was classroom videos, and student artifacts, such as storyboards and desi gn documents, were analyzed to triangulate our analysis. We used an Interaction Analysis (IA; Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to analyze student groups’ final presentations and the interactions be tween the student groups and a facilitator, and highlighting interactions were shared and analyzed in data sessions.

## Findings

Preliminary findings highlighted the importance of balance between structuring the complex design-problem solving task to support students in decomposing complex AI concepts and problematizing the context to explicitly draw students’ attention to gaps in their design so lutions during the presentation. In one example,  student group A presented smart water solution ideas that automatically fill up the bathtub at the right temperature withou t overflowing. They described the specific features, but they did not explicitly explain how AI technolo gy will be applied. After their presentation, the facilitator elicited their learned AI knowledge from the studen ts by saying, “Remind us again, what data does your system need to process?” Then, the student replied, “computer vision...and thermostat.” Again, the facilitator scaffolded, “Ok ay... computer vision that detects what?”  Then, the group of students started talking about how computer vision and the rmostat will be used to detect the water level and the temperature and train and evaluate the model to keep it from overfl owing. In another example, student group B presented a smart entertainment system that recommends the mo st interesting game to play when you put on a VR headset. After the presentation, the facilitator asked a que stion, “How does your team think about inclusion and representation of people who have different siz es, people who have different background, people wh o have different gender, people who have different languages?” This question triggered the student group to think further about their design, such as adjustable size headset, language options in the settings. After this group’s presentation, every group explicitly mentioned how the system will include different backgrounds and languages  during their presentations. In the final  group’s presentation (see Figure 1), the students  clearly explained how their smart temperature system will leverage AI components without any facilitator’s prompts, “At the first time you add your profile, you add that person's regular temperature,  and that is what starts with. Then, it will keep a dding and understand ‘oh, you like it warm, or you like it cold.’ And with that the AI will keep learning from this d ata and it will find the perfect temperature for you.” They also added how the AI system will understand different accents, such as children’s voice or foreigners’ voice.

## Significance

A key takeaway from this work was  to demonstrate how scaffolding can push students to navigate the complex ideas by applying learned AI knowledge at the neede d moment  a nd how  each group’s presentation  and the feedback session were transformed to meaningful learning moments for the entire class. As each group presented their projects and engaged in feedback sessions, the classroom became a dynamic and collaborative learning space for meaningful learning, demonstrating how scaffolding fostered a deeper understanding and application of AI knowledge among students.
