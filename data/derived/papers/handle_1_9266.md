# Beyond the Cognitive Dimension: Emotion Patterns in Productive and Improvable Knowledge Building Discourse

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Knowledge Building is a pedagogical approach emphasizing students' collective responsibility to continuously improve their community knowledge. Various emotions may arise during Knowledge Building activities because of students’ diverse ideas, theory-building and cognitive disequilibrium and equilibrium. These emotions may differ in inquiry threads at different discourse development levels. An inquiry thread is a sequence of notes addressing the same problem or topic. This study examines the frequency and sequential patterns between undergraduate students’ productive and improvable Knowledge Building inquiry threads recorded in Knowledge Forum. We found that emotions reflected in inquiry threads tend to be self-repeated. A series of positive and negative activating emotions were reflected in productive collaborative inquiry threads, suggesting students engaged in the discussion despite conflicting ideas and various emotions. On the other hand, improvable collaborative inquiry threads displayed activating to deactivating emotion transitions, such as joy to boredom, which shows that students might disengage from the discussion.

## Introduction

Higher education has widely adopted Computer-supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) which encourages students to work together to maximize individual and group learning gains with the  support of technology (Jonassen, 2004). Each student can be exposed to various ideas and explanations during the collaboration, which may provide external monitoring, offer feedback on problem-solving skills, and promote  belief changes (Goodyear et al., 2014). Aligning with CSCL, Knowledge Building is a pedagogical approach  emphasizing students’ collective responsibility for community knowledge advancement. Ideas are placed at the center of Knowledge Building, and twelve fundamental principles distinguish it from traditional didac tic pedagogy and activity-centered pedagogies (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2005). To name a f ew of the principles: 1) improvable ideas describe an iterative process of theory building, 2) epistemic agency refers to students’ control over goal setting, strategies execution, and evaluation; 3) community knowledge advancement defin es a collaborative endeavor to extend the collective understanding of a societal issue (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2005). Knowledge Forum is an online platform designed based on the principles of rec ording, tracking, and scaffolding students’ progressive discourse (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2005). Specifically, in Knowledge Forum, learners candocument their discussion of a topic of interest in a threa d. They can input their responses, build on or refer to each other’s notes using scaffolds (e.g., “I need to understand”; “This theory cannot explain”; “My theory”; “Putting our knowledge together”) (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2021). Hence, supported by Knowledge Forum, students can create a conceptual artifact beyond summative notetaking of individual knowledge. Previous research suggests the impacts of Knowledge Building on promoting students’ high-level competencies (e.g., metacognition, collaborative theory building). For example, Hong and colleagues (2020) compar ed direct instruction with collaborative knowledge building in literacy education. They found students in the collaborative knowledge building group engaged more in higher-level discussions, such as integrating ideas and evaluation. Lei and Chan (2018) took a step forward by adding a reflective assessment component to the Knowledge Building process. They asked students to compose portfolios that reflect on their collective inquiry, write rise-above notes referring to Knowledge Forum notes, and conduct presentations to reflect on their knowledge advances. Such reflection promoted students’ collective knowledge, awareness of their knowledge limit ations (i.e., meta-cognition), theorizing efforts (i.e., meta-theory), and tracking discourse development (meta-discourse). Knowledge Building is an iterative and challenging process where students consiste ntly reflect on their knowledge status, identify gaps, evaluate new information and improve theories with their community members. Many researchers highlight the importance of the temporality of such collabora tive learning. While instructors help formulate the initial inquiry structure, students’ interactions will produce  new directions that elaborate and modify the initial structure (Zhang et al., 2018). Understanding the underlying principles of collaborative learning requires understanding  how collaborative processes develop through time. Chen and col leagues (2017) applied Lag-sequential Analysis and Frequent Sequence Analysis on Knowledge Forum note s to uncover the temporal sequence of collaborative learning. They found that a productive thread has a progressive sequence of theorizing CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 68 and questioning theories. Whereas the study highlights the importance of temporality in collaborative learning, it does not fully capture the affective-cognitive dynamics during Knowledge Building. Recent research has emphasized the importance of emotions in learning (Mayer, 2020). Emotions impact cognitive tasks, such as reasoning, and vice versa (D’Mello & Graesser, 2014). Pekrun (2006) proposed the Control-Value theory, which illustrates the cyclic feedback processes among emotions, cognitive effects, and learning outcomes. The theory demonstrates that subjective control and value over a task are linked to achievement emotions (i.e., emotions related to learning activities or outcomes such as anxiety, frustration, and confusion). Pekrun and Linnenbrink-Garcia (2014) also theorized that epistemic emotions (i.e., surprise, curiosity) are related to cognitive processes such as encountering new information. Achievement and epistemic emotions fall under the umbrella of academic emotions (Pekrun & Stephens, 2010). D’Mello and Graesser (2014)  built on Pekrun’s academic emotions and proposed a model of affective dynamics. When learners en counter a cognitive disequilibrium (i.e., obstacles, uncertainty, or novelty), they experience confusion. Subsequ ently, they can feel engagement if the equilibrium is resolved, frustration if the failure persists, and boredom when they give up. Such a model emphasizes the transition among affective states. Zhu and her colleague s (2019) explored the cooccurrence of children’s emotions and idea improvement during  Knowledge Building. They revealed a similar pattern that surprise and neutral emotions co-occur with activities that d emand high cognitive efforts such as explanation, whereas joy happens with lower-level cognitive activities, such as “simple claim, appra isal, and paraphrase.” Similarly, Yang and colleagues (2022) studied low-achieving students’ emotional transition and paths in Knowledge Building and found emotions likely to transit to themselves (e.g., joy to joy) or similar emotions (e.g., frustration to boredom).

Although attempts have been made to study the relationships between the cognitive and emotional aspects of students’ Knowledge Building (e.g., Yang, Zhu, & Chan, 2022; Zhu et al., 2019), it remains unclear what emotional characteristics distinguish productive Knowledge Building discourse from less productive ones. An in-depth understanding of the affect-cognitive mechanisms will be fundamental to fostering optimal collaborative learning experiences (Calvo, 2009). Hence, the current study aims to examine the frequency and sequences of emotions during Knowledge Building inquiry using 6,240 Knowledge Forum notes. The notes were collected from three cohorts of undergraduate students in the same course taught by the same instructor from the 2018 to 2021 academic years. In specific, the following two research questions (RQ) guide this study: RQ1: Are there distinct Knowledge Building inquiry threads in terms of productivity (i.e., measured by discourse moves) and sustainability (i.e., measured by thread length)? RQ2: What are the common and distinct emotional patterns between different Know ledge Building inquiry threads?

## Participants and pedagogical design

This study was conducted in a large public university in China. The participants were 134 undergraduate students who took a compulsory course on Learning Sciences from 2018 to 2021. The purpose of th is course was to help students develop a basic understanding of core learning sciences theories (e.g., computer-supported collaborative inquiry, assessment, learning how to learn) and engage in scientific inquiry and knowledge  building practices. Each semester, the course lasted 16 weeks with two 45-minute lessons each week. The three classes were designed in the same pedagogical manner. The c ourse instructor used a threecomponent Knowledge Building design to facilitate the undergraduates to engage in knowledge building. The course started with creating a collaborative and inquiry culture (component 1, weeks 1-5). The students were involved in a set of Knowledge Building principle-based individual-level activities (e.g., writing weekly reflection journals, signing learning contracts with the instructor), group-level activities  (e.g., collaborative design of research plan for their inquiries) and discussions and whole-class discus sions so that they gradually were immersed into the culture and developed necessary skills of collaboration, inqui ry and reflection for effective Knowledge Building. Next, the undergraduates were scaffolded to conduct problem-centered inquiries and construct theories on the Knowledge Forum (Figure 1, component 2, weeks 6-8). The students were involved in collective reflections on their participation and interaction in collective inquiries  using the Knowledge Forum integrated assessment tools. They continued to deepen their Knowledge Building inquiries and advance collective knowledge by engaging in reflective assessment processes using learning analytics  (component 3, weeks 9-16). Generally, the students conducted two rounds of reflective assessment using a nalytics (e.g., idea social network, the growth curve of community ideas, Figure 1) in Weeks 9 and 13. In these reflective assessment activities, the instructor first provided a direct and meaningful interpretation of the data and the methods for productive using the data. Then the students engaged in small-group activities, including task analysis of their group inquiry using CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 69 analytics, identifying gaps, making action plans for regulating their further inqui ry, and constructing a reflective synthesis note on Knowledge Forum. In the Knowledge-Building process, the students c onducted weekly collaborative reflections on their participation and interaction using Knowledge Forum ’s integrated assessment tools.

Content analysis We first identified 148 inquiry threads among the notes contributed by three classes. We then conducted a content analysis of all notes within each inquiry thread using the Knowledge Building discourse moves coding scheme developed in previous research (Yang et al., 2016; Yang, Zhu, Sun, et al., 2022) and the academic emotions coding scheme developed by Yang and her colleagues (2022; see the link for the two coding frameworks). The discourse move coding scheme (i.e., cognitive dimension) consists of three categories and their subcategories: epistemic dispositions, collaborative interactions and metadiscourse. The academic emotion coding scheme includes anxiety, confusion, curiosity, joy, boredom, frustration, and surprise (Pekrun et al., 2017). Two raters (the third author and another researcher in our team) independently coded the discourse of one class (n = 2,736, >30%). We achieved the following inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s kappa) scores:.82 for epistemic dispositions,.80 for collaborative interactions,.85 for metadiscourse, and.80 for academic emotions. The rest of the notes were coded by the third author.

Clustering analysis We first determined the number of clusters to retain in clustering using th e Parameters package in R studio (Lüdecke et al., 2020). We then ran a k-means clustering analysis. The input va riables for clustering analysis include thread length and the 13 attributes of students’ Knowledge Building discourse moves (Yang, Zhu, Sun et al., 2022).

Lag-sequential analysis We applied Lag-sequential Analysis using the R package, LagSeq, in R studio (Draper & O’Connor, 2019). Lagsequential Analysis transformed the series of emotion codes into a contingency table and calculated each emotion combination's transitional probability and z-score. We chose lag equals as 1, which means this study only investigates two-item emotion sequences that immediately follow each other. The adjusted residuals (the z-score), CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 70 the algebraical difference between predicted and observed frequency, are not nor mally distributed. As Bakeman and Quera (2017) suggested, we used permutation significance level instead of adjuste d residuals significance level to identify significant sequen ces. Yule’s Q represents the effect size of a sequence (Bakeman & Quera, 2017). The sequences with positive z-score are more likely to happen in the inquiry threads, and sequences w ith negative z-score are less likely to occur in the inquiry threads (Yoder & Symons, 2010).

## Discussion

The study examined emotion frequencies and sequences between different types of Knowledge Building inquiry threads contributed by undergraduate students. We identified productive and improvable thre ad clusters through clustering analysis. We found that emotions tend to be self-repeated; positive activating emotions, such as surprise and joy, do not occur more in productive inquiry than in improvable inquiry threads. Confusion and frustration, as negative activating emotions, are significantly more popular in the productive threads, which may indicate that learners are engaged in the discussion to resolve conflicting ideas. Several findings are worth further discussion. From proportion analysis, improvable threads have a significantly higher proportion of surprise. Surprise describes an initial stage where students encounter new information or idea s (Noordewier et al., 2016). In this study, notes mentioning other students ’ unique perspectives or stating a new idea were labeled as surprise. For example, one note labeled as surprise says, “…Angela Duckworth's research on grit has given me an epiphany… Looking back on my own experience, the biggest reason for success or failure is grit! …”. A previous study also indicated that surprise, a positive activating emotion, can benefit learning when it induces cognitive CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 72 disequilibrium, but being in the state of surprise can mean an inadequate understanding of the topic (Worsley & Blikstein, 2015). On the other hand, productive threads have higher percentages of notes labeled as confusion and frustration. Students experience confusion when encountering a cognitive impasse tha t is the antecedent of deep learning (D’Mello & Graesser, 2014). For example, in this study, in a note la beled as confusion, one participant wrote, “…we can monitor emotions to improve our learning efficacy … so wha t kind of emotions should we have? How can we monitor the emotions?” Prior research also indicated that people experience frustration when solving complicated tasks (D’Mello & Graesser, 2014). In this study, one participant expressed frustration in one note in the productive threads, “… but we are unable to find a way to incorpor ate perceptional factors into [measuring learning achievement]”.

From the Lag-sequential analysis, frustration, anxiety, curiosity, joy, and surprise are more likely to be self-repeated. The result is consistent with prior results on emotional seque nces during collaborative learning (Yang, Zhu, Chan et al., 2022), suggesting students might stay in the same emotional state if they have not made cognitive progress during Knowledge Building.

Nevertheless, it is notable that confusion is more likely to be followed by anxie ty and less likely by curiosity in both clusters. It departs from D’Mello and Graesser’s (2014) model of dynamic affects. They theorize that curiosity, as a positive activatin g emotion, is associated with students’ desire to learn something and wil l happen when students feel confused. It is possible that confusion transitions to anxi ety because students do not employ effective learning strategies to transform a question into actional plans  productively (Muis et al., 2015). Another explanation is that anxiety, in the context of Knowledge Building, is not a d etrimental emotion. In Knowledge building, students have the agency and autonomy to advance their conceptual knowledge collaboratively, and there are no pre-defined correct answers. Hence, they will inevitably experience uncertainty, which might have been manifested through notes labeled as anxiety. For example, a note labeled as confusion asked about measuring subjective perspective during learning, which is followed by a note labeled as anxiety that disapproves of the previous note, “I think learning analytics they [the textbook] m entioned is a pedagogical method, through which students can have better performance … [the textbook] is right, but maybe one-sided…”. The first note raised a question, whereas the second critiqued the previous re asoning. Hence, anxiety in Knowledge Building might suggest that students engage with the discussion but struggle to reach an agreement. Another interesting finding is that frustration is more likely to be followed by anxie ty in the productive threads, whereas anxiety is more likely to be followed by frustration in the improvable threads. Anxiety-frustration might indicate that students do not know how to deal with ambiguity during collaborati ve inquiry (Di Leo et al., 2019). On the other hand, frustration-anxiety, a deactivating to activating transition, mig ht demonstrate that community members stay active in discussing the topic even if a member expresses frustration (Pekrun et al., 2011). For example, when a note states, “it is not necessary to discuss grit a nymore,” the following note replies, "I think this question is still valuable in some ways...”. Although the reply did not  contribute cognitively to the topic, the students continued engaging in discussion and did not abandon the inquiry thread. Joy to boredom is more likely to happen in the improvable threads. This emotion se quence describes a situation where students disengage from the discussion. For example, following  other group members’ discussions, one student inserted a long text copied from a research ar ticle without any interpretation. This positive-negative transition might indicate that students’ engagement may decline if no  further questions are raised. Improvable threads groups also have significantly fewer notes coded as c onfusion and frustration, which might benefit deep learning and tend to happen when students experience or fail to r esolve cognitive disequilibrium (D’Mello & Graesser, 2014). Taking these two findings together, we might conjecture that during the improvable inquiry, students are less likely to reconcile conflicting ideas, which could explain why they are less likely to feel confused or frustrated.

## Practical implication and limitations

The current study examined the cognitive and emotional dynamics in Knowledge Building. The findings imply that it is valuable to help instructors understand that eliciting positive emotions is not always beneficial to learning. Rather, it is essential to facilitate students to raise questions and rise-above ideas to sustain discussion even though it might cause negative emotions. In addition, the direction of emotions is important. Activating to deactivating emotions might be detrimental to group discussions, such as anxiety, frustration, and joy to boredom. Instructors need to monitor students’ collaborative learning about when and why they disengage from the discussions so that they can intervene properly.

This study has a few limitations. First, content analysis cannot fully captur e students' emotional experiences. For example, some students might look for answers independently w hen they feel curious and only contribute to the threads when they have an answer. Second, we set lag equals to one in the Lag-sequential CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 73 analysis, which only produces immediate emotional transitions. We might have over looked some extended emotional transitions beneficial for learning, such as surprise-curiosity-confused (Vogl et al., 2020).
