# From Questions to Impact: Unveiling Teachers' Power to Support Actionable Learning Analytics Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
With the widespread adoption of e-learning and advances in educational data mining and AI, research publications in learning analytics (LA) have surged in recent years. However, LA has not significantly impacted learning and teaching in authentic educational contexts. As teachers’ uptake of LA depends on whether their pedagogical concerns are addressed, it is important to understand teachers’ LA needs in relation to their learning design contexts. We report on an investigation of the nature of teachers’ LA questions at different levels of design granularity—a learning task, a task sequence, or a course—identified through a systematic review of published empirical LA studies that engage teachers at some stage of the LA research process. The findings show that teachers seek actionable feedback on learning processes and progress. However, available LA often focus on course-level performance or task-level engagement, yet actionable insights need to consider relationships among a sequence of tasks.

## Introduction

Studies (e.g., Herodotou et al., 2019) show that teachers recognize the educational potential of learning analytics (LA)—the measurement, collection, analysis and reporting of data about learners and their contexts, for purposes of understanding and optimizing learning and the environments in which it occurs (SOLAR, 2011). The potentials include shedding light on the learning process (Knoop-van Campen et al., 2023), supporting assessment (Holstein et al., 2017), orchestrating collaboration (Martine z-Maldonado, 2019), identifying students at risk and providi ng timely intervention (Ouatiq et al., 2022). However,  teachers’ perceived usefulness of LA contrasts wit h their irregular and low-frequency use (e.g., Herodotou et al., 2019). A key problem underpinning this conundrum is the mismatch between teachers’ needs and the LA that they can access (Dollinger et al., 2019). The LA community’s definition of LA (SOLAR, 2011) aligns with research findings from the learning sciences that deep learning does not happen simply through receiving instruction. It  is arguable that the most important role for teach ers is as a learning designer: designing learning experiences a nd learning environments to achieve the targeted le arning outcomes (Mor et al., 2015). Hence, if LA were  to truly achieve the goals of understanding and opt imizing learning, the analytics provided must be relevant to the learning context as designed by the teachers concerned. The need for LA to be closely connected to the lear ning design (LD) context to achieve its educational goals has long been recognized (Law & Liang, 2020; Mangaroska & Giannakos, 2018). However, in what ways and at which stage of the LA development process ha ve teachers’ data-based questions about students’ learning been considered, and how do teachers’ LA questions connect with the features of their LDs? Answers to these questions based on published empirical LA literature would contribute to a better understanding of the nature and development process of LA that would contribute tow ards the development of LA systems that can truly benefit a wide sector of teachers and learners in authe ntic educational contexts.  Given the large number of published empirical research in LA, a systematic review is an appropriate method to address these research questions.

## Literature review

The growing interests in studies on using data to enhance teaching and learning has also given rise to three strands of review studies in learning analytics (LA). Early work focused on analytical techniques, with Chatti et al. (2012) presenting a four-dimensional framework to capture the main components of LA tools: data collected, target users, analytical techniques, and the purpose of LA. While this frame work identifies essential components, focusing solely on technical aspects risks creating a platform that fails to address implementation challenges. Later studies shifted  their focus from technology to educational practice. Bani hashem et al. (2022) examined how LA can support feedback in higher educ ation, identifying pedagogical objectives that can be achieved through LA-supported feedback, such as improving self-regulation and motivating active learner engagement. This strand of  reviews highlighted the benefits of LA in various pedagogical practices, including learning design, management, and implementation.

Most review studies, however, examine LA without co nsidering the learning design (LD) context. Gašević et al. (2015) argued that data-driven platforms alone are insufficient for generating actionable insights or ensuring sustainable use. Mangaroska and Giannakos (2018) used learning activities as indicators of teachers’ LD intentions to identify LA used in different context s, categorizing five learning activities based on a  review of 43 papers. This categorization  provides information on intended learning outcomes in different contexts and highlights the importance of aligning LA with LD to shed light on students’ learning processes for the generation of actionable insights to improve learning experiences and environments. A key limitation of this study, however, is its failure to consider layers of pedagogical decision-making (Goodyear, 2005) beyond learning activities. LD decisions are multilayered, LA should be differentiated based on LD granularities Learning design is complex. Alignment and connection between learning outcomes, learning tasks and assessment methods across different levels of a course design is crucial for achieving the intended learning outc omes. Goodyear (2005) argues that there are four layers of pedagogical decision-making: pedagogical philosophy, highlevel pedagogy, pedagogical strategies and pedagogi cal tactics. Pedagogical philosophy such as constru ctivism has pervasive influence on the design of the entire  course but are often implicit in the design process. High-level pedagogy translates philosophical positions into br oad pedagogical approaches (e.g., problem-based learning) without prescribing specific methods. Both pedagogi cal strategy and pedagogical tactics are actionable  a nd procedural. The former provides a broad plan in the  form of a task sequence to achieve specific learni ng objective(s) and t he latter describes specific ways to enhance the ef fectiveness of a learning task (e.g., using gamification, encouraging feedback, etc. to motivate learners). Given the multilayered nature of pedagogical decisi on-making, the connection between LA and LD should also be multilayered. Based on 60 STEM LDs collected from authentic classroom contexts, Law and Liang

(2020) developed a multilevel framework to articula te the connection between LA to be integrated into LD at

course, curriculum component (i.e., a sequence of c onnected tasks to achieve a specific learning objec tive) and task levels. It provides a framework to couple systematically the intended learning outcomes, student performance, and behavior at each level of LD granularity (Goody ear, 2005) with the corresponding LA measures, data to be collected, relevant LA techniques, and visualizations.

## Research questions

Based on our literature review, it is apparent that  connecting LA questions to LD is more nuanced than  simply matching LA questions with learning tasks. Given th e layered nature of LD decisions, it is reasonable to expect that LA questions could also be mapped to specific granularities of an LD f or more effective guiding of the generation of LA results relevant to teachers. Can LA questions reported in the literature be read ily mapped to the course, curriculum component, and task levels a s described in Law & Liang (2020)? If so, what diff erences exist for LA questions across these mappings?

Before we attempt to answer this question through a  systematic review, it is important to note that research on LA has diverse goals, not all of which cater for the needs of teachers. For LA to provide actionable feedback, the LA questions must answer questions th at teachers care about in relation to their pedagogical practices. In this study, we refer to these as teac her-facing learning analytics questions (TF-LAQ) and these are the LA question that this study focuses on. The val ue of participatory design in the development of LA  systems has been well-recognized (e.g. Buckingham Shum et al., 2019). It is difficult to j ustify specific LA questions as teacher-facing without engaging teachers in the process. However, teacher engagement can vary in terms of when and the roles they play in the process, from the specification of the questions and intent to how the analytic results are presented, interpreted, and whether there is in teraction and support for consequential action. Hence, in this study, we also investigate the value and actionability and of the LA as perceived by teachers. To summarize, this study addresses two research questions:

1. What teacher-facing LA questions can be identified from the published literature and whether these differ

across different LD levels?

2. What LA features are considered as conducive in bringing actionable insights from teachers’

perspective?

## Research design and methods

We have adopted systematic review as our research m ethod for answering the above two research questions. To identify and select publications for this review, we followed the PRISMA guideline  proposed by Petticrew and Roberts (2008). A coding scheme for analyzing the LA questions was developed based on Law & Liang’s (2020) Multilevel Framework to Integrate Learning Analytics into Learning Design. We stopped at 255 hits, because the following consecut ive 10 hits were not related to our research problems. The search string used was (learning analytics) AND ("dashboard" OR "visualization" OR "question") AND (teacher). Si nce the research focuses on learning analytics for teachers, the exact phrase (learning analytics) AND (teacher)  must be included. A dditionally, some empirical studies involve working with teachers in the design of dashboards or other visualizations to facilitate their interpretation of learning analytics. Thus, ("dashboard" OR "visualization" OR "question") were included in the search string to serve as an alternative to “questions”.

Screening and selection process The search process started on Feb.28 th 2024 and completed on March 5 th. We only included studies published in English since 2011 as that was the year the first International Conferenc e on Learning Analytics & Knowledge (LAK) was held, formalizing the term learning analytics. This initial search identified 984 hits. After removing 189 duplicates manually, by Covidence, and by other automatic tools, we screened titles and abstracts, excluding 338 papers that didn't involve teachers, leaving 45 7 publications. Finally, a further 43 studies were removed due to inaccessible full texts. Three eligibility criteria were then used to identify the publications that are relevant for the present systematic review: (1) it was  an empirical study, (2) teachers’ questions were re ported, and (3) information about how the teachers’ questions were identified was described. Using these eligibility criteria, 332 publications were removed from the 414 full-text articles, giving a total of 82 publications for the final analysis. Data extraction, coding scheme development and analysis. All papers were imported into NVivo for segmentatio n and data extraction. Data related to the following six variables were extracted: (1) study contexts (i.e., the purpose of the LA study, subject discipline, students involved, duration, etc.); (2) LA questions asked  (could be as explicit inquiries or implicit questions presented as LA requirements); (3) contextual information related to teachers’ involvement—this includes who (researchers and/or teachers) proposed the questions, when the teachers became involved (ranging from the needs analysis stage to the final evaluation), how the questions were elicited (e.g., through interviews, surveys, co-design activities, etc.) Identification Screening Eligibility Included and why (the teachers’ pedagogical goals for engagement); and (4) teachers’ responses to the LA outputs (mostly dashboards), including their interpretation, perceived usefulness, and actual usage, if applicable. To answer RQ1, all LA questions  (LAQs), supplemented by associated information and requirements, were captured and categorized according to their level of design granularity (Law & Liang, 2020):

1. Task-level questions are those that focus on students’ performance, and/or behavioral e ngagement such

as time on task, etc. in relation to a specific learning task. For instance, “(for a quiz item) which students are “almost there”, and just need a nudge to reach mastery?” (Holstein et al., 2017, p. 22)

2. Curriculum component (CC) level questions are those related to a coherent sequence of tasks addressing

specific learning objectives. These questions may require: (a) LA reports showing progress in the CC as a whole, (b) a set of inter-connected LA results/visualizations that show how s tudents progress through the sequence of closely related learning tasks, and (c) LA reports on one core learning task in the CC that plays a critical role in students’ learning. “How to divide students into groups for group activities in class based on "pre-class session" behaviors and performance?” (Fernández et al., 2018, p. 28) is an example of a CC level TF-LAQ.

3. Course-level questions are those requesting LA reports that visualize students’ learning outcomes for an

entire course, or on a thematic analysis involving an aggregate of several CCs. For instance, “How were learning design elements used by students?” (Schmitz et al., 2018, p. 304) is one such example. To answer R Q2, thematic analysis was conducted on  teachers’ reactions to the LA results offered in response to their questions to identify the LA features that contribute to the perceived usefulness of the LA. First, two coders read and familiarized themselves with th e data, generating initial codes by highlighting si gnificant segments. Similar codes were grouped into themes to  identify key patterns. After refinement and valida tion, the themes were finalized and assigned descriptive names.

In the remainder of this section, we will provide more in-depth, qualitative analysis of the LA questions initiated by teachers at each level of design granularity. Task level questions across different task types To gain finer grain understanding between the natur e of the tasks designed by teachers and the kind of  LA questions they were interested in, we further categorize the tasks described in the publications into four categories based on Law et al.’s (2017) task taxonomy: (a) directed learning  tasks, including receiving and interpreting information, practices, and assessments, (b) exploratory learning tasks, including information  search, engaging in discussions, and tangible explorations such as conducting experiments and simulations, (c) productive learning tasks that involve the creation of  conceptual or tangible artifacts, such as essays or  models, delivering presentations, or creative performances such as drama or dance, (d) reflective learning tasks, including writing reflection journals, revising work, and conducting peer or self-assessments. We observe that the most frequently reported  task type was directed learning and productive learning, while exploratory and reflective learning were mainly used before and after conceptual artifact construction tasks. When directed learning tasks were involved, such as tests and assessments, in addition to the correctness, teachers wanted more process-oriented LA to understand the extent to which students understood the concepts (Chaudy & Connolly, 2018), the factors contributing to their (unsatisfactory) performance (Barzola, 2020), whether the scores were from guessing or genuine mastery (Papamitsiou & Economides, 2015), and whether mistakes stemmed from careless slippage or a knowledge gap (Holstein et al., 2017; Papamitsiou & Economides, 2015). Teachers wanted to be able to provide differentiated feedback such as praise or targeted coaching support based on the LA results. If the directed learning task type was receiving and interpreting information such as listening to a lecture or watching a video, teachers wanted to mon itor students’ participation. For real-time classes, teachers are more interested in getting feedback regarding student engagement than performance, in particular the reasons for disengagement such as distraction (Amarasinghe et al., 2020), technical issues or mis behaviors (Swanson et al.,

2022) to maximize the effective use of  valuable class time.

For learning tasks other than those for directed learning, whether these are explorative, productive or reflective, there were no definitive correct answers. In these cases, teac hers were more interested in having the LA to provide formative feedback to inform the teaching process. For example, some teachers would like the LA to alert them when the students missed important  concepts (van Leeuwen et al., 2017)  or held misconception (Dieckmann et al., 2022; Holstein et al., 2017). Teachers value having a collation of students’  ideas that can be shared to support class debriefing beyond a concern for correctness (Dieckmann et al., 2022). When group work was assigned for completion during class time, teachers wanted to know which groups need immediate intervention, which could involve praise or assistance (Barzola, 2020). Teachers also wish to get information that provide differentiation among grou p members, for example whether individual students held misconceptions, how work was distributed among group members, whether there were free-riders, etc. (MartinezMaldonado et al., 2022). When groups engage in exploratory learning such as conducting experiments (Holstein et al., 2017) or nursing simulation ((Roberto Martinez-Maldonado et al., 2020), LA could be used to visualize the extent to which students have mastered a skill. Instead of just assessing the final outcome, teachers are interested in whether students can approach the task correctly, which could be  whether students were doing things in the right order with the right timing, and whether each step met all the specific requirements  (Martinez-Maldonado et al., 2020).

There were also non-task-specific LAQs that focused on helping teachers to inquire into the effectiveness of their LDs. These can be categorized into three types. The first was about the clarity of course materials (Chavan & Mitra, 2022; Holstein et al., 2017). Teachers expected LA to identify ineffective materi als and suggest improvements by summarizing frequent mistakes in quiz, or common misconceptions in students’ constructed artifacts (e.g. Melero et al., 2015).  The second was about the quality of task design and implementation. Teachers hope LA can help them promptly assess whether the i ntended learning goal was achieved (Martinez-Maldonado et al., 2020), track the impact of their pedagogical action on students (e.g. Amarasinghe et al., 2020), or students’ affective state like satisfaction levels (Melero et al., 2015). Curriculum Component level questions Despite teachers’ repeated calls for  LA that can support  continuous monitoring of the entire learning process (Monroy et al., 2013), relatively few studies reported on LA concerning a series of connected learning tasks. There were three ways how LA was implemented at this leve l. First, teachers want to have information about students’ performance or engagement (e.g., failure to submit assignments several times in a row, no access to essential learning materials, failure to meet the passing grade (Chen et al., 2019), or no engagement in the discussion forum (Akcayir et al., 2021). These are indicators that help to identify students at risk of dropping out or not submitting the next assignment, enabling teachers to make  timely interventions to help students overcome the ir difficulties (Ouatiq et al., 2022). LA over a sequence of tasks offer insights on students’ progress (Chen et al., 2019; Ouatiq et al., 2022). Examples include automatic ranking of individual students ’ programing ability in each chapter (Fu et al., 2023), and the evolution of discussion topics  (Akcayir et al., 2021). Some teachers attempted to integrate LA into a series of tasks, like using pre-class activity performance and engagement to inform in-class grouping of students or lectures preparation like time allocation to differ ent topics, while post-class activities could assess students’ mastery of targeted knowledge or skills (Fernández et al., 2018). Course level questions There are three types of course level LA questions based on their pedagogical aims: predicting students at risk of failing the course before the final assessment, facilitating summative assessment based on both students’ efforts and performance, and improving LD based on course level data. Teachers aim to identify students with a history of failing the subject as "at risk". The essential of course level prediction is to understand what it takes for students to pass and provide targeted, proactive support accordingly (Alhadad & Thompson, 2017). To include engagement and process data in the final assessment, teachers need LA results to provide justification (Holstein et al., 2017), such as summary of performance across all topics/chapters (Mohseni et al., 2021)  and learning materials access statistics (e.g.Schmitz et al., 2018). Teachers also expect course-level LA to suggest improvements in structure (e.g., how different student groups interact with t he overall course structure and materials, and whet her these interactions lead to different learning outcomes), schedule (e.g., comparing engagement data before and after key school events), and implementation (e.g., identifying the most popular discussion forum topics) (Tervakari et al., 2014; Bakharia et al., 2016; Jayashanka et al., 2022).

Teachers’ reactions to LA provided and the features of “helpful” LA questions. Of the 195 LAQs initiated by teachers, 48 did not provide information on teachers’ responses to the LA feedback, 141 reported positive responses by teachers and six reported negative response. A common feature of the reported positive (or “helpful”) feedback was that the se all addressed explicit pedagogical aims  and can be easily incorporated into teachers ’ existing practices. In addition to supporting the summative assessments  (N=2), teachers expected LA reports to be  dynamic and continuous, providing information connecting students’ performance/behavior/affection status over the whole learning process (N=24). Teachers want LA to help identify students who are struggling (N=22) or disengaging (N=23). Teachers found it useful for LA to facilitate personalized feedback (N=4), lead to self-regulation (N=6), or support class debriefing by identifying noteworthy ideas or events (N=14). Teachers also desire the information on common mistakes (N=19) to understand students’ challenges and identify problematic components in their LDs (N=19). However, to improve LDs requires not only information on “what”, but also on “why” (N=8).

Of the  six questions receiving explicitly negative responses to their LA feedback, t he most frequent problem reported by teachers was difficulties in interpreting the information given. These relate to ambiguities in how the information  was expressed (Pozdniakov et al., 2022), high cognitive load in interpreting complex visualizations (Jayashanka et al., 2022), or challenges in getting insights regarding a series of interconnected tasks (Wise & Jung, 2019). Teachers also complained that the over-abundant information may not address their needs (Gruzd & Conroy, 2020). The reluctance to use LA on a daily basis may also be due to the timing of the LA report (Wise & Jung, 2019) and increased workload (Kasepal u et al., 2022). However, even if teachers can understand the information and the implications for possible intervention, teachers may choose to ignore them based on their own professional deliberation (Pozdniakov et al., 2 021) or perceived contextual challenges in their implementation (Aleven et al., 2022).

## Discussion

This systematic review  was prompted by our  curiosity about why teachers recognized the potential of LA to improve teaching and learning, yet its adoption remained low. The findings from RQ2 suggest that the LA provided to teachers must address teachers’ concerns and be presented in a format that is easily interpretable and actionable. On the other hand, teachers may not be able to clearly articulate their LA questions or the kind of LA visualizations that could help them. The analysis results for RQ1 presented in Table 1 s how that irrespective of whether the LA questions were initiated by teachers or LA resea rchers, most were about students’ performance and behavior. Only very few were seeking informatio n with an explicitly stated goal of supporting teachers’ reflection on their LDs. So, in what ways did the teachers expect LA to su pport their work in enhancing student learning? Our findings for RQ2 are rather thought-provoking. What teachers most wanted to know was wh ich students were struggling or disengaging. The “actions” to be taken were primarily geared towards “personalized” intervention or class scaffolding. This implies that teachers mainly wanted to know the reasons why some students might be struggling. One common belief about students’ lack of learning progress is their disengagement. Hence, teachers want to be alerted if students were not engaged, they want to seek way s to re-engage them. Hence, the strategy for “LA-informed intervention” was relatively piecemeal, wi thout addressing “learning design” as conceptualized in the learning sciences literature that comprises layers of connected design decisions that requires constructive alignment across these layers (Goodyear, 2005).

Our findings from RQ1 show that a large majority of the LA questions collated from th e papers were at either the task level or the course level. Only fewer than 12% of the LA questions were asked at the CC level. The small number also limits the insights on how LD can be adapted to leverage the affordance of LA.   The implementation of specific pedagogical approaches can only be manifested throug h a sequence of CCs, and the effective achievement of specific objectives often need well designed pedagogical str ategies that could only be executed through a sequence of tasks, i.e., a CC. We argue that the specification of LAQs and their pedagogical purposes should also be a part of the decision-making during the learning design process.  Based on our findings, we believe that significant advances in LA adopt ion requires advancing researchers and teachers’ understanding of implementing pedagogy as a multilayered design process. Further, teachers should be supported to specify their LAQs, the pedagogical purposes they serve, as well as the insights they wish to receive as part of the LD process such that the LA provided can be design-aware.
