# “The Amount of People that Like Hotdogs or Hamburgers”: How Youth Characterize What Data is and What They Can Do with It

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Data science education has had a recent proliferation of reports, frameworks, and other resources to guide its continued growth. We also see an opportunity to understand the prior experiences that learners bring to their data science education, specificall y the ways they characterize what data is and what it cando. We collected and qualitatively coded open-ended responses from 43 youth in two US cities to identify categories of how young people describe data and ways to use it. Salient in our findings are youth characterizations of data as actively collected and serving a purpose, often of creating new knowledge or communicating. Preliminary differences between 11-14-year-olds and 14-18-year-olds suggest ways these characterizations may change over time. We propose our identified characterizations as resources in studying how youth think about data and how to improve data science education.

As data plays an ever-larger role in daily, professional, and civic life, so too has data science education taken up the mantle of equipping K-12 learners with the skills necessary to navigate and author those intersecting worlds. This young, interdisciplinary field draws on literature about diverse learning contexts and mediators (Wilkerson,

2025) and benefits from recent reports and frameworks outlining the range of data science concepts, skills, and

perspectives (e.g., K-12 Data Literacy and Data Science Learning Progressions, 2025; Lee et al., 2022; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2023). Evidence suggests that young people hold diverse conceptions of what counts as data, where these conceptions range from quantitative measures (e.g., numbers generated through scientific investigation) to particular representations of quantitative information (e.g., graphs and charts) to the collected digital traces of their interactions (Acker & Bowler, 2018; Bowler et al., 2017). Research in mathematics contexts documents learners’ characterizations of data within classroom work (Konold et al., 2015) as we ll as their understanding of key concepts like variability (e.g., Petrosino et al., 2003) and statistical reasoning (e.g., Lehrer et al., 2014; Makar & Rubin, 2018). Situated perspectives seek to understand the ways that learners’ relationships to data sha pe learning (e.g., Roberts & Lyons, 2022; Wilkerson & Polman, 2020). To expand on these frameworks, we seek to characterize the prior knowledge that learners bring to their data science education. Consistent with piecewise views on conceptual change (e.g., diSessa, 1988; Smith III et al., 1994), we seek to identify the conceptual foundations on which learners build their data science learning experiences. We see our work as similar to the “draw a scientist” (e.g., Chambers, 1983) and human-computer interaction studies (e.g., Turkle, 1984) that characterize how people conceptualize key elements of topics they learn about or work with. When a particular word is used (e.g., scientist, computer, data), what other words, images, actions, or ideas come to mind for learners? What are the associations and resources that learners bring to their, in this case, data science education? With this understanding in hand, we aim to inform data science education in si milar ways as the “draw a scientist” and “what are computers” work informed science and computer science education, particularly by identifying overly narrow understandings and informing approaches to expand them. To gain insight into the conceptual pieces youth bring to their data science learning, we adopt a discursive approach (Sfard, 2007) that attends to the words youth use when describing what “data” means to them. As Sfard and Lavie (2005) documented in mathematics learning, words that educators assume to have shared meaning can have very different meanings for learners; these differences can shape or even obstruct learning. Some research has studied the language adults use when describing data (Peterson, 2021). We believe that youths’ understanding of “data” is especially relevant because the word is both prevalent and polysemous; mainstream and academic conversations on the value of data science education often include variants on the phrases “data is everywhere,” “data is all around us,” and “everything is data.” These varied (and often vague) uses beg the research questions: What do learners think that data is? What do they think they cando with it? As one strand of work to answer these questions, we analyze 43 youths’ responses to open-ended questions about what data is and what they cando with it. Rooted in discourse analysis, we use grounded coding, 1602 informed by word frequencies, to expand a framework begun by Lanouette et al. (2025) to characterize youth’s thinking about data. Acknowledging our relatively small sample size, we find that youth characterize data as numbers actively collected for a purpose, where that purpose is often to generate new knowledge or to communicate. Preliminary differences across our participants suggest that with age and experience, youth may more readily acknowledge qualitative data and the ways data contribute back to (rat her than just resulting from) scientific work. We propose that our characterizations of how youth think about data and its uses can inform pedagogical approaches that leverage the conceptual resources youth bring to their data science learning. This study is part of two larger data science education projects that use qualitative and quantitative techniques to develop and study data science education opportunities for youth and adult learners.

## Participants

Participants include 43 consented youth, 11-18 years old, from two US states: 28 youth f in a Southern US state whose teachers participated in a data science professional development program and 15 youth in  a Northeast US state participating in a week-long data investigation camp. Of these 43 youths, 29 were aged 11-14, and 14 were aged 14-18. These two populations were convenient samples from the authors’ existing research networks. Data sources and analysis As part of a larger data science skills assessment, participants were asked to respond to the open-ended prompts “If you had to explain to a friend what data is, what would you tell them?” and “What are things you cando with data?” Surveys were administered via Google Forms. Northeast US participants completed the survey before data science instruction began. Southern US participants completed the surveys in their regularly scheduled science classes, before any overt data science instruction from the research project. Controlling for data science instruction that occurred prior to the focal research projects is beyond the scope of this initial work. We analyzed participants’ responses to the two prompts using separate but related codebooks. To develop each codebook, we first ran a frequency analysis of all words in participants’ responses to each prompt. After building a list of excluded words (e.g., “a,” “the,” “say”), we noted frequent individual words (e.g., “collect”) or word groups (e.g., “chart,” “figure,” “graph”). To emphasize participants’ ideas, we edited the quotations shared below for spelling and grammar.

Noting differences in sample size by age group (29 youth aged 11-14; 14 youth aged 14-18), we also share preliminary analyses of how responses differed by age. 1604 As displayed in Table 1, respondents overwhelmingly characterized data as something that is actively collected (67%), with science experiments as a specific means of collection (19%). Characterizations of data as describing a particular phenomenon (37%) or  serving a specific purpose (33%) were also typical. More respondents characterized data as numeric (28%) than as qualitative (19%) or as in a particular display (21%). In characterizing what they could do with data (Table 2), 53% of respondents mentioned creating knowledge. Communicating and doing science were the next most common uses (35% each). Within communication, argumentation and display were evenly split (14% each). Under a quarter (23%) of respondents mentioned using data to characterize something, split between counting and identifying patterns (12% each). Acknowledging the difference in sample sizes, we tentatively identify differences between how youth aged 11-14 and youth aged 14-18 characterize data and its uses. Half of the older youth characterized data as qualitative compared to only one younger respondent (Table 1). Older youth mentioned using data for scientific work over five times as often as younger respondents (79% vs 14%; Table 2). Identifying patterns and communicating via displays were two other uses of data that seemed to increase dramatically with age (36% each for older respondents; 0% and 3% respectively for younger respondents). In contrast, younger respondents mentioned using data to address problems or to count things (17% each), uses that no older participants mentioned. These preliminary findings merit further investigation with larger sample populations. Taking a discursive approach (Sfard, 2007) to qualitatively coding 43 young people’s open-ended responses, we developed a preliminary framework to characterize how youth conceptualize data and the ways they can use it. Contrary to mainstream narratives about passive datafication (e.g., “data is everywhere,” “anything can be data”), our participants characterized data as something that is actively collected and describes a phenomenon or serves a purpose. The uses of data our participants described – namely, creating knowledge, communicating with others, and doing science – mirror these characterizations and reflect academic descriptions, especially around knowledge building and solving problems (Wiggins & Jones, 2023).

Despite our small sample size, we note preliminary differences across participants of different ages. Older respondents tended to recognize qualitative data and patterns as well as quantitative data, a more sophisticated understanding of data’s multiple forms that aligns with recommended learning trajectories (K-12 Data Literacy and Data Science Learning Progressions, 2025). Our older participants also seemed to emphasize doing science and communicating via data displays more than younger respondents, who e mphasized addressing problems and counting. If verified in larger populations, these differences would be consistent with our grounding in constructivist conceptual change (e.g., diSessa, 1988; Smith III et al., 1994) in that they may reflect the ways students typically work with data in US middle school grades (higher-level problem identification and resolution, interpreting data collected and aggregated by others) vs. in US high school grades (finer-grained work to inform their own data collection and communication). Though preliminary, we interpret our findings as underscoring the prominent role of science classes in shaping how youth think about data as well as the opportunities for other disciplines (e.g., social studies, language arts/writing, arts) to expand that thinking. We also acknowledge the limitations of this work. Our sample size overall, and of 14-18 year olds in particular, is too small to support generalized claims. We also acknowledge potential differences between our participants in different locations (Northeastern vs. Southern US) and with potentially varying levels of data science background, though our sample size does not support analysis of those differences at this time. We plan additional data collection efforts with close to 200 middle and high school students to refine this framework and evaluate co-occurrence rates both within and across codebooks. An expanded population will also enable more rigorous analysis of how youth develop their understanding of data over time. We intend this preliminary framework to complement discipline-specific characterizations (e.g., Konold et al., 2015) and offer guidance comparable to the contributions of the “draw a scientist” and “what is a computer” research (e.g., Chambers, 1983; Turkle, 1984). Chambers (1983) inspired a research tradition that identified youth’s prototypical images of scientists as White males in lab coats, which in turn sparked science education reform efforts to expand the people, places, and contexts in which learners saw science happening. In a preliminary way, we identified that youth think of data as numbers collected for scientific purposes and to address problems. While this conceptualization offers a solid foundation, we propose that data science education can work to expand it, especially in ways that emphasize qualitative data, the arts (e.g., Lupi & Posavec, 2016), everyday thriving (e.g., Wiggins & Jones, 2023), and artistic applications.
