# Writing (Righting) “Should-We” Questions: Tracing Young Children's Ethical Uncertainty and More-than-Human Sensemaking Across an Integrative Unit

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores how young children navigate d ethical uncertainty in an integrative unit that involved more-than-human entities such as plants and earthworms. Using sensemaking theory, the study examines children's negotiations of ethical dilemmas during multispecies encounters, analyzing group conversations, instructional materials, and children's work. Findings reveal that students' ideas about "living things" and their ethical responsibilities emerged from shared debates and hands-on experiences. Moments of disagreement, such as whether plants deserve care like animals, exposed both the cognitive and emotional dimensions shaping their understanding of care in the classroom. By focusing on multispecies vulnerability and relationality, the paper critiques Western humanist perspectives. It introduces an "environmental ethics of vulnerability" into elementary science, suggesting that early years classrooms can serve as rehearsal spaces for developing children's ethical and epistemic agency to nurture interspecies care amid growing ecological uncertainty.

Uncertainty and the navigation of collective (un)knowing have proven helpful in examining learners' sensemaking in science education  (Chen et al., 2019; Manz, 2025; Reiser, 2004; Watkins & Manz, 2022). These insights, however varied, highlight connections both to the epistemic, conceptual, and social aims of science. However, in an era known as the Anthropocene, a concept first coined by Crutzen (2002) to describe the genealogical epoch that describes how human activities have fundamentally and forever changed Earth’s biosphere, discussions surrounding the uncertain ethics of performing investigations with  the more-than-human (e.g., animals, flora), a move that resituates the human within broader ecological systems, remain relatively silenced. Following Ingold (2013), who asks, how we might  formulate our inquiries by "joining with" and "learning from" the more-than-human (pp. 6-9), in this paper, I explore a trajectory of learning across a series of classroom sessions examining the ethics of performing a n integrative  science-literacy investigation alongside earthworms and plants. In doing so, I ask the following: (1) How do Kindergarten children make sense of and recalibrate ethical uncertainty surrounding the ir engagement with the  more-than-human (e.g., animal and plant encounters during an integrative science-literacy unit)?; and (2) How might we read these scenes of more-thanhuman entanglements to envision the elementary science classroom as a space to rehearse an ethics of interspecies care?

## Theoretical framework

Theoretically, this paper takes a " sensemaking" perspective to examine young children making sense of socioscientific phenomena. As  the literature suggests, sensemaking is a social process. Originating in the field of organizational studies, sensemaking is an activity and form of learning that is situated within its cultural and historical contexts. It is collaborative and communicative. According to Weick (1995), the strength of sensemaking as a perspective derives from the fact that it does not rely on accuracy and its model is not object perception. Instead, sensemaking is about plausibility, pragmatics, coherence, reasonableness, creation, invention, and instrument ality (p. 57). Weick and colleagues (2005) later proposed that when individuals seek to organize inputs and make sense of a particular phenomenon, they then "enact this sense back into the world to make the world more orderly" (p. 410). In short, sensemaking helps individuals bracket a phenomenon, give meaning to it, label it, and then capture it as they understand it.

Sensemaking in science education research In science education research, sensemaking is a relatively ubiquitous concept. Following Odden and Russ's (2018) synthesis of the literature, by sensemaking I mean the "dynamic process of building or revising an explanation to 'figure something out ' – to ascertain the mechanism underlying a phenomenon in order to resolve a gap or inconsistency in one's understanding" (p. 192). Sensemaking, as a process, not only underscores where and how 1337 uncertainty emerges, but also how new knowledge resolves gaps in knowledge. Through this activity, learners leverage a range of hermeneutical resources to generate, apply, and extend their understanding. In elementary science, sensemaking often serves to engage students in generating and evaluating claims that explain phenomena. This process encourages children to engage in cycles of problem-solving in which they not only propose and refine ideas but also use experiential data to seek coherence across ideas and phenomena (see, for instance, Fitzgerald & Palincsar, 2019; Odden & Russ, 2018; Schwarz et al., 2009). As research well documents, meaningful science practice in the early years provides learners with the opportunity to " engage in spirited disagreement about what evidence means and come to deeper understandings of how the world works as they make and discuss decisions about investigations " (Manz, 2025, p. 3). In short, sensemaking is not only demonstrative of a particular stance to science learning, but also a particular discursive style and communicative process.

Ethical sensemaking in elementary science from a more-than-human perspective The material and semiotic menageries that make up sensemaking in elementary science do not always attend to the entangled common worlds between the human and more-than-human. Although some work unsettles  the Western world's dichotomy of nature/culture and human/nonhuman (see, for instance, Learning in Places Collaborative, 2020; McDaid Barry et al., 2023; or Morales-Doyle, 2024),  research in science education is relatively anemic in its examination of  children's multispecies encounters. Taking this paucity as impetus for sustained study and guided by the field’s growing interest in untangling “should we” questions (Tzou et al., 2021), my goal here is to shift the sense and register through which we commonly know and name uncertainty in the elementary science classroom. In other words, to adopt a stance to sensemaking that rethinks Western humanism and develops an ethics of interspecies relationality.

Joining a range of other multispecies scholars  in early childhood education (e.g., Cutter-MackenzieKnowles et al., 2020; Taylor & Pacini-Ketchabaw, 2018), this paper takes what Tsing (2013) call s a "more than human sociality" stance to sensemaking. In doing so, it repositions humans as just one species among many that make and remake worlds and re-situates "nonhumans within ethical terms" (Gibson et al., 2015). Ethics, here, is a relationship of responsibility.  More specifically, I adopt what Hird (2013) calls an " environmental ethics of vulnerability," a space in which " humans are vulnerable to living and nonliving earth processes " and are "concerned not only with past and present damage but also with future unknowable vulnerability to harm " (p. 107). This two-way reciprocal process, what Haraway (2008) has named a " becoming with," contributed to a "multispecies ethics of environmental vulnerability" (Taylor & Pacini-Ketchabaw, 2015), a form of entangled kinmaking that exceeds the human. Although much of the work on sensemaking in elementary science has  focused on the production of evidence in the development of asserting and revising claims based on inferences from child and teacher generated data, this work zeroes in on the normative questions surrounding the ethics of performing scientific investigations with the more-than-human (e.g., earthworms, plants) and the consequences of these questions on the development of children's conceptual and epistemic understandings of our natural world. Modes of inquiry This paper draws on data generated as part of a larger revelatory case study (Yin, 2009) examining how young children's writing – with a focus on intertextuality – showcased learners'  brilliance and onto-epistemic heterogeneity (Warren et al., 2020) across an eight-week integrative science-literacy unit. Below, I detail the site and context of the study, the participants, and the modes of data generation and analysis for this paper.

## Context, participants, and the focal unit

From March – June 2025, I partnered with Ms. Saratoga, a 20-year veteran teacher, and her class of  22 Kindergarteners. Ms. Saratoga, although an experience d early childhood educator, was new to Alias Elementary, a PK-5 school in the midwestern United States. Of the 22 caregivers, 18 consented their learners to participate in the study, and the assenting children ranged in age from 5 to 6 years old. Of the 18 participating children, 15 were identified by their guardians as white, two as Latinx, and one as Black. 12 of these children self-identified as boys and 6 as girls.

The eight-week integrative unit, co-designed by me, Ms. Saratoga, and a group of 6 caregivers,  situated science and literacy work in phenomena that extended children's  lived experiences (e.g., sensory nature walks, making meals with caregivers) and supported their investigative work about the natural world. Adapted from the state's distributed science curriculum, we reframed some of the science-specific questions (e.g., how can we tell if something is living or nonliving) into a broader, cross-disciplinary guiding query: How does what you eat, shape who you are? This broader question braided the scientific focus on plants and animals with a genre study on memoir. For this paper, I focus on classroom sessions that investigated  how and why worms  and plants react to 1338 stimuli, and on the socio-scientific practices (e.g., obtaining and communicating information, developing and using models) that supported whole-group debate and discussion. Data generation and analysis Across the unit, an array of data was generated. As an "active member" (Adler & Adler, 1987) of the classroom, primary forms of data generation included participant observation of instruction (≈ 43 hours), written jottings and fieldnotes, instructional artifacts (e.g., slides), writing included in children's  science notebooks, photographs of interaction, and side-by-side "design interviews" (Dalton et al., 2015)  during centers time. In addition to these design interviews with table groups (3-4 children sitting at the same table), I also interviewed Ms. Saratoga twice, once at the beginning of the study (37 minutes) and once at the end of the study (74 minutes). Data analysis was an iterative process. Given my theoretical attention to the more-than-human in children's writing (righting) of ethical uncertainty, my analytic approach was developed through Tsing's (2015) "arts of noticing" (p. 37). This initial noticing was conducted through more conventional qualitative data analysis for moments in which the more-than-human became a focus in both small-and whole-group conversations. Using this criterion, I identif ied six classroom sessions wherein plant and animal sentience was framed as a complex ethical consideration. Triangulating these scenes with writing from the learners'  science notebooks, I took three episodes/sessions – those that centered small and whole group deliberation – forward for further analysis. Using Manz's (2016) epistemic level coding scheme (adapted from Kelly & Takao's [2002]), I then coded turns in talk and composition by their epistemic level (e.g., noticing, data collection). Doing so resulted in seeing greater collective uncertainty as children navigated higher levels of abstracted talk. By analyzing how children debated and discussed their encounters with both earthworms and plants during staged classroom investigations with the more-than-human, the findings highlight how broader questions concerning the ethics of these practices led to both thin and thick multispecies solidarity. Consensus concerning the " what" and "why" of science practice became unsettled and ultimately divided as the concept of " living things" was debated. "Should we be doing this? I untangle how questions of multispecies vulnerability did not result in a vision of shared ecological  futures wherein the human and more-than-human were co-implicated, rather one wherein they were divorced from one another, largely because of children describing a lack of observational evidence (e.g., the plant not moving), warranting shared care. Reading across these scenes, I close by highlighting how these seemingly  mundane moments of multispecies uncertainty, ones that are relatively ubiquitous in early years classrooms,  may prompt children – both as individuals and as a larger collective – to take on the role of "ethicist" (Davis and Bautista, 2025) in socio-scientific investigations. I argue that, even though the packaged science investigation prompted a so-called “staged” encounter in child-animal relations, analytically examining them as what Haraway (2008) calls “multispecies contact zones” (p. 205)  provides necessary insight into  the axiological dimensions of navigating ethical uncertainty in the science classroom. Rendering an epistemologically modified and ecologically sensitive approach to research in early science learning, one not only sensitive to the more-than-human, but "attuned to life's emergence within a shifting assemblage of agentive beings " (Ogden et al., 2013, p. 6), I echo recent  work featuring the more-than-human suggesting that we should “cultivate a learning community that engages with the natural world around them with respect, intentionality, and care” (White et al., 2025, p. 516). In doing so, I hope this work provides new inroads for examining children's dynamic expressions of uncertainty and sensemaking in a time marked by sustained ecological precarity.
