# Student Engagement in Collaborative Problem Solving Through Media Arts Production

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaborative problem solving skills are essential skills for young people that can be gained through artistic media production, yet many educators do not feel prepared to assess media projects. We draw upon examples from two case studies in media arts production to examine collaborative problem solving skills. Building on the PISA framework for collaborative problem solving skills, we demonstrate where and when different skills take place during media production creating directions for classroom assessments.

Major Issues Addressed To cultivate equitable education systems for the 21st Century, it is essential to teach young people collaborative problem solving skills (Ito et al., 2020; Graesser et al., 2017). Media arts productions can be integr ated into classrooms to help students concurrently gain collaborative skills through the act of solving problems with peers (Lee et al., 2015). Yet, teachers often do not know how to assess media productions (Reilly & Atkins, 2013). To address this obstacle, we examine collaborative pro blem solving at different stages of the production process creating direction for classroom assessments.

Collaborative problem solving through artistic media production Media production projects often require the collabo ration of individuals in assigned roles with specif ic duties (Barron et al., 2014). Collaborative learning creat es positive results for learners’ cognitive, meta-c ognitive and social aspects of learning (Johnson & Johnson, 2009) and helps learners achieve higher learning outcomes (Chen et al., 2018) and develop teamwork skills (Curşeu e t al., 2012). We adopt Roschelle and Teasley’s (199 5) definition of collaborative learning as “a coordinated, synchronous activity that is the result of a continued attempt to construct and maintain a shared conception of a problem” (p. 70) as media arts production creates an iterative set of problems to be solved. Well-structured, coll aborative problem solving tasks require distinctive  roles and interdependency of the group members (Graesser, et al., 2017). Media arts productions create prime opportunities for collaboration as students concurrently gain collaborative skills through the act of solving problems with peers (Lee et al., 2015).

Learning through arts production processes are inhe rently deep and robust constructivist learning experiences (Halverson, 2013), providing students w ith opportunities to construct meaning of content w hile simultaneously gaining exposure to skills gained th rough the instruction of these art forms (Gullatt, 2008). Production also lends itself to interdisciplinary learning, where creators make sense of a specific domain or topic while also acquiring literacy practices across doma in areas, including: new media literacy, technology  fluency, artistic expression, traditional computer courses, or media education (Peppler, 2010). Pulling from our combined experience in media production and our research in two creative production classrooms, we set out to answer our research question: What collaborative problem-solving processes do students engage in through artistic media production?

Assessing artistic media production While many educators today integrate media production into their classroom, they often feel unprepared to assess media (Reilly & Atkins, 2013). As a result, they either do not assess the assignments or assess production based on the acquisition of factual content creating a decoupling of production practices and assessment (Silseth & Gilje, 2017; Aagaard & Lund, 2013). With the demands of the formal classroom environments, it is difficult to assess media production as it should be: both embedded int o the process and product (Halverson, 2012). Throug h evaluating collaborative media arts production, edu cators can create assessments that will help shape what students see as valuable to learn (Silseth & Gilje, 2017).

In this paper, we demystify assessment for media ar ts production by demonstrating what collaborative problem solving skills to assess throughout the pro duction process. We use the definitions from the PI SA collaborative problem solving framework, which guides assessment of an individual working with a group rather than the group as a whole (Graesser et al., 2017). Production Process Stages Media production involves the process of turning one’s ideas, knowledge, and skills into a digital representation, from the initial spark to a final product (Jocson, 2012; Halverson & Gibbons, 2010). While production is an iterative practice, most practices fit into three s tages: pre-production, production, and post-product ion (Jocson, 2012). Table 1 reflects the collaborative problem solving skills tied to the three stages of production. Pre-production involves conceptualization from brai nstorming, storyboarding, and creating a plan of action. Initial planning creates direction while students set goals (A2), determine tasks to be completed (B2), and assign roles (A1, B3). During production, students collect materials and lace together all of the elem ents, often readjusting their initial ideas (Jocson, 2012). Stu dents communicate with group members (C1), negotiat ing and renegotiating roles and plans (C2, C3) to meet thei r larger goal (B1). Post-production involves refini ng and modifying the artistic representation toward the fi nal goal (D1, D2). As production is an iterative pr ocess, monitoring, providing feedback and adapting the team organization and roles can occur throughout (D3). Methodological Approaches Data Sources Our research examines two case studies from unrelated media arts productions in public schools in the Midwest region of the United States. In both cases, we indi vidually served as participant-observers engaging a nd collaborating with teachers and students throughout the production process. We both used ethnographic field notes from classroom observations and semi-structured interviews with teachers and students to understand more about group roles in these two collaborative productive experiences. ## Data Analysis

To understand what collaborative problem solving processes students engage in through artistic media production, we used a multiple-case study approach to determine  how the production processes compared in these to very different productions (Yin, 2003). We broke down th e two cases into three stages: pre-production, prod uction, and post production (Jocson, 2012). Through deductive coding using each of the categories of the PISA matrix, we determined where and when collaborative problem solving activities took place. We then triangulated data by jointly reviewing the two cases using the PISA matr ix adding a color-coding system to the matrix based  on production stages.

## Major Findings

In this section we use examples from the video prod uction project and puppet documentary to demonstrat e the collaborative problem solving skills found in the pre-production, production, and post-production stages. We tie moments of each stage to particular skills in the P ISA framework, labelling each with the correspondin g letter and number from the matrix (see Table 1).

Pre-production In the second grade classroom, while the students w ere skilled at using technology for project-based l earning, they were novices to the media production process and being part of a production team. The teachers and teaching artist worked collaboratively to devise production roles for the students. Roles were assigned based on input from students on their preferences and interests, as wel l as the teacher 's perspectives of abilities and g roup balance (A1). Crews were formed of six students made up of two partners to each role: puppet designer, set/prop designer, and writer. These were structured to be interdependent roles to support their collaboration while meeting all the needs of the production (A2, A3). The students each had their own tasks to work on to bring the creati ve vision together (B2). Adult guides helped in supporting al ignment to a single vision as well as group communi cation. However, the need for alignment and communication became readily apparent to the students; as they collected resources and began writing, they realized the need to communicate and work together to a shared creative vision-for example, the writers needed to be on the same  page as the puppet designers to write the character properly

(B3).

Production Marty, Chris, Sigrid, and Mariah set out with inspi rational goals of overlapping two videos in order t o see the same character directly behind the other character.  Their process included trials, revisions, and resh ooting as nobody in the group had attempted this before. They started by filming the actor once in front wearing all white and again in the back wearing all black, but they r ealized two problems (B1): 1) the set of the two vi deos was inconsistent and 2) the two films were inconsistently shaky making it more difficult to match up the two videos. To remedy this, they discussed the actions needed t o remedy the inconsistencies (C1) and enacted these  plans (C2): they had to refilm, figure out a way to stabilize their iPad without a tripod, and re-examine the set to make sure everything was consistent. Marty, the director, helped direct the other group members in their ro les from directing the actor’s movements to prompting others  to move parts of the set to create more consistent backgrounds (C3).

Post-production Marty, Chris, Sigrid, and Mariah had to figure out how to overlay video footage to create the impression that the two characters were in the same room. First, they had to learn how to use new software to create the complicated shot using online tutorials and seeking assistance from their teacher. During editing, the group had t o return to production and refilm a few shots for consistency (D2). The group also participated in critique with t heir classmates where they received feedback that conflicted with their shared vision. The most significant feedback they received was that their peers felt the charact er in the film seemed angry or indecisive rather th an anxious. The group therefore returned to refilming, editing, and added additional music and filters to portray the feeling of anxiety (D1).

## Significance of Work

This work has important implications for teachers integrating media arts production into their curriculum. First, by designating specific collaborative problem solvi ng skills into particular stages of production, tea chers can pinpoint moments in production to assess specific skills creating more direction for those teachers who are unsure of how to assess media (Reilly & Atkins, 2013). Sec ond, recognizing that media arts production involve s the social construction of knowledge (Silseth & Gilje, 2017), our work provides direction for assessing collaborative problem solving as a process.
