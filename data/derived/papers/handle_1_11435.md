# Reaching Without Words: An Interaction Analysis of Silent Intersubjectivity

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Understanding intersubjectivity is crucial for advancing theories of learning in social contexts. While existing research has largely focused on verbal interactions, silence is also a significant aspect of classroom dynamics. This study investigates how intersubjectivity between teacher and students is established through silence, and examines the role silence plays in fostering such connections. Interaction analysis of data from a 6th-grade science classroom in Shanghai reveals how silence shifts students’ focus from peers to the teacher and from verbal to nonverbal cues, encouraging deeper reflection and ultimately facilitating the establishment of intersubjectivity. Additionally, the study highlights the cultural dimensions, drawing on Confucian traditions and showing how silence reflects a bidirectional cultural co-construction. Contrary to views of silence as passive, the findings highlight its role in fostering teacher-student connections and cultural understanding. This research illuminates silent intersubjectivity, offering insights for creating reflective, collaborative educational spaces.

## Introduction and background

Education is fundamentally an intersubjective practice (Biesta, 1994). Understanding intersubjectivity is essential for advancing theories of socially mediated learnin g and designing effective learning environments (Na than, Eilam, & Kim, 2007). While intersubjectivity can be examined empirically (Koschmann, 2018), existing research has mainly focused on verbal interactions (Mortimer & Wertsch, 2003; Freire, 2018). However, beyond verbal expression, silence also plays a vital role in classrooms (Ollin, 2008; Rampton & Charalambous, 2016; Tan, Tan, & Lee, 2024). For a  long time, classroom silence has been predominantl y understood in negative terms. It is often associate d with fear, embarrassment, or humiliation among students (Wenham, 2019) and is perceived as a sign of shynes s or lack of confidence (Sedova & Navratilova, 2020; Tang & Wang, 2020). Furthermore, silence is frequently s een as a result of silencing, reinforcing power imb alances between teachers and students (Hanna, 2021).

While these interpretations are valid, growing scho larship suggests silence holds more nuanced roles i n the classroom. It does not necessarily indicate a l ack of participation (Schultz, 2010). Instead, sile nce can serve as a valuable learning resource (Hu, 2021) and a pedagogical tool that challenges oral-centric educational cultures, promotes diverse forms of expression, and fosters student agency (Su, Wood, & Tribe, 2023). Anthropological studies have demonstrated that sile nce can facilitate the establishment of intersubjectivity in group meditation courses (Pagis,2010). In these contexts, subtle nonverbal mechanisms within silence enable individuals to share experiences and  develop mutual understanding.  In the field of education, however, the role of silence in fostering intersubjectivity remains relatively underexplored.

To address this gap, our study employs Interaction Analysis to explore two key questions: How is intersubjectivity between teacher and students established through silence? What role does silence appear to play in establishing intersubjectivity?  By investigating the mechanisms of silent intersubj ectivity, we aim to provide deeper insights into how learning unfolds in classroom interactions.

## Participants and data sources

This study draws on data from a larger, three-year research project. Our team conducted extensive fieldwork in a 6th-grade classroom at a middle school in Shanghai, cap turing video recordings of lessons and supplementin g these with unstructured and video-cued interviews.  In addition to videotaping the lessons, we document ed contextual details through field notes and direct observation, capturing elements beyond the camera's scope. The participants include students from this class a nd their science teacher, Ms. W. At the time of dat a collection, the students were in their first semester at the school, transitioning from primary to middle school. Ms. W, a female teacher with a master’s degree and one year of teaching experience, was responsible for te aching science to six different classes.

## Data Analysis

The analysis of silen t intersubjectivity was guided by interaction analysi s (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). Our approach began with multiple reviews of the lesson recordings to gain a comprehensive understanding of  the classroom dynamics and the role of silence in these  interactions. Key moments of silence, as well as s hifts in teacher-student interactions, were identified and indexed. We then focused on episodes where silence appeared to influence the development of intersubjectivity. Particular attention was given to instances of "trouble and repair" (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), defined as moments when  the “normal stream of activity is broken in some w ay” (1995:69). To validate our interpretations, we revisited the vide os to assess the typicality of these instances, analyzing the role silence plays in establishing intersubjectivity in the classroom.

In this paper, we present a detailed classroom segm ent transcribed using conversation analysis conventions. To capture the interplay between silen ce and non-verbal cues, such as laughter, body movements, and gestures, we utilized Matsumoto’s multimodal transcription format (Matsumoto et al., 2022).

## Findings

The analyzed segment comes from the latter part of a science lesson on the reproductive traits of plan ts. The teacher explained that plants often produce a large  number of seeds to increase their chances of repro duction, a phenomenon common across the biological world. Duri ng this explanation, the students eagerly began nam ing various organisms, creating a burst of lively noise  in the classroom. In response, the teacher remaine d silent for 11 seconds. During this pause, the students’ voices fluctuated and eventually subsided into silence. This 11-second silence is analyzed to explore how the teach er uses silence and how students respond, demonstrating how intersubjectivity between the teacher and students is established through silence. Our analysis starts by examining this initial "rupture" in interaction. Silence is often recommended to novice teachers as a strategy for “k eeping control” in the classroom (Gilmore, 1985). Just before the moment captured in  the transcript, the classroom became so noisy that  the teacher’s final words were inaudible. In this instance, it appears th at the teacher intended to use silence to halt the noise, restore order, and enable the lesson to proceed. However, the students and the teacher interpreted t he silence differently. The students seemed to take  it as tacit approval, leading to even more enthusiastic participation. S-2 shouted “XIAOQIANG”, a humorous slang term for a cockroach commonly used in in ternet culture, while grinning and exchanging an ex cited glance with S-3 (Table 1: Figure 2), clearly enjoying the moment. This trigge red laughter among many students, further escalating the classroom disruption.

Not all students misunderstood the teacher's silence. For instance, as S-2, S-3, and S-4 were laughing, S5 looked up at the teacher while S-6 looked down at the textbook, both appearing calm and serious (Table 1: Figure 2). They understood that the teacher’s silence signaled a need for quiet. So, how did the other students eventually arrive at  this understanding? In response to the students’ laughter and noise, Ms. W remained silent for an ad ditional 8 seconds. During this time, she first low ered her hands and looked at S-2 (Table 1: Figure 3), but S-2 was turned away and did not notice the teacher’s gaze. Ms. W then slightly shifted her gaze forward (Table 1: Figure 4), and during this moment, the students’ la ughter subsided. While some students continued speaking, t heir voices became noticeably softer. By extending her silence, the teacher heightened students’ awareness  of its unusual nature. Although her gaze  toward S-2 went unnoticed by its intended recipient, other students observed the interaction. They may have recognized that S-2’s use of internet slang and the resulting commotion w ere inappropriate for the classroom context. This l ikely prompted them to reflect on and align their behavior with the expected norms in this situation. However, S-2 continued chatting with a classmate, still discus sing the earlier disruptive topic of “XIAOQIANG.” The teacher turned to look at S-2 again, and this time their eyes met  (Table 1: Figure 5). We observed that S-2 stopped speaking and shifted from a grin to a more subdued smile (Table 1: Figure 6). From the changes in S-2’s behavior and expression, we infer that while he  initially failed to grasp the meaning of the teacher’s silence, the direct eye contact, along wi th the gradually quieting atmosphere among his peer s, led him to realize that the silence was not an endorsement and that it was not an appropriate time to continue speaking. The classroom fell silent, with neither the teacher nor the students speaking. Soon after, a student quietly said, “spider.” Immediately following this, another  student, holding a pen and looking at the teacher,  said, “Go on.” The teacher then broke the silence with a brief “Alright then” and resumed the lesson. According to Matusov (1996), intersubjectivity does not require complete  agreement but involves individuals contributing to  and coordinating joint activities. In this instance, we interpret the brief shared silence at the end of the segment, along with the student’s request to continue the lesson, as evidence that intersubjectivity had been established, enabling the class to collaboratively return to the original learning process. Next, we turn to a closer examination of the questi on: What role does silence appear to play in establishing intersubjectivity?

Schutz’s phenomenological theory offers a basis for  understanding intersubjectivity in the classroom. Teachers and students sharing the same classroom form a community of space and time (Schutz, 1973:64). When individuals in this shared environment direct their  attention to a common object, it leads to the "joi nt flow of our experiences" (1973:64), which, according to Schutz, relies on "reflective acts of attention" (1967:98) to form the foundation for intersubjectivity.

We argue that silence facilitates the formation of "reflective acts of attention," thereby contributin g to the establishment of intersubjectivity.

First, the teacher’s silence can prompt a shift in the students' attention, primarily reflected in changes to the "object" of focus. This shift occurs on two levels. On the first level, the focus shifts from other students to the teacher. According to Mark edness Theory, linguistic symbols or behaviors can be categorized as "marked" or "unmarked" based on their frequency or naturalness in a given context. In classroom settings, within the teaching frame, speaking is unmarked for teachers, while silence is unmarked for pupils(Jaworski & Sachdev, 1998).Thus, when a teacher moves from the unmarked state of spe aking to the marked state of silence, it acts as a cue, redirecting students' attention from their peers to  the now-silent teacher. On the second level, students' attention expands from linguistic cues to nonverbal, physical  signals. In interactions, individuals simultaneous ly attend to both verbal and nonverbal cues. However, the teache r’s silence removes linguistic cues from focus, pro mpting students to direct their attention to the teacher’s facial expressions, eye contact, and body posture. Moreover, the attention behaviors elicited by silen ce are reflective. By removing language, it shifts students’ focus to nonverbal cues, introducing uncertainty in their interpretation of the situation. This uncertainty encourages reflective thinking as students attempt to understand the teacher’s expressions, gestures, and other nonverbal signals. Silence also creates a temporal pause, providing students with additional time to process and reflect. In the observed segment, when an initial t hree-second silence failed to produce the desired effect, the teacher extended the silence. This prolonged pause gave students further opportunity to reassess their understanding within the context.

Beyond facilitating "reflective acts of attention,"  silence also serves a cultural role in establishin g intersubjectivity. As Schutz observes, "Our everyda y world is, from the outset, an intersubjective wor ld of culture...we are always conscious of its his toricity, which we encounter in tradition and habit uality, and which is capable of being examined" (1962:133).

Research on teacher silence in Western classrooms t ends to indicate that it is commonly followed by direct verbal reminders (Tan, Tan, & Lee, 2024) or other sound cues, such as a sharp clap or a loud kn ock (Gilmore, 1986). Yet, Ms. W remained silent, offering no additional verbal or auditory cues. In traditional Chinese Confucian education, silence is regarded as a subtle and positive art, emphasizing the learner’s initiative and intrinsic motivation. Educators are encouraged to maintain silence at app ropriate moments, reflecting the principle that “an ideal teacher should lead but not drag, and shou ld enlighten but not directly conduct to the end” (Li, 2020, p. 760).  In the situation analyzed here, the teacher’s silen ce was already understood by some students, as indicated by their body language (S-5, S-6 in Table 1: Figure 2). Student voices began to quiet down (lines 09 and 10), and S-11 even took the initiative to request resuming the  lesson. At this point, from the perspective of Chinese teaching art istry, there was noneed for the teacher to provide  additional verbal explanation.

Intersubjectivity is not a one-way process where culture is simply transmitted fro m an acculturated individual to one who is not; rather, it is a co-constructive process in which both participants act ively contribute to the joint production of meaning (Biesta, 1994). In the context of our analysis, intersubjectivity i s not solely achieved by students attempting to interpret and al ign with the teacher’s silence. The teacher’s delib erate choice to remain silent and sustain that silence reflects her active role in co-constructing intersubjectivity with the students. This behavior, shaped by cultural context, demonstrates her effort to collaboratively genera te shared understanding with the students.

## Discussion & Implications

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that intersubjectivity can be  established even in the absence of language, through silence. Silent intersubjectivity  is grounded in "reflective acts of attention" and reflects a bidirectional cultural co-construction.

## Significance

The research we have presented here contributes to current scholarship in at least two significant way s. Firstly, by uncovering the mechanisms of silent intersubject ivity in classroom contexts, it extends the study o f intersubjectivity beyond verbal interaction, offeri ng new insights into its nonverbal dimension s. As Pagis (2010:324) observes, silent intersubjectivity "allo ws for a more general and inclusive form of intersu bjectivity" that "does not force a translation of experiences i nto words." This form of intersubjectivity embodies  patience, respect, inclusivity, and a shared expectation of m utual understanding. Secondly, this research aligns with the growing interest in silence as a positive rather than negative pedagogical phenomenon (Su, Wood, & Tribe, 2023; Tan, Tan, & Lee, 2024). This perspective challenges conventional views of s ilence as merely passive or disciplinary, emphasizing its potential as an inten tional and culturally embedded tool for fostering m eaningful educational interactions.

Today, amid increasing conflict and division, explo ring the possibilities of intersubjectivity is more urgent than ever. Educating for world-making requires fostering mutual understanding, shared responsibility, and the capacity to connect across differences. By view ing silence not as an absence but as a space for in teraction, learning, and co-existence, we can uncover its potential to foster deeper connections and pave the way for a more inclusive and collaborative future.
