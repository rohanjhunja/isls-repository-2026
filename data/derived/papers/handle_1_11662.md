# Role of a Physical Computing Project for the Flexible Adaptation of the Curriculum: An Exploratory Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study describes the implementation of two curricular versions (coding-centric and data science-centric) of a two-week physical computing project by one Grade 8 science teacher. In both versions, we conducted students’ pre-and post-surveys, post-implementation semi-structured teacher interviews, and classroom observations. This convergent mixed methods study highlights the role of the physical computing context for implementing either curricular versions, their impact on students, and the affordances and constraints the teacher experienced while implementing them.

## Introduction and related literature

Integrated STEM education is “the approach to teaching the STEM content of two or more STEM domains, bound by STEM practices within an authentic context for t he purpose of connecting these subjects to enhance student learning” (Kelley & Knowles, 2016, p. 3). Over the last few decades, physical computing ha s emerged as a new approach to engaging students in integrated STEM learning. In physical computing projects, learners are naturally involved in intertwined engineering and computational processes because they are engaged in “the creative design and realization of interactive objects or installations, which are programmed, tangible artifacts that communicate with their environment using sensors and actuators” (Przybylla & Romeike, 2018, p.2). Further, by making sense of the data collected by sensors to make subsequent  design decisions, learners are immersed in data li teracy learning. This aligns with the recent call from the  National Academies of Science, Engineering, and Me dicine

(2023) that argued data literacy should not be limited as part of the mathematics curriculum or any single discipline

when taught to students. In this study, we present an exploratory study of the implementation of two v ersions of the curriculum of a physical computing project; one version focused on integrating scientific content such as plant science into a more coding-focused project, while the other version focused on  the infusion of data science into the coding curriculum. Our previous classroom imple mentations indicated that physical computing could create a successful integrated STEM learning experience am ong students, offering multiple potential pathways to integrate different disciplines. Yet, there are very few research studies examining the affordances and limitations of varied pathways to integrate STEM. Thus, in this  study, we compared the classroom implementations o f two versions of the Smart Greenhouse project and invest igated the impact, affordances, and constraints of implementing each curricular version.

## Methods

This study is part of the Smart Greenhouse Project,  which engages middle school students in building a  tabletop automated smart greenhouse to grow basil plants. Students learn block-based coding using the micro:bit to code various environmental sensors installed on the smart greenh ouses to collect and log data. Thus, this integrate d STEM project incorporates physical computing, plant science, and data literacy. This convergent mixed methods study (Creswell, 2022) focuses on two rounds of imp lementation, each lasting 10 days, in the classroom  of one science teacher (Mr. M) —in Spring 2023 (coding-centric) and Spring 2024 (data science-centric), who was assisted by one member from our team. This study ai med to answer the three research questions: (i) What is the role of a physical computing project in implementing two different curricular versions? (ii) Does the data scienceheavy version of the curriculum impact students dif ferently than the coding/computation-heavy version? (iii) What are the affordances and constraints of implementing the two curricular versions in the classroom? The participants included the Grade 8 science teacher (Mr. M), 14 students (57.1% Hispanic; 50% male, 42.9% female, 7.1% chose “prefer not to say”) from his Spring 2023 class, and 20 students (35% Hispani c; 45% male, 50% female, and 5% chose “other” or “prefer not to say”) from his Spring 2024 class. The quantitative data consisted of pre-and post-surveys of students conducted during both implementations. These surveys were Likertscaled (1-5) based on five constructs: interest, competence belief, (Chen et al., 2017; Dorph et al., 2016), science identity (Rodriguez et al., 2019; Stets et al., 201 7), future aspiration, and science and coding (our team created these items). The qualitative data included two semi-structured interviews with Mr. M, after each implementation and student artifacts generated from their final mini-projects in Spring 2024.

## Results

The independent samples t-test results indicated no statistically significant  differences in students’ pre-survey scores in the two implementations for the total sco res and each construct (p < 0.05). This indicates s imilarity in the initial stage of Mr. M’s student groups. Secondly, paired-sample t-test results used to examine student gains, indicated no statistically significant differences in the pre-and post-survey scores for each of the five constructs and their total scores at p < 0.05 for Spring 2023 (coding-focused version). On the other hand, in Spring 2024 (data science-focused version), statistically significant differences or positive gains at p < 0.05 were obtained for the total scores (t = 2.24, p = 0.04, df = 19), as well as for the “Competence Belief” (t = 2.83, p = 0.01, df = 19) and “Science and Coding” (t = 2.45, p = 0.02, df = 19) constructs. Lastly, the multiple regression analysis results after comparing the post-survey scores (all items and each construct) of Spr ing 2023 and Spring 2024 students, while controlling their pre-survey scores, indicated no statistically significant differences between the post-survey scores at p < 0.05 for each construct and total sur vey scores. Thus, implementing the two curriculum v ersions similarly impacted the students who participated in the program from t he respective years while controlling for their pre-survey scores.

The interviews with Mr. M revealed some affordances  and constraints of implementing these two versions. Mr. M’s learning goals for the Spring 2023 program were that students could do the pragmatics of coding while also understanding its logic. However, due to some unexpected technical difficulties, the students could not get into in-depth coding and did not understand the code’s mech anism very well, thus progressing slower than expected. He discussed the scientific concepts and got students to collect and v isualize data manually, but as a group, they could not do advanced coding to log dat a. For Spring 2024, Mr. M wanted students to learn to code for tangible purposes like the greenhouse context. He said these learning goals were achieved and minor technical difficulties were resolved. He was also pleasantly surprised by students’ faster progress. He was happy with how well students understood the coding concepts and could move to their mini-projects. It was observed that students were comfortable doing and e xplaining the project by themselves in pairs with l ittle help. During both implementations, Mr. M’s perspectives were triangul ated with the analysis of the students’ final proje ct artifacts and the classroom observation notes.

Thus, each version of the curriculum offered some a dvantages, which also led to some constraints. For example, in Spring 2023, Mr. M could spend more tim e explaining the science concepts but could not get  to advanced coding and data science. On the other hand, in Spring 2024, Mr. M was satisfied with the codi ng and data science concepts students handled but wished h e could spend more time on science-related topics. In both his interviews, Mr. M mentioned that this project has consistently helped students to stay engaged who otherwise felt disconnected in the science class for the rest of the year. Thus, this Smart Greenhouse Project offered multiple pathways that may interest different teachers and s tudents in multiple ways and helped the flexible ad aptation with multiple foci, as observed in the two versions implemented by Mr. M.

## Conclusion and implications

From the teacher’s perspective, both the curricular versions of the project appeared to have some affordances and some limitations. With the limited implementation t ime in both the Spring programs, focusing on one as pect of the project sometimes led to lim iting some other elements. For example, in Spring 2 023, with the curriculum focusing on coding or computation, despite the unexpected technical difficulties faced by the students, they were able to complete basic coding tasks and spend some time working with data manually; but Mr. M wished that they got more time for an in-depth understanding of coding and data logging using coding. On the other hand, in Spring 2024, within the same time frame, students were abl e to understand coding well and they could spend cl ose to half of their time on data science-related ideas. However, Mr. M wished he had more ti me to explain the plant science-related concepts to students. However, due to his f amiliarity with data science concepts, infusing tho se concepts into the project curriculum was smooth, and the related learning objectives could be substant ially achieved. This indicates that, despite the trade-offs of having different foci, it is still possible  to plan the curriculum flexibly to emphasize one discipline majorly and use the other STEM disciplines as auxiliaries in such a physical computing project with an integrated STE M focus. This study has many implications for teach ers, researchers, and curriculum developers. An integrat ed STEM project like this physical compu ting project with multiple pathways can be implemented in various con texts with a specific focus on specific disciplines. For example, teachers from different disciplines can adapt a project like this one to fit their own teaching philosophies and/or their students’ interests and needs. Curriculum developer s and researchers candevelop and study such a project’s implementation process in those contexts.
