## Introduction

The learning sciences have long been concerned with  STEM teachers’ epistemological assumptions (taken-forgranted notions about the nature of knowing and lea rning; Russ & Luna, 2013)  and how they shape classroom practices and pedagogy. Simultaneously, following the growing concern with equitable classr oom practices, a robust literature has emerged that centers how teac hers’ classroom practices and narrative constructio ns of students may position learners as bearers of various deficits (Valencia, 1997). We bring together these bodies of work to argue that STEM teachers’ construction of l earners’ deficit necessarily entails recruiting (consciously or not) epistemological assumptions that elaborate and /or sustain ideological stances of student deficit.  Indeed, our data suggest that positioning students as learners-with-deficits is fundamentally co-constituted by epistemological assumptions and they cannot be understood separately. This has important implications for teacher education.

## Conceptual framework

We work from a sociopolitical perspective on learning (Esmonde & Booker, 2017) and take it that meaning is coconstructed in/through the ongoing interactions amo ng participants in a practice. Two specific dimensions of a sociopolitical framing are central here. The first concerns the ideological and it trains our gaze on discourses of power and social difference (e.g., Philip, 2011). Roughly put, ideologies are narratives that a social group uses to describe aspects of social reality with the goal of  negotiating the status quo.  Naming learners as fast or slow, motivated or not, etc. all illustrate ideologies of  power and social difference. The second dimension of our sociopolitical perspective regards epistemological assumptions and their key role in teachers’ sensemaking about classroom events. Research on epistemological assumptions (Gupta et al., 2022), framing (Hammer et al., 2005), and beliefs (e.g., Kang, 2008) have collectively ma pped out how STEM teachers’ conceptions of knowing, learning and teaching shape what they attend to when it comes to students’ reasoning and classroom participation moment-by-moment (e.g., noticings; van Es & Sherin, 2021) and over the long haul (Russ et al., 2016).

## Methods and empirical context

Data collection took place in a Project-Based Instruction (PBI) methods course offered in a STEM teacher education program housed at a public urban university in southern USA. The course required preservice teachers to design and teach (individually or in pairs) a th ree-day PBI lesson at a local (upper) middle-or high-school. Upon returning from their field-teach, PSTs reflected on their experiences and presented their reflections in class. Presentations lasted 10-15 minutes and were followed by some 10-15 minutes of questions and comments from the audience. In ethnographically informed manner (Hammersley & Atkinson, 1995), we collected about eight hours of video footage of PSTs’ in-class presentations as well as their presentation decks.

## Analysis and findings

We illustrate our argument with a single, extended case analysis.  Mary and Lara were undergraduates in the geosciences and biology, respectively, and together  they developed and taught a lesson on topographic maps to an 8 th grade class at a local magnet school. The lesson had students creating Play-DohTM terrain models, then depicting such a terrain on paper. The goal was to progressively refine students’ representations toward canonical contour lines as means to precisely representing height, but somehow things did not turn out as planned. The team explained this outcome as follows (4/11/2022): [51:03] “Um:: and based on the feedback ((from ment or teacher and students))… we realized that we jam packed a lo t of information into a very short amount of time. The class is only an hour long. Um:: so:: you know, so much… that we wanted them to understand. We just like expected… um:: we just gave them a lot of information in a sh ort amount of time. I think, um::: coming in… we ex pected students to be at a certain level of understanding by the end of the day. And then like, they're not all gonna be like, as fast as learners than some of the other students …” To justify the class’s less-than-expected progress in the lesson, then, Mary and Lara outlined two main factors: 1) The short time length of the class period and 2) Students are naturally differentially-abled and “… not all gonna be like… as fast as lear ners than some of the other students.” Let us unpack Lara and Mary’s explanation in this episode. Beginning with item  (2), we interpret the teaching duo’s argument to be based on the ideology that there are naturally fast and slow learners and thus some (i.e., “slow”) students will necessarily progress s lower than others (Horn, 20 10). In addition, by invoking the binary fast/slow  to describe learners, they subscribe d to an ideology of differe nce that ascribes  static characteristics/positions to individuals in society (Shah & Coles, 2020). In this stance, learners’ characteristics are taken to be (essentially) immutable across contexts. Fundamentally, Lara and Mary’s discursive construction of students as holding learning deficits did not stand alone as an explanatory mechanism for student s’ lack of progress in the lesson. Rather, it was articulated with—indeed, co-constituted by—epistemological assumptions that gave meaning and made real students’ status as deficit learners. Specifically, we argue that Mary’s reasoning recruited three well documented epistemological assumptions (Hofer & Pintrich, 2001), as follows: a) Teaching as imparting knowledge, expressed in the statement “… we gave them too much information …” This implies a transmission model of learning in which teachers ‘pour’ knowledge into students’ minds (Rogoff, 1995); b) Knowledge as information, a ‘thing’ with definite material properties; c) Given its material quality, knowledge is itself quantifiable (“…we gave them too much information”) and thus it can be administered in different amounts and according to various schedules of delivery (“…we jam packed a lot of information into a very short amount of time”). Together and inseparably, the (i) framing of learners as naturally fast/slow (ii) statically pegged into such social categories and (iii) teaching as imparting knowledge that is (iv) information (v) and a measurable substance, operated (a) to legitimize and naturalize a deficit portrayal of some learners and  (b) to reinscribe traditional epistemologies of science, mathematics, and engineering into the discourse of the PBI class. Mary and Lara’s case was not unique. Another preservice teacher (Angkor) described his su rprise at students’ inventive solutions to his computer scien ce lesson, thereby revealing his implicitly low expectations about students’ abilities and consequently positioning them as holding deficits. In his elaborations, Angkor explained: [2:44] “Um: basically how the class was set up was that he first semester, uh:: the mentor teacher like already taught them all the… like information… on like how to code. And then this entire second semester… it’s just them doing projects to solidify their learning.” Interwoven with his attributing deficits to his stu dents, therefore, were epistemological assumptions according to which (1) knowledge is information and (2) a substance that can be solidified through repeated practice, and (3) learning and practice are divorced from one another.

## Discussion

We have attempted to contribute to research on teac hers’ sensemaking practices by investigating how preservice teachers narratively and collectively constructed students as holding learning deficits and how, in their exchanges with class peers, PSTs’ joint constructions of lear ner deficit were an articulation of ideologies of difference/hierarchy and epistemological assumptions. Indeed, as our data show, Mary and Lara’s positioning of students as fast or slow learners could only be ach ieved in light of various shared, commonsense assum ptions about the nature of knowing and learning.

To elaborate, from this perspective constructing students as fast or slow did not stand on its own as an explanatory cause of their lack of progress in the lesson; instead, a fast/slow student could only be positioned as such if their performance was measured by the amount of knowledge that they could absorb in the allotted amount of time. It was this ‘tactic’ (if unconscious) triangulati on of various epistemological and ideological assumptions (Gupta et al., 2022) that afforded the legitimizing and naturalizing of the deficit categories of slow/fast learner in the PBI class. We believe that documenting and expl aining such a link between these dimensions of teac her sensemaking can offer theoretical insights that can  advance our collective knowledge of teachers’ reas oning and instructional decision making (Russ et al., 2016), and we hope the work reported here is a step in this direction. Based on these observations, furthermore, it follows that teacher education and professional development efforts that seek to disrupt deficit and racist (STEM) classroom practices must target teachers’ ideologies of social difference and epistemological assumptions  concomitantly, and explicitly engage teachers in considering how they co-constitute one another and deeply affect their inst ructional decisions.  Because epistemological and ideological assumptions operate in mutually reinforcing manner, they lend themselves to simultaneous treatment and teacher education and professional development programs may profitably  take advantage of that. Realistically, however, coming to see these complexities in one’s own teaching practices is a matter of long-term ideological change (Philip, 2011) and teacher educa tors must necessarily plan for extended professional development of teachers.
