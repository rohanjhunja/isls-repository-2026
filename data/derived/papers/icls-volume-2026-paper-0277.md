# “Crafting” an Engineering Identity: Intersections between Engineering Undergraduates’ Identities as Crafters and Engineers

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Crafting and engineering, and the practices they entail, have an interwoven and gendered history. Engineering undergraduate students with prior experience in crafting can bring these practices into their engineering education, but those resources and facet s of their identities are not always recognized by both themselves and members of their communities. In this work, we interviewed ten engineering undergraduates about their experiences with crafting and engineering and how it informed their identities as engineers. Our findings suggest that students see their crafting and engineering work as connected and that they inform one another. Furthermore, their insights point to factors that can influence the formation of their identities as engineers, artists, and/or crafters. This work carries implications for the intentional inclusion of crafting in engineering education to foster creativity and attract and retain more students to the discipline.

Undergraduates’ identities as engineers are tied to a number of factors, including perceived competence, recognition as an engineer, and interest in the discipline; and these are often split across students’ genders (Godwin, 2016; Patrick & Borrego, 2016).  Additionally, contrary to the needs and actual work done in engineering, creative practices are rarely positioned or perceived as a set of competencies in engineering education (Wulf, 2001). In examining identity as a crafter, previous authors have descri bed crafting as a social identity related to specialized labor and the production of useful products that may be connected to other aspects of identity, such as gender (Costin, 1998). Similarly, researchers have argued how artisans and engineers sketch out an initial design differ only in their format (e.g., a crocheting pattern versus a schematic) rather than their process (Ferguson, 1994). Despite these similarities, the lack of focus on creative thinking and practices may potentially place limits on undergraduates’ ability to identify as engineers, especia lly if they come from a background with hands-on creative experiences, such as crafting.

In this work, we frame the identities of crafter and engineer to be overlapping: someone with a specialized skill set who creatively makes useful and novel products and solutions. We seek to better understand how to bring students’ pre-existing crafting knowledge into and increase engagement with engineering. In exploring possible connections between undergraduates’ crafting and engineering identities, we address the following research questions: How do engineering undergraduates see connections between their engineering and crafting practices, and how does this impact their identities as crafters and engineers? Researchers have shown that recognizing the skill needed for textile/fiber arts can help engineers and artists alike see their work as similar and less hierarchical (Pérez- Bustos, 2018). Additionally, incorporating textile/fiber arts into engineering/computer science education can increase engagement in both areas by building off the skills and knowledge that students enter their studies with, especially when positive emotions are experienced in those contexts (Buechley et al., 2008). In a comparison of tra ditional and e-textile kits for learning about circuits, the students who engaged with interdisciplinary  e-textile materials experienced greater learning gains (Peppler & Thompson, 2024). Not only does the inclusion of textile/fiber arts (which has often been positioned as more “feminine”) in engineering and computer science education support learning for people who may have not previously seen themselves in such technical spaces, especially because of their gender identities, it provides opportunities for more interdisciplinary learning, which can benefit students of all backgrounds/identities by recognizing and building off of their cultures, hobbies, and interests (Reimann, 2011). This relationship between engineering and art/crafting can be seen in “funds of knowledge” (FoK) research, which suggests that students’ backgrounds, experiences, knowledge, and skills in various areas can contribute to their learning and abilities within other, sometimes seemingly unrelated contexts (Moll et al., 1992). Through this lens, it is possible that engineering students could draw on their crafting experiences to support them in their engineering studies. Other work highlighting the experiences that engineering students bring with them 1872 from their personal histories and interests in making, including participating in textile/fiber arts/crafting, has shown that instructional design that recognizes and incorporates such experiences can help support students in their work towards becoming engineers (Xu & Gravel, 2025). For example, one student used her knowledge of sewing to contribute to an engineering project by helping her in designing a product, choosing materials, and assembling it (Wilson‐Lopez et al., 2016). Further, this increased inc lusion of students and the ways of thinking they bring from crafting in engineering disciplines coupled with an emphasis on the importance of creativity to engineering can help support improved retention rates, bringing more creative engineers into the wor kforce and leading to better, more innovative products and solutions (Danielak, Gupta, & Elby, 2014; Wulf, 2001). Another factor influencing retention and persistence in engineering majors and, later, the workplace, is engineering identity, which undergraduate students define in a variety of ways. These varied definitions combined with factors such as students’ sense of belonging in their studies, understanding of what engineering work consists of, and perceptions of what an “ideal engineer” is can influence whether, or how strongly, they align with the identity of “engineer.” Notably, students whose perception of their knowledge and skillsets closely align with the competencies and responsibilities they see in “ideal engineers” tend to possess stronger engineering identities (Tallman, et al., 2019). Factors such as gender and academic year have also been shown to have strong effects on engineering identity formation. Godwin & Lee found that in one large engineering program, upper-level students generally reported stronger engineering identities (2017). Further, despite women commonly experiencing marginalization within engineering, an analysis of engineering identity within civil and construction engineering found that the cis women  in the later years of their majors experienced stronger alignment with the identity of engineer (Hamlet, et al., 2020).

Through these interdisciplinary ideas, researchers have shown that engineering identity is complex, multifaceted, and influenced by a variety of factors. Therefore, our research seeks to gain insight into whether undergraduate engineering students see their involvement in both textile/fiber arts and engineering as being connected and, if so, how their work/studies in those spaces inform one another. Furthermore, there has been much research into the importance of mathematics skills in crafting (Saxena et al., 2023), but less so on crafting in engineering education. Considering the lack of existing research on the influence of crafting on engineering identity development, we are especially interested in seeing how the intersection of crafting and engineering studies relates to the formation of one’s engineering identity. Ten undergraduates enrolled in engineering majors from two private universities participated in this study, including nine women, one male, and one non-binary student. Students were recruited through flyers posted in one university’s three major makerspaces, emails to faculty who promoted it in their classes, and snowball sampling. While sign-up criteria included participation in a craft and an engineering major, there were no other requirements about self-identifying as a creative or crafty person,  and this was not a framing of the interview. Students participated in semi-structured interviews about how they saw themselves in their engineering studies, which crafts they do, what their crafting process looks like, any possible connections between the ir engineering and crafting practices, how their craft may have helped their engineering competencies and vice versa, their identity as an engineer, crafter, or both, and crafting projects they were most proud of. From the audio and video recordings of these interviews, we developed a coding scheme using grounded theory to analyze the interviews (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). We then coded the interviews for emergent codes and then combined those codes into overarching themes. In the code development, we included codes such as “alignment traditional engineering identity” or “alignment with crafting/art practices.” From this, three major themes concerning identity emerged: Crafty Engineers, Women in Crafting and Engineering, and Identity Incongruence. Quotes from these interviews have been anonymized in the findings.

In this study, we explored how undergraduate engineering students who engage in crafting practices connect their crafting and engineering identities. Across these interviews, through students’ descriptions of their past experiences with crafting and its in fluences on their engineering work, we discerned an identity of a “crafty engineer.” Although most students expressed an alignment with that identity, others demonstrated higher levels of incongruence between their crafting and engineering practices and identities. Data from these interviews suggest that students who identify strongly with being a “crafty engineer” see their crafting skills as a boon to their engineering abilities, even when their own “traditional” engineering identities are in flux. Additionally, these data show evidence for crafting experience as a protective factor for non-male students engaging in engineering. “Crafty engineer” identity 1873 Students shared a range of alignment with the identities of crafter and engineer. One emergent identity from the interviews is that of the “crafty engineer”: a student with prior, positive crafting experiences who believes that crafting skills have made them into better, more creative engineers. Additionally, the majority of students expressed ideas of engineering as having a creative component. They felt that most engineering students do not consider the importance of creativity in their work and struggle with imagining a custom and aesthetic product for a unique user. Students also highlighted the hands-on nature of engineering and how their love for hands-on practices associated with crafting led them to technical disciplines. Avery is a first-year in Human Factors Engineering (HFE) with experiences in sewing, knitting, and jewelry making who claims a strong “crafty engineer" identity. When asked about how strongly she identified with engineering, she said, “I would consider myself an engineer, just a really crafty engineer. [...] I think crafter is the intersection of engineer and artist.” In elaborating on what makes a “crafty engineer,” she explained, “Crafty engineers are [...] better at picking up skills and being okay maki ng mistakes.” Here, Avery connects her experiences with crafting practices with critical aspects of learning engineering, learning new skills, and making mistakes, in her belief that crafters make for better prepared engineering students. We also investigated this emerging identity in our analysis of more experienced students. Emily, a fourthyear student in Mechanical Engineering (ME) and Women’s and Gender Studies (WGS), talked at length about her experiences with learning to crochet and machining and how her hands-on crafting experiences made her a better engineer. She explained, “Working with their (machinists’) hands is a form of embodied knowledge [...] and it’s very similar to when I crochet. [...] I think [understanding how things are made by hand through crafting] makes you a better engineer and more compassionate." Similarly, David, a fourth-year student in HFE and Dance with crocheting experience, claimed a strong “crafty engineer” identity through his crafting projects, saying, “A crafty e ngineer, like,  that' s really who I feel like  I am.  [... ] Like,  I ca n e ven croc het a  prototype  for us. I can probably do this [engineering] because this is just a craft in another form, with a different word on it.” David talks about how he uses his crocheting skills to help in ideation during his engineering coursework and how he sees engineering as craft in another form, which informs his “crafty engineer” identity. From these quotes, students show how their crafting experiences both boost and modify their identities as engineers; not only do these students think hands-on crafting experiences helps them understand engineering better, but they also think it allows them more creative outputs than “traditional” engineering students. Women in crafting and engineering Only one participant in this study identified as male and the remaining nine interviews were with female and nonbinary students. Students suggested that as crafting has historically been positioned as a female-dominated practice in contrast with more male-dominated engineering, the “crafty engineer” identity would be more often found in female and non-binary participants. Here, we analyze how gendered factors in crafting and engineering influenced students’ identities.

Avery talked about how she sees few male engineers who are also openly crafters, saying, “I would say a lot of my female friends in engineering have a sort of craft background. Whereas a lot of the men I know do not, and in fact, a lot of the men I know who are crafters or into arts are not engineers like. Specifically, I feel like I know very few engineering men who are crafty.” Additionally, Sophia talked about how crafts were traditionally a part of more feminine activities and how their connections to engineering were overlooked, stating, “I think traditionally generalized as feminine crafts and stuff and always the amount of knowledge needed, and, like the planning and mathematics put into it is always overlooked for things like sewing and crochet.” Similarly, Olivia, a fourth-year student in Chemical Engineering, talked about how these historically gendered roles impact what kinds of students do both engineering and crafting. She explains how gendered hobby expectations starting in early childhood and perceptions of which genders were allowed to access engineering impact identity formation. I think [crafting and engineering] are really connected because for so long, women were not allowed in engineering spaces, and so I think home economics activities and hobbies are engineering. I think engineering is just being able to break a big problem down to make something and I think that is what all those hobbies are. [...] These hobbies were women's way of being able to access those fields without being permitted into these spaces. There are not many men who are in fiber arts because they were allowed to be in engineering, I think. You get a boy an electrical engineering kit and you get a girl a sewing kit.” These three students’ commentaries on the intertwined nature of crafting, engineering, and historical gender roles provide some possible reasoning for why only one male student was recruited into this study. These students describe how the engineering aspe cts of crafts are overlooked, potentially because they are seen as “women’s 1874 work.” Furthermore, these students suggest that as crafting was or is an acceptable activity for women, it acted as their first forays into engineering that still impact their identities as engineers today. Incongruence between crafting and engineering identities and practices Some students expressed relatively weaker identities as both crafters and engineers, despite describing engagement in both crafting and engineering activities and communities. One third-year HFE student, Sophia, who considered herself a “crafty person,” said, “I'm just making things that I enjoy and not really trying to label myself as anything,” and “[...] there's a lot of the math that I don't understand about engineering in general, so I would be hesitant to call myself an engineer. I just like to make things and to test things out.” Though at the most basic level, engineering could be described as making and testing things, Sophia has trouble seeing herself as an engineer, possibly as math skills are seen as a gatekeeper to engineering (Winkelman, 2009). Another student, Madison, who was in her first year and was deciding between ME and Biomedical Engineering, settled on the identities of “hobbyist” and “incoming engineer.” She expressed that “being an engineer means that you have a degree, and I don’t have one of those yet,” and that she sees herself as more of a textile hobbyist than artist because she has only made crafting projects on a smaller scale. Another student, Emily, who was also one of the strong “crafty engineers” from the previous section, also pointed out in her interview that to her, anyone can be an artist, but even though she is soon graduating with an engineering degree, she feels that she does not fit into the cultural identity of a traditional engineer because “[...] I don’t work on the  mill for fun.” Despite identifying with the creative and crafty practices that are related to engineering in her view, she has difficulty identifying with the more rigid and standardized stereotype of engineering, which may be why she sees the “crafty engineer” label as more fitting. Across these two students, we can see how a degree in engineering can be both prioritized and ignored as facets of an engineering identity, and how their level of engagement in hobbies affects their crafting and engineering identities.

Through this study, we sought to understand if and how undergraduate engineering students who participate in crafting see those practices as being connected, as well as how their identities as crafters and engineers are affected by their participation in them. From our analysis, we found that they tend to see their crafting experiences as assets in their engineering work with a positive effect on their engineering identities, suggesting that crafting can be a valuable FoK for students, similar to Wilson‐Lopez et al. (2016). Variations in students’ ideas of what it means to be an engineer or artist/crafter affects how strongly they identify with either or both labels, aligning with Tallman et al.’s (2019) work on engineering identity. Some students expressed tension between what they perceive engineers and artists/crafters to be and the work that they do with the skills that they have. Here, we saw constructs like degrees and comfortability with mathematics as barriers causing misalignment in that regard, aligning with Winkelman (2009). Students commented on the importance of math in their crafts, yet also saying they were not comfortable using math in engineering work. Additional work on untangling gatekeepers from engineering and the intersections of crafting  experiences may provide further insight into how students use and implement their math, design, and machining skills in a variety of contexts. There is also an element of gendered practices present in the work. A majority of the participants in this study identified as female or non-binary and often commented on how crafting was a more historically feminine activity for them. The ways in which crafting is seen more as a feminine practice may have influenced how these students saw connections between crafting and the more typically male-dominated engineering disciplines. Prior research has shown that male and female first-year engineering students find problem solving skills and creative thinking equally interesting aspects of an engineering education, but female students much more frequently cite the ability to be creative as why they want to be an engineer (Pierrakos et al., 2010).

## Potential significance of work

Engineering students who also engage in crafting practices see an important connection between crafting and engineering that allows them to be more creative and interdisciplinary engineers. Exploring these connections may help engineering educators design curriculum that draws on students’ prior experiences with crafting to foster underrepresented students’ interest and belonging in engineering. Likewise, including crafting in the classroom or makerspaces might help engineering students who have not historically participated in crafting practices bring more creativity into their engineering practices.
