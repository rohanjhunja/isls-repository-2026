# Students’ Changing Understanding of Psychological Evidence: Integrating Evidence Evaluation in Psychology Instruction

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study extends the prevailing emphasis on evidence in the natural sciences by offering a novel fine-grained analysis of how undergraduate students evaluate psychological evidence. The study employs the Grasp of Evidence framework to explore psychology students' epistemic ideals and processes for evaluating popular psychological evidence, and examines how these change following a course that included collaborative evidence evaluation and design tasks. Analyses of pre and post evidence evaluation assessments reveal three key shifts in students’ grasp of evidence: in perception of the sources of psychological knowledge, in views of scientific objectivity, and in definitions of psychological phenomena.

## Introduction

To date, the majority of research on evidence evalu ation practices has focused on the natural sciences  (e.g., McNeill & Berland, 2017), and relatively little att ention has been given to how students engage with e vidence from the social sciences in general, and psychology  specifically. This is particularly problematic bec ause such evidence has become a staple of popular accounts of  human behavior, and often informs public policy (e.g., Einfield, 2019). Moreover, evidence in psychologica l research has unique attributes as it is based on studying complex and unobservable everyday phenomena in ways  that are amenable to empirical examination and replicable by other scientists (Elmes et al., 2011). Attentiveness to these characteristics is particularly challenging as psychology students often struggle to appreciate the scientific foundations of psychology (Balloo et al., 2018). However, we currently know little about how undergr aduate psychology students' competence to evaluate psychological evidence can be fostered. Accordingly, this study examined if and how psychology students’ ideals and processes for evaluating popular psychological evidence change following an introductory psychology course that provided opportunities for engaging in collaborative online evidence evaluation and design tasks. Evidence evaluation poses unique challenges to students and laypersons because they lack the extensive disciplinary and methodological knowledge that is needed to understand and evaluate study designs and measures (Duncan et al., 2018). Thus, lay understanding of scientific evidence is inevitably bounded. Accordingly, Duncan et al. (2018) proposed a theoretical framework, cal led the Grasp of Evidence framework, which unpacks the dimensions of evidence that are relevant for preparing students to engage with evidence as outsiders to science. Duncan et al. (2018) argue that to meaningfully eng age with science, laypersons need to grasp two aspe cts of evidence. The first aspect is experts’ evidentiary practices – how experts gather, understand, evaluate, interpret, and integrate evidence. The second aspect is laypersons' evidentiary practices, that is, of how laypersons can use evidence despite their bounded understanding of science. Within each of these aspects, Duncan et al. (2018) identify specific epistemic aims, epistemic ideals, and reliable epistemic processes for reasoning with and  about evidence (Chinn et al., 2014): (a) Epistemic aims are the epistemic products or outcomes that people set to achieve. (b) Epistemic ideals are the criteria or norms that are used to evaluate whether epistemic aims have be en achieved and the quality of epistemic products. For example, some of the epistemic ideals of expert evidence evaluation are the appropriateness of the methods and measures and the validity of the conclusions. (c) Reliable epistemic processes are the procedures, strategies, or methods that people can use to successfully achieve  epistemic aims. Reliable processes of expert evide nce evaluation involve, for example, scrutinizing the appropriateness of the study design. (Duncan et al., 2018). Though there is a dearth of research on how students evaluate psychological evidence, researchers have identified some overarching challenges for evidence  evaluation in psychology. First, students often fa il to appreciate, or even actively doubt, the scientific basis of psychology as the study of human behavior (Balloo et al., 2018). Second, students often perceive objecti vity in psychology as the negation of subjectivity:  science is objective to the extent that it remains value-free (Douglas, 2009). However, objectivity does not require rejecting values, but arises from the using trustworthy processes for interacting with the world, and from social processes of validation, agreement formation, and debate. Third, students struggle to grasp how psychological knowledge is defined and evaluated through the use of valid operational definitions of subjective and complex phenomena. Introductory psychology courses could provide an opportunity for cultivating such disciplinary epistemic practices. However, such courses are commonly lectu re-based and have prioritized the coverage of conte nt ICLS2022 Proceedings 929 © ISLS knowledge in the various domains of psychology at t he expense of active engagement with epistemic prac tices (Gurung & Hackathorn, 2018). Researchers have stres sed the importance of explicating the psychology’s scientific and offering experiences that illustrate how research findings are underpinned by research methods. Accordingly, this study set out to examine the following research questions: (1) How do undergraduate students’ epistemic ideals and processes for evalua ting popular psychological evidence change followin g an introductory psychology course designed to foster e vidence evaluation competence? (2) How are students ’ evolving evidence evaluation ideals and processes reflected in collaborative evidence evaluation and design tasks?

## Participants and Course Design

The study was conducted in a first-year undergraduate introduction to psychology course in a large university in Israel. The sample included 120 students (24 groups of 5 students, 70% female, 30% male). The course consisted of eight video lectures covering the basic domains of psychology. In addition, to facilitate engagemen t with psychological evidence, three collaborative online evidence evaluation tasks, and face-to-face meeting s that centered around students’ experiences in these tasks, were added to the course. The synchronous online tasks were conducted in the chat application on the course’s Moodle platform. In each task, students were asked to evaluate popular accounts of psychological evidence and to f ormulate more scientifically rigorous ways to study  these phenomena. The information sources (a newspaper piece, a TED talk, and a podcast) and the subjects discussed (e.g., emotions, approaches to psychotherapy) were intended to reflect psychological evidence students are likely to encounter online in their everyday life.

Data Sources and Analysis Data sources included an evidence evaluation assess ment, administered at the beginning and end of the course, and students’ written discussions in the synchronous online group tasks. In the evidence evaluation as sessment, participants read three popular reports that presented psychological evidence of varying quality ranging from low quality anecdotal evidence to a detailed report of a well-designed study. The pre-and post-assessments addressed different popular psychological topics (pre: child rearing; post: sleep), but the reports had highly s imilar source and evidence features and quality. Participants were asked to rate the trustworthiness and quality of each piece of evidence and to justify their evidence ratings in writing. The analysis focused on identifying epistemic ideals and processes of evidence evaluation in participants' j ustifications of their evaluations. We iteratively developed a coding scheme relying on the Grasp of Evidence fram ework (Duncan et al., 2018). Interrater reliability  for all codes was higher than Cohen’s Kappa > 0.78 (M = 0.90, SD = 0.07), which is a substantial level of agreement. The goal of our qualitative analyses  of students' discourse was to  elaborate and refine the quantitative findings. Hence, we first quantitatively identified codes in which there were significant changes between the preand post-assessment. We then qualitatively analyzed  episodes in which these codes were discussed by pa ying attention to the meaning of the codes in students' discourse and how this meaning was negotiated.

## Findings

Theme I: Shifts in sources of knowledge – Decreasing reliance on personal knowledge There was a significant decrease (from 69% to 52%, p =.005) in students’ reliance on the ideal of personal coherence with experience and intuition  to evaluate evidence (“Conclusions align with the reader's personal knowledge, experiences, or intuitions.”). The decrease in personal coherence may reflect a growing rec ognition of the limitations of lay knowledge and a greater sensitivity to the division of cognitive labor between experts and laypersons. Yet, this decrease could also be due to  increased disciplinary skills for evaluating evide nce, which may have taken the place of reliance on personal judgments. Despite this decrease, even after the course, half of students still relied on personal coherence when evaluating evidence. Analysis of the group discussions revealed that students rarely relied on personal information. Moreover, in contrast to the explicit reliance on personal knowledge or experience in the pre-and post-assessment tasks, in the group discussions personal information was auxi liary and implicit. We could not find examples in w hich students openly appealed to personal experience or knowledge in order to justify their proposals or ar guments. This position was reserved for disciplinary knowledge about psychological theories and research methods. Theme II: Shifts in views of objectivity – Towards procedural objectivity There was a significant decrease (from 41% to 17%, p <.001) in appeals to the ideal of objectivity (“Conclusions are not influenced by researchers’ subjective experiences, preferences, or values.”), accompanied by increases in ICLS2022 Proceedings 930 © ISLS use of the processes of replicating results (“Researchers attempt to confirm the results by running the study again with additional samples etc.”; from 24% to 55%, p <.001) and using appropriate instruments (“The use of research instruments that can accurately and reliably measur e the phenomenon at hand.”; from 13% to 23%, p <.05). Taken together, we suggest that these shifts indica te changes in students' understanding of objectivit y. Preinstruction, students leaned towards depicting obje ctivity as achieved when scientific inquiry is unaf fected by researchers' subjective judgments, preferences, and  perspectives. Following instruction, students appe ared to develop a more procedural view of objectivity, with increased mentions of measurement processes. In addition, students attended more to the social process of replicating results. The collaborative tasks required evaluating eviden ce and proposing more rigorous means of studying psychological phenomena, which led students to discuss procedural aspects of objectivity. Nonetheless, issues related to replicating results were infrequently mentioned in the group tasks, which could reflect the fact th at students focused on evaluating and designing a single study.

Theme III: Shifts in definitions of psychological phenomena – Towards operational definitions The third shift involved changing views of the conc eptualization of psychological phenomena. Specifica lly, students focused less on the nature of psychological phenomena and more on how these phenomena can be studied empirically. This shift was reflected in a decrease  in the clarity of definitions ideal (“The phenomen on studied, and the variables, should be clearly and precisely defined.”; from 44% to 18%, p <.001), together with an increase in mentions of valid methods (“Reliance on methods and study design that appropriately represent the phenomenon the researchers wished to study.”; from 2% to 38%, p <.001). We suggest that these changes expose a shifting understanding of the interplay between e vidence and phenomena. Instead of placing the onus on precisely conceptualizing the phenomenon itself, the focus shifts to finding valid ways to empirically  study and measure these phenomena – formulating operational definitions that describe phenomena in measurable terms. In the group discourse, students increasingly disc ussed the need to develop conceptually clear operational definitions. This was most salient in the third task, in which participants were asked to devise a scientific way to examine which therapeutic approach is "deeper". ## Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first stu dy to map in detail the epistemic ideals and proces ses are used by students to evaluate psychological evidence. Par ticipants exhibited three main shifts in their evid ence evaluation practices. First, there were shifts in v iews of sources of knowledge, with a significant de crease in reliance on personal knowledge. Nonetheless, even post-instruction, about half of the students appealed to their personal experience to evaluate evidence, suggesting that what develops is the ability to coordinate personal and disciplinary knowledge. Second, we identified shifts in students' views of objectivity – the emphasis shifted from objectivity as a lack of subjective bias to a more procedural view of objectivity, according to which scientific evidence is justified through the social processes underpinning its production (Douglas, 2007). The ne ed to formulate precise and replicable means of studying psychological evidence in groups tasks, led to an emphasis on procedural aspects such as using reliable research instruments. Third, there were shifts in definition s of psychological phenomena, as students paid more atte ntion not to the phenomenon itself, but rather to h ow it is conceptualized in a way that renders it amenable to scientific study. In the group tasks, students engaged with the challenge of translating everyday psychological phenomena into operational definitions. The necessity to do so in a group, led students to both explicate their ow n thinking, and to critique and develop the definit ions and measures suggested by their group members.

These observations lead us to suggest three emergin g design principles for engaging students with psychological evidence. First, providing students w ith ample opportunities to evaluate psychological e vidence from a disciplinary perspective. This positioning encourages students to rely on disciplinary norms and standards rather than personal knowledge. Second, students' e valuation practices could be honed when they encoun ter evidence of diverse quality such as popular accounts, which include variations both in the evidence’s quality and the rigor of its presentation. This may support a more refined understanding of the scientific work due to the need to disentangle unclear and partial accounts of evidence. Third, students stand to gain when they have opportunities to engage in collaborative evidence critique and design: not only explicating their thinking and critiquing others’, this process is highly reflective of the nature of psychological research – simulating the processes scientists engage in when aiming to critique and improve existing operational definitions and measures. Future research could address these issues while also tackling the limitations of the current study. First, as it did not include a control condition (for ethical reasons), we cannot evaluate the contribution of the different components of the course’s design. Further, student s did not have opportunities to integrate evidence from different sources or engage with more comprehensive  bodies of evidence, which would have allowed them to explore more deeply the social and procedural natur e of scientific evidence. Also, the tasks’ emphasis  on positioning students as experts came at the expense of paying more attention to lay evidentiary practices.
