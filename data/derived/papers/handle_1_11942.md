# On Black Sheep and Harmonic Groups: A Case Study about Group Rules and Social Norms During Collaborative Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Long-term collaborative learning is often used in higher education, but its potential for enhancing students' collaboration skills is rarely fully realized. In this qualitative study, we investigated groups in a multi-week project-based learning course that were prompted to create group rules and reflect weekly on their collaboration process. The analysis of their data reveals that the groups devised rules addressing relevant aspects of collaborative learning, but these rules did not prevent problematic interaction patterns. A detailed analysis of three cases reveals the struggles of two groups, and the success of the third group. Addressing violations of group rules is very challenging when members perceive these violations as personal failures rather than as issues stemming from inefficient collaboration structures

Addressing violations of group rules is very challenging when members perceive the se violations as personal failures rather than as issues stemming from inefficient collaboration structures The role of beneficial and problematic interaction patterns Many instructional approaches rely on long-term collaborative learning in small groups to enha nce students' understanding of a topic and develop their collaboration skills, which are crucial 21st-century competencies (van Laar et al, 2017). The quality of these collaborative processes is vit al for successful learning (Dillenbourg et al., 1995). Without proper support, as often seen in higher education (Greisel et al., 2024), collaborative learning can lead to problematic interaction patterns, making th e learning experience unproductive or even detrimental o n cognitive, motivational, and emotional levels, particularly in developing collaboration skills. Various problematic interaction patterns are well documented (e.g. Karau & Wilhau, 2020). Strauß and Rummel (2021) identified unequal participation, dea dline rush, ineffective communication, and social l oafing as the most frequent and frustrating issues. These neg ative experiences can create a vicious cycle of unp leasant emotions, low motivation, and further unproductive collaboration strategies. Conversely, beneficial interaction patterns can lead to positive and productive collaborative l earning experiences, fostering effective collaborat ion strategies and positive motivational and emotional associations with collaboration. In line with Meier et al. (2007), we understand beneficial interaction patterns as ch aracterized by high-quality communication, joint information processing, group coordination, high reciprocity in  interpersonal relationships, and high individual task orientation. G roup research  (see Ellemers & Rink, 2005)  shows how that beneficial interaction patterns can quickly turn problematic: When people identify with a group, they usually develop positive expectations towards group members and assume similar values and behavio r. However, if someone violates these expectations,  it quickly feels personal for highly identified group members and even a small violation can lead to highly negative reactions and rejection of the “black sheep”. Group norms are crucial in long-term collaborative learning, as they guide behavior and reduce social loafing.

To promote beneficial interaction patterns and avoi d problematic ones, CSCL research suggests two instructional approaches (Dillenbourg, 1995): structuring and regulating. Structuring typically involves using external collaboration scripts to prompt beneficial  interactions throughout the learning process (Fischer et al., 2013), which has been shown to improve collaboration skills (Radkowitsch et al., 2020). Given the importance of group norms (Ellemers & Rink, 2005), scripting groups to discuss their expectations (similar to Sterr, 2011) and make these group norms explicit seems worthwhile. Regulating involves encouraging learners to reflect on their collaboration, for example using group awareness tools (Bodemer et al., 2018)  and has even been found more beneficial than structuring (Strauß et al., 2024). Reflection helps groups identify emerging problems, such as unfair workload distribution, and address them by planning and regulating the collaboration proces s (Fett et al., 2021).

This paper explores how higher education students set explicit social norms and reflect on their collaborative learning processes in a multi-week collaborative learning course, supported by str ategies for beneficial interaction patterns.

## Methods

The study was conducted in a higher education course titled “Designing Trainings for Adult Education” within a B.A. program on educational studies. The participants were female students in their 4th to 8th semester. Initially, 18 students enrolled, but one dropped out due to il lness. Of the remaining students, 15 signed informe d consent forms, allowing their data to be used for this stud y after the course concluded.  The course employed a project-based learning design (Kokotsaki et al., 2016; Savery et al., 2006), where students worked collaboratively in fixed teams for 13 weeks. The project involved responding  to a call for a trainer team to design a training program, including a written needs analysis, a training desi gn, and an evaluation plan. Students also delivered  a shortened version of the training during the course. The cour se consisted of 13 weekly 90-minute classroom sessions, with an additional expectation of approximately 4 hours of team work per week outside of class. Teams had to organize their additional work, often opting for online coll aboration, which included both asynchronous and syn chronous phases. Throughout the course, students submitted weekly individual reflections on their collaboration and project progress.

The study follows a qualitative case study design, analyzing two types of written artifacts created by the students. 1. Group Rules: As part of teambuilding activities in one of the initial collaborative sessions, each team established a set of rules for their collaboration and consequences for rule violations. 2. Reflection Journal Entries: All students provided weekly reflection entries, re sponding to prompts such as “How did you manage the  latest task and the collaboration within the training team?" and “What went well, and what did you find challenging?” The data was analyzed using a thematic coding approach with two coders who first analyzed the data independently and then discussed their results to reach a consensus.

## Results

Group rules: Four out of five groups submitted their group rules, developing between 2 and 6 rules each. These rules fell into five categories: (1) time management:  All groups had at least one rule, such as "Internal  group deadline: Saturday before the general deadline at 5 p.m.", (2) quality of communication and collaboration: Three groups had rules like "Communication on equal footing.", (3) exchange and accessibility: Three groups included rules like "Regular meetings for communication (Zoo m/group room).", (4) task division: Two groups included rules like " Clarify areas of responsibility and fair task distr ibution", (5) engagement and quality: Two groups included rules such as "We do our best! (Pareto effect)." Students’ reflections about collaboration  – three exemplary cases: All three groups encountered challenging interaction patterns at various points during their collaboration. While one group success fully managed these challenges leading to further beneficial interaction patterns, the other two groups did not address their issues adequately.  In the successful group, members' evaluations of th eir collaboration were relatively consistent each week. They reported an exceptionally stressful period that led to a rush to meet deadlines as their main challenge. In response, they took the time to rethink and re organize their working methods. Members also supported each other reciprocally, and when disagre ements arose, they took the time to discuss them.  The other two groups showed more varied evaluations of their collaboration. In group A, most members eventually realized they were dissatisfied with one member's contributi ons, interpreting this as a violation of group norm s related to unequal participation and social loafing. However, they struggled to discuss this openly with the memb er in question and began treating her as a black sheep. The marginalized member sensed so mething was wrong and interpreted it as a violation of the group norm to address problems openly. However, she also did not address the issue openly, leading to further problematic dynami cs and frustrations until the project was completed. Group B struggled from the beginning, as most members had d ifficulty understanding the tasks, and one member s ensed differing expectations regarding academic quality. The dissatisfied member also struggled to address the problem, leaving the other members unaware of the situation. This group did not take the time for regular meetings outside the classroom, relying instead on assumed "efficien t" communication  via instant messaging. Disagreements among group members were seen as inconvenient. Only  at the end did the group take the time for a longe r inperson meeting to discuss issues, which improved overall satisfaction.

## Discussion

Our findings align with Strauß and Rummel (2023), w ho discovered that learners have varied interpretat ions of social norms for collaborative learning, such as eq ual participation. Although the groups in this stud y discussed their rules, not all were able to elicit individual  expectations and establish clearly shared social norms at the beginning of their collaboration. These norms only surfaced later, leading to well-known group effects (Ellemers & Rink, 2005) that jeopardized collaboration. Addre ssing failures to adhere to rules and expectations became a specific problem when learners did not shift their interpretation of problematic interaction patterns from individual failures to understanding these issues as structural problems, in line with Hattie and Timperley's (2007) feedback model. We recommend that instructors provide information on setting up collaboration structures, emphasize team reflections, and remind students that the goal of collaborative learning is to foster discussion and learning, not just efficient project completion.
