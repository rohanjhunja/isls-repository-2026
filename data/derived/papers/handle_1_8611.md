# The Influence of an Intervention on Students’ Historical Arguing Skills

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite the importance of reasoning skills in today's knowledge-based society, students do not appear to possess sufficient arguing skills. This study aimed to examine the improvement of those arguing skills after implementing an intervention in grade 8. In total, 699 students participated, and data was collected using a document-based essay. Results show that students receiving limited and full arguing lessons showed significantly better progression from pre-to post-test compared to students not receiving the intervention. Keywords: historical reasoning skills, arguing, intervention

## Theoretical background

Historical arguing means to formulate and weigh arg uments in support of different conclusions to answe r a historical question. As part of formulating an answ er, students must present arguments in support, cou nter arguments, and rebuttals. Finally, students must be able to base their arguments on several references found in the sources (De La Paz & Felton, 2010; vandrie & van B oxtel, 2008; Voet & De Wever, 2017). A recent study  of Hulders et al (n.d.) shows that students in grade 8 (age 14) achieve poor results for arguing. It shows that students do not manage to give a substantiated answer. When answering a historical research question, most students only give their own conclusion and one single argument i n support, but have no attention for counterargumen ts, rebuttals, or references to sources. However, students’ arguing skills, as one of the key competences of historical reasoning skills (De La Paz & Felton, 2010), are important to make sense of the information in the 21st century (Wineburg & Reisman, 2015). Because, like to the in formation today, historical sources are often contradictory (Vandrie & Van Boxtel, 2008). One promising approach to enhance students’ historical reasoning and arguing skills is ‘inquiry-based learning’ (IBL; Voet & De Wever, 2017). So, the aim of this study is to inves tigate the impact of a new developed lesson series, following the principles of IBL, on students’ arguing skills.

## Participants

699 students from 42 classes in 18 Flemish (Belgium) schools participated in this study. This sample consisted of 367 boys (i.e., 52.50%) and 332 girls (i.e., 47.50%). Their average age was 14 (SD = 0.42). Design of the study An intervention study with pre-post-test design was set up from January until June 2020 (i.e., pandemic). In the experimental condition, teachers implemented a lesson series to enhance students’ arguing skills durin g regular class hours. This intervention lasted for 8 weeks (2 sessions a week, 16 sessions in total). The lessons focused on teaching students how to argue with historical sources (i.e., essential elements of a substantiated answer and many practice opportunities). Teachers in the control condition followed their own curriculum (i.e., business as usual). Instruments Data were collected through a document-based essay during the history-lesson (50 minutes), under the supervision of the teacher. This test contained three authentic historical sources about the death of Emperor Claudius. In each source, information about the author and the context was given. After reading the sources, students were asked to give a substantiated answer to the question “Was Claudius murdered by order of his wife? Explain why you think he was or wasn’t.” The same test was administered as pre and post-test. All teachers had to present the pre-test to their students before the start of the Romans (pen-and-paper-test) and the post-test just after the end of that topic (online test). None of the groups received extra information about the death of Claudius during the intervention. (3) counterarguments, (4) a rebuttal, (5) references to the sources and their value. To answer the research question,

a univariate regression with arguing as dependent variable and condition as fixed factor was carried out.

## Results and discussion

The condition variable consists of three subgroups. 304 students were part of group 1 who followed the business as usual. Group 2 were students who saw the lessons about arguing in a limited way (e.g., shorter, less exercises, …) and consist of 110 students. 285 students were part of group 3 who engaged in all the arguing lessons in class. Originally only two groups were planned (i.e., cont rol and intervention group), but due to the pandemi c not all teachers were able to implement all lessons. However, it might be interesting to include the group of students who saw the arguing lessons in a more limited way, as i t could possibly point out fundamental aspects of t he lesson series. This means that students’ answers on a historical reasoning question are more elaborated after they received the arguing lessons.

## Theoretical and educational significance

In the context of 21st century skills, arguing skills are important (Wineburg & Reisman, 2015). However, research shows that these skills are limited among students (Hulders et al., n.d.). Therefore, this study aimed  to improve students’ arguing skills. For this purpose, a lesso n series was developed. Despite the implementation of the intervention in the pandemic, a significant progres sion of arguing skills between pre-and post-test w as found among the students who had seen the arguing lessons. This is an important finding as it means that tea ching materials can be used to improve students' arguing skills. Although group 3 had significantly better progression than group 2, their main score remained quite low. This means that historical reasoning is a complex skill.
