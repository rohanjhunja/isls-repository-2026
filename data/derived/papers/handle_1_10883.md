# Promoting Embodied Geometric Reasoning Using a Spatial Puzzle Task

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Previous studies have shown that body-based actions such as gestures and individual differences can impact geometric thinking and learning. One important individual difference is spatial ability, a multifaceted construct with demonstrated ties to success in mathematics. Though recent empirical work has also begun investigating whether targeted interventions for spatial ability can transfer to mathematics, studies are often limited to specific populations and pen-and-paper outcome measures. This study (N = 84) expands the work on the possibility of spatial tasks improving mathematics outcomes into verbal, mathematical reasoning outcomes through a randomized control design. Undergraduates were assigned to either complete a control task or a spatial puzzle followed by a geometric reasoning task. Results found that spatial-based Tangram tasks increased the likelihood of producing transformational proof but not mathematical insight. The skills accessed in spatially based tasks may transfer to informal reasoning tasks often seen in classrooms with implications for future curriculum design.

## Introduction

Over the last few decades, there has been increasin g interest in improving mathematics proficiencies. Several lines of research have investigated the influences of motoric (i.e., gesture) and cognitive  (e.g., spatial ability) processes on mathematics abilities. For example, studies have shown that gesture is associated with mathematical reasoning both directly (e.g., Nathan et al., 2021) and indirectly (e.g., Walkington et al., 2022). It has also been widely demonstrated that spatial abilities are associated with mathematical achievement across different ages and domains of mathematics (e.g., Mix et al., 2016). The malleability of spatial abil ity may be especially useful for building mathematics abilities. Spatial abilities can be improved through both direct and indirect training methods and in and out of educational settings (Baennigner & Newcombe, 1989; Uttal et al., 2013). Spatial trai ning may be effective for increasing student success in STEM  fields, as problem solving in STEM areas relies on  spatial reasoning (Stieff & Uttal, 2015).

The literature investigating whether interventions that involve  spatial abilities improve math is ambivalent and includes both studies that show support and a lack of support. There is a growing body of causal evidence that spatial-based tasks improve mathematics achievement (e.g., Adams et al., 2022; Gilligan et al., 2020). Other studies have found no evidence of spat ial tasks transferring to mathematics achievement (Hawes et al., 2015). The varied findings could result from the mu ltidimensional and complex nature of spatial ability and mathematics. Furthermore, these studies are often l imited by their focus on pen-and-paper mathematics assessments as outcomes (e.g., Hawes et al., 2022, Yang et al., 2020). As discourse tasks such as ques tioning, proving, and justifying problem-solving are important for fostering conceptual and procedural mathematics knowledge (e.g., Legesse et al., 2020), it is beneficial to understand the potential of a task that involves spatial abilities for improving these types of mathematics proficiency.

## Theoretical framework

Grounded and embodied cognition and mathematics Grounded and embodied theories of cognition posit t hat meaning-making, even for abstract concepts, can be achieved through body-based experiences (Shapiro, 2019). Several theoretical fr ameworks offer explanatory accounts for how grounded and embodied cognition (G EC) arises from the interaction of cognitive, perce ptual, and motor processes. For example, Barsalou (1999) p roposed a sensory-motor account of mental representations in which mental processes operate with perceptual symbols, activating a perceptual-motor simulation of properties associated with a particular concept. Gestures provide an important avenue of evidence for the role of the body in complex reasoning due to their role in language production, comprehension, and problem solving (McNeil, 1992). Though gestures are seldom in a one-to-one relation with spoken word or meaning, gestures convey meaning by combining semantic and pragmatic content to simulate action (Hostetter & Alibali, 2019) and typically accompany speech or thought (Kita et al., 2017; McNeil, 1992). Gestures serve several functions during communica tion and thinking, such as affecting cognitive processes, co nveying information, and managing cognitive load. G estures help activate, maintain, manipulate, and package vi suospatial and motoric information (Kita et al., 2017). They can keep spatio-motoric information active in working memory, reduc ing cognitive load during reasoning tasks (Cook et al., 2012). Individuals also have a gestur e threshold (Hostetter & Alibali, 2019). This thres hold is the level of motor activation needed for a mental simul ation to be expressed in action and can vary depend ing on factors such as individual differences (e.g., spati al and verbal skills, anxiety, working memory), cur rent task demands (e.g., processing spatial imagery), and sit uational contexts. For example, Hostetter and Aliba li (2007) found that individuals with high spatial visualization skills and low verbal skills produced gestures at higher rates than individuals with different spatial and verbal skill combinations. Mathematical communication is a multimodal discours e practice that extends beyond formal, written forms to include bodily actions (e.g., Edwards, 200 9; Hall et al., 2015; Nathan & Walkington, 2017). Recently, several studies have provided empirical evidence fo r the grounded and embodied nature of geometric thi nking, specifically in proof. The production of mathematic al proofs requires students to evaluate universal t ruths about shape and space and to justify their conclusions. Studies have shown that both professional mathematicians and students ground their understandings of mathematica l content through embodied practices such as gestur es (e.g., Marghetis et al., 2014; Nathan et al., 2021; Walkington et al., 2022). Studies have specifically identified a class of representational gestures, called dynamic gestures, as critical for valid, generalizable geometric thinking (e.g., Nathan et al., 2021). While non-dynamic gestures (such as pointing and tracing) can  effectively simulate and communicate static properties of objects, dynamic g estures depict the invariant characteristics of mat hematical objects as they undergo transformations, such as ro tation, reflection, dilation, and skewing. Furtherm ore, prior studies have found strong associations between dyna mic gestures, spatial reasoning skills, and geometr ic reasoning (Göksun et al., 2013; Nathan et al., 2021).

Spatial ability and spatial training Spatial ability has been a topic of interest and de bate within the scientific community for more than a century, with researchers struggling to agree on a complete definition or list of sub-components. Spatial ability can be generally defined as the ability to imagine and manipulate objects and relations. Decades of research have shown that spatial ability can be linked to mathematics succes s. Research has found connections between success in spatial tasks and mathematical tasks in both children (e.g., Casey et al., 2015) and adults (e.g., Schenck & Nathan., 2020), and several meta-analyses have confirmed positive associations betwe en spatial and mathematics skills (Atit et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2020). Although the  specific nature of these associations rema ins largely unknown, studies have suggested shared processing requiremen ts in both spatial and mathematical tasks (e.g., Mi x et al., 2016). Additionally, researchers have sought to determine which mathematical concepts engage spatial thinking, with some studies finding associations between specific spatial ability sub-components and mathematics concepts (e.g., Cheng & Mix, 2014; Schenck & Nathan, 2020).

Hundreds of studies have explored whether spatial ability can be improved. Overall, studies have shown that spatial ability is sensitive to training effec ts (e.g., Cheng, 2016) and that short spatial training may be beneficial for mathematics outcomes (e.g., Hawes et  al., 2015; Mix et al., 2021). Several meta-analyses have synthesized the results of these studies and provided comprehensive insight into the malleability of spatial skills. For example, Baennigner and Newcombe (1989) investi gated: (1) the rela tionship between prior spatial experiences and spatial ability and (2) the effects  of spatial training on spatial ability. Furthermor e, Uttal and colleagues (2013) completed a meta-analysis of 217 studies on spatial ability training. They found three key results: (1) spatial ability is malleable in people of all ages; (2) training is effective, transferable, and long-lasting; and (3) interventions are effective both in and out of educational settings. Spatial training has been shown to not only improve  spatial ability but also to have positive effects on mathematical abilities. There have been substantive gains in empirical research i nto the effectiveness of spatial training on mathematical ability. At least two recent meta-analyses have focused on understanding the effects of spatial training on mathematics performance, indica ting that even short spatial interventions can be b eneficial. Yang and colleagues (2020) sought to extend Uttal a nd colleagues' (2013) findings to young children's spatial skills and found that early spatial skill training is highly e ffective in improving mathematics performance. Through an examination of 29 pre-post designed studies, Hawes and colleagues (2022) also found that spatial training was effective for increasing success on b oth spatial and pencil-and-paper mathematics tasks. Their analyses also suggested that using concrete manipul atives moderated the effects of spatial training on mathematics. For example, several empiric al studies with both adults and children have shown  that experiences with physical spatial puzzle tasks such as Tangrams  have improved scores on paper-and-pencil geometry tasks (e.g., Cornu et al., 2019; Mix et al., 202 1; Siew et al. 2013). However, no studies have investigated how spatial tasks may impact verbal mathematical reasoning during embodied geometry tasks  when students can use spontaneous gestures.

## Research Question

In the current study, we investigate the impact of a short puzzle activity on students' geometric reasoning as measured through a verbal proof task. Tasks that invoke spatial abilities, such as solvin g Tangram puzzles, are thought to improve access to one's spatial abilities, which have been found to be critical for geometric reasoning. Specifically, we ask: Can a brief experience with a  spatial puzzle task impact students' geometric reasoning, as measured by mathematical insight and transformation al proof? We hypothesize that those who engage in a short spatial puzzle task will be more likely to produce correct mathematical insi ght and transformational proof than those who engage in lower spatial activity.

## Participants and procedure

Undergraduate students were recruited  from a large Midwestern institution (N = 84; x̄  = 19.34, sd = 1.23; 52% female; 20.2% Asian, 65.5% White, 14.3% Other). To be included in the study, students were required to  have completed at least one high school geometry course.  Participants received a $20 gift card upon completion of all study activities.

In this between-subjects, repeated measures research design, participants  were then randomly assigned to either the control condition or the spatial puzzle condition (Figure 1). First, participants completed a set of premeasures, including a demographic and background questionnaire, the Paper Folding Task (Ekstrom et al., 1976), and the Spatial Anxiety Scale (Lyons et al., 2018).  Next, they completed two computer-based working memory span tasks (Stone & Towse, 2015) which were presented using the Tatool platform (von Bastian et al., 2012). The first task was the matrix span which measures visuo spatial working memory. The second task was the rea ding span which measures verbal working memory.

## Schematic of the Experimental Procedure

Then, participants completed either a low  spatial control task or a spatial puzzle task. In the control condition, participants were shown a set of six let ters arranged in a circle via PowerPoint  (Figure 2A). Each set of letters included at least two vowels and could b e combined to form at least one six-letter English word. They were then instructed to write down all the possible  words or common acronyms that could be created fro m a combination of the letters. Each set was presented for four minutes before a new set was automatically displayed. Ten sets were presented for a total task time of 40 minutes. In the Tangram task condition, participants were asked to complete a set of puzzles using physical Tangram  manipulatives (Figure 2B). For each puzzle, participants were shown an image of a composite shape via PowerP oint (Figure 2C) and given five minutes to assemble the shape using all seven Tangram pieces. After the participants assembled the composite shape or after five minutes, an image of the shape with the individual pieces vi sible was revealed  (Figure 2D). Then, participants were instructed to check their assembled solution agains t the displayed solution and make any necessary cha nges to their assembled shape before moving on to a new puz zle. Participants completed as many puzzles as poss ible in 40 minutes to match the control condition's time demands. The mean number of puzzles solved by participants in this condition was eight, with a range of 6 to 13 puzzles. Finally, participants were asked to justify the veracity of a series of 6 geometry conjectures selected from a larger list of conjectures used in a separate study (Schenck et al., 2022). During the justifications, students only had access to gestures and were not allowed to use paper and pencil. These geometry conjectures includ ed both two-and three-dimensional properties of shapes that are commonly featured in high school geometry curricula. ## Coding and data analysis

All clips were initially transcribed using Otter for Business (Otter.ai, 2023), an onlin e automated transcription service, before a researcher reviewed and correct ed each transcript. Video recordings of student’s explanations were then segmented by conjecture, resulting in 504 clips (84  participants x 6 conjectures). Each clip was given a 0/1 (present/absent) code for mathematical insigh t, transformational proof, and gesture. Mathematical insight was coded as present when participants correctly referenced mathematical properties and examples that were relevant and correctly applied  to the conjecture at hand. Transformational proof  was coded as present  when participants’ justifications were  generalizable and involved logical inference and operational thought (Harel & Sowder, 1998). We used mixed-effects logistic regression models on the outcome measures of insigh t and transformational proof using the glmer command in the R software package lme4 (Bates et al., 2014). We calculated d-type effect size measures using the logit method, and odds ratios (OR) were calculated by exponentiating the beta coefficients. Participant ID and conjecture were included in all models as random effects. Spatial ability, spatial anxiety, working memory, gestures, and gender were included as covariates in the models. For analysis, students’ self-reported gender was collapsed into either “Female” or “Non-Female”. The “Non-Female” category represents students who identified as male, non-binary, or other. Mathematical insight Regression results for mathematical insight can be found in Table 3. The results of the covariate model (Model 1) showed that several of the covariates were significantly predictive of insight. Gender was significantly predictive of insight, with females less likely than non-females to produce a mathematical insight (OR = 0.4 6, d = -0.43, p =.007). Both non-dynamic (OR = 3.13, d = 0.63, p <.000) and dynamic gestures (OR = 2.64, d = 0.54, p  =.003) were also significantly associated with the production of insight. When the Tangram task was added to form Model 2, it was not significantly predictive of insight (OR = 1.40, d = 0.19, p =.238). These results suggest that the Tangram task did not affect the participants’ odds of producing a correct mathematical insight. However, gender (OR = 0.47, d = -0.14, p =.008), spatial ability (OR = 1.68, d = 0.33, p <.000), non-dynamic gestures (OR = 3.16, d = 0.55, p  <.000), and dynamic gestures (OR = 2.69, d = 0.19, p  =.002) continued to be significantly associated with insight production. ## Discussion and conclusion

Spatial systems and gesture production each demonst rate reliable associations with mathematical reason ing. While gestures have often been considered to be an effective malleable factor for improving mathematic al reasoning and problem solving, there has been far l ess research exploring the potential for changes in  spatial abilities to alter math performance or consider ing how such interventions would impact gesture product ion. Spatial abilities impact our ability to complete ge ometric tasks. Due to the malleability of spatial s kills, students engaging in geometric thinking may benefit from eng aging in spatial activities prior to completing geo metric tasks. The central goal of this paper was to invest igate the potential of a brief spatial puzzle task for increasing the likelihood of students’ mathematical insight and transformational proof production. Even when controlling for prior spatial ability, wo rking memory,  and gesture production, this study found support favoring the Tangram task for improving geometric reasoning through the increased production of transformational proof, but not mathematical insight. Thus, it appears that the Tangram task does not lead students to be more likely to generate key mathematical insi ghts but benefits students' ability to produce math ematically valid proofs in high school level geometry. Simply recalling mathematical properties and theore ms may not engage spatio-motoric systems in a way that is improved by a spatial task intervention. However, transformational proofs require students to move beyond simply stating mathematical insights to incorporating those insights into argumentations  that are logical, generalizable, and operational (Harel & Sowder, 2005), which is more important for mathemat ics educational curriculum standards. One explanation for how Tangram tasks directly benefit the production o f mathematically valid transformational proofs is t hat the spatial strategies used in Tangram task activities may transfer due to shared domain-general and domain-specific processes. Participants often recruit spatial skill s and representations when solving mathematics prob lems to model, simulate, and manipulate mathematical relati ons and to ground the meaning of mathematical symbo ls (Mix, 2019). This study utilized a physical spatial  puzzle that was likely to engage students' spatial and mot oric systems in ways that are congruent with the task de mands for geometry reasoning. This prior engagement  may allow students to access and employ those systems m ore readily during the geometric reasoning task, in creasing the likelihood that students will successfully construct accurate, transformational geometric proofs. The randomized controlled experimental design of th e current study allows for causal conclusions that strengthen the endorsement for a causal relationship between short spatially based tasks, such as Tangram puzzles, and improvements in geometric reasoning. Recent meta-analyses have shown that spatial intervention experiences may be effective for improving mathematics outcomes  (Hawes et al., 2022, Yang et al., 2020). However, the spatial interventions included in these two recent meta-analyses are limited in scope, often focusing on yo ung participants and employing paper-based assessments. This study corroborates and exte nds prior research that spatial interventions can affect mathematical reasoning by engaging undergraduate participants in discourse-based geometric reasoning tasks and gesture production. I t also contributes to a growing series of empirical  studies on the role of students’ gesture and spatial skills in geometric reasoning (e.g., Nathan et al., 2021; Walkington et al.,

2019) Though this study is only a first step in understanding the role of short spatially based tasks in imp roving

mathematical reasoning, these results demonstrate that the skills learned or accessed in spatial inter ventions may transfer to more informal, verbal reasoning tasks often seen in classrooms and used  as formative assessments by instructors. We look forward to reporting additional analyses on the impact of this spatial puzzle task on students’ gestures to provide additional insights into why short spatiall y-based tasks improve embodied geometric reasoning.
