# Utilizing Activity Theory to Analyze how Tensions were Negotiated in a Teacher Video Club

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Focusing on a semester-long video club with five high school science teachers, the research describes elements of the video club as an activity system and examines how facilitator decisions and design choices surfaced and resolved tensions. Four levels of contradictions emerged during the professional learning: primary (video clip selection), secondary (changing norms and roles), tertiary (eliciting and working with student ideas), and quaternary (reconciling video club goals with district goals). The findings reveal that activity theory provides a nuanced framework for understanding the impacts of facilitator decision-making and highlights the importance of flexible facilitation, carefully selected artifacts, and responsive design in supporting teachers' professional growth and instructional exploration.

## Introduction

Video-based professional development is a promising model  to support teachers’ learning to enact student-centered instruction advocated by science reform ef forts (Richards et al., 2014; Tekkumru-Kisa et al., 2018). Providing teachers with complex representations of classroom interactions coupled with opportunitie s to study those interactions, can enable teachers to develop a professional vision of responsive instruction, and a beginning repertoire of instructional practices (Beisiegel et al, 2018; Luna & Sherin, 2017). Video-based professional learning (PL) settings are complex activity systems guided by goals for teacher learning that shape clip selection, task design, participant structure, discursive prac tices, and assessment of learning (van Es et al., 2020). These design choices are contingent on each other and require flexible and responsive facilitation (Baecher et al., 2018). The complexity of designing and carrying out video-based PL that is productive for teacher learning ra ises questions about how facilitators navigate design considerations both in planning and carrying out these programs. Informed by activity theory (Greeno & Engeström, 2014), we investigate one facilitator’s design decisions in the process of planning for and enacting a video-based PL program for secondary teachers. Specifically, we ask: how do a facilitator’s design decisions respond to contradictions that arise in a video club? Conceptual background Video-based PL can be a generative context for teachers to develop ass et-based noticing of student thinking  and responsive teaching practices (Tekkumru-Kisa et al., 2018; van Es et al., 2020). Activity theory provides a lens to conceptualize video-based PL  as a system that consists of multiple elements that  work in concert to advance learning (Engeström, 2011). This perspective  recognizes that conditions emerge and are contingen t on who participants are, their goals for learning, and how participants interact with each other and material objects in their context (Greeno & Engeström, 2014). These conditions – which activity theory refers to as tensions – require adaptation and flexibility to move toward desired o utcomes. Engeström (2011) identified four levels of inner contradictions: primary contradictions occur within an element of t he activity system; secondary contradictions occur between two or more elements within an activity system; tertiary contradictions occur between a new form of activity generated by the activity system and previous forms of activity; and quaternary contradictions occur between the new activity system and external activity systems with which the new system interacts. Contradictions and tensions, despite the negative connotation, are generative in evolving activity systems because their resolution is the driving force for learning (Engeström, 2011). Analysis of contradictions and how they are resolved (or not) can help researchers, designers, and participants b etter understand how tensions emerge, what types of learn ing occur, what resources are leveraged to generate learning,  and why unanticipated new forms of activity occur (Yamagata-Lynch & Haudenschild, 2009). Thus, in this study, we investigate a.) contradictions that arose during the enactment of the video club design; and b.) how facilitator de cisions influenced change in the video club activity system to resolve contradictions.

## Methodology

This study took place in the context of a semester-long video club (VC) with five secondary science teachers and focused on developing their vision of responsive teaching and experimentation w ith eliciting and working with students’ ideas. The group met about once monthly after school. Each teacher h ad at least ten years of teaching experience and taught a variety of science disciplines. The first author was the primary designer and facilitator of the VC. The design included attention to how different tool s, discourse norms, and goals interacted to support teacher learning (Bannister, 2015; Horn et al., 2017; Luna & Sherin, 2017; Tekkumru-Kisa & Stein, 2017). Tools included artifacts of teaching, both published and from participants’ own classrooms, and the activities the facilitator used to frame attention to details of student thinking. Norms were influenced by both participants’ previous experience collaborating on district assessment design and the set of facilitation practices used to orient participants to the task of examining the artifacts – promoting an inquiry stance, maintaining a focus on the details of the artifacts, and supporting collaboration (Barnhart, 2021). We utilized the facilitator’s design and meeting notes and meeting transcripts to analyze the facilitator’s design choices and adjustments between and within m eetings in relation to the activity system componen ts. Through an iterative analytic process (Corbin & Str auss, 2015), th e authors first reviewed the design notes to characterize elements of the VC activity system  and identify perceived contradictions as the VC design was enacted. We then analyzed the corresponding meeting  transcripts to identify confirming and disconfirmi ng evidence of these contradictions and categorized the contradictions as one of Engeström’ s (2011) four levels. Finally, we returned to the data to identify how the facilitator attempted to resolve each contradiction.

## Findings

Here, we report on the types of contradictions that  emerged in the enactment of the VC and illuminate how the facilitator leveraged different elements of the activity system to respond to these contradictions. Primary contradiction: Affordances of published versus own video clips What video segments represent, the quality of what is captured, and the source of the video has consequences for teacher learning (Amador et al., 2020; Baecher et al., 2018; Sherin et al  2009; Zhang et al., 2011). Published, facilitator-selected videos can model and spark interest in unfamiliar forms of instruction and develop noticing of student thinking, whereas videos from teachers’ own classrooms can help develop analytic skill and gain support from colleagues, despite potentially lacking the quality of published videos. This tension surfaces a contradiction related to enactment within the “tools” element of the VC activity system, prompting facilitators to consider what type of video to feature, when, and for what purpose. For example, with the goal  to establish norms for using video as a source of evidence and to learn practices to center and make sense of student  thinking, the early VC clips needed to feature worthwhile student thinking. Published videos afforded accomplishing this goal as  they were captured to illustrate and illuminate complex student thinking. The details in these published clips allowed the facilitator to launch discussions of artifacts by modeling the “rules” of the VC. For example, the facilitator introduced the first clip in the first meeting by pointing out details of the student’s model of how gases behave in a container as they cool: “So we do see some arrows here on the bottom diagram  on the last one going down…It doesn’t look like the arrows are different lengths like in the middle diagram...Ok, now he said there’s pressure on the inside, it’s getting warmer, and it’s trying to escape.” Since these artifacts captured details in both students’ drawn models and their comments about their model, she could model how toneutrally describe evidence. Another consideration concerned when to use clips o f participants’ experimentation with new practice. The facilitator’s reflection  notes after meeting two recognized some participants  did “not seem quite ready to share.” Deferring the use of participant’s  own clips allowed them to gain confidence in their new practice and trust with each other. In later meetings, when the group shifted to analyze clips from their own practice, the artifacts had limited windows into student thinking compared to the published clips. However, because the norms and rules for analyzing clips were well-established by this point, participants were able to take  on roles  of highlighting and analyzing evidence  that had been filled by the facilitator in early meet ings. This shift in roles highlights another contradiction in the activity system, that of changing participation during the course of the VC. Secondary contradiction: Establishing and maintaining asset-based noticing of student thinking versus distributing participation Leveraging the affordances of high-quality artifacts (tools) required more direct facilitation in early meetings (division of labor) while norms were established. This contributed to a new contradiction, how to establish and maintain asset-based noticing of student thinking and distribute participation among the teachers. Research documents the key role of facilitators to maintain frames for video analysis and the different moves they make to direct teachers’ noticing and systematic analysis of teaching (Coles, 2019; Tekkumru-Kisa & Stein, 2017). During discussions, the facilitator must strike a balance of  “supporting learning toward carefully defined g oals without undermining learners’ sense of agency” (Tekkumru-Kisa & Stein, 2017, p. 4). This contradiction – to participate in ways that overtly model a focus on evidence-based inquiry while also cultivating trust and the norm that everyone contributes – was resolved by relying on high quality artifacts. The facilitator highlight ed details of student thinking and redirect ed participants back to these details when discussions  strayed to anecdotal or evaluative talk. An example of a redirection occurred in the first meeting as the group analyzed students’ models and discussions about why a steel tank collapsed that was steam cleaned then sealed shut. The facilitator initiated the discussion by pointing out features of the students’ models indicating pressure and molecule movement. Mitch then added: Mitch: I thought her come back to the large versus small molecule point ah, was a weakness. That often happens, right? The kid comes up with so mething, and technically, its chemistry, you know nothing’s interacting to change the molecules, so you don’t want the kid to think that way. And sometimes, I’ll say flipp antly some advanced piece of content. Like she did, right? She says, “oh, yeah, but it’s not combining with the other molecules, so it’s not going to be smaller, right, or bigger.”  She kind of, you  know, brushes his idea aside.

Facilitator:  I thought it was interesting that, and this was the only group where this came up, something about the size of the molecule changed, and that was what was causing the change in the inside. Although he didn’t explicitly link it to pressure, and I thought that’s interesting.

Rather thandirectly  correct Mitch that our focus was on student thinking,  the facilitator redirected back to the description and interpretation of evidence of stude nts’ thinking in the clip. This reinforced the VC “rules” for maintaining an inquiry focus of students’ ideas without shutting down participants’ early efforts to contribute. By the third meeting, participants began to take up the role of highlighting evidence of student reasoning and nominating a n artifact for discussion. The group was discussing  students’ responses to a summative assessment prompt to describe how sound traveled at  a concert. Vincent raised up a particular students’ artifact for discussion announcing that “I want to talk about Lisa”: I like the fact that she’s using dots because they are particles, and this is kind of what I imagine. You see the pattern of dots from the top and bottom are similar, but you don’t see as many dots [pointing]. So, I notice that she has the spacing here about the same, but you don’t see as many dots being compressed here, obviously because it’s farther away. Some of the energy from that sound will kinda dissipate. So, I mean she even uses the word rarefaction. Vincent’s pattern of describing and interpreting de tails from the artifact mirrors the pattern modeled  by the facilitator in earlier meetings. The affordances of the artifacts in the VC allowed the participants to take up this new role in the VC. However, participants experienced different challenges when their growing interest in students’ ideas and subsequent experimentation with instructional practices stimulated changes in their classroom practice. As they experimented with eliciting students’ ideas abo ut science phenomena, a new contradiction emerged related to how to work with students’ ideas to move students’ learning forward. Tertiary contradiction: Eliciting students’ ideas versus working with students’ ideas We conjectured that to achieve the objective of the VC, teachers needed to hone their noticing of students’ science thinking. This required first raising interest in and awareness of students’ ideas, then gaining skill in attending to and interpreting students’ ideas, and finally, experimenting with more responsive practices. As participants began exploring tasks that made students’ thinking more visible, they realized that their classroom context had changed. This raised a contradiction when new teaching metho ds stimulated by the VC conflicted with the established routines in their classrooms. The more ambitious ta sks they designed triggered a change in both the di vision of labor as well as the norms of classroom discourse a s students were positioned to take on a more active sensemaking role. Consequently, the teachers expressed disc omfort with the new patterns of discourse for interacting with students and the ir ideas. At around the midpoint of the VC series, Mitch mentioned that he attempted to enact a version of the lesson we viewed in our first meeting with his students. He described some of the challenges he faced with this new model of instruction saying, “I did this to practice not giving away answers. This is hard going, and I knew it would be after wa tching the video.” Similarly, when analyzing artifa cts from William’s chemistry class in which students were at tempting a gas laws activity similar to the l esson featured in meeting one, the group expressed some frustration w ith the ambiguity of one group’s drawn model.  This led to Mitch asking, “How do you encourage students to draw things that don’t leave us with questions?” This exchange indicates that the participants became curious abou t students’ ideas and attempted to enact instructio n that made student thinking more visible. However, the artifacts that served as models for what new mod es of instruction could look like were useful when the goal was eliciting students’ ideas but were less helpful in providing a vision for working with students’ ideas. The facilitator noted after this meeting that the participants felt the need to “ask better questions and scaffold good science writing.” Eliciting and working with students’ ideas are diff erent skills (Windschitl et al., 2018). Viewing and analyzing videos with different affordances would l ikely have helped resolve this contradiction had th e VC not come to its end. In an attempt to continue to support the participants in resolving this contradiction after the VC ended, the facilitator  noted speaking  with one school principal about a potential scaffol d for working with students’ ideas and the need for coaching around th e use of this scaffold. Unlike the shift in tools t he designers made to resolve the shift in roles within the VC activity system, the shift in tools did not adequat ely support the participants in resolving the contradiction between  the new roles the VC activity system compelled them to take on outside the VC activity system during the course of the PL. Quaternary contradiction: Video club goals versus district assessment goals Teachers expressed several motives for participating in the VC, including wanting to know more about ambitious practices and to see how other teachers create learning conve rsations (Barnhart & van Es, 2020). Additionally, they mentioned a district initiative to develop a c ommon argumentation task and an associated rubric. It is well documented that professional development should align with district goals as well as be responsive to participants’ input (Desimone & Garet, 2015), therefore, work on the rubric task was included in the VC. How the rubric design was integrated in the VC created a contradiction between the  districts’ goal to assess students’ efforts to create evidence-based arguments and the VC goal to enact more responsive science instruction. The facilitator viewed developing an inquiry lens to noticing student thinking as a mediating practice to achieve the goal of instructional change. However, when the facilitator put the rubric in conversation with student work, the discussion around student thinking became largely evaluative. An example of these different discourse practices occurred in meeting three as the group worked with the students' sound models. After lengthy turns describing the details in stu dents’ drawn models and interpreting what that meant about their understanding, the facilitator brought in the evidence-based explanation rubric the group developed that included descriptions of three levels of quali ty for various elements of an explanation. The facilitator first asked where on the rubric the student work would fall: Vincent:  I would go low medium. Maybe medium, but I see more  low medium. Um, he understood somewhat what’s happening with Gina, but I would say he just took a guess with Jill. You know I guess I see some parts correct with Jill but I, he’s having a hard time… Ron:  Well, I think what they, what  Renaldo wrote for Gina is a much better answer than what Renaldo wrote for Jill. For Jill he wrote the guitar will sound low. Well to me, low means bass. Low frequency. I don’t think that’s the right answer for sitting farther away. It’s going to sound softer.

The intent was to co-construct a rubric that would provide useful information to teachers and students about how to support evidence-based argument building. Instead, the group used words like “correct,” “wrong,” and made comparisons across students like “better” that were not common during the artifact discussions in which the rubric was not utilized. Invoking this tool likely called up a different activity system that teachers are fa miliar with – that of assessment as evaluation. The rules and roles for the participants and the object of noticing student thinking with an inquiry lens were temporarily set aside as the group instead triggered the rules and roles associated with assessment. When the work of the group returned to noticing student thinking without the rubric, conve rsation norms reverted to an inquiry lens. This contradiction was not resolved during the VC, but had the facilitator been more aware of the contradiction in the moment, different facilitation moves could have reconciled the VC object of noticing student thinking using an inquiry lens and the district’s object of rubric design.

## Conclusions and implications

Numerous frameworks exist to inform the design of PL for teachers in terms of what artifacts to select, what tasks to engage in, and what facilitation moves might be employed to achieve productive discussion s about teaching and learning (Beisiegel et al., 2018; Tekkumru-Kisa & Stein, 2017; van Es et al., 2020). Despite the extensive documentation on how to structure PL, classroom practice remains stubbornly inert (Kennedy, 2016). Much like the work of classroom teaching, t aking recommended design elements off the page and putting them in a living, breathing PL environment is complex and challenging. Activity theory can be useful for revealing  challenges of enactment but more importantly the location (tools, object, norms, etc.)  and nature of these challenges (primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary) so informed adjustments can be made to support their r esolution. Understanding the contextual elements and how they align or not has the potential to assist those attempting to bring PL programs to scale while still accounting for the uniqueness of each activity system. As is often the case, we, as designers, often have little to no control of external forces acting on PL participants (Jaworski & Potari, 2009). Proactive awareness of both internal and external contradictions using activity system analysis can permit more responsive facilitation within PL, and therefore enhanced teacher learning.
