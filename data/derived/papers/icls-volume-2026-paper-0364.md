# Measuring Student Engagement: Leveraging Multimodal Data to Compare Human and AI Tutoring

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study compares differences in student engagement across tutor types (human, human-like AI, and nonhuman-like AI) in asynchronous online learning. Data were collected from 20 university students using eye-tracking, electroencephalography, and facial expression analysis. Results show the human-like AI tutor attracted visual attention and elicited the highest emotional responses. The human tutor sustained deeper attention and moderate emotional states. The nonhuman-like AI tutor elicited the fewest emotional reactions. This study highlights a hybrid tutoring design.

Asynchronous online learning has become a predominant mode of course delivery. While this approach increases flexibility and access, it limits real-time interaction and reduces social presence  (Baber, 2022). This lack of immediate interaction may undermine student engagement, which is particularly important in such contexts. From an engagement perspective, learning involves both cognitive effort and emotional responses, reflecting learners’ active involvement in learning activities (Reschly & Christenson, 2022). To further enhance social presence in asynchronous learning, visible human instructors  are ofte n included in videos, support ing engagement and learning (Mayer, 2021). Moreover, AI tutors also complement human instruction by offering personalized, scalable support, generating questions, monitoring progress, and fostering motivation (Kim et al., 2025; Song et al., 2025), thereby mitigating  these challenges. However, they remain limited in emotional, creative, and ethical reasoning and may raise concerns about  overreliance or academic misconduct (Crompton et al., 2024). While anthropomorphic agents can increase perceived interactivity, highly hum an-like tutors may trigger the uncanny valley  effect, reducing engagement (Mori et al., 2012). Therefore, n on-human AI agents, such as cartoon avatars, zoomorphic characters, and chatbots, can be used without causing discomfort, though empirical evidence of their effectiveness in asynchronous learning remains limited.

Although Zhang et al. (2024) reviewed pedagogical agent designs showing mixed effects of humanoid versus non-humanoid agents in primarily K-12 contexts. Little is known about h ow different tutors' designs influence students' cognitive and emotional engagement in higher education, particularly when measured using multimodal methods (D’Mello et al., 2017). To address this gap, this study investigates:

1. How do students’ cognitive engagement differ when interacting with a human instructor, a human-like

AI tutor, and a nonhuman-like AI tutor?

2. How do students’ emotional engagement differ when interacting with a human instructor, a human-like

AI tutor, and a nonhuman-like AI tutor?

3. How do students’ perceptions of engagement differ when interacting with a human instructor, a human-like AI tutor, and a nonhuman-like AI tutor?

This study was conducted at a Sino-British joint university in China, with 20 participants recruited through purposive and snowball sampling. A within-subjects design was employed, in which all participants completed three sessions under the same experimen tal conditions, with the order of instructional videos counterbalanced to minimize order effects. Each session lasted approximately 30 minutes, totaling around 90 minutes per participant. Three 20-minute instructional videos were developed covering cognitive learning theories, including cognitive load theory, chunk theory, and Bloom’s Taxonomy. Each uses a consistent instructional approach but focuses on different sub-topics to reduce order  effects (Moreno & Mayer, 2007; Li et al., 2024), ensuring differences in learner responses reflect instructional delivery rather than content repetition or fatigue. The humanlike AI tutor and a nonhuman-like (cartoon) AI tutor were created using HeyGen, featuring AI-generated slides and synthesized speech, with content developed and tested based on prompts from ChatGPT 4.0. 2518 Cognitive engagement was measured using eye-tracking and electroencephalography (EEG), with eye-tracking data (fixation duration, count, and location; Ma et al., 2024) collected via the Tobii Pro system and EEG theta power recorded from eight scalp electrodes at 2000 Hz and preprocessed in EEGLAB (Delorme & Makeig, 2004). The theta band is associated with attention levels (Sauseng et al., 2007). In addition, emotional engagement was assessed from facial videos using OpenFace 2.0 (Baltrušaitis et al., 2018). Facial Action Units were mapped to seven basic emotions (happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, contempt, and surprise; Ekman & Friesen, 1978). A one-way repeated-measures ANOVA and Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons were conducted to compare cognitive and emotional engagement across different tutoring conditions. After the experiment, semi-structured interviews (60– 90 minutes) were conducted to explore participants’ perceptions of engagement with the different tutors. Inductive and deductive thematic analysis (Clarke et al., 2015) was applied, with themes validated through member checking and team consensus (Glesne, 2016). Students’ cognitive engagement across different tutors Differences in cognitive engagement emerged across tutor conditions. Fixation duration was higher in both AI tutor conditions than in the human tutor condition, with the human-like AI tutor showing the most pronounced levels (M = 382.16, SD = 160.84), followed by the nonhuman-like AI tutor (M = 213.42, SD  = 93.50), and the lowest value for the human tutor (M  = 74.49, SD = 65.51). These differences were statistically significant, F (2,

38) = 75.66, p < 0.001, η² = 0.80.

In addition, theta power also varied across conditions, F (2, 38) = 4.36, p  = 0.02, η² = 0.19. Compared with the AI tutors, the human tutor yielded the highest mean (M = 8.88, SD = 3.33), whereas the human-like (M = 8.04, SD = 2.90) and nonhuman-like AI tutors (M = 7.48, SD = 2.86) yielded lower means. Students’ emotional engagement across different tutors Emotional responses to the human-like AI tutor were significantly higher than those in the other conditions (p < 0.001). Students showed the strongest reactions in happiness (M = 0.52, SD  = 0.54), sadness (M = 0.55, SD  = 0.32), anger (M = 1.67, SD = 1.01), surprise (M = 0.42, SD = 0.20), fear (M = 2.42, SD = 1.13), contempt (M = 0.43, SD = 0.18), and disgust (M = 0.31, SD = 0.18). The human tutor elicited moderate responses (e.g., anger: M = 0.29, SD = 0.18; fear: M = 0.29, SD = 0.18), while the cartoon-like AI tutor evoked the lowest intensities (e.g., anger: M = 0.37, SD = 0.21; fear: M = 0.24, SD = 0.12). Students’ perceptions across different tutors Students perceived differences in engagement feelings across tutor types. The human-like AI tutor was described as more engaging due to their multimodal presentation, which combined visual elements with synchronized explanations to sustain attention and enhance focus. The nonhuman-like AI tutor was sometimes perceived as distracting. In addition, human tutors, who mostly just talk, were viewed as less engaging.

Using multimodal data, this study investigated differences in university students’ cognitive and emotional engagement across asynchronous instructional videos featuring a human tut or, a human-like AI tutor, and a nonhuman-like AI tutor. This study found that students exhibited greater visual attention under the human-like AI tutor condition, likely driven by its multimodal presentation, while the human instructor elicited higher overall attention levels, which may facilitate more efficient information proce ssing and meaningful learning (Netland et al., 2025). In terms of emotional engagement, students reported the highest levels of emotional response and more negative emotions when interacting with the human-like AI tutor, likely due to the uncanny valley phenomenon (Mori et al., 2012). Therefore, this research suggests  a hybrid human-AI tutoring model. AI tutors can enhance engagement through multimedia and personalized prompts that stimulate curiosity, while human instructors can provide social cues and guided explanations to promote deeper understanding. This study has some limitations that should be considered in future research. The small sample size limits the extent to which the findings can be applied. In addition, our study focused on short, controlled sessions, which mainly capture learners' initial reactions rather than the deeper thinking and engagement that occur during mo re complex tasks or discussions. Future studies should examine longer-term learning with more diverse groups of students. Using multimodal measurements in these real-world learning scenarios will help us understand how differences in culture, background, and experience affect learning and guide the design of asynchronous  online learning that is effective and inclusive for everyone.
