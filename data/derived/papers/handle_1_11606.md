# Cultural Context and Participation in Online Learning Communities in Post-Secondary Environments

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Critical to success in online education is the ability for students to navigate online spaces and to participate in learning communities. If pedagogical characteristics of online learning environments emphasize, and rely upon, interaction and collaboration (Garrison et al., 2000), it is critical to examine digital spaces and resulting online learning communities for cultural inclusivity. This systematic literature review outlines some of the arguments for using critical pedagogy to design online learning communities that are inclusive of diverse learners, creating space for multiple epistemologies and cultural contexts. It will begin with a brief outline of the search strategy used to source fifteen peer-reviewed journal articles, followed by benefits and challenges for students from non-dominant cultural contexts.

## Context

A scan of relevant literature indicates that digita l technology has the potential to provide access and opportunity for learners across educational systems (Eady & Woo dcock, 2010; Kral, 2010; Simon et al., 2014; Phiran gee, 2016; Sisco, 2010). Yet, a gap exists between the completion rates of Indigeno us and non-Indigenous learners in online environments (Sisco, 2010; TRC, 2015b). Combined with the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) mandates to decrease the documented disparity in educational outcomes for Indigenous peoples in Canada (2015a, 2015b), this potential has influenced educators to transform digital spaces to meet educational goals (Kral, 2010). Despite this view of online education as democratic, digital spaces are not neutral, value free or culture free (Gunawardena, 2020) and the same tendency of users and designers to include dominant paradigms can be incongruent for learners from non-dominant cultures (Caruthers & Friend, 2014). Search criteria, inclusion and exclusion process Fifteen articles were included that contained an overlap of Indigenous or critical pedagogy with an inquiry into how diversity can be cultivated. The following keyword combination was used: “online learning communities.” Using “ Indigenous” “ critical” “ cultural” reduced  the volume of literature. Using k eywords “ online learning communities” “inclusive” and “Indigenous” resulted in 273 articles. A search of two research databases resulted in five articles through ERIC and 76 through LearnTechLib u sing the search string: online learning community (indigenous OR aboriginal OR "indians of north amer ica") "first nations" from:2000 to:2020. Removing duplicates, irrelevant articles, and including only  peer-reviewed studies in journal articles resulted  in the final articles chosen because they dealt with non-dominant cultures and online learning communities.

## Findings

Expansive growth in online enrollment and low retention rates deem it important to examine the spaces in which student interaction occurs, as one aspect integral to learning (Anderson, 2017; Garrison et al., 2000). Across disciplines, scholars explored models of participation in online discourse and how  these improve learning (Jaggars, 2014; Phillip et al., 2018). Participation in online discourse may be culturally mediated, and potentially, exclusionary to those from non-dominant cultures (Caruthers & Frie nd, 2014). For example, research indicates that Indigenous education must be culturally cohere nt (Sanguins, 2015; Pigeon, 2018). Phirangee (2016)  states that online facilitation requires a different skill set, one that requires training and professional development, and without, instructors struggle to understand which strategies and interactions are effective for online environments. Learning space s are virtual location s in which people enter to engage in knowledge acquis ition as a central activity (Oblinger, 2006). The LMS can provide an opportunity to develop learning communit ies using technological tools that afford communication and c ollaboration (Castañeda & Selwyn, 2018). Typically, the discussion space allows for multiple threads, topic s, or conversations to be created by any participan t. However, responsibility rests with those that design digital  educational spaces to engage in critical and progr essive design, using technology to drive critical engagement. To d o this, they must be aware of their own worldviews and how those impact their design choices and commitment to instructional design paradigms (Dreamson et al., 2017). An assimilationist approach to teaching replicates social inequities because it does not account for ways in which students create or acquire knowledge diffe rently based on cultural background (Gutiérrez & Ro goff, 2003). Indigenous knowledge and relational ways of learning do not align with the policies and practices encoded by our institutions (Battiste, 2000; Dreamson et al., 2017; Pidgeon et al., 2014).  The LMS, where most online learning takes place, reduces collective culture to  individual differences, restricting interculturali ty with policies that do not respect cultural diversity (Dreamson et  al., 2017). Critical approaches to online discours e (Caruthers & Friend, 2014) align with Indigenous education goa ls, especially if they transform spaces into equita ble places of discourse. Inclusive learning environments foste r a sense of belonging to and participation in a le arning community (Gunawardena, 2020; Morris & Stommel, 2018; Phillip et al., 2018 Benefits Multiple benefits exist of online communication and  “unprecedented possibilities and combinations for learning spaces and pedagogies” (Al-Mahmood, 2006, p. 43). Flexibility, convenience, and accessibility (Bolliger & Inan, 2012; Phirangee, 2016; Sanguins, 2015) are lauded as major benefits of online education allowing students to plan around their unique circumstances (Bolliger & Inan, 2012; Hara & Kling, 2001) with the option of asynchronous, synchronous, or a combination of both modalities. F or example, it affords students in remote locations  the opportunity to enroll in post-secondary without relocati ng, reducing financial barriers (Simon et al., 2014). Technology can improve access to online instructors (Bali et al., 2020), to other students (Hilton, 2013) and virtual communication can provide opportunities students who require/prefer to reflect and compose text communication (Phirangee & Malec, 2017; Bali et al., 2020). The ability to create a social presence and space f or affective expression was shown to impact persistence and pote ntially lead to an improved sense of belongin g (Hannon & D’Netto, 2007).

The temporal aspect of the digital space offers tim e to engage in reflective practice and begin to fos ter critical consciousness. A course that is des igned to facilitate opportunities for effective crit ical dialogue also provide opportunities for identity formation (Phira ngee & Malec, 2017). An unlimited number of student-driven online spaces can be created for learners to study collaboratively, problem solve, critically analyze (Eliyahu-Levi, 2020; Hilton, 2013) or simply discuss assignment guidelines (Bali et al., 2020). Online spaces can be used to share resources and engage in several online discussions simultaneously without F2F “turn-taking” (Phirangee, 2016). Challenges Despite above benefits, online learners experience physical isolation, reduced sense of community, lac k of interaction between and among their classmates whic h can foster feelings of alienation, isolation, dis connection, and distraction (Phirangee, 2016) and that this is the main cause of attrition rates in online education (Angelino et al., 2007; Kanuka & Jugdev, 2006). Gunawardena (2020) posited that online education amplified inequities, rather than creating equitable spaces. Certain interactions can weaken the sense of community (Phirangee, 2016). Low levels or a lack of interaction, minimal support (Carr, 2000), and reduced social cues (Gunawardena, 2014; Hughes, 2015) contribute to dissatisfaction  (Hannon & D’Netto, 2007). Dominant students have the potential to crowd out alternative voices. With efforts to engag e in active forms of teaching for inclusion and cri tical pedagogy, diversity can be improved (Phirangee, 201 6) but remains dependent upon the instructor, their worldviews and concept of their role as an instructor.

Historically, barriers and even systemic violence h as been experienced by those students from non-dominant groups (Phillip et al., 2018). Redesigning online spaces to  include authentic and active learning is recommended by several scholars (Caruthers & Friend, 2014; Gunawardena, 2020) rather than  using the LMS simply as a resource repository. A transformational pedagogical (Caruthers & Friend, 2014) approach aligns with Freire’s (1972) philosophy on critical pedagogy.

## Conclusion

Technology mediates all experiences in online communities and cultural biases shape digital tool and environment design (McLoughlin & Oliver, 2000). With attention, these same tools can be used to c reate inclusive learning experiences (Gunawardena, 2020; Rice et al., 2020).  Digital environments can constrain or afford oppor tunities for critical interaction and collaboration between participants (Caruthers & Friend, 2014; Eliyahu-Levi, 2020), leading to a rich learning community inclusive of all cultu res (Al-Mahmood, 2006; Caruthers & Friend, 2014; Gunawardena, 2020; Rice et al., 2020).  The pedagogical characteristics of online learning environments emphasize, and rely upon, interaction and collabora tion (Anderson & Garrison, 1998). Instructors and skilled students can foster environments that normalize diverse perspectives by engaging in critical discourse, making this a pedagogical priority.
