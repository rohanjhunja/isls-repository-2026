# Making is not Just About Making: Female Collectivity and Agentic Non-Humans in Clay Pottery and Oven-Making in Brazilian Quilombola Communities

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores the cultural nuances of clay pottery and oven-making in two quilombola communities in Brazil, offering a critical perspective on the prevailing narratives around maker education. Our research in the Brazilian Amazon reveals a profound connection between the community and the Mãe do Barro, a revered non-human entity involved in the making process, significantly contributing to its technical rigor and effectiveness. In another site in the Southeast of Brazil, the oven-making process facilitates and strengthens conviviality and solidarity among female oven makers. These findings challenge the dominant, often US-centric paradigms (and critiques) in maker education by highlighting diverse, culturally rich making practices. We argue for a more inclusive and globally aware making education curriculum, emphasizing the need to recognize and value the making traditions of historically marginalized communities outside of the US.

On dominant and counter-narratives on making The resurgence of making physical objects as part o f students’ educational experiences in the early 21 st century, through the Maker Movement, has introduced positive  changes to schools, including the rekindling of constructionist, project-based STEM learning in mainstream education. However, much of what happens in these spaces and curricula often implicitly reflects domi nant US-based values and epistemologies (Blikstein, 2008; Buechley et al., 2008; Buechley, 2013; Vossoughi et al., 2016). While anthropologists, sociologists, and historians have long documented varied and globally dispersed making practices (e.g., Gosselain, 2018), these insights have had little influence on STEM or maker education. Do minant discourses in these settings typically empha size problem-solving, invention of gadgets, technological advancement, and economic growth. Even if inadvertently, this emphasis ignores the wide range of motivations  and desired outcomes found in making practices (of  nondominant communities) that do not directly support strictu-sensu academic learning or contribute to existing economic structures. For the maker movement to be g enuinely equitable, we must understand the assets, values, and purposes of making that are absent from dominant discourses. Recent critiques of normative discourses have spurred efforts in maker education to acknowledge cultural variations in making practices across various commu nities – but mostly US-based ones – while concurrently formulating equity-oriented strategies and principles for maker education. Buechley’s (2013) pioneering analysis of the MAKE! Magazine content pointed out that its target population, projects, and tools cater to the  interests and cultures of “white Bay Area boys” (cars, robots, rockets, entrepreneurship) and ignore possibilities and ideas such as celebratory costumes and complex traditiona l metalworking and pottery. Barajás -López and Bang’s

(2018) study of clay making within an indigenous ST EAM program demonstrates how indigenous making

operates as a form of storytelling and is deeply connected to the  natural world. Sengupta-Irving and Vossoughi

(2019) examine the subjective experiences of two mi noritized girls in STEM learning environments, argu ing

against the “US global hegemony” as a rationale for  STEM learning, and questioning dominant definition s of science while valuing its convergence with creativity and joy. Durán-López (2023) showed that basket-making in a Pomo Nation community had a role that went much b eyond the utilitarian, carrying intergen erational connections and keeping alive the community’s cosmo vision. Blikstein (2020) questions US-centric critiques of maker education and argues that they have often unc ritically adopted an idealized view of making as in herently liberating or democratizing. He advocates for a culturally situated approach  that engages with local populations to co-determine what tools and practices could be mutually enriching, avoiding the romanticization of the “local.” In our study, we aim to advance this agenda by docu menting making practices in non-dominant populations in the Global South. Leveraging insights from US-based studies and anthropological research into the human activities of doing and making, this paper re ports on our research into pottery and oven-making in two quilombola communities in Brazil, one in the Amazon, and another in the Southeastern region of Brazil. Through collaboration with local researchers in Brazil who have deep connections to these commu nities, we have been studying these practices with the goal of broadenin g definitions and practices of Maker Education, ask ing: What are culturally-specific elements of these quilombola communities’ approach to making practices? Anthropology of technique and the study of human making Drawing on work of scholars such as Mauss (2006), L emmonier (1992), Ingold (1997), Leroi-Gourhan (2012), and Coupaye (2009; 2015), contemporary approaches to the anthropology of technique seek to understand humans doing and making things through a systemic perspective centered on “technical processes” or chaînes opératoires

– cultural aggregates that combine raw materials, too ls, knowledge, representations, and agents. The nat ure and

arrangement of these elements, they argue, derive f rom a complex network o f social, historical, and ecological relations that not only establish the sociohistorical context – the circumstances of practice – within which human making takes place, but also influence how people form and interpret their participation in the making. The notion of chaînes opératoires (operational sequences) is both the unit of analysis and the analytical tool used to document this process (Gosselain, 2018, pp. 2-3), and it involves “breaking down a given process into step-by-step actions” (Coupaye, 2009, p. 439), or interlinked acts or ope rations involved in any human transformation of mat ter (Lemonnier, 1992, p. 26). It comprises both major o perations defined as strategic (cannot be eliminate d or postponed) and non-strategic (their presence or position within the chaîne opératoire  does not condition its outcome). These operations are broken down into sma ller units for comparative purposes between chaînes opératoires dispersed in time and space in anthropological and historical studies (Gosselain, 2018, p. 4). Coupaye

(2015) brings in the idea of a “transect” (in ecology, a line drawn between two points enabling observers to register

species found along the line). Conceiving operational sequences as transects allows us to follow the making of an artifact and to make visible elements recr uited by people in the making process, which might otherwise go overlooked.

Over the last two decades, Brazilian anthropologist s have shown increasing interest in technical processes, especially concerning indigenous peoples  (Mura & Sautchuk, 2019). For example, Oliveira (20 19) makes visible the techniques through which communit ies in Serra da Lua handle manioc and thereby const ruct collectivities. While such studies have enriched our understanding of technical processes within communities in Brazil, they have yet to be explicitly framed withi n the context of education research. In this paper,  we draw on the anthropology of technique to broaden the perspe ctives and methodological tools brought to making i n education. Using the concept of chaînes operatoires conceived as a transect as a methodological tool, w e sought to understand how raw materials, tools, knowledge, representations, and human and non-human agents are combined into a cultural artifact, as well to illum inate the surrounding social, historical, and ecological relationships that make making meaningful in two quilombola communities in Brazil. We do this by examining the series of major operations involved in the tran sformation of matter in these two communities and t he central social, historical, or ecological relations recruited in these processes.

## Research context and authors’ backgrounds

Our research contexts are situated within two Afro-Brazilian cultural/territorial settings-the quilombola communities of Maruanum (Northern Brazil) and Ausente (Southeastern Brazil). While the term quilombola was initially used solely in relation to escaped slaves ’ dwelling places, recent conceptualizations are br oader and include a range of communities based on their space, territoriality, and culture. Such modern interpre tations of quilombola shift the emphasis from its origins in slavery to a  contemporary appreciation of community and cultural identity (Schmitt et al., 2002).

Maruanum gets its name from a local animal and is c omprised of various villages of quilombola origin. Over the last three decades, pottery production – an activity exclusively driven by women and deeply rooted in the community’s heritage – has evolved into the community’s primary source of income. Elderly women, known as louceiras (pottery makers, in English) are at the heart of this activity, providing for their families and ensuring the preservation of their craft. Maruanum has pique d the interest of researchers keen to understand th e people’s lifestyle and their relationship with the environment.

Ausente is home to around 200 residents in the rura l area near a municipality with strong cultural and touristic appeal. The elderly women of Ausente, oft en referred to as quitandeiras (baked goods makers, in English), have been at the forefront of various ini tiatives related to family agricultural techniques,  seed banks, organic produce sales, and the revitalization and sale of traditional community baked goods (quitandas). While individual team members’ levels of involvement with the communities vary, we all conduct work that centers on and seeks to uplift the cultural kn owledge and practices of non-dominant communities in Brazil and beyond. Joana is an anthropologist with extensive research in traditional knowledge, including that embedded in the cooking and farming practices of Ausente, wh ere she spends most of the year. Through previous r esearch projects, Joana has formed many relationships with the women of Ausente and also supports community is sues outside of her research (e.g., helping the communit y obtain funds). Romaro is a math educator and univ ersity researcher focusing on Ethnomathematics in quilombola communities in Amapá, Brazil. He has worked with the Maruanum community for several years since his doct oral studies, becoming a strong advocate for their pottery and a constant presence within the community. Loren a is an anthropologist with experience working with Amazonian indigenous people in Brazil. Her research interests  lie in ethnology, food anthropology, and the anthropology of technique. Thiago is a cultural his torian with research interests in indigenous histor y and experience as a professor in indigenous higher education. Raquel and Paulo are both learning scientists. Raquel’s research explores emerging technologies in learning  from a culturally situated perspective, while Paul o focuses on STEM and maker education worldwide through a cri tical pedagogy lens. Eliton a nd Valente are both educational researchers who study constructionist education in  Brazil. All authors are Brazilian citizens with deep roots in the country.

## Findings

Here, we focus on the most salient elements recruit ed in pottery-making and oven-making practices within the two quilombola communities. Although our analysis highlights these key features, they represent only a fraction of the complex web of relationships that permeate making in these communities. Non-human participation in pottery-making The making of clay objects in the Maruanum community is intricately tied to an ancestral connection with a nonhuman entity known as the “Mãe do Barro” (Clay Moth er). This entity is integral to pottery making in t he community, with her participation eviden t in every identified operation. Locating the clay,  determining the availability and purity of clay in the land, as wel l as the ultimate quality and durability of the cla y pieces, are directly linked to Mãe do Barro’s assistance. Previ ous successful and unsuccessful experiences in the making of pottery have led the community to amass a wealth of  knowledge and highly technical empirical procedure s that are seamlessly encoded in an ethical conduct toward s Mãe do Barro and the landscape, establishing a ra tionale that connects the landscape, humans, and non-human beings. Following this ethical conduct and ra tionale is extremely important since it directly influences th e technique’s effectiveness. Relations with a range  of “non-human agents” (from worms to butterflies to spirits,) including with a “mother” or “owner” of clay, who set ethical and moral rules for the effectiveness of techniques  and even for the well-being of the community, is extensively documented among Indigenous Amazonian peoples (Bela unde, 2017). In various communities, securing the cooperation of – and avoiding nuisance to – non-human agents significantly improves technique by incorporating diverse influences, conditions, and materials (Coupaye, 2009). During initial interviews, the potters explained th at the Mãe do Barro will provide, from a single pit excavated only once per year after the rainfall season, all the clay necessary f or that year’s production, which potters will store in their houses. At least seven days before the extraction, women engage in a safeguarding ritual, including a sexual fast and avoiding loud or harsh speech. Pregnant or menstruating women, sick women,  and those who have lost family members within the past 30 days are excluded  from participation in the extraction process. Women are required to use turbans during t he clay extraction. When discussing reasons behind these rules, Dona Marciana talked about clay contamination (the use of a turban is linked to hair falling in the pit), and this contamination was always referred to as a nuis ance to Mãe do Barro, who is considered the clay pr otector. The prohibition of pregnant and menstruating women,  as well as avoidance of sex in the preceding days in environments “controlled” by non-humans, is also documented in anthropological liter ature as having a direct relation to smell and bodily fluids (Belaunde, 2017). In his study on Awajún potters (Jívaro people, P eru), Belaunde documents that it is agreed upon among the  women that, to obtain good quality clay that allow s for making durable pottery, it is essential to observe a period of safeguarding (p. 188). Here again, logical and ethical rules are tightly interconnected – recruiting the participation of the Mãe do Barro, a voiding her nuisance, is key to the purity of the clay and, ultimately, to the high quality of the community’s pottery. On extraction day, Dona Marciana and her son (who w ill start digging the pit) are the first to enter t he mudflat where the clay will be extracted. Dona Marc iana then offers her prayer to the Mãe do Barro, as king her permission to extract the clay. During interviews w ith Romaro, Dona Marciana recounted that after her prayer, she knew exactly where to excavate the pit. She attributes this knowledge to an intuition gained during her prayer to the Mãe do Barro. All other community members th en enter the mudflat and offer their prayers, each in their own way, asking the Mãe do Barro permission to enter the mudflat. Dona Mariana shared with Romaro that when she is not present during the pit excavation, the eldest of the group going to extract the mud in a given year is the one to offer the initial prayer to the Mãe do Barro. At the end of the process, each potter offers a t ribute to the Mãe do Barro in gratitude (Figures 3 and 4). When a sked if they would be interested in having a machin e or mechanical assistance t hat could extract larger quantities of clay to incr ease production, they unanimously said no, because of how much it would disrupt the “Mãe.”  Dona Marciana elaborated further on how this logic (encoded as ethical conduct) manifests in the clay extraction process, recounting a 2021 incident in which she invited some young men to assist in the extraction (the only step in which men participate). These men  brought beers and speakers with them and, despite replicati ng the same excavation techniques from previou s years, they could not collect the usual quantity of clay. Dona Marciana attributed this shortfall to the men’s mis behavior during the extraction process, which would have ups et the Mãe do Barro. Dona Marciana further explaine d that shortages of clay are not mere coincidences but a clear sign of disapprov al from the Mãe do Barro. In each other operation, from clay preparation to polishing, unsa tisfactory results (e.g., contamination, not achiev ing desired consistency, cracks in the ceramic after firing) we re attributed to some sort of nuisances caused to t he Mãe do Barro. For example, one of the louceiras explained that they do not allow anyone to come to her house when they are producing pottery pieces and recounted an episo de when her pieces cracked after a visit. When aske d why, the louceira mentioned that it is impossible to predict the ener gy people bring to her house and that specific energies might upset the Mãe do Barro.

These narratives reflect the intertwinedness of a n on-human entity and making within the Maruanum community. The potter's relationship with the Mãe do Barro demonstrates the significance of following an ethical conduct towards the Mãe do Barro, asserting that th eir unique advantage in pottery-making is not due merely to skill or resources but to the Mãe do Barro’s participation in the making. Despite these resemblances, it is still crucial to focus on how these quilombolas’ perspectives are situated in relation to their cultural practices, beyond applying US-centric perspectives. While narratives about feminism are n ot very explicitly present in Ausente, we observed that relationships between women are marked by feminine solidarity and greater autonomy concerning men; which aligns with previous observations of other quilombola communities (e.g., Nascimento, 1985). This solidarity is derived not from a confrontation al stance against male counterparts but rather from shared understandings and experiences. In a particu larly telling incident, when asked why only women w ere involved in a community meeting, one woman humorous ly responded, “Men do not even know what collective means,” which was met with laughter and nods of agr eement from the other women. Quilombola women, much like their counterparts in urban peripheral communities, often rely on each other for support, especially in family contexts where the traditional male provider may be  absent. Although many women already had personal o vens, the oven construction in the community’s cultural center was fully embraced as a way to reinforce their collective bonds. In discussing the proposal brought by Joana for building a new oven, the women saw it not only as practical for food preparation but also as a central feature for community engagement. Solidarity among the women during the oven’s construction was also evident. During th e oven construction, the division of tasks among th e women was seamless, resembling a single entity operating without a designated leader. There was a horizontal distribution of construction functions and tasks, self-assigned as needs arose. For instance, when more clay was required, one would volunteer for the task, just like when it was time to prepare lunch. Decisions and strategies for construction, such as selecting a specific type of wood for the structure covering the oven, determining the ideal consistency of the clay, or deciding on the angle of the broken ti les for the oven walls, were all collaboratively ma de. These choices were not only shared but often demonstrated, deliberated, and collectively agreed upon without  any conflict throughout the process. The affectionate and respectful interaction among the three women and the playful tone and laughter that characterized their coexistence during the construction were also notable. Oven-making and the traditional form of baking associated with it are interwoven with the movement of quilombola women throughout Brazil, seeking to assert and main tain their cultural identity and challenge a mainstream historical narrative that has marginaliz ed their contributions. Their solidarity and collec tive effort in constructing the oven reflects a broader narrative of Black women in Brazil actively resisting the erasure of their cultural significance (Moura, 2001) and consciously trying to retain and celebrate their heritage. ## Discussion and significance: Making is not just about making

Pottery and oven-making in the quilombola communities are far from being mere crafts. In Maru anum, pottery making is conducted in a harmonious relationship wi th the environment by recruiting an agentic non-human, the Mãe do Barro, constituting a careful, respectful, and sustainable clay extraction process. Oven-making in Ausente is a collective endeavor among the women, embodying  solidarity and mutual support. The making process is distinguished by its seamless execution and colle ctive approach, with the co nstruction of the oven evolving through the collaboration of the women involved. Ea ch step, from planning to execution, unfolds organi cally, with tasks being naturally distributed among the women and decisions made collectively. Our work suggests that, in these communities, shaping and creating objects is deeply connected to other aspects of livelihood rather than being a mere goal-based activity to “solve a problem.” Building objec ts goes beyond their strict utility. The operations involved in creating these artifacts combine materials, tools, knowledge, and representations with social and ecological relations shaping these making practices. For example, it could be that once-a-year extraction relates to the need to protect the environment or avoid an excessive supply of clay, and the complex rules imposed by the “Mãe do Barro”  act as a way for local women to assert and protect  their roles and leadership in the community and in the ec onomically-important activity of making clay pot s. The unforgiving presence of the “Mãe do Barro” plays a crucial role in maintaining the integrity of the gr oup, their identity, and their particular processes of making the pots. Now let us imagine that we would transpla nt the clay making from the Louceiras to a school makerspace, in an attempt to have “cult urally-meaningful” maker education or even to honor their traditions; reduci ng it to a mere making of pots would miss all the c omplexities and histories of how their making ecosystem and pro cess came to be; the pots themselves are, perhaps, more “objects to bond people with” than to solve a problem about food preparation. Similarly, oven-making in Ausente also demonstrates new elements of a particular maker ecology. Roles are horizontally distributed, and democratic, rathe r than hierarchical, and planning is emergent (echo ing constructionist ideas of epistemological pluralism and “tinkering versus planning,” Turkle & Papert, 1990). This might go against the grain of traditional engineeri ng or computer science practices, in which operatio ns are codified, and team members are assigned fixed roles for maximum efficiency. Considering that the women in the village have built hundreds of ovens, their approach to the process is not only very successful but achieves many additional goals. It promotes group cohesion, asser ts identity, provides a protected space in which me n are not allowed, and reasserts female leadership and protagon ism in an otherwise male-dominated culture. Again, if we imagine an oven-making activity in schools, the mere construction o f the object would not only miss these elements but possibly revert to more standard ways of organizing engineering teams. In mainstream urban communities, technology is seen mainly as a way to solve everyday problems, and materials are seen as mere inanimate matter to be s haped, used, and discarded. School makerspaces refl ect that mindset and are well-stocked with abundant materials and varied machines. While work in such spaces can indeed be engaging, rich, and agentic for youth, it does not expose students to other mak ing paradigms, which could be windows into understanding other cultures and ways of being. Makerspaces need to internalize that making — perhaps more prominently outside mainstream urban c enters and middle-class culture —is rarely just about making. Since the way objects are constructed is embedded with multiple layers of communities’ socio-historical lives, buildi ng such objects could constitute generative activit ies geared towards understanding those layers. Makerspaces, thus, should become spaces not only to build things, invent, and understand STEM content but also to talk holistically about how objects of daily life get made, understand and experience other cultures and worldviews, elevate ways of being that are excluded from mainstream life, and discuss and problematize inequities and environmental impacts. That way, makerspaces co uld transcend their “factory of inventions” paradig m and become environments where multiple cultures and maker practices coexist. This is not to say that the technical or “invention” work in makerspaces should cease to exist, but that educators could creatively combine the technical, cultural and ethnographical aspects of making to create new types of activities, spaces, and explorations. In contrast to the dominant perspectives in maker e ducation, which often emphasize a narrow focus on technological advancement and entrepreneurship appeali ng to specific demographics, the pottery and oven-making practices in the quilombola communities of Maruanum and Ausente provide a vivid  and grounded illustration of the complex cultural values and worldviews that can permeate making.
