# From Talk to Evidence: Using Conversation-based Assessment to Elicit Scientific Reasoning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigated the use of a conversation-based assessment (CBA) on storm formation and prediction to elicit high school students’ scientific reasoning. The task integrates AI-based agents to engage students in interactive dialogue across activities that involved science practices such as data analysis and constructing arguments. Results from a usability study revealed that scores from student-agent conversation s, particularly those focused on causal explanation and data interpretation, were significantly associated with students’ final storm prediction performance while traditional item scores were not. Furthermore, analysis of student-agent dialogues showed that the AI agents supported students in revising and improving their responses. These findings highlight the potential of AI-enabled CBAs to surface evidence of scientific reasoning and support student learning and reasoning.

As STEM education increasingly emphasizes engagement in authentic scientific practices, there is growing recognition that assessments must capture how students apply knowledge to make sense of complex phenomena, rather than simply  what they can recall (Harris et al., 2023). Scientific practices include processes such as analyzing and interpreting data, constructing explanations, and justifying claims with evidence  that unfold dynamically as students reason through problems. Traditional assessment formats often capture only a single snapshot of understanding, making it difficult to capture the development and use of scientific reasoning during task performance.  Conversation-based assessments (CBAs)  offer a promising approach for addressing these challenges by engaging  students in interactive dialogue  with conversational agents  to elicit their thinking and create spaces for them to articulate, reflect on, and refine their ideas in real time (Yildirim-Erbasli & Bulut, 2023; Zapata-Rivera et al., 2023). Rooted in sociocultural theories of learning (Vygotsky, 1978), CBAs  leverage discourse as a mechanism for making thinking visible and supporting sensemaking. Through dialogic interaction, students are prompted to articulate ideas, respond to questions or counterpoints, and refine their reasoning across turns. Prior research in science education has demonstrated that such dialogic engagement candeepen conceptual understanding and support explanation-building and justification  (Gillies, 2020; Li et al., 2023; McNeill & Pimentel, 2010; Nennig et al., 2023).  Furthermore, research has shown that dialogue systems with natural language conversations can support meaningful exchanges with students, prompting elaboration, clarification, and reflection (Baylor & Kim, 2005; Biswas et al., 2010; Ward et al., 2013). Recent advances in AI, particularly large language models (LLMs), have made it increasingly feasible to implement CBAs at scale by improving efficiency in development and enabling conversational agents capable of engaging students in naturalistic dialogue (Forsyth et al., 2024; Zapata-Rivera et al., 2025). Unlike earlier rulebased systems (e.g., Jackson et al., 2018), LLM-powered agents  can support more adaptive and flexible interactions (Zapata-Rivera et al., 2025), enhancing the potential of CBAs to surface meaningful evidence of reasoning processes during complex STEM tasks. In this study, we examine an AI-enabled CBA designed to elicit high school students’ scientific reasoning about storm formation and prediction (namely, “Weather Task”). We focus on how  different forms of dialogic interaction function as evidence of scientific reasoning and how these interactions relate to students’ task performance. Specifically, the study addresses the following research questions:

• RQ1: What is the relationship between scores derived from student –agent conversations and  students’

final storm prediction performance, relative to traditional item scores embedded in the task ?

• RQ2: How do conversational interactions with AI agents support students in revising and refining their

task responses in the Weather task?

## Participants and procedure

Participants were 37 high school students  (grades 9-12th) recruited through convenience sampling.  Participants represented diverse ethnic backgrounds  (White (8), Hispanic or Latino (9), Black (3), Asian (14), two or more races (3)) and had a variety of educational backgrounds (GPA range: 2.7 – 4.3). Thirteen participants identified as female and 24 as male.  Participants completed the Weather Task in a virtual meeting with a facilitator and observer where they interacted with the task while sharing their screen.  Sessions were recorded for analysis. Following task completion, participants responded to a set of usability questions from the facilitator. Only task interaction data are reported here.

Task The Weather Task engages high-school students in a scenario in which they learn about the mechanism of storm formation and collect data from weather stations to predict the likelihood of a storm in the U.S. Southern Region (see task example in Figure 1). The task is aligned with Earth science content as well as science practices from the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS), a U.S.-based framework, such as engaging in argument from evidence and planning and carrying out investigations.  The task sequence is summarized  in Table 1. Task activities were designed using evidence-centered design (ECD) principles (Mislevy et al., 2003) to elicit specific forms of evidence aligned with targeted claims about students’ reasoning. Each activity includes conversation-based prompts with two AI-based conversational agents – a scientist (Dr. Garcia) who acts as a scientifically authoritative figure and a student peer (Art) who is a novice student character. We used an LLM-based approach to program the agent interactions using prompt engineering to support elicitation of student thinking about storm and weather concepts  and guide data collection and analysis to make an evidence-based prediction.  The system also includes non-visible agents that operate in the background to support assessment. A watcher agent coordinates conversational flow and monitors compliance with interaction rules, while a formative assessor analyzes student turns to inform subsequent agent prompts. Evidence identified by the formative assessor is aggregated by a summative assessor to log student discourse in relation to NGSS-aligned performance expectations (Hou et al., 2025).

Traditional items were included as a comparison baseline to contextualize the extent to which conversational evidence captures aspects of scientific reasoning beyond what is reflected in conventional assessment formats. Students’ final storm predictions were scored on an ordinal scale (0–3) that reflected both the accuracy of the prediction and the quality of the accompanying explanation, with greater weight placed on justification. The three task conversations  were scored using rubrics aligned to the evidence elicited in each interaction (Conversation 1: explanation of  storm formation mechanisms, score range 0-3; Conversation 2: weather station selection accuracy and justification, score range 0-5; Conversation 3: interpretation of weather station data  to support prediction, score range 0-3). Two raters scored all constructed response items and conversations and interrater agreement was satisfactory across scores (quadratic weighted kappa  k =.74 for final prediction explanation; conversation score k range.82 to.94). Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

To address RQ2, we focused on Conversation 2 which afforded the best opportunity to examine whether the AI agents led students to revise or refine their responses.  For each student, initial and final station selections were compared to determine whether revisions occurred and whether changes improved, worsened, or did not alter response quality. We further conducted a qualitative review of student-agent dialogues to examine the mechanisms underlying the change, including what the agents may have done to influence the change. RQ1: What is the relationship between scores derived from student–agent conversations and students’ final storm prediction performance, relative to traditional item scores embedded in the task?

Spearman rank-order correlations were co nducted to examine the rel ationship between conversation scores and the storm prediction score and the traditional item scores and the storm prediction score. Conversation 1 score (ρ =. 35, p =.0 32) and Conversation 3 score (ρ  =.49, p =.0 02) showed moderate, positive correlations with prediction score. The combined conversation score (sum of all conversation scores)  also showed a moderate, positive correlation with storm prediction score  (ρ =.44, p =.006).  In contrast, scores on traditional items assessing related content knowledge (e.g., concept map, weather station selection accuracy, and TTI calculation) were not significantly associated with prediction performance (all p’s >.05). Given the significant correlations between C onversation 1 and 3 scores and the prediction score, we further examined these conversations to explore  what they elicited that may support positive task outcomes. We found that Conversation 1, focused on eliciting information from students about the mechanisms underlying storm formation, particularly supported students in constructing multi-step causal explanations  across turns. These explanations can help build a coherent mental model  of the storm formation process  which could improve accuracy as they provide reasoning  to justify predictions. Conversation 3, which focused on supporting weather station data analysis and interpretation, particularly supported students in revising or clarifying their ideas after uncertainty or agent feedback. This can help students replace incorrect or incomplete reasoning with scientifically accurate explanations that may better support later predictions. 1309 RQ2: How do conversational interactions with AI agents support students in revising and refining their task responses in the Weather task?

In the weather station selection activity  (shown in Figure 1), students shared their initial choices with Dr. Garcia (the expert, scientist AI agent), then engaged in a discussion with Dr. Garcia and Art (peer agent) about their choices and finally had the opportunity to revise  and finalize their selections of the weather stations.  Among 37 students, 24 made revisions that improved their initial selections, while 10 chose to keep their initial selections. Only three students made changes that resulted in less accurate responses. Qualitative review of student– agent interactions revealed several recurring features associated with productive revision. Agent prompts frequently asked students to articulate their reasoning for initial selection s and often drew attention to gaps or inconsistencies in their explanations (e.g., Dr. Garcia: “Can you explain why you think Station E is a good pick? Specifically, how do you think its location will help in capturing useful data for predicting the storm near the prediction point?”). In many cases, the agents challenged unsupported selections by prompting students to consider additional criteria (e.g., Dr. Garcia: “Interesting choice! What makes you think that proximity alone is the best indicator for predicting the storm? Could there be other factors at play?”). Other prompts encouraged students to integrate information across multiple weather stations rather than relying on a single station. These interactional moves often led students to reconsider their initial assumptions and refine their selections accordingly. In cases where student changes resulted in less accurate responses, conversations suggested that students  were either confused  or persisted in incorrect reasoning despite feedback  (e.g., Dr. Garcia: “Can you explain how the data from Station F, considering its position relative to the storm's path, would effectively help in predicting the thunderstorm at the prediction point?”  Student: “I am not sure”). For cases in which there was no change in weather station selections, students either already had the correct selections in their first attempt (5 instances) or the agent appeared to struggle when students provided unexpected responses. This study examined  how an AI-enabled CBA can elicit and support high school students’ scientific reasoning during task engagement. Results from RQ1  showed that student-agent conversations focused on explanationbuilding (Conversation 1) and iterative refinement for data interpretation (Conversation 3)  were more closely associated with successful task performance than traditional item responses, providing initial evidence that dialogic exchanges with AI agents can capture meaningful aspects of scientific reasoning aligned with knowledgein-use. RQ2 further demonstrated the potential of formative conversational interactions, as interactions often supported students in revising and refining their task responses, particularly by prompting them to articulate their reasoning, reconsider selection criteria, and integrate multiple sources of evidence. This process of revising ideas not only supported students in refining incorrect or incomplete ideas but also fostered deep engagement with scientific practices such as evidence-based argumentation and data analysis. These results reinforce sociocultural theories of learning which emphasize the centrality of discourse in making thinking visible and supporting conceptual understanding (Mercer & Howe, 2012; Mortimer & Scott, 2003; Vygotsky, 1978). Prior research has demonstrated that dialogic interaction, whether with peers, teachers, or artificial agents, candeepen conceptual understanding, foster metacognitive reflection, and support the development of scientific practices (Gillies, 2020; McNeill & Pimentel, 2010; Rav enscroft, 2007; Zapata-Rivera et al., 2023). The current study extends this literature by demonstrating that specific dialogue features, specifically explanation-building and refinement-oriented interactions, can play a complementary role in facilitating understanding and successful performance. These findings highlight the dual role of CBA as both an evaluative and instructional tool, as it can not only elicit evidence of student thinking for assessment purposes, but also actively support students in refining their ideas through adaptive dialogue. This has important implications for formative assessment design, as CBAs can move beyond static measurement to capture how  students reason, communicate, and revise their ideas in response to feedback. The findings also point to the potential of CBAs in supporting equity and engagement in assessment. By offering multiple opportunities for students to articulate, clarify and justify their ideas, conversational agents can potentially help reduce barriers associated with traditional test formats. Several limitations should be noted. The study involved a relatively small sample and focused on immediate task performance, limiting generalizability and inferences about longer-term learning outcomes. In addition, the conversational agents operated under  design constraints that shaped interaction patterns, underscoring the need for further research on agent adaptivity and robustness. Future work should examine how CBAs function across diverse contexts and explore how conversational evidence relates to broader constructs such as engagement, motivation, and self-regulation (e.g., Gladstone et al., 2025). Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates the promise of AI-enabled CBAs for capturing and supporting scientific reasoning through dialogic interaction.
