# Supporting Dental Posture Learning through VR Scaffolding

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how virtual reality (VR) technology can support embodied learning in professional education, focusing on posture training in dental education. We developed a VR-based training system that integrates motion tracking technology with real-time postural feedback based on rapid upper limb assessment (RULA) criteria. In an experimental study with 36 dental students, we examined both system usability and effectiveness in improving ergonomic posture. The results revealed high system usability scores with no significant differences between experimental and control groups, and low perceived workload. For effectiveness, the experimental group showed significant improvement with feedback and maintained this improvement after feedback removal. This study demonstrates the potential of VR environments to support embodied learning through immediate feedback, suggesting implications for how VR technology can bridge the gap between knowledge and physical practice in professional education.

## Introduction

Maintaining proper posture during clinical procedur es is fundamental to both practitioner health and p atient care quality in dentistry. Poor posture not only negativ ely impacts practitioners' long-term health but also affects the precision and efficiency of dental procedures (Cerv era-Espert, 2018). Studies report that over 58% of dent al practitioners experience work-related musculoskeletal disorders annually (Kumar e t al., 2020), with similar concerns emerging among dental students during thei r training (Ng  et al., 2016; Movahhed et al., 2013). The nature of dental work, requiring precise operations  within a confined visual field for extended period s, makes practitioners particularly susceptible to accumulated muscle fatigue and tension. This challenge represents a complex learning proble m that goes beyond simple knowledge acquisition. While dental students receive extensive education a bout proper posture, including the use of magnifyin g loupes and surgical microscopes to maintain better p ositioning, they often struggle to translate this k nowledge into practice (Sasso Garcia et al., 2015; Pîrvu et al., 2014). McLaren and Parrott (2018) found that almost no students maintained proper posture during clinical practice,  particularly struggling with wrist, neck, and trunk positions despite understanding correct ergonomic principles.  Traditional educational approaches have shown limi ted success in providing appropriate feedback and supporting the development of proper postural habits. Virtual Reality (VR) technology offers promising op portunities to address these challenges in professional skill development. VR environments can  provide immediate, personalized feedback while maintaining authentic practice contexts (Chen, 2009). This tech nology enables repeated, safe, and resource-efficient practice, supporting skill transfer to real-world scenarios and meaningful learning experiences (Hamilton et al., 2021). A recent systematic review has shown that VR-based training with real-time feedback can effectively support students' knowledge and practical skills in dental education (Koolivand et al., 2024). While VR has been increasingly adopted in medical and dental education (Huang et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2022), existing applications primarily focus on procedural skills rather than su pporting proper posture development, leaving a crit ical aspect of professional competency development underexplored.

This study investigates how VR-based training with real-time postural feedback can support dental students in developing proper clinical postures. We  integrate the Rapid Upper Limb Assessment (RULA) framework with motion-tracking technology to create a nov el learning environment that provides immediate feedback while maintaining authentic task conditions. Our research addresses two key questions as below: RQ1: How do dental students perceive the usability of a VR-based system for postural training? RQ2: How effective is a VR-based postural training system with real-time feedback in improving ergonomic posture among dental students during scaling procedures?

## Theoretical framework

Professional expertise in dentistry involves comple x embodied knowledge that goes beyond intellectual understanding. Recent work in embodied learning the ory emphasizes how professional skills are develope d through the dynamic interaction between perception,  action, and environmental feedback (Skulmowski & R ey, 2018). Studies in medical education particularly highlight how bodily awareness and physical practice are crucial for developing professional competence (Kelly et al., 2019). While traditional approaches emphasized knowledge transmission and mental representations of correct posture, they often failed to support its consisten t application in practice (Sasso Garcia et al., 2015).

The role of feedback and scaffolding is crucial in supporting this embodied learning process. Bagher

(2020) demonstrates how embodied affordances in VR can enhance learning through carefully structured support

and bodily engagement. Their research suggests  that when technological scaffolding is properly int egrated with authentic practice contexts, it can effectively support embodied learning while maintaining cognitive efficiency. Technology-enhanced learning environments, particularly VR, ca n offer opportunities to integrate embodied learning with appropriate feedback and sca ffolding (Koolivand et al., 2024). Studies have shown that VR technologies, when embedded within well-designed instructional contexts, can support the de velopment of procedural skills and bodily awareness by enabling immersive, interactive, and learner-centered learning environments (Mao et al., 2021).

The technology's ability to provide multimodal feed back while maintaining authentic practice contexts makes it particularly suited for professional educa tion. Recent studies have shown that VR-based posture estimation can accurately track and provide real-time feedback on practitioners' movements during se ated tasks (Homayounpour et al., 2021). Based on these perspectives, we developed a VR-based dental training system that provides real-time postural feedback and investigated its effecti veness in supporting students' development of proper clinical postures.

## Methods

VR-based postural training system The VR-based system was developed to support effective posture training dur ing dental procedures. It was designed to provide immediate, meaningful feedback on postural alignment without distracting participants from the clinical task. Participants wore a Meta Quest 3  Head-Mounted Display (HMD) and eight motion tracking sensors (EBIMU 24GV52) attached to the chest, abdom en, shoulders, elbows, and wrists.  An additional sensor was placed on a physical mannequin head, which was spatially aligned with a virtual patient model in t he VR environment. The system recorded posture data at 25 Hz, enabling  real-time movement analysis.  The scaling procedure was selected for its standard positioning requirements and prevalence in dental practice, making it ideal for postural assessment.

Based on continuous RULA assessment, the system del ivered calibrated visual and auditory feedback. RULA scores were calculated based on joint angles c aptured by motion sensors, using threshold values a dapted and customized from Blume et al. (2021). Each body region (e.g., wrists, arms, neck, trunk) was assign ed a risk score based on posture deviation. The final score r anged from 1 (low risk) to 7 (high risk), indicatin g overall ergonomic strain. Visual cues were presented as color-coded indicators within the user’s peripheral vision: green for RULA scores of 4 or lower, yellow for scores of 5, and red for scores of 6 or higher (Figure 1). Auditory alerts were triggered when suboptimal postures were sustained: a warning tone was played three times if RULA scores of 5 or 6 persisted for over 3 seconds, and continu ous tones were played at one-second intervals if a score of 7 was maintained for more than 2 seconds.

## Experimental Design and Data Collection

To evaluate both the usability and effectiveness of the system, an experiment was conducted with dental students using a repeated-measures design. An a priori power analysis was con ducted using G*Power 3.1. Assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25), α =.05, and power =.80, the required sample size for a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) within-between interaction was calculated to be 28 partici pants (14 per group). A total of 36 dental students (55.26% female; M = 25.37 years,  SD = 2.20) from three dental schools were recruite d and randomly assigned to either the experimental group (n = 18) or control group (n = 18). The experimental protocol consisted of three VR-based scaling sessions. Both groups completed the first and third sessions without feedback, while only the  experimental group received real-time postural feedback during the second session. Each session lasted approximately 50 minutes, including a 10-minute orientation (study overview and system training), a 30-minute main task period, and a 10-minute post-session evaluation. Postural risk was assessed using the RULA, which ge nerates scores from 1 to 7 based on upper limb, trunk, and neck positions. Scores were automatically calculated in real time using motion tracking data. Usability was measured using a 10-item questionnaire adapted from Barkokebas and Li (2023)  with items about  realism and ease of use of the VR environment, rated on a 7-point Likert scale. Perceived workload was measured  using the NASA-TLX, which includes six subscales (mental, physical, and temporal demand, performance, effort, and frustration), also rated on a 7-point scale.

For data analysis, RULA scores were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA to examine within-and between-group effects over time. Post-hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction were con ducted when significant effects were found. Usability and perce ived workload scores were compared between groups using independent t-tests.

## Discussion

This study contributes to our understanding of how VR technology can support embodied learning in professional education through two key aspects: the design principles for VR-based posture training systems and their impact on developing embodied professional knowledge.

Regarding VR simulation design, our findings demons trate how technology can scaffold bodily awareness through carefully calibrated feedback. Wh ile traditional VR applications in medical educatio n have focused primarily on procedural skills, our system shows how multimodal feedback can support postural awareness without increasing perceived workload. The results also demonstrate ho w VR-based feedback can facilitate the development of proper posture in den tal procedures. The experimental group's maintained improvement after feedback removal suggests the dev elopment of sustained internal awareness rath er than mere temporary compliance (Skulmowski & Rey, 2018).

The study's limitations include its short duration and the system's multiple sensor requirements. Futu re research should explore longitudinal effects and investigate ways to simplify tracking while maintaining accuracy. These findings suggest new directions for technolog y-enhanced professional education, particularly in do mains requiring sophisticated bodily awareness and motor control. While the study was powered for the repeated-measures ANOVA, the sample size may not have been s ufficient for between-group comparisons using independent t-tests; therefore, results from these analyses should be interpreted with caution.

## Conclusion

This study demonstrates how VR technology can be ef fectively designed to support embodied learning in professional education  with real-time feedback with authentic practice contexts. Our  findings offers two key contributions to learning sciences research.

First, we provide empirical evidence for how techno logical scaffolding can support the development of embodied professional knowledge. The improved perfo rmance that persisted even after feedback was remov ed suggests the potential of well-designed VR environments to support meaningful learning. Second, our progressive feedback design offers a model for how VR technolog y can support embodied learning without increasing perceived workload. These contributions suggest new directions for both  research and practice in technology-enhanced professional education, particularly in domains where physical practice and bodily awareness are crucial components of expertise development.
