# Generative Inquiry: Creativity Assessments of Youth Epistemic Agency and in a Computational Data Science Workshop

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Computational data science (CDS) is relevant in areas of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) for reasons related to field innovation and public literacy. Much is known about pre-college learning with guided CDS in traditional subjects, and comparatively less about how to design and identify generative moments in open inquiry, which are inherent and valued in the field. We examine two illustrative cases of high school-aged youth’s final projects to assess strategies used when enacting a self-directed inquiry project. We report how creativity indicators show up in activities emphasizing learner agency. Our findings represent an initial effort to use creativity to assess process outcomes in the context of open inquiry. We recommend creativity-based assessments of inquiry-driven by generative acts to promote knowledge construction and learning. Insights are discussed in terms of how they extend ideas about assessing constructs such as epistemic agency in open-ended learning.

## Introduction

Computational data science (CDS) forms the bedrock for advancement in emerging computing areas such as artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML), where innovation can be field-defining (Education & Human Resources, 2020). This recognition motivates a growing body of relevant literature about how CDS might support technical and public literacies (Tang & Wil liams, 2019). Most existing efforts focus on using CDS to support traditional academic goals, such as achievi ng learning objectives in mathem atics, life sciences, social sciences, and humanities. In contrast, fewer studie s adopt more generative approaches that iteratively  examine and emphasize open inquiry activities to foster con ceptual understanding, practical skills, sociopolit ical awareness, and innovation in these rapidly evolving fields (Jiang, Lee, & Rosenberg, 2022). This is relevant in middle and high school education (grades 6-12) because youth in these age groups use learning experiences to inform their future career interests and pursuits. Thus, activities that offer opportunities to participate authentically and generatively represent a valuable path toward realizing priorities for the field and the public. Addressing these issues is elusive in pre-college learning environments for two reasons. First, authentic open inquiry in CDS requires an open library or repository of materially flexible data to develop and pursue novel research questions and pursuits. Second, few existing educational frameworks account for learning processes and outcomes in CDS contexts where open inquiry practices vary by learners and their diverse epistemological styles (Turkle & Papert, 1990). Social media platforms suc h as X/Twitter and YouTube, with their publicly acc essible Application Programming Interfaces (APIs), have lon g been used by industry and researchers to carry ou t sophisticated inquiries (Bazzaz et. al., 2021). Efforts to leverage these access points for pre-college learning have shown early promise (Barany et al., 2023; Walker et al., 2023) in part because social media is a relevant space for youth (Acquah et al., 2024; Barany et al., 2024; Wa lker, Slater & Kafai, 2019) who tend to use such pl atforms regularly. Prior works use a sandbox metaphor to de scribe CDS as an open inquiry, where learners have agency to define research questions and make decisions abo ut data and st rategies for collection, analysis, and representation—all of which position learners as epistemic agents,  cognitive authorities, and constructors of knowledge. These developments create opportunities to begin assessing agency's generative nature in th e CDS context. Starting here may inform the development of education frameworks to support epistemic agency (in terms of process and products) in open inquiry learning designs and practices. This initial study examines projects from seven hig h school-aged youth who participated in a ten-day online CDS learning workshop using a sandbox approa ch. The curriculum was designed to encourage learne r agency (by giving youth access to social media-based data repositories) and ownership in both the proc ess and final products achieved (by giving youth authority over epistemic decision-making). Using deductive qualitative analysis of video recording transcripts of final pr oject presentations and presentati on slides (to triangulate and confirm), we address the following research question: How did learner project narratives and presentations reflect enacted creativity in their epistemic practice? Tra nscripts were a vital data source because they repr esent firsthand narratives of participant rationales for strategies taken to carry out an inquiry. We reasoned that while many choices participants made could be construed as agentic, only a few had generative and original qualities that align with creativity, perhaps more consistent with what might support learner interest and sustained engagement. Our observations in two illustrative cases presented he re suggest that the extent to which learners could enact agency that is generative could be u nderstood and operationalized in terms of creativit y. These two cases provide initial insights sufficient to illustrate and describe how creativity shows up in the context of CDS. We discu ss these findings in relation to the literature and our work ing hypothesis that learning in open inquiry environments that encourage agency can be understood in terms of how much creativity a learner can enact.

## Background

Computing education, epistemic agency, and creativity Computing education has made significant inroads in  supporting authentic and expressive pre-college learning activities, wherein learners use computational tool s and resources to inquire and construct knowledge (Odden, Silva & Malthe-Soreenssen, 2021). This can be attributed, in part, to the development of data mining approaches and related technologies that reduce the entry thre shold needed for meaningful participation that is b oth flexible and sophisticated (Resnick & Silverman, 2005). Data  mining is the process of discovering patterns, tre nds, and useful information from large datasets using statistical analysis, machine learning, and computational  techniques to analyze data and extract insights that may not be immediately apparent (Romero & Ventura, 2013). As the field expands, learning to mine data can empower pre-college learners to engage with the skills and conc epts driving innovation (King & Satyanarayana, 2013), including in AI and ML, where these skills are essential. Data mining provides a concrete avenue for exploring open inqui ry, where learners can autonomously identify patter ns, generate insights, and make decisions using real-world data. This aligns with the growing importance of equipping youth with computational thinking and data literacy  for academic and societal needs. Tools used in data m ining include a diversity of programming languages (e.g.,  from block-based to text-based), digital tools (e.g., AppInventor, NetLogo, etc.), and physical computing electronics (e.g., Microbits, LilyPad Arduinos). These have not only made computational inquiry more accessible  but also made it possible for learners to pursue p ersonally relevant and complex inquiries. While this progress  has provided foundational examples for designing a nd supporting learning experiences suited for the 21st  century, we know comparatively less about data sci enceadjacent fields like CDS, where open inquiry is clo sely tied to epistemic practice. We also know littl e about identifying when learners are novelly enacting agency in learning, and there are currently no unifying frameworks in computing for operationalizing these moments.

Epistemic agency in education can be understood as the myriad ways learners form and enact knowledge and practices in a given community (Scardamalia & B ereiter, 1991). This includes the ability to define  or direct epistemological goals and make decisions about the knowledge of processes taken up to carry out or engage with those priorities (Damşa et al., 2014; Rosenberg & Jones, 2022). From an equity perspective, epistemic agency broadens pluralistic conceptualizations of authentic participation in knowledge construction (see Turkle & Papert,

1990) to include learners as legitimate actors in the process of mobilizing resources and configuring systems that

contribute to knowledge production (Miller et al., 2018; Varelas, Tucker-Raymond & Richards, 2015). A growing body of literature examines how epistemic agency is  negotiated between learners and teachers (Ko and K rist,

2019) about education priorities (Miller et al., 20 18). This represents a shift away from science educ ation

instantiations that privilege scie nce as an accumulation of knowledge, theories, or l ogic and toward conceptualizations emphasizing knowledge as a set of generative practices shaped by social and cultural contexts (Stroupe, Moon & Michaels, 2019). In academic disci plines (Odden et al., 2021), learning is situated (Brown, Collins & Duguid, 1989), and activities support enc ulturation into practice communities, with their va lue determined by their alignment with authentic scienc e inquiries. Odden and colleagues (2021) helped to operationalize and assess epistemic agency in computational inquiry by examining how college student ownership manifested at different inquiry stages. Despite thi s progress, further research is needed to understand how epistemic agency can be generative during investiga tions. In the next section, we describe the literatu re on creativity, how this theoretical lens provides relevant vantage points for understanding epistemic agency, and how it is can be enacted novelly in computing education and adjacent fields. Creativity is the capacity to generate novel, valua ble, and appropriate products (e.g., ideas, solutio ns, patterns) beyond conventionality (Sternberg & Kaufman, 2010). Creativity researchers have used proxies such as divergent thinking to operationalize creative think ing. Guilford (1956) operationalized creativity as divergent thinking with four sub-facets: fluency (the ability to produce many relevant ideas), flexibility (the ability to come up with relevant ideas from different categories or  themes), originality (the ability to produce statistically rare ideas), and elaboration (the ability to implement an idea in detail and hig h quality). Such multi-faceted models and theories can help researchers develop accurate creativity process assessments and target creativity sub-facets to enhance creativity (e.g., Rahimi, 2020; Rahimi e t al., 2023; Rahimi & Shute, 2021). In this study, we also leverage the creativity assessment of a product that emerged from Rahimi et al., 2023 in their eval uation of a computationally rich learning environment to include surprise, complexity, and effort as three additional elements of creativity. Moreover, Rhodes (1961) introduced a  four-P model of creativity, suggesting that creativity c an be understood relative to the (1) person (e.g., person ality, intellect, habits, and traits), (2) process (e.g., perception, learning, thinking, and communication), (3) press (i.e., the environment that one lives in or interact s with), and

(4) product (e.g., a communicated thought to others in words, paint, clay, metal, stone, fabric, or other materials).

In this study, we focus on the process and the prod uct as represented in f irst-hand participant accounts and their final projects.

Scholars have also considered the potential relatio nship between agency and creativity —including the possibility that agency might facilitate or even encourage learner cr eativity or vice versa (Karwowski, Zielińska, & Jankowska, 2022). This can be understood through Self-Determination Theory (SDT; Ryan & Deci, 2002), which accounts for how much autonomy can lead to en hanced internal motivation (i.e., doing something f or the sake of the task rather than for external reasons), creativity, and engagement to facilitate flow (Csikszentmihalyi, 1997). During flow, learners may lose sense of time, res ulting in an enjoyable experience that can enhance learning. In this way, creativity and epistemic agency may positively influence each other. In computing education, creativity is often used to  examine how learning designs might empower learners to personalize their products rather than stances that examine how creativity might be a generative means of enacting agency in the learning process (Bu echley et al., 2008). We take up creativity to also represent an epistemic characteristic (or virtue as described by  Kieran, 2018) that candefine the extent to which scientific practice can produce knowledge in ways that are gen erative in meaning, utility, or context (Sanchez-Dorado, 2020). In other words, creativity is a lens that he lps understand how epistemic computing processes mi ght be functional and generative. This vantage point allows us to discern the potential fo r creativity might drive agency in a particular learning environment design (e.g., open inquiry or sandbox learning environments). Thi s conceptualization is the starting point in our effo rt to assess what  it looks like to be agentic in CDS and how learning designs might support enactments considere d generative. Thus, our examination of two vignette s seeks to illustrate the potential relation between learne r agency in a CDS activity and how learners can be creative in learning and while enacting inquiry (i.e., the ways epistemic agency might be understood as a creative endeavor).

## Context and participants

This study took place during a 2-week (Monday-Friday), 4-hour-a-day, online workshop using Zoom and Google Colaboratory. The workshop used a modified 17-module “Coding Like a Data Miner” pilot curriculum (Barany et al., 2023; Walker et al., 2023a; 2023b). Participants learned about data science, statisti cs, and computational data mining during the first week. Inquiries were s caffolded, e.g., they could modify existing inquiry  examples and codes provided by instructors. During the second week, the inquiry was fully open. This open inquiry framing encouraged participants to take ownership and agency in their projects. This agency was epistemic because it was couched in computational data mining phases that fa cilitate inquiry. To set this up, participants were  given instructions to identify their research questions, devise their own data col lection and analysis strategies, and generate visualizations using productive means. On the last day, the participants were asked to delive r a 10–20minute final presentation about their project, including details about the  processes and strategies they chose to carry out their work. Participants were instructed to introduce and justify the topic they were explor ing and then detail their processes for data collection, proces sing, statistical analysis, and visualizations of f indings. Participants were then instructed to, when possible, annotate their methods and strategies in the slid e portion of their presentation. This included annotations of code and process drafts. Eight youths, ages 11-17, registered and subsequently participated in the publicly available workshop. Participants identified as three Asiandescent, three African American/Black, one Latinx, and one Caucasian/White. Four participants self-identified as male, and four as female. Participants had varying experiences with programming and data science, from very experienced to no experience in coding or carrying out statistics. Th e data included in our analysis comprised seven par ticipants because one did not participate in the final presentation due to technical issues. Data sources and analysis Data sources included transcripts from video record ings of participants' final presentation narratives  and slides (Google or PowerPoint) of their final projects (use d to triangulate with narratives). Transcripts of youth presentations gave insights into aspects of their s elf-described creative processes, while slides were ass essed as youths’ summative creative products to confirm and validate what the youth explained. While student preparation and work time recordings were available on Zoom, these moments primarily consisted of quiet, off-screen focused work and were not included in the analysis. The vid eo recordings ranged from 9:13 to 19:18 minutes. Vi deo recordings were transcribed verbatim using Otter.ai  and were edited as needed when transcription error s arose. Pseudonyms were assigned to all participants in the  workshop. Transcriptions and final presentations w ere organized in a table for qualitative coding —analysis of transcripts involved primarily deductiv e approaches (Ravitch & Carl, 2019) wherein authors applied crea tivity codes derived from published creativity assessment in Rahimi et al., 2023. Specifically, author one coded  transcripts using creativity facets: complexity, e ffort, elaboration, flexibility, fluency, originality, and surprise. Author one also noted the features of transcript text that warranted each code application (e.g., aspects of p articipant explanations that could be construed as complex in the context of CDS). Author one applied codes indep endently and then met to discuss observations with authors two, three, and four. When interpretive consensus c ould not immediately be established, all authors de liberated until a consensus (i.e., 100% interrater reliabilit y) was reached (Ravitch & Carl, 2019). Two particip ants were purposefully sampled and presented in the findings to, consistent with the goal of this study, illustrate initially the quality and scope of how we observed creativity in the context of open inquiry-based CDS.

## Findings

Acts of creativity signal epistemic agency in computational data science Our analysis suggests that acts of creativity serve d as reasonable indicators of epistemic agency in C DS because they signal instances of when and how learners were  generative rather than replicate a strategy develo ped by others, which could also be construed as agentic (b ut not generative). This included indicators such a s when participants stated they deviated from guided examples provided in the first week and how these deviations could be characterized by features of the creativity cons truct we operationalized. We observed seven creativ ity facets across projects. The facets observed included creativity features like complexity (the various ways they interpreted data or offered nuanced interpretations), effort (instances when participants stated spending considerable time on an approach), elaboration (when a participant's sta ted data processing strategies required considerati on of data structure), and flexibility (when participants iden tified multiple strategies for a strategy). We also  observed instances of fluency (when participants provided multiple rationales or strategies for a pursuit), originality (when a participant stated having to devise an inquiry or plandifferent than what had been taught in the workshop), and surprise (moments when the participants revealed so mething about their project or finding that is unex pected). Epistemic agency, creativity, and the relationship between the two This research is motivated by a need to design and identify educational experiences that situate learn ers as knowledge producers (Damşa et al., 2014) rather than participants in t he productions or inquiries of others. We address this need in computing education, where ach ieving public literacy (Tang & Williams, 2019) and participation equity (Stroupe, Moon & Michaels, 201 9) are persistently elusive. While there have been steady inroads to address these issues by expanding access to fields such as CDS (Jiang, Lee, Rosenberg, 2022), there is a dearth in the literature on how to enact these ar eas in ways that support and sustain generative mom ents in epistemic agency (Odden, Silva & Malthe-Soreenssen, 2023)—which in turn can encourage learner engagement, interest, and motivation. Consistent with the central aim  of this exploratory study, we address these issues  by identifying where we observed youth describe action s that can be understood as acts of creativity (Ste rnberg & Kaufman, 2010) when participating in an  open inquiry CDS workshop. We highlight two illustr ative case examples of Alex and Janet because the explanations  provided about their inquiry processes revealed cr eativity facets (Guilford, 1956). This is remarkable because  it suggests creativity is a plausible vantage poin t for identifying when learners engage with agentic and g enerative processes. We observed this specifically in how participants described enacting their projects, whi ch indicated their originality and fluency in flexi ble, complex, elaborate, and sometimes surprising ways. In other words, we found that creative acts in an epistemic inquiry can indicate generative forms of agency since learners can make decisions consistent with their epistemological styles (Resnick & Silverman, 2005), pri orities, and needs. We contribute to the literature  on epistemic agency and creativity in two ways. First, we provide starter i nsights about epistemic practices that can serve as  creativity indicators at various stages of open inquiry in CDS. This builds on Odden, Silva & Malthe-Soreenssen’s (2023) work to include computational data mining and opera tionalizing creativity to nuance how we understand the merits of inquiry practice that is generative. Seco ndly, we provide initial insights into how we might  broaden conceptualizations of what is considered mastery in  an academic discipline or community of practice to  include indicators beyond instantiations that emphasize cre ativity in what a learner might produce and include  practices or behaviors a learner might enact.

Considering the relationship between epistemic agen cy and creativity, our findings showed a close connection based on participants’ presentations of their data mining processes as reflected in their f inal product. Epistemic agency, the capacity for individuals to act independently and make choices and decisions (Odden, Silva & Malthe-Soreenssen, 2023; Rosenberg & Jones, 2022), plays a pivotal role in fostering creativity (Rhodes, 1961; Sternberg & Kaufman, 2010). When participants feel a sense of agency, they are empowered to explore new ideas, take risks, and experiment with different approaches (Ryan & Deci, 2002). This autonomy allows for a more fluid and open creative process, as rigid structures or e xternal limitations do not constrain participants. Mor eover, agency provides the freedom to engage with one’s un ique perspective, interest, and experiences which m ay lead to novel and imaginative solutions (Damşa et al., 2014; Kieran, 2018). Creativity, in turn, can enhance a sense of agency by reinforcing the belief that one’s ideas a nd actions have the potential to make a meaningful impact (Sternberg & Kaufman, 2010). This reciprocal relati onship between agency and creativity highlights the importance of fostering environments that encourage  individual autonomy (Ryan & Deci, 2002), as it lay s the groundwork for the emergence of innovative and original ideas. The nature of the relationship between epistemic agency and creativity needs to be further examined.  However, based on these initial findings in our st udy, we postulate that epistemic agency may lead to creativity and, vice versa, creativity can be a driving force for enacting agency. To make creative products, one has to go th rough a process (Rhodes, 1961) that is usually agen tic. We do not think these two constructs are isolated, one-time episodes during a learning experience. Instead, as mentioned earlier, creativity and agency might have  a cyclical relationship wherein one amplifies or i nvites the other, leading to an ongoing engagement (flow; Csikszentmihalyi, 1997) with internal motivation (SDT; Ryan & Deci, 2002). More work is needed to explore the relationship between agency and creativity, examining momentto-moment processes. For instance, we could gather data from learners’ interactions with the learning environment to reveal the process they went through from a more systematic point of view. This may include direct observation across the workshop experience to assess moments of agency and creativity iterations that may not have appeared in final presentations. Future work along these initial insights might also include studies to validate the relationship between the epistemic agency and creativity and evaluate the efficacy of CDS epistemic practices to shape formal learning, social, and affective outcomes.
