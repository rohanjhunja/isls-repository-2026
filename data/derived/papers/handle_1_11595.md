# “Biological Controls Will Best Your Beasties!”: Visitor Learning at Outreach Events in Novel Informal Learning Spaces

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Concepts central to scientific understanding, including evolution, species distribution, and the effects of invasive species, are often misunderstood by or are unappealing to non-practicing scientists. Such limited interest in science can pose challenges in developing a science-literate populace. Connecting peoples’ existing interests to science topics might alleviate scientific illiteracy. We describe three instances of public outreach which occurred at two comic cons and a brewery in which scientists helped people connect their existing interest in pop culture to science topics. We collected responses from audiences following each event, finding that audiences were somewhat able to connect science with their existing interests. Situating science to align with peoples’ existing interests might be a solution for increasing science literacy.

## Introduction

Science literacy is defined as being able to unders tand scientific practices and how science works, be ing able to evaluate scientific products (e.g. the efficacy of vaccines), and the ability to civically  engage with others about science (National Academy of Sciences, 2016). Some researchers have postulated that science literacy n eeds to be centered on meeting people where they are in terms of their interest and expertise in science (Yager, 1996). An example of meshing science literacy with peoples’ existing interests is through pop culture. Pop culture is th e practices, beliefs, artistic output and objects tha t are prevalent in society, such as video games, te levision shows, or music (Szeman & O’Brien, 2017). Spaces where peoples’ interest in pop culture can be leveraged to increase science interest and literacy is at comic conventio ns (i.e. comic cons) (Lundgren et al., 2024)  and breweries (Barrett et al., 2024). These spaces, when transformed into pop-up museums and staffed by scientists who are dressed in cosplay, have the potential to become sp aces that offer opportunities for informal science learning. These kinds of spaces and the research that happens  in them are important because most research on sci ence learning that happens outside of school takes place in “traditional” out-of-school environments, such as museums, aquaria, or afterschool programs (Falk et al., 2016). We ask: How did visitors connect their interest in pop culture with science concepts they learned about at outreach events in novel informal science learning spaces?

## Theoretical framework and literature review

We have elected to use situational interest as it has been extensively used to study learning and interest in informal science learning environments (Dohn, 2010; Habig & Gupta 2021). Studies that feature situational inter est in informal contexts range from interviews with amateu r astronomers and birders (Jones et al., 2016) to y outh who self-identify as interested in science (Jung & Zimmerman, 2024). Most efforts to involve people in science in informal spaces take place at spaces where people more than likely already have an established interest in science-for example science cafes, science festivals, or ev ents at museums (e.g. Nesseth et al., 2021). Public  outreach usually entails scientists or outreach specialists setting a table up in a public space, often associated with festivals (e.g. Perez et al., 2020, Peterman et al., 2020). To attempt to draw novel audiences to public outreach and identify the ways in which existing interests can be used to  affect science literacy and interest, the Cosplay for Science Initiative created three events in which public outreach of science was combined with cosplay.

## Study context, data sources, and analysis

Cosplay for Science  is a nonprofit educational initiative dedicated to making science accessible through immersive experiences, storytelling, and cosplay by designing and hosting pop-up museums and outreach events that feature scientists who share how their science  relates to pop culture (Lundgren et al., 2019; Lundgren et al., 2024). For this research, we looked at three instances of public outreach: The Museum of Natural Wonders, a popup museum that was at a comic con in the western Un ited States; the Pokémon Ecology Academy,  a panel discussion held at a comic con in the northeastern United States; and A Feywild Incursion, an interactive outreach event held in the beer garden of brewery in the mid western United States. All data were collected under USU IRB#14907. We sought to characterize visitor interest and learning at these events. Attendees answered questions scientists and educational researchers created in r eference to each event, such as, “What was somethin g you learned at [Outreach event]?” Authors took pictures of visitors’ sticky notes at A Feywild Incursion and The Museum of Natural Wonders, while visitor responses from The Pokémon Ecology Academy were collected via Google form and then collated into a Google sheet.  The first author transcribed pictures of the sticky  notes, collating them in Google sheets, while doing this s he also wrote analytic memos as a first pass of dat a analysis. The second author then examined the transcribed res ponses and created first cycle codes (Saldana, 20 13), for example, “bug” was the code for the response “Bugs can count years/seasons based on nutrients from the roots of trees.” The first and second author then examined first cycle codes together, grouping codes that were similar (e.g. “bugs” and “cicadas”) and then made summary tables of the coded responses.

## Findings

For A Feywild Incursion, audiences described general aspects about a topic of interest  targeted by the event. We relate our findings back to situational interest: T hese kinds of responses might show the limited conn ections that audiences made between the interactive events and l earning. However, the fact remains that audiences i ndicated that they knew that, for instance, invasive species were harmful. But it remains unclear if the event itself and the Dungeons and Dragons themed interactive booths help ed them come to these conclusions or if their past experiences (e.g. schooling, other outreach events)  played a part. The Museum of Natural Wonders, a pop-up museum booth at a comic con, featured museum specimens such as a Tyrannosaurid skull, extinct mammal teeth and skulls, and mineral specimens. After interacting with cosplaying scientists, audiences coul d use sticky notes to respond to the prompt, “Something I learned at the Museum of Natural Wonders…” Our data shows that people learned about topics on display at the pop-up museum, but did not explicitly make connections between their interest (i.e. Dungeons and Dragons) and the scientific materials that were at the pop-up museum booth. Audiences at the Pokémon Ecology Academy event related their understanding of Pokémon to the  natural world. Responses showed audience members took information from the panel discussion and described it in their own words. These kinds of responses are important, as they show the ways in which audience members seemingly used the information from the panel to put their questions and thoughts about real world science into ways they understood and could connect to.

## Discussion and conclusion

In previous work on situational interest, researche rs studied how situational interest developed in bi rders and astronomers (Jones et al., 2016) as well as children’s interest in science in museums (Jung & Zimmerman, 2024). We add to this body of literature by showcasing how, when engaging with public outreach materials that  feature topics people are interested in (i.e. Dungeons and Dragons and Pokémon), people relate their interest to science, which can result in increased curiosity and science liter acy. We sought to characterize visitor learning about science concepts at outreach events in the novel in formal science spaces of comic cons and a brewery. We see this as a way to reach people who might not otherwise feel welcome in traditional informal science learning spaces as well as a way to help people feel empowered in their ability to engage with scientists in ways that validate their interests, perspectives, and curiosity.
