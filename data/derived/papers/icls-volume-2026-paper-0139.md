# Teacher Autonomy in Organized Informal Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Researchers often refer to teacher autonomy in either how they learn in professional development or how they make choices about what and how to teach. These two areas of teacher autonomy are usually studied separately, which we argue is problematic, and thus bring them together. We focus on a 4-day summer workshop where teachers were given unusually high levels of freedom to set their own goals and learning. By looking at the choices teachers made we explore what it means for teachers to exercise different kinds of professional judgment. We draw on Boote’s (2006) idea of professional discretion, illustrating how some relied on existing materials and organized them for their classrooms, while others developed new materials that addressed local student needs. By examining these choices, we highlight potential opportunities for how teacher autonomy can be supported to foster more innovative professional practice. Objective Research on teacher autonomy is fragmented across two main bodies of research. The first stems from teacher education and professional development literature and deals with different forms of teacher autonomy or its absence in their learning (e.g., Horn & Garner, 2022; Kennedy, 2019; Lefstein et al., 2020), and the second stems from policy and curriculum investigations and deals with teachers’ freedom to make choices about what they teach and how (e.g., Hong & Youngs, 2016; Pearson & Moomaw, 2005). The gap between these two bodies of knowledge is problematic because autonomy cannot be “provided” without proper support to realize it (Hargreaves, 2016; Marco et al., 2025). For these reasons, we position this paper and investigation at this intersection, in line with scholars who bridge that gap (e.g., Boote, 2006; Severance et al., 2016). From this stance, we argue that teacher curricular autonomy is both necessary and requires support for teachers to exercise it. Our overarching goal, in both practice and research, echoes Boote’s (2006) argument that “developing innovative professional discretion should be the long-term goal of teacher development and curriculum policy” (p. 468). Towards this goal, in this exploratory paper we first define teacher curricular autonomy as teachers’ capacity to make curricular choices and to implement them (Hong & Youngs, 2016; Pearson & Moomaw, 2005). Then, we present Boote’s conceptualization of three levels of teacher professional discretion marked by increased competence: procedural, substantive, and innovative professional discretion. Finally, we illustrate teacher choices in a professional development setting we call organized informal learning, distinguished from more typical PD activities by the high level of autonomy teachers are provided with. Within the context of this 4-day-long summer workshop, we ask, What type of choices did participants make about the structure and content of the curricular materials they developed? What choices did participants make about their own activity within the PD? We explore these questions with an eye towards providing fertile ground for future research and professional development designed to develop teachers’ ability to exercise innovative professional discretion. In response, researchers advocate for a variety of scaffolding rich representations of teaching (Weingarden & Ehrenfeld, 2025) to ensure, to a certain extent, more learning-rich conversations. In this study, we take an opposite approach, exploring what it means to move the needle of collaborative learning with less structure, offering teachers fuller autonomy in pursuing more choices in their own learning in the context of curriculum design.

1177 Teachers’ curricular autonomy and professional discretion Curricular autonomy has been defined as having the right to make professional choices about the selection of activities and materials, instructional planning and teaching methods (Hong & Youngs, 2016; Pearson & Moomaw, 2005). Literature on teacher (curricu lar) autonomy often frames teachers’ “shrinking autonomy” (Archbald & Porter, 1994) as a problem, highlighting the importance of autonomy and local knowledge of school, students and educational goals in pedagogical decision making, teacher motivation, and professionalism (BenPeretz, 1990; Pearson & Moomaw, 2005). Put simply, teachers are best positioned to “mediate between the needs of students and external expectations” (Boote, 2006, p. 462). Notwithstanding this widespread stance, scholars also note its tension with organizational scholarship’s view of schools as resistant to change and to implementations of reforms, which implies an assumption that teachers might actually have too much autonomy and space for local sensemaking (Archbald & Porter, 1994). The core criticism of strong professional autonomy for teachers can be described as follows: “teachers will be free to exercise poor judgment as well as good judgment” (Hargreaves, 2016, p. 121). Furthermore, cases of teachers’ negative experiences with autonomy were also demonstrated (Hong & Youngs, 2016; Marco et al., 2025). Bridging the two notions of teacher autonomy As we mentioned above, we position this work at the intersection of these two bodies of literature, arguing that curricular autonomy is much needed insofar that it is coupled with teacher learning autonomy that ensures teachers’ ability to utilize it. In a similar vein, Severance et al. (2016) illustrated ways to organize collaborative design space that promotes teachers’ agency in designing curriculum materials. Across the project, the research team aimed at providing teachers with both the opportunity to produce classroom materials and with opportunities for teachers’ learning through developing those materials. Their analysis, respectively, included evidence of agency in determining the structure of curricular materials as well as evidence of agency in shaping the work process. We find Boote’s (2006) notions of three levels of professional discretion helpful in marking the learning we aspire to support teachers in developing: procedural, substantive, and innovative. Procedural discretion deals with teachers' basic ability to work with and utiliz e a ready-made curriculum and teach it. Substantive discretion includes the capacity to take a self-critical perspective on practice and consequently make appropriate changes, such as adopting a textbook  or choosing another curriculum resource. Finally, the hallmark of innovative discretion includes going beyond “merely choosing among established and sanctioned curriculum options” (p.

468) and instead creating new curricular and instructional practices that takes into consideration the dilemmas of

the discipline, students, and other aspects of their unique local contexts.

## Research context: The summer workshop

The context of this study is an intensive 4-day summer PD workshop (5 hours a day) where teachers from a range of disciplines who chose to participate articulated their own learning goals (individually or in pairs) with regards to inventing, developing, or refining instructional materials for their classrooms. The 10 teachers who registered were invited to work in summer 2024 towards their stated goals in their own way, with the whole community meeting for an opening and closing activity each day, all facilitated by the first author. Teachers spent the ‘nongroup’ hours in a shared space where the design of the learning space encouraged collaboration but did not force it. In contrast to typical collaborative learning, the learning content, methodology and learning goals were not identical across the group of participants. In this sense, the learning was less formal, yet took place in an organized space. Because of the nature of the PD design, teachers had an atypical amount of decisions to make about their learning before and during the PD, in shaping both their own activity during the PD and in determining the structure and content of the curricular materials they develop for their students. During every day of the 4-day workshop, as participants settled down in their chosen workspaces, we set up audio recording devices where there were two or more participants whether or not they had made a deliberate decision to work together.  As participants moved around and groups shifted, we moved the recording device to document the new grouping and conversation. All of the conversations are documented in an excel spreadsheet organized by participant and by day showing the flow of what conversations took place and which group workshops the participant participated in on a given day. We also video-recorded the opening session, closing session and optional content session of each day. Data  include 40 audio-recorded small group conversations and 11 full workshop video-recordings. In addition to the audio and video data, each participant submitted a brief informal 1178 indication in writing of what they intended to work on during the workshop (pre-workshop), the lesson plan or unit plan that they had developed during the workshop (post-workshop), and a written task where each participant chose three sources to respond to and integrate into their unit plan (asynchronous task). Data analysis: Exploratory analysis of teacher choices in organized informal learning Recall our research goal to unpack choices participants have made about curricular materials as well as about their own activity within the PD. For this paper, after looking over participants’ stated goals, submitted unit plans and listening to a number of  conversations of various participants, we chose two cases which based on these data seemed illustrative of procedural and innovative professional discretion. Note that we do not make claims in this exploratory paper about teacher learning in the workshop as a shift in their professional discretion competencies. Instead we illustrate decisions to better characterize and understand teacher autonomy-in-action within each of these categories. The first case illustrating procedural discretion is that of Sophie, a middle school English teacher with 5 years of experience who came to the workshop on her own. The second case illustrating innovative discretion is that of Talia and Alan (5 years of experience and 9 years, respectively), high school Hebrew language teachers who came to the workshop as colleagues teaching in the same school and planning to develop materials together. Sophie, Alan and Talia all participated in the same workshop in previous years. The case of Sophie Based on Sophie’s stated goal for the workshop, the unit plan she submitted at the end of the workshop, and recorded conversations from throughout the workshop, we categorize Sophie’s mode of autonomy and decision making as procedural professional discretion. Time and again we saw Sophie sticking closely to conventional written curricular materials and making plans for how she would use those materials. Sophie’s stated goal for the workshop Before the workshop began, Sophie submitted her goal: “to create an overall plan for grammar for 7th grade with the textbook + other textbooks.” She chose a focus area (grammar) and came to the workshop with the textbook her school has chosen for 7th grade  English. She did not include a specific pedagogical goal or what she hoped her students would gain from the materials she aimed to develop. While this is not conclusive evidence for procedural discretion, as no specific instructions were given by the workshop facilitator about including these considerations when setting goals, it does provide us with a starting point for our investigation. Sophie’s decisions about teaching In audio recordings of conversations with Sophie, she explained that while the book includes vocabulary and grammar, she wanted to focus on teaching grammar and for students to learn vocabulary more organically through the grammar teaching and reading text s. The materials Sophie mentioned and referenced are primarily ready-made materials that she reorganized to use with students. One such example is of a teacher-made presentation that she found on the internet that presents material that she would be teaching and included it in her unit plan. Sophie states that students would practice by doing exercises in the textbook they would be using with additional photocopied worksheets from other relevant textbooks.

Sophie’s decision about learning Throughout the workshop, Sophie primarily chose to work on her own. Over the course of the workshop we documented Sophie in 11 conversations, all except one of which were spontaneous. The one pre-planned conversation was not initiated by Sophie, but was part of the workshop in which every participant was paired up with another participant to share what they had been working on for a mutual feedback session. As her final product of the workshop, and the tool she developed during the workshop, Sophie created a chart organized by pages in the textbook she was working with. The chart included columns for what to emphasize in her teaching, where students would be asked to produce language and what exercises they would do for homework. The case of Talia & Alan Alan and Talia came to the workshop with a pedagogical problem that had been bothering them and that they were looking to solve with the unit they planned to develop. Their pre-workshop stated goal, how they explain what they are trying to do and how they work to develop their own fitting materials to achieve their goal are what lead us to characterize their mode of autonomy as an instance of innovative professional discretion. 1179 Talia & Alan’s stated goal for the workshop Talia & Alan’s stated goal was to “develop materials for reading comprehension in their high school Hebrew language classes.” They explained, already in their informal written pre-workshop goal, that when teaching the mandated Hebrew language curriculum they focus on discrete language skills covered on the matriculation exams and not nearly enough time (in their opinion) on reading comprehension. They were frustrated that this is the case and were determined to create materials and a system that will enable them to incorporate more reading comprehension work into their daily teaching.

Talia & Alan’s decision about teaching On the first day of the workshop, during a coworking conversation with Alan, Talia said that she was going to spend time choosing texts. She mentioned that during the opening session earlier that day she had set a goal for herself to choose texts that would be both interesting and have pedagogical value. Here we see Talia choosing texts strategically as a basis for which to create lessons and teaching activities focused on reading comprehension. For example, when saying: “This is really making me look [closely] at good texts. With an eye toward the desire for students to learn writing from them. It really makes me think about how we’re teaching.” Talia & Alan’s decision about learning Talia and Alan signed up to come to the workshop together and spent all of their time in the workshop working together. The only substantial conversations they had with other participants was during the session where each participant was paired up with another for a mutual feedback session. Out of three optional content sessions, Alan chose to join one on the second day of the workshop and both he and Talia came to the optional session on the last day. In a written task for the workshop, Alan took a unique approach to the assignment that highlights his autonomy as a learner. The task assigned asked participants to read or watch material about teaching and learning and integrate insights about said source into their unit plan. Participants were invited to cho ose from a bank of sources or choose their own. As opposed to choosing a new video or article, Alan chose to rewatch a video that he had watched for this assignment in the previous year’s workshop. He explains that he had remembered the impact the video had had on his thinking and that he wanted to revisit the ideas to reflect on how they had influenced his teaching and how they connected with his thinking in the current workshop. Alan chose to take the innovative path and deepen his learning.

Boote (2006) argued that developing innovative professional discretion should be the long-term goal of professional development. Based on the cases we looked at and the choices the teachers made in the workshop, a picture is beginning to emerge as to what procedural (Sophie) and innovative (Alan & Talia) professional discretion look like in action. Once further developed, these visible indicators can serve as a tool for researchers and PD leaders to better understand and foster teachers’ professional discretion. We aim to extend this study to identify more indicators of each type of discretion. In this investigation we looked at cases we categorized as procedural and innovative. Our next steps will include searching for potential instances of substantive discretion, as well as evaluating whether additional types expand Boote’s categories. As we noted, this study did not actively attempt to investigate how participants developed professional discretion. It did, however, provide a model of PD in which teachers had unusually high levels of autonomy both about their goal and desired end product and how they would spend their time throughout the workshop. This high level of autonomy enabled us to examine how teachers exercised their autonomy when given broad space to do so yet also raises questions about the limits of such autonomy. If anything, our model of organized informal learning evokes Hargreaves’ (2016) warning about autonomy without support. In Sophie’s case, where many choices were procedural, more support may have guided her to more innovative decisions in planning and time use. Alan & Talia, having come to the workshop displaying innovative decision making, capitalized on the autonomy provided to make more innovative decisions, both about their teaching materials and learning processes. While organized informal learning such as the workshop in this study offers a promising model for future teacher learning opportunities that foreground teachers’ autonomy, the question remains: what support might develop teachers’ professional discretion and enable those using innovative discretion to extend how and where they use it? How can PD developers balance space for real autonomy while offering scaffolding in service of that autonomy? Finally, what would it take for PD designers to intentionally foster teachers’ move from procedural to substantive and innovative discretion?
