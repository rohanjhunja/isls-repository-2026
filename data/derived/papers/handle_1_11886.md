# Mirroring What’s in the Shared Repertoires of Two Communities of Practice: A Cultural-Historical Perspective Using Generative AI

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores the development of the shared repertoires (Wenger, 1998) of two web-supported Communities of Practice (CoPs). The co-authors of this paper were the initiators of these CoPs and continue to be involved in them. Using the shared repertoire of each of these communities as a lens, the evolution of their activity was explored. Referring to the CHAT’s change lab process of mirroring to participants their past activity (Engeström, 1987, 2015), two generative AI tools were prompted to focus on the websites of the two CoPs, especially collaborative activities and artefacts, and to ‘report on’ the evolution of their shared repertoires. Accounts underwent human validation. For ethical reasons, no transcript was analysed of the interactions between the participants that may have led, through meaning negotiation, to the production or use of artefacts as well as to problem setting, problem solving or knowledge building. Results show that 1) the generative AI tools were accurate in their ‘reports’; and 2) identified possible limitations in the shared repertoires; such observations providing topics for future negotiation of values among members and between these communities.

## Introduction

Communities of practice (CoPs) have been widely ado pted as an informal or non-formal model of professional learning and have been studied in various fields, including business, education and healthcare. They can be formed around a practice that needs to be  improved or around a new practice that needs to be developed. In his seminal work on communities of practice, Wenger (1998) iden tified three characteristics: the mutual engagement  of participants, a joint enterprise and a shared reper toire. Since then, se veral studies have focused on the shared repertoire of CoPs (Gau, 2016; Hall et al., 2022; R ichmond et al., 2024; Smith et al., 2014), but thei r evolution remains largely unknown. Overall, however, practiti oners have little to support the development of a shared repertoire that is truly collaborative, and researchers could have access to more substantive and meaningful data. Commonly defined as a set of elements (tools, rules  and guidelines, individual stories of practice or conceptual essays), a shared repertoire refers to “a system of  shared language, ideas, and various representation s that help to shape the cultural and organizational identity within the CoP” according to Richmond et al. (2024, p. 421). Our understanding has been that  the value of a shared repertoire for a CoP is based  on substantive and meaningful interactions between participants: They share knowledge and imagine new possibilities for t heir practice. At its best, a shared repertoire includes  moments when participants  engage in a dynamic process of negotiating meaning, and practical knowledge emerge s (1). This requires participants to interact with each other around a specific problem of practice – sharing their perceptions and experiences of the pr oblem, reconciling views and practical/theoretical knowledge, coming to an agreement on how to tackle the problem next, reflecting on what has worked and what has not, validating and adopting a common new practice, and continuing to improve it. Then, the ‘regime of competence’ of the CoP expands and its collective identity develops (Wenger, 1998). The two CoPs in this study, one Francophone and one  Anglophone, based in Canada, have evolved alongside CSCL’s own evolution as a research commun ity––a CoP in itself, given that research is its practic e! New generations of researchers move in as experienced members leave. We therefore feel that this short paper is timely in articulating a process that is likely to highlight the evolution of the shared repertoire of a domain-specific CoP supported by an enduring website. Given the current rapid development of LLMs (Large Language Models) and their time-saving promise, two of them (ChatGPT 4o and NotebookLM) will be in use here. The aim of this short paper is to illustrate the pr ocess of combining insider views and generative AI (GAI), as a possible future methodological step, th at accompanies the tracking of and the reflection o n the evolution of the shared repertoires of CoPs from a cultural-historical perspective.

## Background

At the dawn of the Internet browser, there was a ne ed for educators to unpack what teaching and learni ng with ICT meant. CoP1, a Francophone community, was creat ed. As the initiators wanted ICT use to be drawn by pedagogies marked by reflexivity and sensitivity th ey were inspired by The Tact of Teaching: The Meaning of Pedagogical Thoughtfulness (van Manen, 1991). CoP1 was introduced at the first  meeting (Montreal, 1996) of the TeleLearning Network of Centres of Excellence (Canada, 1995-2002). Much attention was first devoted to collaborative project-based learning with the help of digital tools. As t he community’s practice grew, the focus shifted towards collaborative knowledge building. C oP1 developed its shared repository using interacti ve online platforms (members only) and openly sharing resources on the Web. The starting point for CoP2, an Anglophone communit y, was an initiative to address the perennial problem of how to promote scalable educational chan ge based on theory and empirical evidence – a question reflected on within the CSCL community (Hod et al., 2018). Bringing together a small group of practitioners and education researchers from local colleges and unive rsities, the identified opportunity was the growing  interest in a pedagogy called active learning among the post-secondary practitioners within ST EM fields. Notables such as Carl Wieman and Eric Mazur, both prize winning phys icists, were heading the call for change (Wieman, 2 007) and intentionally appropriating and adapting designs and principles from learning theories and popularizing them

– e.g., peer-instruction (Crouch & Mazur, 2001), flipped classro om. While the expanding awareness among

practitioners, and support from discipline-based researchers, was encouraging for the early Co P2 initiative, it surfaced a conundrum voiced by a researcher in the field of learning scientists: What is active learning? Answering and understanding the nature and design elements of  this pedagogical approach has become a focal objec tive in the development of its shared repertoire – exemplified in its workshops and presentations at its annual conference

– and shared resources, openly available on its website.

Both CoPs included CSCL researchers. For instance, the notion of a knowledge building community supported by Knowledge Forum (Scardamalia & Bereite r, 1994; McGilly et al., 1994) was put forward duri ng CoP1 codesign activities that led to innovative school-based settings (initiative one, 1997 to present and initiative two, 2002 to present). CoP2 embarked on unfolding w hat active learning with ICT meant in postsecondary education, guided by collaborative learning theory (Stahl, 2005), as well as research on new learning spaces (Lui & Slotta, 2014). In addition, it adopts a guiding f ramework that underpins its activities and values, the researchpractice partnership (RPP; Coburn et al., 2013) and  co-design (Gomez et al., 2018). This symmetrical approach to peer collaboration means inviting practitioners to not only co-design but also co-participate in the examination and evaluation of these evidence-informed approaches. In sum, in its efforts to make  sustainable large-scale changes to high education practice the  community’s commitment to knowledge dissemination t akes on a new flavour embracing the practitioner, giving them a ‘ legitimate voice’ in the RPP design process (Hod et al., 2018) and new roles in the process of making sustainable principled change.

## Methodology

The value of mirroring (Engeström, 1987, 2015) has been documented in the change lab studies (Engeströ m, 1987, 2015; Hopwood, 2024; Virkkunen & Newnham, 201 3). For Engeström (1987, 2015), ‘mirroring’ is an integral part of the ‘expansive learning cycle’ for studying and transforming activity systems. It involves showing participants data or artefacts that reflect their p ractices and challenges for stimulating reflection,  if not change. Given that LLMs (or GAIs) are gaining recognition for their synthesis capacity (Giglou et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023), it is our contention that they may act as ‘m irrors’ for a CoP wanting to examine the evolution of their shared repertoires (basically, texts, images, and videos as reification of participation). To illustrate this possibility, we interacted with ChatGPT 4o asking prompts. Given  that its responses needed rare corrections, we set tled on the following prompts: ● Provide an historical overview of the website of CoP1. Doing so, go exclusively to website1 ● Provide an historical overview of the website of th e CoP2 community. Doing so, go exclusively to website2 ● Provide a historical outline of CoP1’s shared repertoire ● Provide a historical outline of Cop2’s shared repertoire ● Provide a historical account of the practical knowledge visible through their shared repertoires Secondly, we created an early draft of this paper, inclusive of ChatGPT 4o’s responses that were inclu ded in the Results section of this early draft. We inserted the early draft in NotebookLM and used the following prompts: ● What was the impetus behind the launching of these two communities? ● Theoretical foundations of CoP1 and CoP2 ● What was the nature of the interactions among participants of these two communities? ● Describe the shared repertoire of each of these communities ● Glimpses of meaning negotiation in the shared repertoires ● Provide a historical perspective on the evolution of the shared repertoires of CoP1 and CoP2 Thirdly, after human validation by the two co-researchers (criteria: accuracy, relevance, meaningfulness, and comprehensiveness), we fed the outputs of these prompts into NotebookLM and asked it to: ● Produce a synthesis of 500 words of its responses to our previous prompts. Fourthly, we wanted to zero in on practical knowledge. We prompted both GAIs: ● Focus on the practical knowledge visible through the shared repertoires.

## Results

Authors’ disagreement with the GAI summary statements are indicated as ADPS (authors’ disagreement with previous statement) and  followed up with a brief explanation.

The sources provide a rich account of the evolution  of shared repertoires within two CoPs: [CoP1] and [CoP2]. These communities exemplify the dynamic interplay between technological advancements, pedagogical innovation, and the colla borative construction of practical knowledge in education.

[CoP1's] journey is marked by a strong emphasis on responding to the evolving landscape of ICT in education. Starting with a focus on basic ICT skills like web page creation and asynchronous communication, the community's rep ertoire progressively embraced tools and frameworks for collaborative knowledge building, such as shared inquiry approaches and Knowledge Forum. This shift [ADPS:  diversified practices, not a shift] suggests a grow ing recognition within the community that effective ICT  integration requires not just technical proficiency but also a deeper understanding of how technology can support collaborative learning processes. [CoP1's] commitment to keeping pace with technolo gical change is further evidenced by its integration of interactive platforms and multimedia elements into its repertoire. The community's dedication to practical knowledge i s underscored by the creation o f a centralized repository of resources, including cour se materials, research publications, and teacher training modules. This repository likely served as a valuable hub for educators seeking to translate theoretical insights into actionable classroom strategies. [CoP2's] trajectory is defined by its focus on active learning and the translation of this pedagogical approach into concrete resources and to ols. The community's foundational question, "What is active learning?", highlights a deliberate effort to establis h a shared understanding of this core concept. This focus on conceptual clarity likely provided a solid basis for the subsequent development of [CoP2's] repertoi re. The creation of tools like [platform1] and [platform2] exemplifies [CoP2’s] commitment to aligning theory with practice. These tools provide educators with practical means to design courses and engage students in active learning activities, demonstrating a deep understanding of t he challenges and opportunities associated with implementing this p edagogical approach. The annual [CoP2] conferences play a crucial role in fostering community interaction and shaping the shared repertoire. These events provide a platform for showcasing innovative practices, cri tiquing existing tools and strategies, and generating new knowledge through collaborative dialogue. Both [CoP1] and [CoP2] share a commitment to empowe ring educators through professional development, collaborative learning, m eaningful technology integration, and sustainability. These shared values underscore the common goal of both communities: to support educators in creating more effective and engaging learning environments. Despite these strengths, the sources also reveal areas for critical reflection and further investigation. The lack of detailed accounts of mea ning negotiation processes within both communities limits our understanding of how shared understandings were reac hed and what tensions or disagreements may have surfaced. A more nuanced understanding of these dynamics requires examining the conversations and debates th at shaped the evolution of the repertoires, which raises ethical considerations about accessing  and analyzing member interactions. Additionally, the sources raise concerns about pote ntial biases within each repertoire. In [CoP2's] case, [ADPS:  the strong emphasis on technological advancements raises the question of whether pedagogical innovation received sufficie nt attention.] did the focus on ICT tools sometimes overshadow deeper discussions about teach ing and learning approaches? For [CoP2], the prominent focus on [platform1] and [platform2] raises the question of whether other tools or strategies within the community deserve more attention. [ADPS: these digital tools are a subset of the tools used and documented. However, they are the only active learning tools that the community has developed.] Did this emphasis potentially limit the exploration of alternative approaches to active learning?

Finally, the sources provide limited insight into h ow both communities have addressed issues of equity and inclusion in their shared repertoires. [ADPS: possible contradiction to be analyzed]. Given the increasing emphasis on these values in education, further rese arch is needed to understand how CoP1  and CoP2 have incorporated principles of diversity, accessibility, and culturally responsive pedagogy into their res ources and practices.

## Discussion & Implications

In conclusion, the sources offer a valuable but incomplete picture of shared repertoire evolution in CoP1 and CoP2. While these communities have demonstrably contributed to the advancement of educational practices, further research is needed to explore the complexities of meaning negotiation, address potential biases, and shed light on the integration of equity and inclusion principles within their shared repertoires.

## Discussion

This cultural-historical perspective, an output produced with tim e efficiency, appears to be worth consideration for the evolution of the shared repertoires of the two linguistically different communities involved. They help identify foundational elements and they highlight sustaining preoccupations and activity threads. Currently, GAIs’ produce outputs are often considered as general and vague, but this has not been our experience. On the contrary, as researchers, we found that the generated responses were mostly accurate and, surprisingly, even provocatively sensible. Only small corrections were necessary to the ChatGPT 4o’s outputs that were sent to NotebookLM––as sources on which to draw on ––during the conduct of the second set of prompts. Wi th three exceptions ––see authors’ disagreements––we agree with the synthesis produced by NotebookLM (Results section). Therefore, we submit that these GAIs’ outputs to the prompts cons titute time-savings for researchers and practitioners during a mirroring process where a CoP aims at reflecting on  the evolution of its shared repertoire and identifying wa ys forward for enhancing it.

However, there are limitations: 1) well-aware of the distinct conceptual frameworks’ develo ped by Engeström and Wenger, which differ in focus and application, we crossed their respective boundaries for the sake of exploring what the two GAIs could contribute; 2)  we omitted to consider tensions and contradictions (no transcripts were submitted to the GAIs), and which resolutions are key for change; 3) the surface documentation to which ChatGPT 4o had access did not allowed either for deep artefacts to be accessed; 4) although NotebookLM gives us full control on the sources to feed it, we  refrained from adding CoPs’ participants   transcripts, thus limiting its sight over meaning negotiation and pra ctical knowledge; and 5) Gemini, the LLM behind NotebookLM, extracted one striking element that was  out of NotebookLM’s access to sources on the one h and, namely issues of equity and inclusion, whereas such  preoccupations were embedded in CoPs’ membership a nd participants’ transcripts.

Arguably, reflection on practice is essential for the development of productive communities, and perhaps particularly important at this historical period when founding members are stepping back or retiring. Examination of the large copra of resources that embed the values and shared beliefs of these communities are time consuming and often costly. However, we have shown that the p rompts designed for the GAI queries can facilitate and jumpstart the types of dialogical discourses betwee n leads of the two comm unities featured. The success of this experiment is encouraging and opens the possibility  that the process used can be extended to working w ith other CoPs within the CSCL and Learning Sciences communit ies. Mapping out similarities and differences betwe en the shared repertoires appears to be a productive ap proach to sustaining and possibly expanding collabo rations between smaller communities that have developed wit hin the CSCL network and share core values of collaboration.
