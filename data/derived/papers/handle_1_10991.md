# Justice and Creativity: Leveraging Creative Learning Principles for Youth-Led Social Change

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Young people who come from marginalized identities and are given access to creative learning spaces and tools are positioned to address social change and implement tangible solutions. This paper describes how youth in The Clubhouse Network are using creative computing tools to develop social justice awareness campaigns and considers how they have the potential to spark change within their communities.

Equity in STEM K-12 education is often discussed in terms of “broadening access” – an effort to engage historically underrepresented groups while overlooking the conditions of the learning environment (Vossoughi, et. al, 2013). This approach overlooks how these groups already engage in STEM activities, undermining the expertise and creativity they possess. In this paper, I propose that young people who come from historically underresourced communities and marginalized identities  who have access to a creati ve learning environment are best positioned to create social change. Their lived experiences make them experts on issues their communities face, and a creative learning environment provides a space where young people can prototype, improve, and implement solutions that have a positive impact on their community, moving them towards justice. Creative learning environments: The Clubhouse Network Traditionally, education is approached from an instructionist methodology, constraining the learner to only discover what the instructor predetermines as important. An instructionist approach to education does not center the learner or their needs and inst ead focuses on academic performance. In contrast, a creative learning environment is learner-centered and takes an asset-based approach to empowering learners to explore and construct their own knowledge through creating and tinkering. In this paper, I use the Clubhouse Network as a case study for using creative learning as a place for implementing and sustaining justice-centered projects that have the potential to spark change in historically underserved communities. The Clubhouse Network is a global after-school program that serves youth ages 10-18 across more than 100 locations in 20 countries. Each of the locations is located in a historically underserved community. (Resnick, et. al, 1998). The Network has engaged youth in creative learning activities for the past 30 years, encouraging them to work on projects they are passionate about while under the guidance of adult mentors and using technology as a tool. The four guiding principles of the Clubhouse Network are to support learning through design experiences, help members build their own interests, cultivate a community of emerging leaders, and create an environment of respect and trust. These principles build on a creative learning  approach, where youth construct their own knowledge by working on projects (Resnick, 2017). Transformative justice is a framework used to respond to violence and harm. The goal of the framework is to create social change through harm reduction, addressing the root causes of issues, and building thriving communities. While the framework was designed to address individual harms, the principles “can be applied to any context where there exists harm caused by systemic oppression” (Erete, et al, 2021, p.5). The Clubhouse Network applies these principles by intentionally placing Clubhouses in unde r-resourced communities and providing young people with a thriving creative learning environment and access to state-of-the-art technological tools. This framework allows for both acknowledging the past harms and co-creating a sustainable future that dismantles oppressive systems and mitigates harm. While it is not possible to guarantee a place without harm, one can co-create an environment that allows  people’s voices and opinions to be valued and considered, creating a path forward. Researchers, educators, and others engaging youth from marginalized communities in creative learning environments must explore the historical context of their own work, self-reflect, and listen to differing opinions (Erete, et al 2018). In the Clubhouse Network, young people are trusted and encouraged to follow their own interests and can lend themselves as experts to STEM education researchers as co-designers. Leveraging creative learning tools for social justice At Clubhouses young people have access to high-quality computational  and creative learning  tools that can amplify their social justice campaigns. With these tools, young people create animations, games, videos, music, websites, robots, and more. On their own, learning how to create with these tools allows young people to develop 2311 professional skills that can be used to further their education and career goals. However, many young people in the Clubhouse use these tools not only to build skills but  to amplify social justice messages to create awareness of an issue within their community.

One example is WeCity, a game developed by members of the Clubhouse @ Tecnocentro Somos Pacifico in Cali, Colombia, using the Scratch programming platform. The 13-and 15-year-old creators explain that their game is based on one of Colombia’s sustainable d evelopment goals. The goal of the game is to teach their peers how to reduce environmental harm and increase the quality of life for those who live in their community. In the game, users buy new buildings or improve older buildings to make them more sustainable with items such as solar panels. To earn money to purchase sustainable materials the user  plays a waste sorting game, placing items in either the trash, recycle, or compost bins. Their project consists of more than 40 sprites, each with its own unique blocks of code, indicating the high level of detail and attention that went into creating the game. Another example is Food Insecurity, a 60-second public service announcement video created by youth at the Phase 4 Learning Center in Pittsburgh. In the video, the youth start by saying “Let’s talk about it”, followed by “I’m hungry”. They then share what food insecurity looks like to them, using words like “snacks” and “cheap” before describing what it would look like if they had access to quality food, where they use words such as “nutritious”. They also explain that poor eating habits lead to long-term health issues. The video ends with a call to action “Let’s tackle food insecurity”. The wealth of information shared in just 60 seconds demonstrates their ability to investigate the root cause of an issue. The video production is high quality and reflects the work and thought that the youth invested in editing the video in a way that could get their message across concisely. In both project examples, Clubhouse youth used creative learning tools to bring awareness to a social justice issue that impacts them.  This indicates a desire to create positive change within the communities where they reside. While both projects were awareness campaigns, there is potential for these types of projects to spark tangible change.

There is an opportunity for educators and researchers to repair and mitigate harm and engage marginalized youth in creative learning by implementing principles of creative learning and transformative justice in their learning spaces. In an effort to better understand how Clubhouses are positioned to empower young people to create social change, I worked with the Clubhouse Network to launch the Youth Activism and Advocacy program. The program was proposed in 2020, shortly after George Floyd was murdered by the Minneapolis Police Department. There are five Clubhouses located in the city of  Minneapolis. It was evident that young people who attend Clubhouses are impacted by social justice issues in their community and have the ability to address social change. Ten sites from the global Clubhouse Network were selected to  participate in the Youth Activism and Advocacy program. Each site was awarded a stipend to support their young people in creating and implementing their own social justice campaigns within their communities. The sites addressed  issues such as gentrification, food insecurity, transphobia and homophobia in schools, environmental justice, economic disparities, and transportation justice. While the Network has historically supported young people in aw areness campaigns, they were short-term or one-time projects that were not sustained over time. The process and outcomes of these social justice projects will be shared across the international Clubhouse Network and made available to youth program leaders in other communities.
