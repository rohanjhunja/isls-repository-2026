# Painting Identities: Artmaking as Identity Work with Youth

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Recent scholarship has called for the examination of young people’s identifications in relation to histories of marginalization and oppression, and as part of learning trajectories that are simultaneously social, cultural, and historical. The Rehacer el mundo project is a participatory design project with youth, museum educators, artists, and researchers to engage Latine and other historically marginalized youth and families in developing a youth-professional collaborative art exhibit. The project, located in an urban Latine arts museum, was designed to create opportunities for communities of historically marginalized youth to engage in reimagining public discourse. In this paper, we draw upon notions of artmaking to describe the ways youth from historically marginalized communities engage in identity building as historical actors. Our findings illustrate these metaphors have potential to expand understandings of learning and identity development as an inherently cultural process.

Studies of identity development have a long history in the learning sciences in educational research broadly (e.g., Cole, 1996; Polman & Miller, 2010; Nasir & Cooks, 2009; Wertsch, 1997); yet, there remains a need to deepen understandings of the ways young people and their elders carve identities across contexts and within communities (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Recent scholarship has called for the examination of young people’s identifications in relation to histories of marginalization and oppression (e.g., Gutiérrez et al., 2019), and as part of “learning pathways” that are simultaneously social, cultural, and historical (Nasir et al., 2020). Taking the stance that learning and identity are deeply intertwined, Nasir and colleagues (2020) describe a gap in the ways many studies approach learning as “related to culture, rather than a necessarily cultural process” (p. 195). These scholars call for new tools to build understandings of learning and identity as inherently cultural. The Rehacer el mundo  project (all names pseudonyms) is a community-based participatory design project (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) to engage Latine and other historically marginalized youth and families in developing a collaborative youth-professional art exhibit. The project took place in an urban, mountain-west Latine arts museum and was collaboratively designed (co-designed) as a way to both create opportunities for communities of historically marginalized youth to engage in reimagining and restorying (Stornaiuolo & Thomas,

2018) public and dominant discourses in the city, while building understandings of the ways learners build

identities, both individually and collaboratively, through artmaking (e.g., Enyedy et al., 2014). We were specifically interested in the ways that youth  leveraged practices of artmaking to recall their lived and ancestral histories (Stamatis et al., 2023). In this paper, we draw upon a process of Latine artmaking—retablo painting —to describe the ways one self-identified Mexican American youth engaged in this kind of identity building. In this short paper, we explore notions of artmaking as a means of deepening understandings of prolepsis (e.g., Polman & Miller, 2010; Cole, 1996; Nasir et al., 2020) and analepsis (Stamatis et al., 2023; 2024) as useful theoretical tools for understanding learning and identity development as deeply rooted within histories and cultures. We ask:

• How do young people engaged in artmaking in a participatory design project describe and illustrate their

identities as artists and learners within and across complex cultural contexts?

• How can processes of artmaking expand the ways we understand youth’s engagement in historicizing

themselves through an art-based museum project?

## Literature review and conceptual framing

We define learning as ontological, a process of becoming someone new with certain kinds of knowledge and dispositions (e.g., Cole, 1996; Parker & Goicoechea, 2000) and draw upon the notion of prolepsis as trajectories of identification that take place when youth engage with elders to project themselves into the future (e.g., Cole, 1996; Polman & Miller, 2010; Nasir et al., 2020). In this piece, we also draw from literary theory to add the notion of analepsis (Genette, 1980; Stamatis et al., 2023; 2024) to our analysis to consider the ways that youth draw upon their ancestral pasts to make sense of themselves and their politicized identities (e.g., Gutiérrez et al., 2019). In literary analysis, these terms refer respectively to flash-forward and flash-back, describing the moments when 1912 narrative develops outside of linear timescales. In studies of identity development, we see analepsis as a useful term to describe the ways youth take up their elders’ stories and their cultures as part of their own identifications. Nasir et al. (2020) described the ways that identities are dialogically formed, changing over time as learners negotiate cultural contexts alongside their daily interactions. In this piece, we take the sociocritical view that as individuals learn, they develop new identities, both in relation to cultural and historical materials, tools and activities embedded within learning environments and communities (Nasir, 2002; Nasir & Cooks, 2009) and in relation to historical power and privilege (Esmonde & Booker, 2016). In this study, we took up perspectives that recognize historically marginalized youth as historical actors —able to navigate societal structures grounded in histories of oppression to redefine both themselves and to reimagine the world through ‘consequential action” (Gutiérrez et al., 2019, p. 293). Artmaking drawn from traditions of cultural preservation and particularly those, like the southwestern and south American traditions of retablos, that reclaimed or reimagined histories of oppression (Enyedy et al., 2014; Halverson, 2023; Woods et al., 2024) can illustrate the ways young people engage across modalities in the construction of identities as historical actors.

In this paper, we examine the way that retablos  might offer a productive example of the ways artmaking can provide rich space for youth to develop as historical actors. We focus on retablos, which have historically been painted with natural pigments, foraged and crushed with mortar and pestle, to blend European religious art with Latin American Indigenous traditions (Duran & Massey, 1995). Gessoing refers to the preparation of wood for painting. Artists typically adopt natural pigments from natural resources to portray scenes of piety that connect directly to their homelands. Retablo painting is an artform found throughout Latin American in which artists both depict and disrupt Catholic iconography. In this project, Jaime, the Chicano artist who led retablo  making, identified as Queer and used his art to reimagine the narratives in his religion that had not historically included his own intersectional identities (Crenshaw, 1989). Jaime was also the director of education at the museum and explained that he saw his as a process of mentorship, passing down his traditions to the next generation of artists. For Jaime, retablo making was both a process of connection with the past and a symbol of stewardship, preserving the land so that he could continue to engage in the art he loved. This qualitative case study (Yin, 2013) was conducted from the fall of 2024 through the summer of 2025 with Remy, a 15-year-old who self-identified as Chicano  and Mexican American, “but I don’t really speak Spanish,” participated in three of the four multi-week art programs to make art for the museum exhibit. Along with Jaime, the authors acted as participant observers (Spradley, 1980) designing and facilitating the program and collaborating with 58 youth across 200 hours of programming between fall 2024 and spring 2026. The programs took place before, during, and after Trump’s second presidency, characterized by violent rhetoric and actions, particularly against Latine and other historically marginalized peoples in the United States. In this paper, we draw upon Remy’s case because of his specific interest and engagement with retablo painting. Across the project, we collected artifacts, fieldnotes, photos, audio, and video recordings. Additionally, we interviewed Remy three times, at the end of each youth program session. Recordings were transcribed and translated after each session. For this paper, we collectively built understandings of retablo making alongside our artist collaborators and then engaged in cycles of etic and emic coding, first using codes drawn from the initial descriptions of retablo making, such as connections to land, discussion of tradition, religion, and ancestors. We also sought examples of identity building, such as discussion of self, past, present, future, and family. Together, we looked at data from across two years, seeking both confirming and disconfirming evidence for the ways Remy engaged in both building and historicizing his identities alongside and within in his artmaking. In the following sections, we describe several instances of Remy’s artmaking, culminating in his experiences with his retablo. We then offer a discussion connecting our observations of Remy’s artistic practice with his developing identities as a historical actor. In the fall of 2024, we asked youth to examine the artworks in the museum to begin imagining the galleries as a place where they would have opportunities to exhibit their own art. The exhibit illustrated migration across borders and across the two-week program, Remy repeatedly returned to a painting of a group of figures riding a crocodile or caiman. On the creature’s head was a man with a coyote’s head, holding a harness strung between the crocodile’s teeth.  Behind the figure were seven figures, holding luggage, bundles, and even children, all riding the crocodile across a river. In a journal entry, Remy wrote, “The feeling [of this piece] is sad, disastrous, because of everything they’re trying to do. What I’m connecting to is the coyote person helping people on the crocodile go over the border.” He explained he liked the idea of leading others to safety, like coyotes, which he pronounced in Spanish, slang for someone who helps to smuggle others across borders. In 1913 conversation he explained that though coyotes were not always good, Remy felt that he, as a coyote, might be able to help and protect people.

The artmaking task in the fall of 2024 session, supported by Andrea (a professional artist and the fourth author on this paper), was a collage where youth were charged with telling their own stories by remixing other people’s imagery into something new. Remy created a collage pulling drawings of Mayan temples into a grayscale photo of a marsh. In the top corner of his piece, the head of a black bird with a bright orange beak watched over the scene. On the other side of the image, he had pasted an illustration of people with brown skin fighting with one another across a deep ravine. In his description, Remy wrote, the bird “watches over the people that it protects. Then, over the months winter happens, it has to leave its cave. War happens. It began to help the people it protects but it was too late” (Figure 1). While facilitators questioned the initial illustration, which had been torn from an old National Geographic magazine and seemed to essentialize Indigenous cultures toward stories of primitivism, Remy explained that for him the images illustrated protection rather than war. We noted this repetition of “savior” in Remy’s discourse around the coyote and in his collage as illustrative of his own identities as someone who could and who wanted to take action to protect others. This observation was reinforced in the ways that Remy engaged with three of his siblings who were also enrolled in the art programs, disrupting bickering and often moving to sit between them if they began to disagree.

He gathered the teens around a screen where he walked them through the history of retablo making, the histories of the U.S. southwest both its colonialism and the histories of Indigenous artists who had shaped his artworks and the materials he used. He explained that retablos  were a reclaimed artform, something that reimagined Spanish Catholicism toward “my people, our people.” In an interview, Jaime explained the retablos were a way for him to reckon with his own family history, both Spanish and Indigenous. He told the young artists, “The retablos  are a chance for us to connect to our histories…we are going to depict the sacred heart, and I want you to think about what’s in your heart, what you are, what’s important to you.” He paused, giving space for his words before handing the youth pre-gessoed retablos. He explained the process of gessoing and acknowledged that there were too many young artists to take them through the whole process. “They’re ready to paint!” he exclaimed as he handed Remy the whitewashed piece of wood.

Remy painted slowly, beginning with a pencil sketch of the sacred heart, mimicking a retablo Jaime had offered as a model for the youth. As Remy illustrated his heart, he slowly diverged from the model (see Figure 1), cutting off his heart midway and illustrating what appeared to be a brick wall. He then picked up a mortar and pestle with a piece of charcoal sitting in the basin. Turning his wrist in small circles, Remy crushed the briquette into a fine powder that could be mixed with oil and used as paint. Jaime walked over and explained that Remy should be careful, as it was the darkest pigment he had. “It’s hard,” Remy said to Jaime but then spent the next 30-minutes working with the pigment. Remy was methodical, both in crushing the pigment and with his retablo. When the pigment was ready, he set it aside and picked up a small bowl of light green powder. He dipped his paintbrush into a small cup of liquid and began to trace his brush along the lines he had drawn, green pigment slowly filling the spaces around his heart. He repeated the process, tracin g his penciled lines in different colors until only the brick wall remained without pigment (See Figure 2). As Remy was finishing his image, Jaime asked him to choose three words to describe “your own sacred heart.” Remy chose the words create, solution, and culture. In their piece describing the moves youth make to become historical actors throughout their lives, Gutiérrez et al. (2019) suggest that youth with historically marginalized identities often historicize their own identities by encountering a double bind, something that leaves them questioning the world. At the beginning of the programs, Remy explained he had never been in a space devoted to Latine art before. As he examined the artworks in the space, his affinity for the coyote  and his recognition of its  complex symbolism as both potential menace and protector in Mexican migration to the U.S. pointed to this kind of enduring question about the world. Remy repeatedly expressed wanting to be in a world where he could protect others. His proleptic engagement with the materials and ideas in his artworks and the relationships he had with his siblings each pointed to the ways that he was working to embody this identity as someone who would stand up for others (Nasir & Cooks, 2009). Based on his journal entries and his conversations with facilitators, we also noted that Remy appeared to be engaging in analeptic analysis of his pasts, examining himself as a Chicano and as someone who did not yet speak Spanish, and who expressed feeling guilty about his lack of linguistic connection to his heritage, but who depicted Mexico and images of Indigenous Latin America across his artworks. Remy’s slow grinding of the pigments for his retablo, which began after Jaime introduced the pigments as connected to Indigenous homelands, further points to the ways that he was working to embody his heritage as part of his interactions as he engaged in examining his own identities as Chicano and Mexican American. These ideas were further reinforced in his retablo, where he had cut out and pasted the word culture directly underneath the empty sections of his illustrated heart. Read from left to right, the retablo suggests that Remy wanted to “create solutions” that ended with culture. The artists on our team noted that surrounding something unfinished with these words suggested that Remy’s solutions to emptiness might be his culture. Jaime suggested that Remy’s empty spaces also reminded him of a prison. Contextualized by the violence perpetuated by Trump’s immigration and customs enforcement (ICE) in the U.S. against people of color, our team began to read Remy’s artworks as material moves to both historicize himself in relation to his culture and to read the world in the face of constant threats to himself, his family, and others who looked like him (Freire, 1970). Looking across the artifacts he created, Remy’s artwork offers an illustration of the ways he situated himself analeptically within the histories of art in his culture, contemporarily as a political and historical actor (Stamatis et al., 2024), someone who could leverage histories toward new and better futures (Gutiérrez et al., 2019), and proleptically alongside his mentors to project himself as a protector in the world he imagined for his future (Cole, 1996; Nasir et al., 2020; Polman & Miller, 2010).

In this paper, we seek to examine the potential for using artmaking as both method of engagement and set of objects for building and seeing identities. In future work, we hope that scholars will examine the ways youth across sites take up artmaking to develop more historicized notions of themselves and their cultural pasts. However, our analysis also pointed us to realize the ways that artmaking can create boundary objects (Akkerman & Bakker,
