# Beyond Linear Learning: The Role of Epistemic Strategies in Students’ Trajectories of Explanations Construction

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Mechanistic explanations are essential in science education, helping students understand phenomena through exploring entities and their interactions. This study examines how high school biology students construct mechanistic explanations iteratively and how epistemic strategies shape their learning trajectories. Using the AIR theoretical framework, we analyzed students’ strategies within trajectories as they explored the tanning mechanism through an intervention involving “black box cards”. Data from 65 students revealed three distinct learning trajectories: linear, outcome-centered, and non-linear. Each was correlated with different combinations of six strategies, with simpler strategies more common in outcome-centered groups. ## Introduction

Mechanistic explanations are essential in science b ecause they help scientists understand, control, and predict phenomena (Craver & Darden, 2013); therefore, they have become significant in science education (NGSS Lead States, 2013). These explanations focus on the mech anisms underlying natural phenomena by describing entities and activities organized in a specific sequence from initial conditions to final outcomes (Craver & Darden, 2013). From an early age, students demonstrate an ability to reason mechanistically and a tendency to prefer mechanistic explanations (Shtechman et al., 202 5). When students encounter complex explanations, they perceive them as more scientifically valid than simpler ones (diSess a, 1993; Kapon, 2017). However, many students encounter challenges in constructing these explanations, such as identifyin g and reasoning about unobservable entities or failing to perceive gaps between stages in a mechan ism (Bachtiar et al., 2022). Another challenge lies  in the iterative nature of constructi ng mechanistic explanations. Explanation constructi on is described as an iterative process involving repeated identification of gaps, or “black boxes”, unpacking them, and linking them to one another (Krist et al., 2019; Haskel-Ittah, 2023).

Although the iterative nature of constructing mecha nistic explanations is well recognized, it remains unclear why students unpack certain black boxes whi le leaving others unexplored. To address this quest ion, we examined two aspects of students' unpacking process es: the order (trajectories) in which students unpa cked specific black boxes and the strategies facilitating their unpacking (Chinn et al., 2014). Mapping students' learning trajectories and the strategies facilitating them p rovides insight into the  diverse ways students navigate information while constructing explanations, and it may also indicate varying levels of epistemic knowledge about mechanistic explanations, as not all trajectories a re equally productive. Therefore, analyzing these t rajectories— examining the process of construction step by step along with the strategies behind it—can reveal potential gaps in students’ epistemic understanding and abilities.  This, in turn, can help educators better support s tudents in integrating the epistemic elements necessary to construct mechanistic explanations.

## Theoretical framework

Chinn et al.’s AIR framework identifies three components of epistemic thinking: aims and values (A), representing objectives; epistemic ideals (I), the criteria for evaluating these aims; and reliable processes (R), referring to strategies used to achieve epistemic aims (Chinn et al., 2014). We use the term "strategies" to describe the reliable processes students use to construct their mechanist ic explanations. For example, an aim may be to expl ain the mechanism underlying the phenomenon of lung cancer caused by smoking and the ideal is that a good explanation links between cause and effect. Several strategies could be employed to achieve this aim. One strategy  might involve identifying all entities that contribute to  the phenomenon, while another might focus on a sin gle entity, examining its properties and activities within the mechanism. The strength of the AIR theoretical fram ework, in the context of mechanistic explanation, lies in its  ability to examine the epistemic elements and skills that lead students to unpack specific black boxes over others.

This analysis is part of a broader study on epistemic elements; however, this paper specifically examines epistemic strategies guiding students' trajectories in constructing mechanistic explanations.;

## Research questions

1. What are students' trajectories when constructing mechanistic explanations?

2. What are the strategies that are underlying students' trajectories?

## Methods

Four biology classes (n=65) from grades 10-11 in public high schools were selected, including one coeducational secular class and three male-only classes from state-religious schools.

The study incorporates an intervention focused on t he tanning mechanism, chosen for its relevance to c urricular topics such as genetics, making it suitable for integration at various teaching stages. Since the mechanism itself is not explicitly part o f the curriculum yet involves components that are a ll included within it, exploring this mechanism provid es a unique opportunity to examine how students con struct mechanistic explanations. Moreover, it off ers valuable insights that can inform our understanding of students' learning processes within the curricular context.  The intervention included two expert-validated qualitative research tools used by students working in groups of 3-4 for approximately 90 minutes. Our data included results from 16 groups (65 students). The first tool, a digital questionnaire, guided students to create an initial mechanistic explanation of tanning, to evaluate it, to select a question from a set of “black box cards” (research tool 2), and to iteratively revise their explanations based on the information revealed on the card, completing four cycles in total. The second tool is physical (not computational) black box cards, wi th mechanistic questions and answers about different parts of the tanning mechanism (Oren & Bartek, 2007). Each c ard presented a question on one side and its mechanistic answer on the other, representing t he black boxes on one side and an unpacked mechanis tic explanation on the other. These mechanistic explana tions were adapted to high school science students.  For instance, terms such as “melanocytes” and “keratinocytes” were simplified to “internal” and “external skin cells,” and the process was broken down into 17 sequential questions from cause (sun radiat ion) to effect (tanning).  Throughout the study, the emphasis was on the learning process rather than on the quality of students' final explanations, focusing on the epistemic strategies they employed in constructing these explanations. Since each question card corresponded to a specific  part of the mechanism (closer to the cause, middle, or closer to the effect), data on students’ progres sion trajectories were based on the sequence of car ds selected during the task. To identify students' strategies for constructing a mechanistic explanation, Students’ justifications for selecting specific cards, in response to the question, 'Why do you think flipping the card will help you construct the explanation?' were analyzed. We performed a content analysis of students' answers. For this analysis, we first used a top-down approach by coding strategies that align with Russ et al. (2008). Following this, we applied a bottom-up coding process to classify the remaining data that could not be assigned to any of the top-down codes. Finally, we examined the strategies within each trajectory to explore their interrelations.

## Results

We identified three distinct trajectories:

• Linear forward or backward movement: Four groups followed a structured sequence, either progressing

forward through the mechanism from cause to effect or moving backward from effect to cause. This trajectory involved systematically selecting questions that addressed each part of the mechanism in order.

• Non-linear movement: Five groups selected questions in an inconsistent sequence, jumping between

different parts of the mechanism. For example, some  groups began with questions about intermediate processes between the cause and the effect, then mo ved to processes closer to the cause, and finally addressed processes closer to the effect. Others st arted at the cause, skipped to the effect, and late r returned to explore intermediate processes.

• Outcome-centered movement: Seven groups focused primarily on questions related to the final stages of

the tanning process, particularly those addressing changes in skin color at various levels of organization, such as the molecular, cellular, and tissue levels.

Students’ strategies were grouped into five mechanistic strategies, aligned with the work of Russ (2008) and Krist et al. (2019), and one non-mechanistic strategy that emerged from the data analysis:

1. Linking Start and Termination Conditions: Students focused on bridging the gap between start conditions

(cause-sun exposure) and final conditions (effect-darker skin), as seen in statements like, “The card will help us understand how the sun changes skin color.”

2. Unpacking Observable Effects: This strategy involve d focusing on tanning as a visible change in skin

color, with responses such as, “This will explain the process of color accumulation in skin cells.”;

3. Exploring Relationships Between Entities: Students searched for connections between linked entities, as

reflected in statements like, “We’ll understand how  the sun’s rays damage DNA, building a more complete answer.”

4. Focusing on a Specific Entity: This strategy involv ed focusing on a specific entity and unpacking its

properties or interactions. For example, “Learning about DNA damage will help us write a more accurate explanation.”

5. Forward and Backward Chaining: Students sought to understand what occurred before or after a certain

process. For instance, one group stated, “Because m elanin is synthesized inside the inner skin cell, a nd we want to know why it moves to the outer skin cell.”

6. Accumulating Information: In this non-mechanistic strategy, students aimed to add more in formation

without specifying the type of information they wer e seeking. For example, students mentioned, “It can add information to help better understand the process.” Since strategies guide the movement along trajector ies, we sought to identify a link between specific strategies and specific trajectories. Given that each trajecto ry contained multiple groups, and each group employ ed several strategies, we calculated the distribution of strategies within each trajectory. The findings indicate that the distribution varied across the different trajectories (Fig. 1). In the linear forward or backward trajectory, five of six strategies were observed. "Unpacking relationships between entities" was most common (50 %), with students aiming to explore connections, as  in understanding how the sun affects melanin synthesis. "Linking start and termination conditions" was also notable (31%), as students connected causes (e.g., the sun)  to outcomes (e.g., color change). The other three strategies were less frequent (about 6% each), reflecting a fo cus on connecting processes from cause to effect.  In the outcome-centered movement trajectory, all six strategies ap peared, with "unpacking observable effects" (25%) and "accumulating information" (21.4%) being the most common. Students emphasized visible outcomes, such as skin color change, and sought additional informa tion without specific details. Other strategies app eared less frequently, with "chaining" being rare (3.6%), suggesting that students primarily collected information related to the visible outcome.  In the non-linear trajectory, "chaining" was most common (30%), as students l inked prior knowledge to preceding processes, like how DNA dama ge triggers melanin synthesis. Other strategies occ urred less frequently (15% each), with "unpacking observa ble effects" being rarest (10%).  This trajectory involved students skipping between different points and connecting black boxes to build their mechanistic explanations. ## Discussion

Constructing explanations has been described as an iterative process. This process includes considerin g lower levels of organization, identifying gaps (black boxes), unpacking these gaps by identifying and character izing entities, and connecting them from cause to effect (Krist et al., 2019; Haskel-Ittah, 2023). This study aimed to characterize this process by examining students' trajectories while constructing explanati ons and the strategies they use to shape them. Curricula often seem to assume linear learning processes (Taber, 2018). However, closer analysis of students' learning processes, compared to the sequences outlined by curricula, textbooks, or teachers, reveals that learning is not necessarily linear. Inst ead, students follow a mosaic of progressions that occasionally; do not align with the sequence of knowledge presented to them (Lombard et al., 2018). In our research, we asked students to construct mechanistic explanations independently to identify progression patterns. Through this analysis, we identified three trajecto ries: (a) a linear trajectory, where students systematically unpacked black boxes in a consistent  order from cause to effect (or vice versa); (b) an  outcomecentered trajectory, where students focused on the outcome and observable effects, unpacking related black boxes; and (c) a non-linear trajectory, where students moved flexibly be tween causes and effects. While these may not be the only possible trajectories, identifying multiple ones shows that students independently explore and unpack black boxes in varied, not solely linear, ways.

Our analysis shows that the iterative process descr ibed by Krist et al. (2018)  - considering lower levels of organization, unpacking entities and activities, and linking them-is necessary for unpacking but is insufficient for connecting a mechanism's starting point  to its endpoint. This is because it may leads students to focus on specific areas of the mechanism without addressing how these areas relate to the overall process. In o ur study, groups following linear and non-linear trajectories moved across the mechanism from cause to effect. However, groups on an outcome-centered trajectory unpacked only the final part of  the mechanism at various levels of organization and did not explore other parts. This suggests that the iterative process of unpacking is  insufficient to guide students in unpacking black boxes across the entire mechanism because it does not help them determine what to unpack and what to leave as black boxes.

We examined the epistemic strategies that shaped th e identified trajectories by applying the AIR theoretical framework (Chinn et al., 2014). We identified six strategies used throughout the process, which were used to different extents  by groups moving along different trajectories. We p ropose that the differences in strategies employed are influenced by two main factors: studen ts' epistemic knowledge of strategies and their prior knowledge about the mechanism. In the following sections, we will discuss these two. Students' epistemic knowledge of strategies Students' epistemic knowledge of strategies and their ability to apply them can vary significantly. Some strategies, such as accumulating information or unpacking observable effects, are considered simpler to apply. Accumulating information involves access ing and adding details that were not previously inc luded in the explanation. This strategy does not require sorting information (e.g., by relevance or usefulness), making it less compl ex than strategies that require both accessing and sorting.  Unpacking observable effects, while involving both  accessing and sorting information, is still considered simple r because it focuses on information directly tied t o observable outcomes. Consequently, these two strategies are re garded as easier to apply. In contrast, reasoning a bout unobservable processes and linking them to observab le outcomes is more challenging (Wilensky & Resnick, 1999). Given this variation in complexity, it is important to consider how students select and use strategies. In our study, students in the outcome-centered group predominantly employed more straightforward  strategies, even when multiple strategies were available. This sugge sts that students may prefer strategies that are ea sier to apply or those with which they are more familiar. Convers ely, students in the linear and non-linear tracks employed more complex  mechanistic strategies, demonstrating higher episte mic competence. According to Barzilai and Chinn (2018), developing proficiency in epistemic strategies requires practice rather thandirect instruction alone.

Our findings highlight the importance of providing students with opportunities to independently apply strategies and strengthening their ability to choose appropriate strategies effectively Students' prior knowledge of the mechanism As noted, strategy choice reflects students' episte mic knowledge and prior knowledge about the mechani sm, gained from past experiences or during unpacking. W hen prior knowledge is limited, students often star t by accumulating information before using more complex strategies to connect parts of the mechanism. The influence of prior knowledge on learning is well-established (e.g., Linn & Eylon). However, our stud y demonstrates how the knowledge acquired during learning affects both  the learning process and the strategies students c hoose to apply. For instance, when  students gain information about multiple entities i nvolved in a m echanism, they may shift their focus to seeking information about the interactions between these entities. Conversely, wh en students acquire information about a single entity, they may  concentrate on deepening their understanding of th at entity’s role in the mechanism by searching for additional related information. In summary, this study illustrates how students emp loy various strategies to unpack mechanisms, underscoring the importance of equipping them with a repertoire of strategies and guiding them in sele cting the most suitable one for specific contexts. Students can more effectively explore mechanisms and deepen their mechanistic reasoning by choosing and applying diff erent strategies. Supporting this process involves encouraging students to independently construct mechanistic explanations through activities that actively engage them in building and analyzing mechanisms.;
