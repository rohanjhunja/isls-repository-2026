# Assessing the Effectiveness of Automatic Feedback in Self-Assessment for Enhancing Student Learning from Mistakes

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning from mistakes is essential for students' learning, with self-assessment being a key method. Existing self-assessment tools help students reflect on their mistakes but lack automatic feedback. Thus, this study investigates the impact of a newly designed self-assessment tool incorporating automatic feedback, leveraging knowledge graphs to enhance student learning from mistakes. A quasi-experiment was conducted with 51 middle school students to compare the new tool against traditional self-assessment methods lacking automatic feedback. The findings show that the new tool significantly enhanced academic performance, especially for students with lower prior knowledge.

## Introduction

Learning from one’s own mistakes is essential in education, as errors are an inevitable part of the learning process (Swartz, 1976). Correcting mistakes allows students  to fill knowledge gaps and enhance academic perfor mance (Pan et al., 2020). However, learning from mistakes  can be challenging, as errors sometimes hinder rat her than promote learning (Zhang & Fiorella, 2023). Self-assessment, which involves comparing, reflecting, and evaluating one’s own work, has been identified as a n effective strategy to facilitate learning from mi stakes (Panadero et al., 2016; Yan & Carless, 2022; Yan et al., 2023). Nevertheless, unsupported self-assessment can be ineffective, leading to inadequate mistake correcti on and limited academic progress (Panadero et al., 2012). To support students in self-assessment, scaffolds like rubrics and scripts have  been used to guide reflection and adjustment of learning behaviors (Panadero et al., 2013; Panadero et al., 2023). However, these tools lack automated feedback on the quality of self-assessments and are unable to provide personalized follow-up resources aligned with students’ self-regulation goals. As a result, students often need additional instructional feedback from teachers (Yan et al., 2023; Zamora et al., 2018). Given the typically low student-to-teacher ratio, providing timely individualized feedback is challenging and labor-intensive (Hao et al., 2022). While generative AI tools can assist with feedback, students often lack the prompt liter acy to effectively guide these tools for step-by-step self-assessment, and the reliability of such feedback remains a concern (Hwang et al., 2023; Jia et al., 2024). Without quality external feedback, improvements in students ’ self-efficacy, self-regulation skills, and academic performance are often limited —especially for those with low prior knowledge who r equire further guidance (Panadero et al., 2016; Yan et al., 2023). Thus, this study aims to explore whether automatic feedback provided by a new self-assessment tool based on a knowledge graph can enha nce students’  academic performance compared to traditional tools lacking such feedback. We also seek to determine whether the effectivene ss of automatic feedback is moderated by students’ prior knowledge levels. Specifically, we address the foll owing research questions: RQ1: Does automatic feedback improve students’ learning performance when learning from mistakes? RQ2: How does prior knowledge influence the effectiveness of automatic feedback in self-assessment?

## Participants

The study was conducted at a middle school. Two 7th grade classes with a total of 51 students participated in the study with 30 from Class A and 21 from Class B (randomly set A as the experiment group), with 23 females and an average age of 12.3 years (SD=0.469).

A newly developed Tool with automatic feedback based on a knowledge graph A newly developed self-assessment tool with automatic feedback, functioning as an assistant cha tbot, was used in this study to facilitate students' self-assessment and reflection on their mistakes within an online learning system. Students locate their incorrect answers in the mistakes notebook inte rface and click the "Mistakes Assessment Analysis" button to access the self-assessment assistant tool. The tool guides students through six stages: 1) Preparation: Reviewing incorrect answers and unders tanding the problem description.  2) Review: Reviewing and identifying underlying knowledge points. 3) Reflection: Comparing with reference and pinpointing mistakes based on problem-solving steps.  4) Self-Explanation: Analyzing the causes of mistakes.  5) SelfRegulation: Setting goals to overcome mistakes. 6)Feedback: Receiving and processing automatic feedback. The primary difference between this new tool and traditional ones is the automatic feedback provided in the final step (see Figure 1). This tool is specifically designed for junior hi gh school mathematics. In terms of the mechanism of providing automatic feedback of the new self-assessment tool, this study first built the knowled ge graph of typical mistakes (using linear equations in middle math education as an illustrative example). This gr aph structurally represents knowledge points, related questions, sol ution steps, typical student mistakes  (along with strategies to overcome the mistakes), and high-quality micro-lesson resources. Therefore, when students make self-assessments in each solution step, it can be connected with corresponding mistakes and  high-quality microlesson resources. ## Procedure, instrument

Firstly, students from both groups completed a pre-test, and then engaged in self-assessment learning activities to address the wrong questions identified in the pre-test (this study takes learning linear equations for example). During this process, the experimental group utilize d the new self-assessment tool which can provide automatic feedback, while the control group employed a tradit ional self-assessment tool without automatic feedback. Finally, students from both groups again completed a post-test. Pre-and Post-Tests consist of 11 items (max score: 55 points) to assess knowledge of linear equations, which were developed in collaboration with two mathematics teachers with ten years of teaching experience. These items assess three aspects including concepts, computation (solving linear equations) as well as applications of linear equations. The pre-test and post-test are homogeneous tests.

## Results

RQ1: Does automatic feedback improve students’ learning performance when learning from mistakes?

Firstly, this study calculates the overall learning gain and three dimensions of learning gain by post-test minus pre-test. The overall learning gain, conceptual learning gain, and computational learning gain did not satisfy t he normal distribution requirements, while the applica tion learning gain did (p=0.741 > 0.05; p=0.33 > 0. 05) and satisfied variance homogeneity requirements (p=0.36  > 0.05). Therefore, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the overall  gain, conceptual  gain, and computational learning gain between the two g roups. For the application learning gain, an independent samples t-test was conducted. The results indicated no significant differences between the experimental and control gr oups in each sub-learning gain (conceptual: p=0.397> 0.05; computational: p=0.089> 0.05; application: p=0.201>  0.05). However, a significant difference was found  in the overall learning gain (p=0.039< 0.05) (see Table 1). The experimental group showed a significantly higher overall learning gain (16.4) compared to the control group (11.9). This suggests that students using the new s elfassessment tool with automatic feedback achieve bet ter academic outcomes  development than those using traditional self-assessment without feedback.

In order to investigate the effect of students’ pri or knowledge level on their academic performance du ring selfassessment, this study divided each group into high VS low according to the mean scores of the pre-test. Therefore, there were four categories: the experimental group-high, 13 students, the experimental group-low, 17 students; the control group-high, 9 students, and the control group-low, 12 students. As shown in Figure 2, we can see that the low-level students in the experimental group have overall higher learning gai n (Mean=21.4) compared with the other three types of students. Especially for the learning gain in computation and applicatio n, those with low prior knowledge in the experiment group receive better gain than those wit h low prior knowledge in the control group (Mean: 4.94>1.08; Mean: 6.12>5.08), and it al so greater than that of those with high prior knowl edge regardless of group. In terms of learning gain in concept, although those with low prior knowledge in the control group achieved the highest learning gain (Mean=10.4), the difference between them with those in the experimental group (Mean=10.3) is small. In sum, it can be inferred that students with low pr ior knowledge can benefit more by self-assessment with automatic feedback when learning from error, especially computational knowledge.

## Discussion and conclusion

This study found that the experimental group demons trated significantly greater learning gains compare d to the control group, highlighting the effectiveness of the new self-assessment tool with automatic feedback based on a “typical errors” knowledge graph. Unlike traditiona l self-assessment tools, which lack automated, personalize d, and timely feedback, this tool provides immediate, specific guidance that enhances learning outcomes. This finding aligns with prior research (e.g., Panadero  et al., 2023; Yan & Carless, 2022), which emphasize s the importance of follow-up feedback after self-assessment. However, unlike instructor-dependent feedback, the automatic feedback in this study is more personaliz ed, detailed, and timely, which likely contributes to its effectiveness. By providing immediate external input, students can compare feedback with their self-assessments, correct biases, and adjust their learning strategie s, emotions, and cognition more effectively, as sug gested by Bailey & Austin (2006) and Sargeant et al. (2008).  Notably, this study contrasts with findings from Pi nedo et al. (2023), where feedback did not significantly improve perfor mance, possibly due to its indirect and non-specific nature. In contrast, the tool in this study directl y addresses errors, provides evaluative feedback, a nd offers actionable learning suggestions, such as  skill improvement videos and self-regulation advice.  These findings suggest that direct, detailed, and timely feedback from automated tools effectively enhances learning after selfassessment. In addition, this study found that students with low  prior knowledge significantly improved their academic performance after completing self-assessment activities with automatic feedback, compared to students with high prior knowledge. This contrasts with stud ies like Panadero  et al. (2023), which suggest that students with higher prior knowledge benefit more from self-assessment. The key difference lies in the inclusion of automatic feedback in this study, which enhances th e accuracy of self-assessment and provides clear, objective references, particularly benefiting low-level learners. This aligns with findings by Carrol l (2020) and Liu & Hwang (2024), which highlight that timely, targeted feedback can significantly aid novice learners. Because lowachieving students have more room for improvement, their learning outcomes show greater gains from thi s approach. These results suggest that providing feedback to low-and intermediate-level students can significantly enhance their academic outcomes and should be a focus for educators. In summary, this study aimed to improve self-assessment tools by addressing their limitations in providing automatic feedback. To achieve this, a new self-assessment tool was designed to identify mistakes and offer automatic feedback. A quasi-experiment was then conducted to evaluate its effec tiveness on students’ learning performance. Results highlight that the new self-assessment tool with automatic feedback outperforms traditional self-assessment tools without feedback in enhancing stud ents' academic performance, particularly for those with lower prior knowledge, thereby achieving greater learning gains. The findings provide valuable insights for practical educational applications and implicat ions for teachers and educational researchers. For educators, the study suggests incorporating self-assessment tools with automatic feedback into their  t eaching strategies, especially for students with lower prior knowledge,  to enhance academic performance and persistence in  selfregulation. For instance, when conducting a lesson on mistakes, teachers can first have students complete  a selfassessment of their errors in the system and receiv e feedback. Then, they can further explain the reas ons for the mistakes in class. For researchers, this study underscores the importa nce of further exploring personalized feedback mechanisms and their differential effects on various student groups. However, this study also has some limitations. Firstly, this study had a relatively small student sample size; further study should recruit more participants. Secondly, this study only assessed the academic performance after the self-assessment, further study may need to explore whether self-assessment with automatic feedback can enhance othe r abilities, i.e., self-regulation skills. Finally, based on this short paper, future research will focus on optimizing feedback content and having a longer intervention duration to validate its usefulness.
