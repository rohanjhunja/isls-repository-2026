# An Interactive Tool for Using Digital Music Note Manipulatives to Compose Rhythms

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Previous research utilized paper music note manipulatives to help jazz students brainstorm rhythms to use during improvisation. However, there were issues that can be mitigated by digitizing these music note manipulatives. Therefore, this interactive demo presents a website with these digital music note manipulatives, called Measure, to show math, music, and STEAM educators how to use them with their students. Problem context In standard music notation, longer notes often take up less space on the staff than groups of shorter ones, and therefore, notation and audiation are not always logically related, causing students’ confusion (Gordon, 2012). Because of this, in previous work, the first author conducted a study at a summer jazz camp which included paper music note manipulatives were designed and students were taught to use them to brainstorm rhythms to use during improvisation (figure 1). While the use of these manipulatives did impact students’ performance (DiCioccio, in review), there were some practical and educational obstacles involved that can be mitigated by digitizing these manipulatives. For instance, cutting the music note manipulatives was a time-consuming task that can be eliminated by putting the manipulatives on the computer. Also, the manipulatives were very small and thus would regularly fall on the floor and get lost, which would not be an issue with digital manipulatives. Educationally, there is concern that disconnect from audiation while teaching notation, students will not learn to read the notation fluently and make auditory meaning of it (Gordon, 2012). In my aforementioned study, I taught the students the meaning behind the notation, connecting the symbols to sound, and when students independently brainstormed rhythms, I encouraged students to clap, hum, or play the rhythms on their instruments. However, the paper manipulatives themselves did not enforce the auditory connections like digitized manipulatives can. Figure 1 Paper Music Note Manipulatives E xisting tools There are computerized fraction manipulative tools that do not make connections to music, such as the Fraction Strips tool on Toy Theater (Gaspard et at., 2018). Also, there are computerized tools for music composition. For instance, there are tools such as Sibelius (AVID, 2019) that allow the composer to input standard music notation, which do not utilize fraction bars to help users visualize note duration. Also, there exists tools such as GarageBand (Apple, 2025), which allow the user to record musical performance, either on the built-in computer instruments or in real life. Garage Band allows users to clip and organize sections of these recordings but does not allow the user to see the duration of or manipulate individual notes, unless the user records each note individually. Furthermore, there are such tools that are designed for the purpose of music learning. For instance, there is a tool called Learning Music that guides students through lessons on music theory and composition using computer music tools. While this tool utilizes rectangles to show note duration like the tool in this demo, it does not connect these to the numerical values of the note length, nor does it allow for note durations that are smaller than the beat or other, more complicated rhythms, such as swung notes, triplets, or dotted notes. In addition, there is a computerized tool for music composition called Impromptu that, while not built intentionally for interdisciplinary math and music composition, has been studied to be used in this way (Bamberger & DiSessa, 2003). Finally, Toy Theater also has an online tool for composing rhythms using fraction bars. However, Toy Theater’s tool does not use standard music symbols for note durations, nor does it provide time signatures or measures to fit the fraction bars into. Also, Toy Theater’s tool only provides options for whole, half, quarter, and straight eighth notes. Thus, the current tool fills a gap by linking fraction bars, a visual representation of the fractional length of notes, with their corresponding standard music symbol and putting this in context of the measure and time signature, as well as expanding the note options to include dotted notes and swung eighth notes. Theoretical framing As referenced in the problem context, this tool draws upon Gordon’s (2012) Music Learning Theory, as this tool aims to make connections between symbols and audiation explicit. Furthermore, this tool draws upon Constructionism (Papert & Harel, 1991), as it encourages “learning-by-making” by guiding students to learn standard music notation by facilitating rhythm composition. Without the tool, students could compose a rhythm by writing various symbols for durations of notes in standard music notation without learning the auditory meaning of the symbols that they wrote. However, the combination of fraction bars, standard music notation, and auditory feedback is designed to help students learn the meaning of the symbols as they compose. Tool overview To use Measure (Goehrig & DiCioccio, 2026), the digital music note manipulative tool, users will first pick a time signature and tempo. To input a rhythm, they will click on the button with their desired note duration and then click on the measure where they would like to place the note. This will display a fraction bar with the fractional length of the measure for the user to visualize the length of the note. It will also have the numerical fraction and standard music notation symbol so that the user can connect the numerical value with the music notation. If a user tries to input a note in a measure where there is no room, the tool will split the note into two tied notes, where the first measure is filled and the second measure completes the note duration, teaching the users which fractional values sum to the time signature. Once users have input their rhythm, they can press a button which will play the rhythm so that they can hear how it sounds. We opted to put this tool on the internet so that it is free and available to the public and able to be used on any operating system. Uses of the tool This tool has several educational uses. First, it can be used by music educators to teach students how to read rhythms by visually connecting the length of the note to its symbol. To do so, music teachers can have students input rhythms into the tool from pieces they are studying in class and have students practice performing the rhythms along with the tool or after watching and listening to the tool. Also, it can be used by music educators as a tool to teach improvisation and composition, as in my previous work (DiCioccio, in review), where I demonstrated how to use the manipulatives and tasked students to use them to compose their own rhythms. Furthermore, it can be used by math educators to provide an auditory representation of fractions to deepen understanding of the meaning of fractions and fraction operations. To do so, math teachers can represent fractions and fraction operations in the app alongside other representations when teaching, and they can encourage students to model problems in the app when working independently. S etup for the interactive session We will first demonstrate the tool and give an overview of its educational purpose. After that, we will ask the attendees if they have any questions. For in-person attendees, we will then have iPads available for conference attendees to test the tool out for themselves. We will also share the link to the tool with attendees on Whova for online participants to use during the session and for all participants to have available for their future use. Furthermore, we will have paper manipulatives available for in-person attendees to test and compare them to the digital tool. We will share a link for all attendees on Whova with a PDF of the paper manipulatives (figure 1) so they can print and use the paper manipulatives in the future, and for the online participants to print and use during the conference to compare to the digital tool. Discussion While the use of digital manipulatives has its advantages, there are also limitations to their use in comparison to paper manipulatives. First, digital manipulatives are not as tangible as paper manipulatives, making the manipulatives more abstract and possibly losing some of their educational value. This has been shown in several studies that compared student achievement from learning fractions through concrete and digital manipulatives, many of which found that student achievement was higher for those that learned fractions with concrete manipulatives compared to digital manipulatives (Al Farra et al., 2014; Vessonen et al., 2021). However, others have found that digital manipulatives are at least as effective as concrete manipulatives, and that they are more time efficient (Mendiburo & Hasselbring, 2014). Another concern is that while technology is increasingly available (Gray et al., 2021), not all schools will have access to the necessary technology to use the digital manipulatives, where paper can be more accessible due to its lower cost.
