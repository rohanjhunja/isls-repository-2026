# Moments of Pedagogical Feedback With Explanations: Interactional Foundations for Supporting Educational Dignity

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores pedagogical design processes of educational dignity by analyzing data across three learning contexts and configurations–a facilitator and student in a museum-based tinkering environment, a professional choreographer and dancers in their dance studio, and two students in a high school history class–to show how moments of feedback with explanations can contribute to the process of coordination in joint activity, invite meaningful participation, and support educational dignity. In particular, we present three case studies to illustrate two primary findings: (1) through moments of feedback with explanations, participants invited one another to embody forms of meaningful participation, and (2) these invitations to engage in meaningful participation created a collective, educationally dignifying conceptual space in which activity was continued forward. These findings offer developing insights into the how and towards what ends of designing for educationally dignifying learning environments, contributing to discussions on the revisioning of equitable education systems.

Continuing the Reimagining of Equitable Education Systems In the U.S., we are experiencing a public contestation around the design of education contexts sparked in part by the pandemic and growing racial justice movements. Nasir, Bang, and Yoshikawa (2021) reflected on this moment as “a once-in-a-generation opportunity to make real  progress toward a system of education that truly s erves all students, providing the kinds of teaching and learning that enrich lives, spur innovations, and reflect a deep and abiding commitment to equity” (p. 54). As more dist ricts move back to in-person schooling, we have the opportunity–and responsibility–to continue this rei magining by offering empirical analysis on the pote ntial cultivation of equitable education systems in the 21st century. We heed this call by exploring the pedagogical desi gn processes of educational dignity; “educational dignity is the multifaceted sense of a person’s val ue generated via substantive intra-and inter-personal learning experiences that recognize and cultivate one’s mind, humanity, and potential” (Espinoza et al., 2020, pg. 326). Understanding educational dignity as an interaction al and discursive achievement, we offer data across  three learning contexts to demonstrate one way meaningful  participation and educational dignity unfold and m ove activity–or learning–forward. Specifically, we attu ne to moments of feedback with explanations, which reflect moments of one person sharing their perspective, ideas, questions, or concerns on another's participation/activity using forms of narrative, descriptions, and definition. We argue that moments of feedback with explanations can contribute to processes of intersubjectivity in joint activity and serve as indicia of educational dignity. This finding offers developing insights into the how and towards what ends of designing for educationally just and dignifying learning environments, offering empirical examples that expand current and future discussions on the revisioning of equitable education systems.

## Literature Review

As more research incorporates the needed political interrogation of the ways sociopolitical systems sh ape and reinforce oppressive hierarchies, we continue beyond this interrogation by asking what systems or values should guide the design and joint activity in learning environments. For those committed to the creation of equitable and just education, researchers have explored the diffe rent aspects of research that must be reconsidered:  from the design of the research collaboration (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016), the organization of social structures (Gutiérrez, 2008), to the ways of seeing learning as interrelat ed social and conceptual processes (Vossoughi et al., 2020); the cultivation of equitable education requires a m ultifaceted, multimodal, multi-epistemological inte gration. Across these different categories, we see a consist ent theme: social interaction. What is the role of social interaction in pedagogically uplifting and sustaining educational dignity? Espinoza and colleagues (2020) provide a detailed f ramework for seeing educational dignity as social accomplishment mediated by meaningful participation. Dignity itself is not something that can be creat ed or destroyed, everyone has dignity; rather, the goal is to design dignity-affirming learning environments that uplift and feed students’ dignities. The emphasis on social interaction in the experience of educational dignity has been explored through axiological innovations (Bang et a l., 2016), epistemic heterogeneity (Warren et al., 2020), ICLS2022 Proceedings 1585 © ISLS embodied pathways (Vossoughi et al., 2020), and ped agogical improvisation (Jackson, 2021a). Across the se works and others, researchers continually evidence educational dignity as an ethical process supported  through the moment-to-moment interactions. As the Learning Sciences continues to build a diverse pool of empir ical evidence on what educational dignity looks like in practice, in this paper we aim to contribute with new examples that additionally address towards what ends (Philip, Bang, & Jackson, 2018) these dignity affirming pedagogies work. The participants providing feedback across ou r three empirical examples not only provide feedbac k interactionally, responding to other participants in situ, but pedagogically structure their explanations in dignityaffirming ways. Surfacing the interactional structure of this pedagogical move across contexts and configurations is essential for continuing to cultivate equitable educational spaces. Methodological Approach This paper explores the following research question s: what did moments of feedback look like across th ree different settings organized around values of educa tional dignity? How did these experiences with feed back contribute to –if at all–the fostering of educational dignity? To answer these questions, we explore three different learning contexts drawn from each authors’ individu al research projects: between a facilitator and hig h school student in a museum-based tinkering environment (Author 1), a choreographer and dancers in a professional dance company (Author 2), and tablemates in a high school US history class (Author 3). Although each data set was collected around different questions of learning and development, we took similar approaches in the development of data sourc es, which included developing field notes, video-recording participants’ activities, and conducting interviews that foregrounded documenting interaction from a sociocultural perspective of learning (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003; Lave & Wenger, 1991). Having the benefit of similar  data sources, we aimed to answer our research question b y drawing on Matusov’s (1996) participatory notion of intersubjectivity as a process of coordination among participants in joint activity. With joint activity as the unit of analysis, we traced activity directions by dividing the video data into activity directions, and transcribing verbal and nonverbal actions, such as gestures, to explore the ways the participants structured the interactions (Erickson, 1992). Then, we analyzed how the qualities of feedb ack with explanations contributed to the process of coordination between participants to progress activity. We also analyzed how explanations served as in dicia of meaningful participation and educational dignity in  joint activity using micro-ethnographic analysis (Erickson, 1992). To this end, we looked beyond the binary of adult-centered and child-centered models of pedagogy and explored joint activity as a pedagogical practice where all participants involved were seen as active co-participants (Vossoughi et al., 2021a). Since intersubjectivity, meaningful participation, and educational dignity are distinct interactional achievements, our findings suggest that moving joint activity forward does not automatically imply that participants’ contributions are taken seriously or that their full personhood is seen and considered. Similarly, affirming learners’ dignity does not necessarily en tail moving joint activity forward. Thus, our empir ical contributions help reveal how these three notions c an come together when participants in joint activit y offer feedback to move activity towards more meaningful and just ends.

## Findings

We present three case studies to illustrate two pri mary findings: (1) through moments of feedback with explanations, participants invited one another to e mbody forms of meaningful participation and (2) the se invitations to engage in meaningful participation created a collective, educationally dignifying conceptual space in which activity was continued forward. The potent ial for pedagogical action to shape dignifying lear ning processes is an argument made by previous scholars (Vosoughi et al., 2020); through these findings, we offer both an extended view of what one pedagogical practice–feedback with explanations– mediated forms of educational dignity across different social configurations and organizations of learning. Furthermore, by offering  examples from different data sets, we highlight the potential for this practice to further activity and extend the learning of not just the individual, but the collective experiencing of (and contributing to) educational dignity. The first case study draws from a 16-week ethnographic study of a museum-based tinkering program for high school students called the FabYouth program (E chevarria, 2021). During an interaction between Ger ard (facilitator) and Darrius (student), Gerard crafted  an explanation that invited Darrius to think and l earn about troubleshooting, the flow of electrical energy, the manipulation of the flow of electrical energy, the terminology of short-circuiting, and the cause of short-circuiting. I found that Gerard, the facilitator, provided feedback with explanations that contributed to the process of coordination in joint activity, invited meaningful participation, and supported educational dignity. Feedback went beyond correct or incorrect evaluations and included explanations with learning as the endpoint. Therefore, explanati ons were directed towards understanding the roots o f unintended outcomes. As a result, Gerard provided feedback with explanations in a way that affirmed students’ potentials by creating openings for students to learn and iterate to work through activities. During this time, Rebecca’s posit ion changed from peer-dancer to leader-choreographer and she shared how she approached developing choreography in pedagogical ways, drawing on her own experiences as a dancer to create dignity-affirming experiences for the dancers. Rebecca saw being transparent, or providing feedback paired with explanation, as caring for her dancers and intentionally giving them “what she feels like she needs from choreographers” (Field Note, 12/14/17). I found that Rebecca communicated changes to developing choreography typically not tied to dancers’ abilities, but rather because of the experimental approach to creating ch oreography. Thus, when being transparent and provid ing feedback with explanations, dancers did not have to locate the reason for changes in any particular dancer. Instead, they focused their energy on the process of creation and fine-tuning as a group. Rebecca’s feedback also invited multiplicity in dancers’ interpretations of feedback, further demonstrating the co-creation of choreography. Finally, the third case study is a moment between two students, Bolaji and Sarah, in which feedback and explanation encouraged new forms of collective, dig nifying participation in a US history class. Drawin g on a year-long ethnographic study (Jackson, 2021b), I found that during discussions, Sarah became a primary source of knowledge and validation to Bolaji by way of fee dback and explanation. Sarah’s explanation created the conditions for supporting Bolaji’s educational dignity by reinforcing and continuing his meaningful participation in the developing collective inquiry when he quietly asked a question. Because Sarah made this pedagogical move towards her peer, she ensured Bolaji was still join tly-coordinated in thinking, allowing for both of t heir participation in the large-class discussion to cont inue. Therefore, Bolaji was able to not only receiv e a detailed explanation for his question but also have further validated in his positioning as a meaningful participant alongside Sarah in the class’ collective thinking, demonstrating how engaging feedback and explanation functioned to create and extend educationally dignified activity.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Feedback with explanations invited participants to embody forms of meaningful participation characteri zed by agency through positioning (Davis et al., 2020) and moved activity forward. In the first case study, Gerard offered feedback that positioned Darrius as a learner by affirming that Darrius was prepared for conceptual engagement with the circuit with proper support. Gerard did this by accompanying his feedback with an explanation that took the shape of storytelling and personification of electrical energy. As a result, Darrius was invited to engage with the feedback to understand the roots of unintended outcomes. In addition, although Darrius did not ask  for an explanation, Gerard provided one. In doing so, Gera rd showed Darrius that his wondering was important and affirmed Darrius’ potential to learn the concept of short-circuiting and circuit design, when it was not necessary to move the activity forward in that moment but would be generative in the future for Darrius. In the second case study, Rebecca positioned the da ncers as co-participants, exploring the unknown of the final choreography through a process of experim entation and multiplicity of interpretations that o pened up creative possibilities. Rebecca also carefully crafted her feedback to communicate that revisions to choreography were not critiques of dancers’ abilities, but rather a part of the process to create a larger cohesive creation together. This is important because using dancers’ artistic choices and ideas in a piece of choreography can feel exploitative when dancers are positioned as needing to be good enough to fit a choreographer’s vision rather than contributing to the co-development of a shared inquiry.

In the third case study, Sarah’s explanation answer ed Bolaji’s question by first exploring shared knowledge and then providing a summary of a lecture. As a result, Sarah positioned Bolaji as having the answer to his own question, and then she provided Bolaji w ith a summary of a lecture to further support his m emory, thinking, and participation in whole-class discussions. Together, all three cases show how the explana tions that accompanied feedback worked to support participants  by way of positioning to invite meaningful partici pation that invoked participants’ agency and affirmed their dignity as valid and generative members of these respective learning communities. Feedback with explanations al so invited participants to engage meaningfully, cre ating a collective, educationally dignifying conceptual spa ce (Espinoza et al., 2020) in which activity is con tinued forward (Matusov, 1996). These three cases illustrate how feedback with explanations can serve as mome nts to shift activity in immediate ways, but can also serve as reference points in the future, suggesting that feedback not only drives activity, but it can also serve as a se ed for continued learning (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003; Lave & Wenger, 1991).

We are pedagogically inspired by the humanizing end s these instances of feedback with explanations move towards in their respective learning ecologies  and we are excited at the new theoretical and peda gogical questions they raise. In particular we are interest ed in the new relations that these forms of feedbac k with explanations can support (Vossoughi et al., 2021b) in terms of the interwoven nature of intersubjectiv ity and ethical relations between participants and the domain, such as how Gerard positioned Darrius as a learner while ICLS2022 Proceedings 1587 © ISLS positioning himself as someone who Darrius could wonder with and seek support to learn more about circ uitry. We are also interested in the motivation for when moments of feedback are paired with explanations in moments of joint activity because our three case studies il lustrate times when participants supported one anot her without being asked to go into such depth. For example, Darrius did not ask for an explanation and neither did Rebecca’s dancers. While Bolaji did ask a question, it was in directly asked, and Sarah decided to answer his que stion pedagogically. However, she could have given Bolaji a one-sentence answer that would have omitted the kinds of positionings that her original explanation provided, or even positioned Bolaji in negative ways. Ultimately, we are inspired to continue to learn from and with our  research collaborators and embark on new efforts to support the engagement of meaningful and dignity-affirming participation interwoven in pedagogical moments of feedback with explanations.
