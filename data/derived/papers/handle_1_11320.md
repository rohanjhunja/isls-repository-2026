# Storiza: A Platform to Support Children’s Oral Reading Fluency Development with Generative AI

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Oral reading fluency (ORF) is critical for reading comprehension and success in academic and professional goals. Children in elementary school frequently struggle to develop ORF, particularly those with specific learning disabilities, such as dyslexia. This study describes the initial development of an app to enable students to improve ORF by practicing reading stories created with generative AI according to the student’s interests and cultural background and aligned with a reading curriculum. The study describes the collection of the teacher, parent and school administrator interviews and qualitative analyses that provided information to create the app’s prototype. The app follows universal design principles and learning experience design, incorporating a range of assistive technologies to ensure the success of students with learning differences. Future directions for development are discussed.

## Introduction

Ensuring that elementary children, both with and without disabilities, achieve a high level of oral reading fluency (ORF) is vital to reading proficiency and reading comprehension (Paige, 2020).  ORF is the ability to read quickly, accurately, and with expression. It is especially critical for child success in STEM disciplines and positive outcomes across an individual’s life span. Specific ally, developing ORF can have a positive trickle-down effect on children, increasing the likelihood  that they will graduate from high school, become employed, and ear n a living wage —all factors that can impact overall health and well-being (Lansford et al., 2016). Developing children’s ORF eases the task of reading and frees cognitive resources that a child can then use for comprehending text (Cooper et al., 2024). Rather than laboriously decoding text word-by-word, a fluent reader can concentrate on extracting meaning, which is the ultimate purpose of reading. Thus, ORF is a  particularly important instructional target.

Although it is optimal for children to work directl y with a teacher or tutor on ORF whenever possible, the reality is that public schools frequently have scarce resources (e.g., teaching or tutoring staff,  teacher instructional minutes, budget dollars) to provide sufficient opportunities for many children to adequately develop the level o f reading proficiency that would enable them to exp erience academic success across their lifespan. Technology applications that provide children with individual-specific feedback to support ORF development can help fill a critical resource gap.

The objective of this paper is to describe the init ial development of Storiza, an app that engages the creativity of elementary school children to facilitate reading by enabling students to create and illustrate their own stories. Storiza uses the GPT-4o large language model  (OpenAI., 2024) to provide children with stories that are aligned with a reading curriculum (Lane & Contesse, 2022)  that is used at scale nationally and internationall y (Gage, 2023). The app fills a void in ORF technology for students with specific learning disa bilities, such as dyslexia. It distinguishes itself through its ability to foster  student creativity and maintain engagement. Storiza follows universal design for learning principles  and learning experience design  (Schmidt & Tawfik, 2022), incorporating a range of assistive technologies to ensure the success of students with learning differences. Storiza is designed for in-school or at-home use. In-school use can be done using a range of modalities, including wholeclass, small-group, or individual instruction.

## Theoretical framework

ORF is a critical reading skill important for  reading comprehension. Moreover, the ability to rea d with ease and accuracy can enhance reading enjoyment and engagement. Students can improve their ORF by simply reading connected text such as passages or books (Maki & Hammerschmidt-Snidarich, 2022). Instructional techniques that support ORF development  include Repeated Reading, which involves repeatedly  reading a passage of text multiple times to “practice” the text, or continuou s reading, which consists of reading continuously w ithout repetition (Hammerschmidt-Snidarich et al., 2019). Meta-analyses and research syntheses support the effectiveness of these methods  (Maki & Hammerschmidt-Snidarich, 2022; Therrien, 2004). Overall, reading practice strategies result in positive ORF growth with moderate to large effect sizes. Despite the importance of developing ORF, many students do not get enough reading practice. Reasons can include  inattention, disinterest, or simply avoidance because of the effort involved (Stanovich, 1980). Teachers need reading resources (e.g., passages, stories)  that help to increase  students’ reading interest, engagement, and, ultimately, their success. For example, books  on topics that represent students’ interests and cultural backgrounds can increase interest, which can, in turn, lead to increased reading engagement. In addition, given a student’s current decoding skills, texts mu st be appropriately difficult. Texts that are too difficult can be frustrating, while texts that are too easy provide little challenge or opportunities for growth. The recent development of large language models (LLM) (Wang et al., 2024) has allowed the possibility of developing advanced learning technologies where students provide prompts to create stories they want to read at the right difficulty level. The architectural framework for the development of Storiza is the one shared by Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS)  (D'Mello & Graesser, 2024), which can provide  personalized learning materials, turn-by-turn scaffolding, and just-in-time feedback (VanLehn, 2011). There are extensively studied ITS focusing on reading comprehension (e.g.,  iSTART; ITSS) (McNamara et al., 2022; Wijekumar et al., 2020), but few target ORF  (Mostow et al., 2013), and none use generative AI t o provide stories that align with students' interests and cultural backgrounds and attempt to boost engagement with reading materials.  Allowing children to create their own stories for reading practice also addresses the limitation of current ITS that rely o n algorithms that do not fully account for child preferences (Mo stow et al., 2013). Furthermore, personalization of  stories for reading practice promotes culturally relevant instruction because the linguis tic and cultural backgrounds of children can be reflected in the stories they create. Therefore, personalization of stories can increase engagement and maintain child motivation. It also encourages children’s creativity and indirectly helps with writing because choosing story topics  is an aspect of writing.  This study  addresses the following research question: What challenges encountered by reading teachers, coaches, tutors, and specialists can be addressed by an AI-powered reading app?

## Methodology

A multidisciplinary team was tasked with investigat ing how generative AI could support student’s developme nt of ORF within a school context. The first step implemented by this team was to develop a set of hypotheses that they aimed to evaluate. These hypotheses are shown in Table 1 in the results section. Data to evaluate the hypotheses were obtained from 30-minute semi-structured interviews with elementary school teachers, reading specialists, tutors, and coaches  about the  methods and challenges of teaching  reading. A few interviews with parents of dyslexic children were also conducted to understand the challenges parents face in supporting children’s reading practice at home. The team also interviewed school administrators to understand the technology adoption process in schools and districts. The team developed protocols for these interviews  with broad, open-ended questions that were presented to all participants, as well as specific follow-up questions to be used as needed (1). Detailed notes were taken from these interviews, but they were not recorded. Sample There were 85 parti cipants, including  30 teachers, 12 reading specialists, six reading tutors, nine special educational resource teachers, six coaches, 16 school administrato rs, and six parents. These participants  were geographically dispersed in 11 states in the U.S., including participants from Hawaii and Alaska, as well as Canada and Hong Kong. The teachers and reading tutors were  recruited by posting an announcement on the Facebook group page of the UFLI Foundations curriculum (Lane & Contesse, 2022), with over 250 thousand members at the time the posting was made. School administrators were recruited for the interview through the contacts of the team members.

Analyses To analyze the qualitative data collected through semi-structured interviews, we utilized MAXQDA (Kuckartz & Rädiker, 2019)  for thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Each hypothesis was systema tically coded into predefined categories based on teacher responses and their alignment with the research questions. This  process involved multiple iterations to ensure the reliability of codes. For instance, statements that reflected challenges in engaging students were tagged under "Challenges for  ORF\Students lack motivation and concentration," while suggestions for improving ORF practice were mapped to "Suggestions\increase engagement." These codes were further aggregated to get insights into how teachers perceive the potential o f generative AI and technology integration in supporting ORF development. In addition to manual coding, we leveraged MAXQDA’s frequency analysis tools to identify the prevalence of key themes. Codes such as "Practice for ORF\Use of technology tools" and "Practice for ORF\Gamified learning strategies" frequently emerged, i ndicating strong interest in leveraging technology and interactive methods for ORF practice. Code co-occurrence analysis allowed us to uncover relationships between challenges and strategies, re vealing actionable insights for future tool develop ment. The notes were also examined to identify quotes representative of support for each hypothesis. We also analyzed the interview notes with BERTopic, which is a topic modeling algorithm that combines pre-trained language models with clustering analysis mo dels (Grootendorst, 2022). To analyze the topics in our interview texts using BERTopic, we first preprocess ed the interview texts and split all the texts into  individual sentences. Next, we used the pre-trained language model all-MiniLM-L6-v2 as the sentence embedding model for BERTopic and separately analyzed two main categories of interview questions: questions about general topics related to oral reading fluency education and quest ions about the use of online platforms in oral read ing fluency education. During this process, we continuously adj usted BERTopic's hyperparameters to achieve higher coherence scores.

Table 1 shows the percentage of interviews that supported each hypothesis and quotes exemplifying the support. Teachers shared that their biggest challenge is keeping students engaged, especially those with dyslexia, dysgraphia or learning difficulties, mentioned 15 t imes. Another 15 mentions highlighted how hard it i s to find materials that work for students at different level s because resources are often not  engaging, kid-friendly, or flexible. Limited school support and the requiremen t to stick to prescribed materials added to the dif ficulties. To tackle this, teachers frequently turned to gamified  teaching strategies, transforming instructional ac tivities into engaging games. Examples included card games, rewar d charts, and other incentive-based systems to capture students’ interest. Many teachers (21 mentions) emphasized finding decodable passages and using platforms like IReady and Lexica to locate more en gaging content. Seventeen teachers talked about per sonalized instruction, using the UFLI curriculum, assigning homework, and modeling correct reading with error correction. Some teachers also provided suggestions for improving tools like Stori za: Ten teachers recommended incorporating visual elements, such as images, to keep students engaged, while five emphasized focusing on specific ORF skills like prosody. More than ten teachers stressed the i mportance of a simple, intuitive software design th at could be used both at school and at home, accommodating both students and teachers. These insights shed light on practical ways to enhance ORF practices and the tools supporting them. The results of the BERTopic analysis confirm that teachers have trouble engaging students in oral reading fluency practice, particularly those with specific learning disabilities  (hypothesis 1). Using games or high-engagement activities may be one way for teachers t o deal with this type of problem. Students may be m ore interested in materials that are interesting and interactive, but the "materials students helped create" mentioned in the hypothesis did not explicitly appear in BERTopic results (hypothesis 2). Teachers will actively search for technology, resources, and various reading materials at the appropriate reading level for students to improve oral reading fluency practice  (hypotheses 3, 4, 5). Meanwhile, teachers are likely to use an AI reading app to create stories collaboratively with the whole class using a projector, for small-group instruction, and as homework assignments (hypotheses 6, 7, 8).

Beyond the hypotheses we mentioned, the results of the BERTopic analysis also show other current situations: Teachers often share and obtain oral reading teachi ng resources through social media and attach great importance to the assessment and recording of students' oral reading fluency. Limited time, budget, and resources may limit teachers' use of AI reading app. They have clear needs for specific technological equipment and tools and may need more user-friendly and economical solutions. It is crucial to design attractive content when usi ng AI to assist teaching, whether designing classroom teaching or homework. In addition, some students lack learning resources at home, which affects the effectiveness of homework.

## Discussion and conclusion

Based on the results of the interviews, the team (2) created a list of features of the Storiza reading app mentioned by teachers and prioritized them according to both frequency in interviews and the previous literature  on the effectiveness of strategies to improve ORF. Using l earning experience design (Schmidt & Tawfik, 2022), we created personas of elementary school teachers, spe cial education resource teachers, and reading speci alists who would use the reading app with their students, mapp ing their motivators, goals,  and behaviors.  We then used GPT-4o to create a prototype of Storiza, which creates and illustrates stories based on child-generated prompts. Users can choose to generate stories between 75 and  400 words. Several teachers mentioned the importan ce of decodable stories, so we attempted to generate deco dable stories aligned with lessons 10 to 128 of the  UFLI Foundations curriculum (Lane & Contesse, 2022). This ensures that teachers adopting the curriculum can use the stories generated by Storiza to support student rea ding practice in the same sequence used in the clas sroom. We also added assistive technologies to Storiza, such as word highlighting, text-to-speech (Wood et al., 2018)  and text chunking (Casteel, 1990), to support the success of children with learning differences. A Storiza user session goes through the following steps: (1) Prompt teachers, tutors, or parents to select a target lesson from the UFLI Foundations curriculu m; (2) Prompt students to write story topics and ke ywords from their own interests and cultural backgrounds; (3) Use generative AI to create and illustrate a story that matches the student’s interests and is aligned with  the scope and sequence of the curriculum; and (4) Prompt students to read their story and offer support for students with specific learning disabilities, such as chunking of text and modeling.

Storiza extends traditional ITS design through its ability to provide highly personalized reading materials, although it does not incorporate a full suite of learning supports as found in more mature ITS (Xu et al., 2019). Preliminary tests indicate that the stories created  incorporate the phonemes being targeted in each lesson of the UFLI Foundations curriculum. However, the performance seems to vary across lessons, and the story size requested, with shorter stories working best for earlier lessons. Development of additional features that would enable Storiza to provide support aligned with modern ITS technologies, such as formative measurement of ORF and fine-grained support while reading,  is possible in the future.  Thus, the Storiza app is a promising tool for providing children with effective reading practice to develop ORF (3).
