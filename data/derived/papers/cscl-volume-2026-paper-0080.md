# “It Wasn’t So Outside of the Box, But It Wasn’t Something I Would Have Thought About”: AI as a Creative Collaborator in Student Problem Solving

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how students experience collaboration with generative AI during creative problem-solving. Using post-interview data, we analyze students’ perceptions of creativity and agency across three human –AI writing modes. Findings show that stude nts treated AI as a responsive collaborator that expanded ideas and supported refinement, but was most valued for structuring and improving efficiency. The richest collaborations occurred when students remained active in both generating and refining ideas. These results highlight how generative AI differentially supports divergent and convergent thinking and suggest that balanced, iterative interaction is key to preserving agency in human–AI co-creation.

Creativity is widely understood as a universal human capacity involving the ability to imagine possibilities, connect disparate ideas, and produce work that is both novel and meaningful (Runco & Jaeger, 2012). Far from being limited to the arts, creativity plays a central role across the sciences, humanities, and professional domains (Brown & Kātz, 2009; Kaufman & Beghetto, 2009). Given its importance, researchers have long sought to understand the cognitive mechanisms that support creative thinking and how  emerging technologies might scaffold the creative process (Lubart, 2005; Sawyer & Henriksen, 2024). One promising area of inquiry is the use of generative AI as a collaborative partner in creative writing. Creative writing involves imaginative thinking, emotional expression, and the deliberate crafting of language that draws on imagery, rhythm, and metaphor to give form to abstract ideas (Hanauer, 2012). Writing a poem, for example, typically unfolds through iterative phases of idea generation, exploration, revision, and refinement, processes that align with established models of creative problem solving (Guilford, 1967; Treffinger & Isaksen, 2005). Across these phases, writers move fluidly between divergent thinking, generating multiple possibilities, and convergent thinking, selecting, evaluating, and organizing ideas into a coherent composition. Because creative writing integrates affective, cognitive, and aesthetic dimensions, it provides a rich context for examining how AI may mediate or extend human creativity. Unlike earlier tools that primarily automated routine tasks, generative AI systems can engage interactively by offering alternative perspectives and linguistic variations that can stimulate new lines of thought, while the writer retains control over which suggestions to adopt, revise, or reject.

These capabilities, however, raise important questions about AI’s role in creative work and how learners negotiate agency within human– AI partnerships. Recent  studies suggest that AI can enhance originality and fluency by expanding idea spaces and supporting creative risk-taking (Davis et al., 2024). Others caution that heavy reliance on AI may reduce cognitive engagement, lead to formulaic outputs, or diminish individuals’ sense of ownership (Dhillon et al., 2024; Frich et al., 2024). Examining students’ iterative collaboration with generative AI during creative writing tasks provides a way to understand how creativity emerges through this human– AI partnership, including how ideas are generated, taken up, and transformed across different configurations of human and AI contribution. In this study, we investigate how students experience collaboration with AI in creative poem-writing tasks that vary in their balanc e of human and AI contributions. Specifically, we  ask: How do students experience and perceive collaboration with generative AI during creative problem-solving tasks? Creative problem solving and human-AI co-creation Creative problem solving involves applying creative thinking within the purposeful act of addressing a challenge or creating a product (Treffinger et al., 2023; Treffinger & Isaksen, 2005). Central to creativity is the dynamic interplay between divergent a nd convergent thinking (Guilford, 1967; Cropley, 2006). Divergent thinking supports exploration and the generation of multiple ideas, while convergent thinking enables the evaluation and integration of those ideas into coherent outcomes. Creativity flourishes when individuals can fluidly move between these two modes, expanding and narrowing ideas through iterative cycles (Runco & Acar, 2012). Poetry writing exemplifies this process, requiring cycles of idea generation, revision, and reorganization to communicate 499 meaning and evoke emotion, while also supporting metaphorical reasoning, emotional expression, and perspective taking (Faulkner, 2023; Hanauer, 2012; Januchowski-Hartley et al., 2018). Because creativity rarely occurs in isolation, researchers have increasingly highlighted its social and collaborative dimensions (Paulus & Nijstad, 2019; Sawyer & Henriksen, 2024). Collaboration allows individuals to access diverse perspectives, critique i deas, and co-construct solutions (Paulus & Nijstad, 2019; Sawyer & Henriksen, 2024). In human –AI co-creation, these collaborative processes are reconfigured, as AI systems contribute suggestions, analogies, and structural possibilities, while humans interpret, evaluate, and contextualize them (Davis et al., 2024). Prior work suggests that the level of AI support plays a critical role in shaping both creative outcomes and learners’ sense of ownership. While more substantial AI contributions can improve quality and productivity (Dhillon et al., 2024; Huang, 2025), it is unclear whether students who partake in creative and personal endeavors (such as writing poetry) consider the productivity gains of AI to offer authentic improvements to their creative processes. From a learning perspective, collaboration between learners and AI raises questions of epistemic agency, including who generates ideas, evaluates contributions, and determines what counts as a satisfactory outcome (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2020). Additionally, students’ perceptions of AI, including its capabilities, trustworthiness, and perceived “social” role, further shape how they engage with it, and influence whether they critically probe, adapt, or defer to its suggestions (Rezwana & Ford, 2025). As such, examining how students collaborate with AI during poetry writing provides a focused lens for understanding how creative processes, agency, and co-creation unfold in human–AI interaction. This exploratory qualitative study (Creswell & Poth, 2018) examined how students experience and perceive collaboration with generative AI, specifically ChatGPT, during creative problem-solving. The study was conducted virtually over Zoom in summer 2025 across three consecutive days, with one-hour sessions each day. Students engaged in poem-writing tasks under three modes of human–AI collaboration. The tasks were designed to be comparable in difficulty while varying in social context to support creativity without privileging any session. Seven students (all girls), aged 11– 16, participated (four middle school, three high school). This all-female composition reflects the available participant pool for this pilot and is not intended to represent broader gendered patterns. All participants completed the three collaboration modes, engaging with AI as a human– AI dyad. All reported prior experience with generati ve AI and familiarity with basic prompting. Sessions were facilitated by the authors, who introduced prompting strategies and guided students through each activity. Parental consent and participant assent were obtained in accordance with university ethical guidelines. The first session introduced generative AI and prompt engineering, including strategies such as clarity, context, and iterative refinement. Students also completed a brief “prompt detective” activity to highlight how prompts shape AI outputs. Students then completed three creative tasks reflecting different collaboration modes, ranging from more student-directed to more AI-mediated engagement, each emphasizing different phases of divergent and convergent thinking. In AI-supported expansion, students independently brainstormed and drafted a poem on teen mental health, then used ChatGPT to generate additional ideas before independently synthesizing both sources into a final piece. This mode foregrounded divergent thinking by expanding the range of ideas available to students while still requiring them to evaluate and integrate these ideas into a coherent product. In AIsupported synthesis, students independently brainstormed ideas for a poem on belonging or pressure, then used ChatGPT to organize their ideas into a structured plan before composing their final poem. Here, AI primarily supported convergent thinking by helping students evalua te, organize, and refine their ideas into a cohesive structure. In AI-initiated drafting, students prompted ChatGPT to generate a poem about wildfire safety, iterated on prompts to refine outputs, and optionally revised the final product to personalize it. This mode shifted much of the initial idea generation to the AI, positioning students to engage more critically in convergent processes such as evaluating, selecting, and modifying AI-generated content. These conditions allowed us to examine how varying levels of AI involvement shaped students’ engagement in divergent and convergent thinking, as well as their perceptions of collaboration, authorship, and creative ownership. Data sources and analysis Data sources included a pre-survey, three screen recordings of sessions, seven chat logs, and two group post-interviews (approximately 102 minutes total). The pre-survey captured prior AI experience. Screen recordings documented students’ reflections after  each task. Chat logs captured prompts and interaction strategies. Post-interviews, conducted over Zoom in groups of three to four students, focused on students’ perceptions of AI’s role in their creativity, originality, and efficiency. For this paper, we draw primarily on post-interview data, using other sources to contextualize students’ reflections. Interviews were transcribed using Zoom’s automated service and manually reviewed for accuracy. We conducted an inductive, two-cycle coding process (Saldaña, 2021) 500 informed by our focus on collaborative and creative problem solving. In the first cycle, we generated 15 descriptive and value-oriented codes (e.g., AI as partner, preference for personal authorship, appreciation of AI support). In the second cycle, we grouped these into broader categories related to perceptions of AI as collaborator, its role in shaping creativity and originality, and students’ sense of authorship and ownership. Our analysis attended to both within-mode patterns and cross-mode comparisons to examine how different levels of AI involvement shaped students’ experiences and perceptions. Experiencing AI as a responsive and respectful collaborator Across the three collaborative modes, students interacted with AI as if they were in dialogue with a creative partner that could listen, respond, and adapt to their intentions. They often spoke about AI  as having a sense of awareness and sensitivity that shaped the rhythm of collaboration. Several students were surprised by what they perceived as empathy or emotional connections in the AI’s responses. For example, Amy (pseudonym) remarked, “It was interesting how it [AI] was able to capture human feelings… it kind of captured the essence of what a human could be going through.” Others appreciated AI’s ability to honor conversational boundaries and respect instructions. Emma described how working with the AI in the first two modes felt like working with a peer because it could remember and sustain shared understandings across interactions. She elaborated: “One thing that was surprising was that I would tell the AI not to write the actual poem … and I was skeptical if the AI would be able to remember … and surprisingly it did.” Students interpreted this consistency as evidence that the AI was participating in a mutual process, holding context and maintaining respect for their creativ e space. Students also appreciated how the AI remained responsive even when their prompts were unclear. Eva explained, “Even if my question was kind of confusing, it still answered my question the way I wanted it to.” For her, this quality mirrored a supportive peer who strives to understand or anticipate her thinking process. These interactions reflect students’ positioning of AI as a collaborative partner in co-creation, where meaning emerges through iterative dialogue rather than one-sided generation.

AI appeared most helpful for refinement, structure, and efficiency When describing how AI shaped their creative process, students emphasized that it contributed new perspectives and stylistic suggestions that expanded their ideas. During the AI-supported expansion mode, they highlighted AI’s capacity to provide “solutions I did not think about,” generate evocative metaphors, and refine the tone and rhythm of their poems. Tracy shared that while she generated most of her own ideas in the first two collaboration modes, AI “made my writing a bit clearer, but without losing my original vision.” Eva echoed this, stating that AI made her work “more original because it gave me… unique ideas that I didn’t really think of before.” These comments suggest that students viewed AI as a creative partner that provided access to ideas and expressions beyond their usual imaginative range. In this way, AI-supported expansion primarily contributed to divergent thinking by broadening the range of ideas and perspectives available to students.  Yet not all students shared this sense of the AI providing unique contributions. Some remained cautious about the originality of AI-generated ideas. For instance, Amy noted that while the AI’s ideas helped her see the “big picture” and organize her thinking, they were not “completely out of the box.” Across participants, AI was most valued for widening the range of creative possibilities and helping them articulate ideas they already held, rather than for inventing entirely novel concepts.

Although students appreciated AI’s ability to extend the fluency and flexibility of their ideas, many noted that their most productive exchanges with AI occurred during convergent phases, when both partners worked to synthesize and bring structure and meaning to their shared ideas. Many described brainstorming as “pretty easy,” but recognized AI’s contribution in “putting it all together” and helping structure their poems more cohesively in the second mode. Amy explained, “It was pretty easy for me to brainstorm and come up with bullet points of ideas. But I think where AI really became helpful was like putting it all together … It helped  me structure it better.” Emma similarly noted that AI’s primary role was “refining” her ideas, while Eva appreciated the constructive feedback and editing support it provided. Students described their final outputs as “more polished,” “proper,” and “professional.” Importantly, students who reported lower confidence in poetry writing found AI especially helpful during brainstorming and synthesis. Tracy admitted, “I was having problems finding rhyming words… [because] I'm not that great at writing poems [but] AI helped me.” Efficiency also emerged as a recurring benefit. Across tasks, students noted that writing “took way less time with AI.” T hese patterns suggest that students perceived AI not only as a source of ideas but as a tool that more strongly supported convergent processes of refinement, coherence, and efficiency within creative problem solving. 501 Negotiating control, quality, and collaboration Students’ experiences also highlighted the negotiation of epistemic agency within human– AI co-creation, particularly in how control over idea generation and refinement shifted across modes. While students valued AI’s feedback and efficiency, they also encountered challenges in maintaining creative control. Some reported moments when AI’s suggestions diverged from their intentions. Tracy described having to “redirect it and give it something different” when AI’s initial feedback did not align with her vision. Eva also noted that AI-generated poems were “shorter” and lacked “small little details” compared to those she co-created more actively. She explained: “My task three [poem], which was mostly the AI, was a shorter poem … in task two and one, it was kind of a longer poem just because that was more like my ideas.” For this student, efficiency came at a creative cost, with the poem being cleaner but lacking emotional depth. Additionally, when students struggled to articulate their thoughts in prompts, they expressed frustration, with Nora noting, “I wish it could just like read my mind because sometimes I didn't know how to put the things I was thinking into words.” Students differed in which collaboration mode they experienced as most balanced, and their sense of collaboration was shaped more by the quality of interaction than by the amount of AI contribution. Amy felt AI was most collaborative in the second mode when she “spent more time explaining [her] thinking and giving feedback,” describing that process as when the poem “felt the most like what I was trying to write.” For her, the alternation between generating and refining ideas created a “50-50” partnership. Eva and Emma, on the other hand, felt more collaborative in the third mode because they “prompted it a lot more” and saw the AI contributing more ideas and revisions. Conversely, T racy felt most partnered with AI in the first mode, when she and the AI “fed off from each other.” When reflecting on their poems, students consistently linked creative quality to their perceived share of effort and emotional investment. Eva described her first poem as her best because it had “a lot of deep meaning,” while Emma preferred her second poem for showing “a lot of my creativity and imagination.” Despite appreciating the clarity of AI-generated work in the third mode, most agreed that their earlie r, more self-driven poems in the first two modes felt “more creative” and “more complex.” These findings suggest that students’ sense of collaboration was shaped less by how much the AI contributed and more by how interaction supported their agency in both  generating and shaping ideas.

This study examined how students experienced collaboration with generative AI during creative problem-solving, focusing on how different collaboration modes shaped their engagement in divergent and convergent thinking. Across modes, students treated AI as a responsive partner that could extend ideas and support refinement, while also revealing tensions in how AI contributed to, and sometimes constrained, the creative process.  A key contribution of this work is showing how generative AI differentially supports phases of creative problem solving. Consistent with CPS frameworks (Guilford, 1967), students’ independent and AI-supported idea generation reflected divergent thinking, while their use of AI for structuring, refining, and editing aligned with convergent thinking. Although AI-supported expansion broadened idea spaces, students consistently found AI most valuable during convergent phases, where it helped organize, clarify, and finalize their work. This suggests that, in practice, AI functioned less as a source of entirely novel ideas and more as a tool for shaping, refining, and making ideas usable. This tension is reflected in students’ own accounts of AI as “not completely out of the box,” yet still generative of ideas they “would not have thought about.” The three collaboration modes further revealed how shifts in AI involvement redistributed students’ roles. In AI-supported expansion and synthesis, students retained responsibility for generating and shaping ideas, with AI extending or organizing their thinking. In contrast, in AI-initiated drafting, students were positioned more as evaluators and editors. While this increased efficiency and coherence, outputs were often perceived as less original, detailed, and personally meaningful. This helps explain why more balanced modes were experienced as more creative and collaborative. Creativity, in this context, depended less on the amount of AI contribution and more on students’ active engagement in both generating and refining ideas. These findings complicate c laims of AI as an equal creative partner. While students described AI in relational terms, this reflects an interactional experience rather than an equivalent distribution of agency. Instead, AI functioned as a form of adaptive scaffolding, enabling students to move more fluidly between divergent and convergent processes by offering justin-time suggestions, alternative framings, and structural support. From a design perspective, these findings highlight the importance of structuring human– AI interaction to  preserve student agency while leveraging AI support. Tasks that require students to generate initial ideas, engage in iterative exchanges, and evaluate AI contributions appear to better support creative problem solving than those that position AI as the primary generator of content.
