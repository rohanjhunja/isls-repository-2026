# Appropriating Tools to Foster Productive Professional Learning Conversations in a Literacy Coaching Program

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Drawing on sociocultural theory, this study explores how literacy coach-teacher dyads use cognitive tools to foster productive professional learning conversations with respect to evidence-based specificity, establishment of a dialogic stance, and connecting specifics of lesson interactions to broader principles. The results reveal that, overall, the use of cognitive tools increased the quality of the coach-teacher professional learning conversations across all three dimensions. However, the integration of the tools in coach-teacher conversations was not consistent. Implications for coach professional development are discussed.

## Introduction

Engaging in well-implemented dialogic text discussions can enhance s tudents’ reading comprehension  and reasoning skills. Repeated participation in coach-guided reflection around artifacts of practice is key to developing teachers’ adaptive expertise for facilitating dialogic classroom discussions (Lefstein et al., 2020). Developing the coaching skills needed to engage teachers in productive professional learning conversations  that can improve dialogic teaching skills, however, is a challenging  endeavor. Recent studies have revealed the potential of cognitive tools, developed based on teaching princi ples, frameworks, and models, to support  productive professional learning conversations. Drawing on sociocultural theory, the relationship of people toward mediated tools can be characterized in terms of appropriatio n. Appropriation refers to the process through whic h a person adopts the tools available for use in particular so cial environments and, through this p rocess, internalizes the cultural practices associated with these tools (Grossman et al., 1999). Building on this concept, the study explores how coaches appropriated cognitive tools in their conferring with teachers  and how this impacted the quality of the coach-teacher professional learning conversations. Specifically, we examine the following research questions:

1. Where and how frequently are tools used in the coaching routine?

2. How does the use of tools influence the quality of the professional learning conversation with respect

to evidence-based specificity, dialogic stance, and connection of specific  lesson interactions  to broader instructional principles?

## Study context

The study is based on a larger study of the Online Content-Focused Coaching (CFC) model developed at the University of Pittsburgh’s Learning Research and De velopment Center. Online CFC aim s to enhance teachers’ dialogic teaching skills by engaging in cycles of one-on-one, coach-guided reflective dialogues around their videoed classroom discussions (Matsumura et al., 2019). In the coaching cycle, upon receiving the videoed lesson, coaches develop three reflection questions for teachers to respond to, setting the stage for the post-conference. Then, the coach and teacher meet in a post-conference to collectively reflect on the videos. T he post-conference comprises two subroutines: Identifying a Problem Space/Success  (Subroutine 1) and Considering alternatives (Subroutine 2). The purpose of Subroutine 1 is to support teachers to identify successes or problems in their teaching by reasoning about how their pedagogical choice supports or impedes students to achieve learning goals. The purpose of Subroutine 2 is to encourage teachers to reflect on how employing different talk moves in  the context of identified problem space might better support students' thinking. Two cognitive tools-the Framework for Effective Text Discussion (FETD) and Accountable Talk Moves and Functions (ATMF) – are developed for coach-teacher dyads to establish evidence-based specificity, cultivate a dialogic stance, and connect specific teaching aspect s to broad principle s in post-conferences. The FETD specifies the teacher's and students' moves within various dimensions of dialogic teaching, suc h as posing questions to construct the gist and pos ing cognitively demanding inquiry questions. The ATMF summarizes teacher moves under three accountabilities: accountability to the learning community, accountab ility to accurate knowledge, and accountability to rigorous thinking. This tool provides a clear definition for each move and offers language examples that teachers might integrate into their practices.

## Data collection and analysis

The sample for this study consist s of four coach-teacher dyads. All four literacy coaches  are highly trained and possess expertise in dialogic teaching. Four teachers work in different grades: one teacher from the 4th grade, two teachers from the 5 th grade, and one teacher from the 7 th grade. Data were collected from two sources. The first dataset consists of transcriptions from 13 post-conferences. The second data set  comprised 39 written reflection questions and the corresponding teacher responses f rom the 13 coaching cycle s. To analyze the data, the transcripts were first segmented by  the two post-conference subroutines, and then the subroutines were grouped by whether the FETD and ATMF were or were not used.  In addition, when tools were referred to in the written reflection, the related synchronous reflection subroutine was  identified as the subroutine where  the tools were used. In total, 55 subroutines were identified, with 29 of them involving the use of tools. A scoring rubric was developed to evaluate the quality of professional learning conversations within each subroutine, focusing on evidence-based specificity, dialogic stance, and connecting specific instances to broader principles. In terms of evidence-based specificity,  a score of 4 is assigned to a subroutine only when the conversation effectively integrate s identified success or problem  with overarching principles. A score of 3 is assigned when the conversation effectively identif ied successes or problems based on specific evidence of teaching and learning but without connecting to broader principles. A score of 2 is assigned when the conversation only vaguely name s successes or problems. A score of 1 is assigned whe n the conversation fail s to identify successes or problems. In terms of dialogism, the highest score, 3,  is assigned when coach-teacher dyads collaboratively reflected on videoed lessons. Score 2 is assigned when the coach  predominantly undert ook the cognitive work of reflection with some efforts to involve the teacher in a dialogue.  Score 1 is allocated when the coach took a more directive stance without engaging the teacher in the conversation. The first and second authors independently rated all subroutines' conversation q uality, achieving 84% and 95% agreement on evidence-based specificity and dialogic stance, respectively. Discrepancies in scores were further reconciled to achieve consensus. Finally, a descriptive statistical analysis was conducted  to examine whether the use of tools could enhance th e quality of coaching conversations.

## Results and implications

Coaches predominantly used the FETD and ATMF when crafting reflective questions rather thanduring real-time dialogue with teachers in post-conferences. While the tools were designed for both  subroutines, they were more likely to be used to think about alternative pedago gical choices than to support reasoning about the influence of pedagogical choices and student thinking.  The study also  showed that the use of tools increased the quality of professional learning conversations across both sub routines. Specifically, with the support from the tools, the professional learning conversations were more likel y to center around specific evidence of teacher-student interactions and tended to be more dialogic. However, the support pr ovided by these tools was not always consistent, as evidenced by instances where coach-teacher dyads failed to establish connections  from specific instances to broader teaching principles. Especiall y when considering alternative s, only 50% of conversations incorporated the tools connected to specific alternatives to broader dialogic teaching principles. Our study indicated that while cognitive tools hold promises for enhancing coach-teacher conversations, the appropriation of tools by dyads often failed to realize their intended functions and full potential, especially in connecting specific lesson interactions to broader teaching principles. As internalizing the new ways of thinking and acting associated with these tools necessitates a  deep understanding of their conceptual underpinnings (Grossman et al., 1999),  we suggest that coaches could potentially benefit from  professional development that focuses on the strategic use of cognitive tools to improve the quality of their professional learning conversations.
