# Codesign and Translanguaging as Tools to Challenge Monoglossic Spaces in Teacher Preparation

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In an era where knowledge and identities have been dismantled by reductionist views and voices, this paper examines how co-design, a process of shared power and equitable voices, and translanguaging, a framework for identity development and social and linguistic justice, draw from each other to reimagine a teacher credential program. Within the context of redesigning a teacher credential program, we study how co-design processes leverage past ‘pre-service teachers’ expertise as plurilingual individuals and examine hierarchical relationships to disrupt traditional instructor roles. We examine the reproduction of power, privilege, and inequity in educational domains, employing frameworks to affirm diverse identities and challenge monolingual epistemologies. Methods include constructivist grounded theory and critical discourse analysis to understand participants' language use. Co-design sessions with faculty and alumni aim to foster equitable classroom discourse, enhancing pedagogical repertoires and challenging traditional roles to support English Learner/ Emergent Plurilingual (EL/EP) students effectively. Purpose Research highlights the importance of creating spaces for pre-service teachers to evaluate their language and languaging practices and the subsequent impact on lesson design, implementation, and assessment for English Learner/Emergent Plurilingual (EL/EP) students (e.g., Yoon, 2008; España, Herrera & Garcia, 2019; Yilmaz, 2019; Cárdenas et al., 2024). Fostering linguistically inclusive and just classroom discourses ensures students: a) participate in experiences celebrating, embracing, and amplifying bilingualism (Dover & Rodriguez-Valls, 2018); b) achieve academically and socio-emotionally (López et al., 2015); and c) develop critical consciousness (Cannizzo, 2021). This proposal focuses on how two faculty members engage in a co-design process focused on linguistic inclusion and justice with students and alumni from our teacher preparation. The following research question guides this paper: What language and languaging practices guide, persist, and emerge from co-designing and redesigning university courses that focus on linguistic inclusion? This research is part of a larger grant-funded project that examines how pre-service teachers, clinical coaches, and in-service teachers redesign learning experiences to support EL/EP students by reaffirming their identities and including their language and languaging in the core of learning and teaching praxis. Spanning from October 2025 to September 2030, the larger project seeks to leverage past teachers’ expertise in a co-design and redesign course process. We aim to examine hierarchical relationships in learning sites, disrupt typical instructor/pre-service teacher roles, and amplify students’ perspectives through a co-design process. ## Theoretical perspectives

Theories of critical literacy and translanguaging and linguistic justice provide the analytic lenses we use to understand the co-design space discourses and how they intersect with structural, cultural, disciplinary, and interpersonal domains (Collins & Bilge, 2016). Theories of critical literacy (e.g., Freire & Macedo, 1987; Janks,

2010) argue that language forms and shapes experience (Anderson & Irvine, p. 98); furthermore, languaging  is

always situated and contextualized (Zuñiga, 2019). Aligning with other critical literacy researchers, our research continues to work toward the goal of action, which challenges social inequality and is informed by the interrelationships between theories of domination and power, identity and social difference, equity and access, and multimodal design and redesign.

Translanguaging as a concept/construct captures how many named language scholars conceptualize and operationalize and how language users create meaning with their linguistic and semiotic resources (García, 2011). García (2011) argues that when people translanguage, they are using knowledge of multiple named languages and varieties (their linguistic resources) in “a single language system that creates meaning and accomplishes goals” (p. 45). Translanguaging informs and frames our research to examine how language and languaging practices could contribute to or deny identity development, equity-centered practices, and social justice (Dover, RodríguezValls, Ott & Bryant, 2025). Finally, the concept “linguistic justice” (Baker-Bell, 2020) is a useful analytic to ol 1642 because it helps us conceptualize when participants act in ways that disrupt monocultural and monoglossic spaces. Recognizing how systems oppress plurilingualism requires reconciling how society silences or enables language. Collectively, translanguaging emphasizes affirming the identities of culturally diverse students and disrupting processes that privilege White, monolingual epistemologies. Our goal is to identify how, why, when, and where white-gaze ideologies enacted by teachers interrupt and deny emergent plurilingual students’ opportunities to fully contribute to the learning and teaching occurring in their classrooms, in their schools, and in their communities (García-Mateus, Strong, Palmer, & Heiman, D., 2020) Methodological approaches and data sources We shaped this paper by analyzing how co-design sessions shape teachers' identities and languaging. Participants included faculty who teach project courses and alumni from our credential program. Our geographical context is a suburban city in Southern California. Within the credential program, university faculty take leadership roles as “course custodians” for the six prerequisite courses pre-service teachers must take before entering the credential program. Faculty holding a “course custodian” role were in vited to participate in the research project and work with alumni of the prerequisite courses for the co-design sessions (participants were both graduates of the credential program and students still enrolled at the university). In recruiting alumni of the courses, we prioritized plurilingual participants committed to culturally and linguistically sustaining pedagogy and linguistic justice. Participants were suggested by the course custodians and through the Bilingual Authorization program. Co-design sessions occurred over Zoom and in person. Each faculty member met with their co-design group six times. Faculty 1 chose to hold all meetings virtually. Faculty 2 held four meetings virtually and two meetings in person. All sessions were audio and video recorded. Transcripts of co-design sessions were then created. In addition, artifacts from the co-design sessions were collected, and participants, including faculty, were interviewed at the start of their participation in the co-design to develop context on their co-design experiences. We use a constructivist grounded theory approach (Cresswell, 2003), refining analysis through iterative processes. Analytic techniques from critical discourse traditions (Gee, 2002; Halliday, 1978) helped us examine how participants are socialized to talk,  think, act, and use language. Analysis was iterative and recursive. First readings of transcripts involved inductive open coding and thematic analysis (Miles & Huberman, 2014). A descriptive analysis of turn-taking was conducted (see Table 1) to examine t urn-taking and the positioning of identities. Additional rounds of analysis focused on the ways in which talk shaped social identities, power, and ideologies around language.

## Emergent findings

Co-design, an equity-centered practice, and translanguaging, a linguistically inclusive framework, seemed complementary and well-aligned to create spaces where participants felt inclined and comfortable to fully use their linguistic repertoires, thus challenging monoglossia and English-only (EO) discourse. The first sessions of co-design indicated that language and languaging practices continue to be dominated by linguistic hierarchies that place and sustain faculty’s languaging and English as the dominant structures controlling languaging and what “academic” language should be used to convey knowledge. “We need to understand what matters to you.” Though our project framed co-design as a design process focused on collaboration and leveraging students’/alumni’s expertise, in many ways we found the co-design space to mimic a “student-centered” classroom. Faculty held their institutional authority by taking up at least one-third of the discursive virtual space. They facilitated the co-design session through various tools and practices one might observe in a typical classroom: agendas, question-focused prompts, and strategies for participation. Table 1, a snapshot of our descriptive analysis, shows that the average number of “conversational turns” taken by faculty across four sessions ranges from 38% to 62% of the total number of turns taken by all participants. We juxtapose the discursive space that faculty participants take up in the co-design sessions with how Faculty 1 (F1) framed the purpose of alumni's participation in the course redesigns: “What should we be contemplating across all the courses? What do we want students to learn? And throughout all of these discussions, it's like we have a deep, deep recognition that we need you to co-design. We need to understand what matters to you. We need to understand what you care about like I said, I've never taken the course” (F1).

Noting the way that structural power exists in schools, the separation of the student/alumni community from the faculty once again mimics the classroom. Although there were no strict boundaries or rules on how co-design sessions should go, these two groups authentically developed responsibilities for their work. Alumni were asked questions to prompt their memories from the class and given time to brainstorm using a virtual brainstorming tool that all teams used for effortless collaboration.

We did observe one recurring event where alumni seemed to exert more agency in the co-design space: when alumni were specifically asked to draw on their experiences as plurilingual individuals or teachers who supported plurilingual students. In these instances, alumni drew on their experiences as classroom teachers, their experiences in the bilingual authorization program, or their experiences as students who were plurilingual: “You’re being met with opposition, especially like thinking back to when I was teaching ELD my 1st year and I was department chair. I was ELD coordinator for all of this in my 1st year, and so a lot of the other department chairs voiced that they were against translanguaging and that the sole focus should be English only. So maybe, like, some mock scenarios or something along those lines that could help us defend our students' linguistic repertoires, because I was kind of caught off guard. And so, I was like, Oh, this is the real world” (ABP1, 310 codesign team) coordinator. In these instances, alumni built on each other’s talk, which shifted the discourse pattern from a) faculty question → alumni answer to b) faculty question → alumni answering → alumni building on answer → alumni building or asking a question. Students/alumni building on each other’s responses is important, as it disrupts traditional teacher/student discourse, often referred to as an IRE pattern (Cazden, 1998), and in a virtual space, it is a critical way that they hold space and let their expertise shape the redesign  of the course. English-only spaces haunt us Despite over half of each co-design group identifying as plurilingual, the transcripts were made up of 95% English-only discourse. There were three instances across the nine sessions where an alumnus spoke in Spanish. The faculty utilized Spanish at least once per session. Alumni frequently introduced the topic of how schools normalize English. ABP1 in the above quotation speaks to the challenge of working with colleagues who believe that schools’ primary focus should be on English proficiency. Alumni anecd otes from their teaching lives were not surprising to faculty but were grounding in how they thought about preparing students for institutional resistance.

Even for many of the alumni who identified as plurilingual, growing up in a schooling system that privileges English forever haunted them, even in classes with professors where the intention was to learn pedagogy to teach plurilingual students. The co-design sessions created a needed space where alumni could use their experiences as students to potentially shift how pre-service educators develop their own critical consciousness about learning and teaching in historically monolingual environments.

In the words of Jacques Derrida (1998), often language users communicate with a language that is not their own. It seems like both bilingual and monolingual pre-service teachers also learn to use a language that is not theirs: “academic [English-only] language.” Emergent findings from our close analysis of discourse across two course redesigns show that faculty and alumni use  language to maintain institutional roles. We believe that the faculty and students did want to create spaces for translanguaging, but we found that even in the collaborative, ideally coconstructed space of a co-design session, allowing language practices that take on their authentic heteroglossic nature takes time, transparency, and risk-taking on the part of faculty who already hold institutional power. Deconstructing languaging practices and dynamics of power in teacher education programs is a constant struggle educators face, specifically in an era where research is constantly devalued by casual opinions and demode ideologies (Otheguy, García, & Reid, 2015; Alfaro & Bartolomé, 2023). Programs are taught from a monolingualism and monoglossia frame, enclosing, in some instances, the design of syllabi, methodologies, and assessments of pre-service teachers’ knowledge. Knowledge is associated with the dominant named language, English, and “student-centered practices” guided and structured by faculty. The aim of our work is to critically analyze how to dismantle the white gaze lenses that perpetuate the role of faculty members as the listener who controls and manages the right, appropriate, and correct way of languaging. Additionally, we reclaim the concept of co-design as a practice that fosters the idea of a collective classroom discourse collaboratively developed by both pre-service teachers and faculty members.
