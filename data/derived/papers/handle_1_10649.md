# An Exploration of how Adults Guide Play in Ways that Foster Mutual Intellectual Respect: The Cases of a Kelp Forest and a Guess My Pattern

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As a non-binary pedagogical model, guided play involves a delicate dance between child-centered play and adult guidance. Using interaction analysis, we examine two contrasting cases of learning through guided-play activities. Our analysis expands the theory of guided play by articulating how adults can foster and maintain mutual intellectual respect during play across contexts, conceptualizing particular versions of guided play as joint inquiry with children.

## Introduction

Play is an essential part of childhood and is critical for children’s learning and social and emotional development (Fisher et al., 2010), especially in the aftermath of the COVID-19 outbreak (Rogers, 2022). Following the conference theme, “Learning as a c ornerstone of healing, resilience, and community,” we are interested in how adults can guide play in ways that preserve its ima ginative and inventive freedom. Hirsh-Pasek and colleagues

(2009) define guided play  as a way where educators can support learnin g through play. Because guided play

breaks dichotomies between adult-led (e.g., “sage on the stage”) and child-centered (e.g., “guide on the side”) pedagogical models, it can foster mutual intellectu al respect (Rose, 2014). Mutual intellectual respect is characterized as when both parties’ ideas, question s, and activities are taken seriously. This honors children’s educational dignity as their minds, humanity, and c reative potential are valued and expanded (Espinoza, et al., 2020). In this paper, we ask: How can adults guide play in ways that support mutual intellectual respect? Framework: Guided play as a pedagogical model As a non-binary pedagogical model, guided play involves a delicate dance between child-centered play and adult guidance, where adults support, validate, and expand on children’s curiosities, wonderings, and ideas that manifest during play. Adults can guide play by creating opportunities for playful teaching and learning by re-thinking how they design playful learning environments, includin g “the pedagogical decisions, techniques and strate gies they use to support or enhance learning” (Wood, 2004, p.  27). Even more, adults might engage in guided play  by noticing and commenting on children’s activity, ask ing open questions grounded in what children are do ing, playing along with children, or exploring materials in ways children might not have previously considered (Fisher et al., 2010). In this way, the construct of guided  play allows us to move away from the binary of adu lt versus child-centered interactions (Paradise, et al., 2014), ack nowledging that dignity-affirming learning can happen across the spectrum o f child-centered and adult-guided interactions. When adults can guide and co-explore children’s wonderings in play, it can elevate child ren’s agency and sensemaking resources (e.g. gestur es, affect, imaginative embodiment, Keifert & Stevens, 2019). W e view guided play as a form of joint inquiry in which intergenerational collaboration leads to mutual lea rning (Vossoughi et al., 2021). Our across-context analysis examines what it looks like in guided play when adu lts reinforce children’s voices and ideas and  expands the theory of guided play by articulating how adults can foster and maintain mutual intellectual respect during play.

## Methods

To examine how adults can guide play in ways that support mutual intellectual respect, we report on two cases in which adults and children engaged in joint inquiry.  We use interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1 995) to examine the moves adults made during joint inquiry that affirmed children’s educational dignity and po sitioned children as knowledgeable and valuable participants. The cases were selected because they provide uniq ue information on the phenomenon of guided play that f eatures mutual intelle ctual respect (i.e., extreme cases, Flyvbjerg, 2006). This allows us to expand theories of guided play as we look across in-and out-of-school settings. Case selection Both cases were drawn from larger play-centered research projects. We first viewed the ent ire video data corpus across the two studies. This led us to our first ob servation that there was a spectrum of ways adults and children were interacting in these gui ded play interactions. Within this spectrum, we ide ntified two cases of how adults can guide play in ways that support mutual intellec tual support, leading us to analyze focal episodes where both adults and children took each other’s ideas, questi ons, and activities seriously (Rose, 2014; Vossough i et al., 2021). The first case occurred at a progressive elementary school in a mixed-age classroom of 1st and 2nd graders (6-8 years) in California in t he United States. Data comes from a larger four-month-long participatory design-based research study on a play-based curriculum on interdependent relationships in  marine ecosystems, which included activities like art-making, play, costume-making, and pamph let-making. The focal episode examines how the teacher guided play in ways that affirmed and valued children’s ideas during inquiry. The second case is from a corpus of 345 children at a mathematical pla yground, Math-On-A-Stick (MOAS), at the Minnesota State Fair in the United States. Fairs are annual large p op-up leisure spaces. Our focal episode occurs while C helsea (pseudonym) and her Grandpa engage in joint inquiry  while playing an invented activity of “Guess My Pa ttern” at the Tiles and Patterns exhibit.

Interaction analysis Attending to the enactment of educational dignity r equires studying the meanings interactors make in e ach moment. Thus, a study of mutual intellectual respec t in guided play aligns with a theory of Knowledge in Use (Hall & Stevens, 2016), the underpinning of Interac tion Analysis methods (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). I n our study of the enactment of mutual intellectual respect in guided play that features joining inquiry between children and adults, we must attend to what is going on for different participants, what participants are oriented to, what the working consensus of the interaction is, and ho w through their actions participants display and ad just their mutually constructed orientation to an interaction (Hall & Stevens, 2016). Our transcripts (Jefferson, 2004) detail the interactional moves adults and children make to affirm one another’s ideas, questions, and activities.

## Findings

Case 1: Adults noticing and wondering about children’s thinking and activity In Case 1, we examine the teacher, Lily (pseudonym), when she led the class outside to explore and imagine how smaller sea creatures might feel while living in the kelp forest. To inspire discovery of the size of kelp, the children played around Redwood trees that represented kelp, which can grow as tall as 175 feet to the top of the ocean water.

Figure 1 Transcript from Case 1 The interaction (Figure 1) began with a child playi ng as a Sea Urchin sharing with Lily how she could eat and take down kelp. She shouted loudly “MISS LI LY” (Line 1), and then told the teacher that she co uld take down the kelp by pointing at the tree. Lily responded in Lines 2 and 4, as she listened and reacted to Sea Urchin’s information, and then extended an invitation to further explain “How?” in Line 6. By listening to Sea Urchin and then requesting clarification and further insight, Lily positioned Sea Urchin as the expert in this interaction. This is important because Lily did not move to immediately agree with Sea Urchin. Instead, she noticed and set up the stage for Sea Urchin to continue sharing her expert ise in her own words. This led to Sea Urchin, who t ook up Lily’s invitation in Line 7, to further break down how urch ins consume kelp: they first eat the roots (Lines 7-8) before it floats away (Line 9). After Sea Urchin’s explanation of how she, as an urchin, would eat and take down kelp by the roots, Lily responded with “Ahhhh” (Line 10) as if she just had an epiphany. Her prosody (Goodwin, et al., 2002) encouraged children to continue shari ng their ideas. From her “Ahhhh” (Line 10) as if sh e just had an epiphany learning something new, to her “How?” i n Line 6 as she asked the Sea Urchin to say more, a ll positioned the children as the experts who had valuable information to share with her. This further solidified Sea Urchin as an expert in the activity while Lily remained wondrous and curious. After Lily carefully listened to Sea Urchin’s exper tise, she then moved to include the other children gathered around the tree (Line 12). Once the childr en realized what Sea Urchin would do, Kelp Bass (Li ne 15) immediately turned around and ran away. Lily observed and noticed how quickly Kelp Bass ran away  and thus questioned in Line 16 where he was running away to.  What is most important about Lily’s narration of K elp Bass’s play is that she equally valued non-verbal forms of participation. Her noticings of children’s play became a valuable part of inquiry as children collectively  embodied the kelp forest ecosystem. Thus, Lily con tinued to hold children in play as the experts of their marin e roles, inviting children to show and share knowle dge of the food chain and relationships in the kelp forest ecosystem. Specifically, Grandpa responded and positioned Chelsea as the expert as he attempted to make sense of Chelsea’s pattern. They both worked to maintain each other’s engagement in the activity: their use of local phrases, famili ar tones of voice, and positive affirmation —from Grandpa’s soft hints at the challenge (e.g., “Holy Cow”) to Chelse a’s excitement at Grandpa’s success (e.g., “Correct !”)— allowed them to maintain a shared orientation to play.

## Discussion

Our analysis highlights the complexity of the inter actional and relational work that interlocutors acc omplish. Across the two studies, we identified how adults ma intained and cultivated an interactional space wher e both children and adults took one another’s ideas and wo nderings seriously in joyful ways. In Case 1, we il lustrated how Lily noticed and wondered alongside children as  she asked them to share their thinking. She also c losely observed and narrated what children were doing and valued these interactions as an example of their expertise. In Case 2, we provided more evidence of adults valuing children’s ideas as experts during “Guess My Pattern”. This illustrated how Grandpa maintained an exploratory stance as he struggled to understand Chelsea’s pattern. Despite how puzzling Chelsea’s pattern was, Grandpa remaine d curious and engaged, paused to give himself time to wonder, and continuously tried to guess the pattern despite not knowing how the pattern or activity would end. In education, the term guided is often used to illu strate adults intervening or driving children’s intellectual work. However, the adults in both case s guided the children’s activity in ways that cente red children as experts. In the Kelp Forest, Lily guided childre n to understand the role of kelp in the marine ecos ystem by noticing and wondering about what her students were up to, seeking to understand and narrate their marine activity rather thandirecting or intervening in it. In Gues s My Pattern, Grandpa  continued to play along with Chelsea, supporting her in refining her pattern to be more “ complex” yet still guessable. He acted intrigued and strove to make sense of Chelsea's thinking rather than telling her that the pattern was unsolvable or incorrect. Guided play and joint inquiry can indeed occur simultaneously i n ways that affirm children’s dignity and foster mu tual intellectual respect. This is more than balancing a dult guidance with supporting children to exercise their expertise—our examples illustrate children and adults improvisationally working to include each other and build on each other’s perspectives.
