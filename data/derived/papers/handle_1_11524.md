# Fostering Science Understanding with Imaginative Digital Drawing

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Drawing and imagination are integral to scientific thinking and reasoning. This study explored how elementary students learn astronomy through engaging in imaginative drawing with digital tools. Data were collected from multiple sources and analyzed qualitatively. Findings from this study indicated that imaginative drawing with digital tools enabled students to explore topics that were beyond their immediately experienced world and construct refined, plausible knowledge claims.

## Introduction

In science and for scientists (e.g., Einstein, Feyn man, Faraday), it sounds a truism to claim that imagination is central to science. However, in science education, with some dominant paradigms driving science educat ion pedagogy (e.g., conceptual change, inquiry),  imagination has not received enough attention (Hadz igeorgiou, 2016). Even science inquiry, which requires imaginative or creative thinking, has been implemented in a step-bystep fashion, and more often than not, it was a tea cher-controlled process (Hadzigeorgiou et al., 2012). St udies have shown that, during students’ science explorati ons, imagination has the potential to enrich and br oaden students’ interests and concerns and promote their engagement (Caiman & Lundegård, 2018). Nevertheless, our understandings about incorporating imagination in science learning are still limited. To contribute on this issue, this research aims to explore how elementary studen ts learn science through engaging in drawing with imagination. In particular, this study focused on s tudents’ sense-making of  astronomical phenomena that are usually difficult for students to understand and ch allenging for teachers to teach (Chastenay, 2018; I sik-Ercan et al., 2014). Another focus of this study is to understand the af fordance of employing digital tools in student drawing. The inquiry is guided by two research question s: (1) How do elementary students make sense of astronomical phenomena through drawing with imagina tion? (2) How do the digital tools influence students’ science learning through drawing?

Theoretical frameworks Visual representations such as drawing are integral  to scientific thinking and reasoning (Prain & Tytl er, 2012; Tang et al., 2019; Tytler et al., 2020). In science education, researchers broadly agree tha t students need to learn how to interpret and construct visual representatio ns of science concepts and processes (Ainsworth et al., 2011; Prain & Tytler, 2012). There is an increasing focus on student-generated drawings since research suggests engaging students in the process of visual represen tation construction supports learning better than s imply interpreting experts’ visualization (Cox, 1999; Galano et al., 2018; Yoon et al., 2021). Drawing (both constructing and refining drawings) is a core epistemic activity within science, and sh ould therefore be a major emphasis in the science classroom (Prain et al., 2009). In school science context, researchers (Prain et al., 2009; Prain & Tytler, 2012; Tytler et al., 2020) suggest that to construc t and refine open-ended representations of scientific processes and phenomena help students learn about conceptual knowledge, the nature of science, and communicate scientific evidence. Rather than “tools for something more fundamental, such as conceptual knowledge”, students’ drawing as a process is “enacting science learning and reasoning” (Prain & Tytler, 2012, p. 2757). Draw on the work of Vygotsky (1978, 2004), research ers argue that imagination should neither be understood as a specialized mental faculty nor the creation of unreal fantasies; instead, imagination is a socially and culturally mediated process. Zittoun and  Gillespie (2016) defined imagination as “the proces s of creating experiences that escape the immediate setting, whic h allow exploring the past or future, present possi bilities or even impossibilities” (p. 2). Imagination is critical for exploring and learning astronomical topics which are beyond the immediate setting (Chastenay, 2018). Imagination allows students to explore topics that extend in space and time beyond their immediately experienced worlds, such as phenomena ranging from microscopic to planetary spaces and timescales.  With/through imagination, students’ scientific reas oning and meaning making expand in time and space and become progressively more refined (Hilppö et al., 2017). The use of digital tools contributes to the development of skills and strategies that are highly relevant in science learning, such as problem solving (Greefrat h et al., 2018), model-based reasoning (Buckley et al., 2004) and visualizing complex relationships (Koklu & Topcu, 2012). Seeing from socio-cultural perspectives, drawing to reason is the use of cultural symbolic and semio tic tools and various media to make sense of the phenomena (Tytler et al., 2020; Yoon et al., 2021), thus is mediated by the tools used (Gooding, 200 6; Prain & Tytler, 2012; Quillin & Thomas, 2015).

## Methodology and methods

This study took place in a grade 5 science classroom in a public school in Canada. One teacher and her 19 students participated in this study. Throughout the four-month long research period, the teacher and student s worked on an astronomy unit. Students were expected to design and draw a technolo gy to explore these planets and/or their moons to advance current space exploration. Student s’ technology design was to be based on their exper iences, knowledge they had learned in this and previous uni ts (e.g., Air and Aerodynamic), and online research on  their topics. Each student had a Google Chromebook for th eir online research. Students used digital tool i.e., Google Drawings to create and refine their drawings. At the end of this unit, students presented their design to the class. This research was designed as a qualitative case study to conduct a concentrated inquiry (Merriam, 2016). For triangulation of data sources and analysis, thi s case study employed multiple methods for data col lection, including classroom observation, video-recorded classes, individual student interviews, and collecting artefacts created by students (e.g., drawings, final presenta tion files, and the notes they took while working o n this task. Data from multiple sources were analyzed together f or thematization to understand how students learned astronomy by constructing and refining drawings with digital tools. Data analysis took a i terative process including opening coding, axial coding, and selective coding.  Throughout the data analysis and the following presented research findings, pseudonyms were used to ensure the anonymity of participants.

## Findings

Imaginative drawing enabled sense-making of astronomical phenomena With the two dynamics, expansive and refining, imaginative drawing enacted and enabled students to reason, make sense of, and develop plausible knowledge claims about astronomical phenomena. The topics that students worked on (i.e., characteristics and surface conditions of other known planets in solar system) are challenging for them to understand as there are very limited materials or hands-on inquiry activities that students can engage with to make sense of these topics. Through drawing, students employed their imagination  to design technologies to explore these planets. Imagination allowed students  to work on these topics that were beyond their imm ediately experienced world i.e., other planets and moons in the solar system. To make a plausible design, students needed to learn and synthesize the characteristics and sur face conditions. For example, after learning that s ome planets and their moons have icy surface, a student designed rovers that had wheels with cleats to catch and move on the icy surface.  By achieving the coherency between their drawings a nd what they learned about the planets and moons, students refined their designs to make their  design plausible. In this way, imaginative drawing with the expansive and refining aspects enabled students to make sense of and construct plausible knowledge claims about astronomical phenomena which are beyond their immediately experienced world. The use of digital tools facilitated drawing to reason and drawing for communication In this study, it was noticed that some students pa rticularly choose to draw with digital tools. For example, a student decided to use Google Drawings to draw his design. During the interview, he explained his decision. … because Google Drawings is good for this task, be cause you can use different colours, tools, shapes … you can also import images, and videos, if  you want … I used it for other assignments … I candraw … better than using pencil. [pointing on the background image he imported] I cannot draw well like this, this is exactly what I want which I cannot draw by hands, if I use pencil or colors. It was evident that this student was aware of the advantages of drawing with this digital tool and confident of his skills of using this tool as well. He shared, with digital tool which is Google Drawings in this study, he was able to create something that he could not draw by hands. This student as an example showed that digital tools have the potential to facilitate creating visual representations for the purpose of learning and reasoning.

## Discussion and conclusion

With imagination and supported with digital tools, students in this study engaged  in constructing and refining visual representations  and developed their knowledge about astronomical phenomena. Since realistic representations of or hands-on activities to explore astronomical phenomena are  not readily available in classrooms, teachers tend to use reading materials (e.g., textbooks, trade books) and photos and video s to teach astronomy topics (Chastenay, 2018; Isik-Ercan et al., 2014). It is not easy for students to  make sense of these topics by just reading texts or viewing videos. Stu dents’ more active engagement is needed. Based on f indings from this study, it is suggested that engaging students in imaginative drawing has the potential to scaffold students’ sense-making of astronomical topics. Moreover, it was also found that digital tools have the potential to facilitate student drawing and mitigate the limitation of students’ drawing skills on their visualization construction.
