# Understanding Art and Each Other with Tabletop Games: Designing for Culturally- Expansive Public Pedagogy

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this poster, we present our co-design partnership with a contemporary art museum toward culturally-expansive public pedagogy. To challenge social and cultural boundaries prevalent in our society, w e propose an approach of co-designing tabletop games and conducting public programs in connection with contemporary art exhibits as cultural critic s. Our co-design partners span curators, artists, and public program participants.

To challenge social and cultural boundaries prevalent in our society, w e propose an approach of co-designing tabletop games  and conducting public programs in connection with contemporary art  exhibits as cultural critic s. Our co-design partners span curators, artists, and public program participants. Partnered with a contemporary art museum in Calgary, Canada, this ongoing participatory design-based research adapts game design practices from formal educational settings for use in informal learning spaces.  The project centers on creating art exhibit-themed tabletop games with artists, curators, and visitors, treating art engagement as a playful educational endeavor that cultivates critical perspectives.  Although studied infrequently, tabletop games have shown their potential to engage learners in disciplinary or cultural practices (Nasir, 2005; Saxe, 1992). While the actions of play directly communicate players’ desires and goals, designing games helps learners invent alternative ways of knowing (Civil, 2002). By co-designing games, participants negotiate the meanings of narratives, components, and rules, while playing with and reflecting on the exhibited artworks themselves (Civil, 2002; Kim & Bastani, 2017). The game becomes an artifact that mediates learners’ conversations in exploring identities and cultural understanding, thus fostering cultural pluralism (Alim & Paris, 2017; Kim et al., 2015). This makes tabletop games  particularly well-suited for culturally-expansive learning that extends our understanding of the world beyond the immediate cultural settings (Bastani et al., 2023; Poppendorf et al., 2024). Culturally-expansive public pedagogy and art engagement Critical arts perspectives challenge cultural boundaries prevalent in our society (Zorrilla & Tisdell, 2016). Public institutions in countries with settler-colonial histories have tended to instill dominant cultures and their practices (Alim & Paris, 2017). When differences are perceived as deficits rather than resources, individuals may be seen or see themselves as incompetent. Though many institutions have since sought to address deficit views, the lingering effects of such structural privileges and the corresponding deprivation of cultural rights (Caust, 2019) are rarely acknowledged in scholarly discussions (e.g., Jenkins et al., 2016; van Laar et al., 2017). Our focus on co-creation of tabletop games with curators and artists seeks to acknowledge these privileges and claim the cultural rights of diverse populations by democratizing critical arts perspectives. Drawing on sociocultural perspectives on learning that see differences as resources (e.g., González et al., 2005; Gutiérrez, 2008; Ladson-Billings, 1995; Wenger, 1998), w e view cultures as the shared meanings and practices of groups to which individuals belong, whether inherited or newly formed through collective experience (Bastani et al., 2023; Kim, 2018). We argue that creating such negotiated spaces, where diverse voices interact, reinterpret, and reimagine cultural artifacts, enables authentic opportunities for culturally-expansive learning. We conceptualize culturally-expansive public pedagogy building  on Engle et al.’s (2012)  expansive framing and Biesta’s (2014)  public pedagogy. In expansive framing, s cholars position learners as active contributors across diverse settings, intertwined with lived experiences of power, oppression, and privilege (Engle et al., 2012; Freedman et al., 2025). For Biesta (2014), public pedagogy is inherently tied to the principle of plurality, recognizing and respecting diverse perspectives, identities, and voices while encouraging democratic engagement. Public pedagogy works in the interest of the public condition of plurality, creating a realm where engagement and discourse can exist openly, shifting away from homogeneity toward embracing diversity. Playing and designing tabletop games in informal learning settings embody this ideal of public pedagogy by disassociating from traditional schooling  and creating spaces of multiple pedagogies. We propose that tabletop game play and design enable learners to draw on their cultural resources in ways that transcend dominant educational boundaries. Through gameplay and collaborative design processes, players en gage with diverse cultural practices and ways of knowing; games then become means of critical and culturally-expansive public pedagogy. 2758

## Designing tabletop games with gallery curators and participants

Extending previous frameworks of co-design, participatory research, and participatory design approaches (Greenhalgh et al., 2019), we engage with multiple perspectives, logics, knowledge systems, and ways of working to define participation as explorative and informative (Harris et al., 2024). In this section, we describe our design iterations of one exhibit-theme tabletop game and on-going work in partnership with an art museum. Creating the initial design. Featuring multinational artists, the exhibit, “Presence” (Figure 1a), contemplated space and community as socio-temporal-cultural constructs that are constantly produced, contested, and transformed (Contemporary Calgary, n.d.), which strongly resonated with our project  aims. The curators provided the exhibition information in advance, and the research team visited the exhibition soon after its opening to understand the characteristics of the exhibition and identif y possible game formats. In conversations with the curators, we initially considered  using actual images of the artworks in a card  game, s eeking the artists ’ agreements. After reflecting further on the theme of the exhibition, we designed a map-labeling role-playing game based on Beak, Feather, & Bone (Possible Worlds Games, 2020), whose narrative creation concerns for perceived meanings of places. In our game Snail Community Garden (SCG), players take on the role of different groups within the community, represented by cartoon anthropomorphic snails. Hinting further on cultural expansiveness and inclusiveness, one of the character groups is “turtles” within the Snail Community (Figure 1b). While players define their own groups and create narratives about various garden objects in detail, they explore the meanings of the community, echoing the voices and themes found within the exhibit. We used the floor plan of the exhibit and the forms of the artworks to inform the design of the games map and the buildings on it (Figure 1c). Testing with the public. After playtesting and adjusting the game within our  team, we brought it to two museum’s outreach events, called Free First Thursday, which had hundreds of attendees. Visitors to our table came from a variety of demographics,  including young children. Researchers noticed that the current version of the game was challenging for young participants  during the first event. This led a researcher creat e a “junior version” on the fly  and then a formal one  for the second event. Junior version had pared down details and mechanics and included child-friendly elements like colouring pages. Additionally, one of the artists featured in the exhibit visited and played the game; their insights further informed the game design and the workshop format. Specifically, we discussed the sequence of workshop activities, wondering if it would be best to have participants visit the exhibit before playing the game or after playing the game. Ultimately, we chose to have participants play the game, visit the exhibit, then return and redesign the game based on this conversation. The workshop as co-design iteration. In the workshop, participants played SCG as their first activity and then toured the exhibit guided by a curator. Our goal was to deepen participants’ engagement with the artworks by first having them engage with themes of community through game play. After the tour, the participants redesigned the game  as a way to reflect  further on the artworks they viewed. The participants engaged in conversations on what community means, connecting themes of the exhibit to the game, and then redesigned the game to express their ideas (Figure 1d). From this process, participants changed the game rules to bring outsider perspectives to the structures in a community and incorporate past/future generations’ views of community. For some participants, these reflective conversations connecting the themes of the exhibit to the games facilitated new understandings of, and ways of engaging with, the artwork. The design partnership and iteration will continue with future workshops and new exhibitions that will come to this contemporary art  museum. Our approach encourages meaningful and playful participation for visitors of gallery spaces and breaks down traditional barriers to art engagement. Experiencing art through interactive play and expressing thoughts and feelings through redesigning games enhance visitors’ engagement with the artworks while amplifying artists’ and participants’ voices and perspectives.  This collaborative partnership demonstrates our evolving understanding of culturally-expansive public pedagogy and how we could co-design cultural artifacts that enrich experiences with arts and diverse ways of seeing the world.
