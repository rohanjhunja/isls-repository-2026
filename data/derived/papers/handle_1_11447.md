# Examining Self-Regulated Learning Processes in Design Tasks with Large Language Models

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Focusing on human-AI collaboration process, where humans and AI work together toward shared goals, has implications for designing AI-enabled learning tasks. We explore self-regulated learning (SRL) in two tasks leveraging generative AI (Large Language Models; LLMs): (1) co-write a story with GPT-4 and (2) brainstorm ideas for design sketches with GPT-3.5. We develop a codebook that accounts for SRL phases (forethought, performance, self-reflection) and fine-grained cognitive and socioemotional interactions with LLMs. We conduct Ordered Network Analysis to compare SRL processes between participants scoring higher and lower in design artifacts. The higher-scoring participants more frequently utilized metacognitive strategies like monitoring and self-instruction to maintain focus in performing tasks, in conjunction with cognitive interactions to prompt and evaluate LLMs’ outputs. We discuss the design of learning systems and tasks to promote evaluative strategies in working with LLMs.

## Introduction

Emerging artificial intelligence (AI) technologies like Large Language Models (LLMs) enab le human-system exchanges in natural language and provide opportuni ties for new forms of human-AI collaboration in learning contexts (Fuchs, 2023; Kasneci et al., 2023). Human-AI collaboration can be defined as a process where humans and AI work together toward shared goals (Memmert &  Bittner, 2022). This definition involves back-and-forth exchanges to adapt and expand human and AI-generated output, for example, to generate new idea s, co-create design sketches, and support writing iterations (Bozkurt et al., 2023; Duan et al., 2024). Focusing on the process of human-AI interaction has important implications for desig ning learning systems and tasks. This focus builds toward understanding how AI can replace human efforts in certain tasks and extend human cognition (Cukurova, 2024; Molenaar, 2022a). It also reveals how to support learners to strategize and interact with AI productively. In this research, we examine self-regulated learning (SRL) process (Järvelä et al., 2 016; Zimmerman & Moylan,

2009) in two different LLM-integrated, user experience (UX) design tasks: co-writing a design story with GPT-4

(n participants=10) and brainstorming design ideas  with GPT-3.5 (n=17). The following questions  guide our research: RQ1: How do participants engage in SRL during design tasks with LLMs? RQ2: How are SRL processes linked to the quality of the design artifacts created by humans and LLMs? For RQ1, we qualitatively coded the SRL processes in think-aloud data as participants interacted with the LLMs. To answer RQ2, we conducted Ordered Network Analysis (Tan et al., 2022) to understand the relation between SRL processes and design artifacts’ quality  (higher-scoring and lower-scoring groups). AI-integrated learning environments introduce additional considerations for SRL. For instance, AI tools can support humans in several SRL phases, such as understanding task requ irements (forethought), monitoring learning progres s (performance), and reflecting on the outputs (self-reflection; Järvelä et al., 2023; Molenaar, 2022 a; Tam, 2024). Learners alsoneed to plan prompts  for AI and evaluate AI’s output, while reflecting on the learning goals and monitoring progress (Nguyen, A. et al., 2024; Nguyen, H. & Nguyen, 2024). Our findings suggest that participants who engage in more cognitive interaction (prompting, evaluating AI’s output) in conjunction with perform ance (self-control) tend to score higher in their design artifacts. The se findings highlight the importance of evaluative strategies in utilizing LLMs and shaping the quality of human-AI co-created learning artifacts, with implications for designing learning systems to support these strategies.

## Background

Large language models (LLMs) in creative design Emergent research has applied LLMs to creative design tasks, such as supporting group idea generation, creating sketches, simulating personas of target users, and providing feedback on design prototypes (Duan et al., 2024; Salminen et al., 2024; Shaer et al., 2024). LLMs can be applied across different phases in the UX design process, from context gathering to ideation and usability te sting (Schmidt et al., 2024). The models’ capacity for understanding and adapting to natural language provides flexibility and control, as designers can iteratively refine their prompts to guide LLMs’ responses (Hou et al., 2024). Such iterative human-AI exchanges necessitate careful prompt engineering  (framing and reframing prompts to LLMs to achieve the desired output), ong oing evaluation, and progress monitoring of human’s  and AI’s co-created results. In addition, users need to consider the us efulness, relatability, and potential bias in AI-generated information (Salminen et al., 2024). Take  an example of using LLMs as a co-writer in idea generation (Dhillon et al., 2024). Here, a partner (e.g., human) writes a sentence, and the other partner (e.g., LLMs) suggests the next sentence. While the unexpectedness of LLM-generated text can spark creativity, users need to evaluate and edit the text to achieve coherent and relatable products (Liu et al., 2024). The focus on planning, reflectio n, and monitoring of AI uses reflects SRL processes—our key theoretical lens to explore human-AI collaboration. Self-regulated learning in AI-integrated tasks We build on SRL cyclical phases model (Zimmerman & Moylan, 2009) to understand how designers apply SRL in interacting with LLMs. This model posits that learning occurs through three phases: forethought, performance, and self-reflection. In the forethought phase, learners set goals  by analyzing task requirements  and planning strategies. In the performance phase, learners execute the tasks while monitoring  and using self-control (metacognitive or motivational) strategies such as self-instruction and help-seeking. In the self-reflection phase, learners assess their performance and make causal attributions about success or failure. These phases are not linear but cyclical, meaning that learners might engage in different SRL processes simultaneously. We further draw on the augmentation perspective of human-AI collaboration —viewing AI as a partner that complements and enhances knowledge construction (Molenaar, 2022 b)—to consider cognitive and socioemotional interactions within SRL (Järvelä et al., 2016). Cognitive interaction captures efforts in collabora tive settings to deepen one’s understanding, such as sharing, gathering, and co-constructing knowledge. Socioemotional interaction refers to how learners react to each other and perceive the collaboration (Isohätälä et al., 2020). Learners deploy SRL throughout design tasks (Callan et al., 2021; Graham et al., 2018). For instance, they engage in forethought to analyze the design contexts and define problems, performance to ideate and sketch design, and self-reflection to reframe the design based on iterative evaluation s (Nguyen, H. & Nguyen, 2024). The deployment of SRL is critical in AI-integrated learning environments (Järvelä et al., 2023; Molenaar, 2022a; Tam, 2024). Learners need to evaluate AI-generated ideas in forethought and performance and adapt these ideas for task completion  (Kasneci et al., 2023, Nguyen, A. et al., 2024; Tam, 2024). T hey may show cognitive interactions such as iterating upon prompts to the AI and evaluating its responses in relation to the learning goals, while demonstrating socioemotional interactions like reacting positively or negatively to the AI output. We focus on SRL due to its positive association with learning performance (DiBenedetto & Zimmerman, 2010). Specifically, higher performers tend to engage in m ore SRL phases (Clearly & Zimmerman, 2006). Our study extends prior research that has considered th e relation between SRL phases, interactions, and learning performance to AI-integrated contexts. Building on theories of human-AI collaboration (Cukurova, 2024; Molenaar, 2022b) allows us to attend to different c ognitive, metacognitive, and socioemotional  interactions that relate to the quality of learning artifacts created by the human and AI partners.

## Method

Study settings We focused on two design tasks: (1) co-writing a design fiction (termed Design Fiction tas k) and (2) creating a design sketch for a learning management system (ter med Design Sketch task). We selected these tasks because they represented common collaboration in design tea ms. They also showed different task interactions (e.g., cowriting, getting information, brainstorming, critiquing ideas) to examine SRL processes. Task 1: Co-writing a design fiction with GPT-4 (Design Fiction) Ten graduate students (M.S., Ph.D.) studying learning sciences and design at a public university in the Western U.S. participated in a co-writing session with GPT-4 (integrated into Google Docs using Apps Script). Participants responded to the prompt: “Write a story set in a distant future that propose s a solution to address the future of work.” This task utilized design fiction, a human-computer interaction approach to use creative writing to imagine technology design  (Blythe, 2014).  P articipants generated the story’s context (e.g., pr oblems, characters, and design solutions) for five minutes and then wrote the story for 10-12 minutes. In both tasks, participants took turns with the LLM (i.e., participants wrote a sentence, and the LLM generated the next sentence for context and story writing). The initial prompts for the LLM were embedded into Apps Script, for example: Let’s co-write the story. The story must include details abo ut: the design problem we're trying to solve, the design solution, and examples about how fictional c haracters interact with the solution. We will take turns writing lines of the story that will address the problem and solution. Every response must include both Sentence and Quest ion: Write “Sentence” for your sentence of the story. 1 sentence only. Then, write “Question” and ask me 1 question to promote my own learning, like understanding and planning for the tasks, evaluating, and reflection. Participants were encouraged to verbally articulate the reasoning behind the sentences they wrote and t heir thoughts about the sentences that the LLM generated. At the end of the session, participants had a two-minute debrief about their writing experiences. The session was audio-recorded via Zoom and transcribed. Task 2: Creating a design sketch for a learning management system with GPT-3.5 (Sketch) We conducted 45-minute, audio and screen-recorded interviews with 17 designers with various backgrounds: six undergraduates, four graduate students (M.S., Ph.D.), and seven alumni from a UX/UI and instructional design program in  a public university in the Western U.S. Participant s engaged in a design task  to “redesign the navigation of a learning management system”. They first brainstormed ideas independently (i.e., without the LLM) for 10 minutes, refined ideas with an LLM (ChatGPT-3.5) for 15 minutes, and sketched design solutions for 5-7 minutes. We did not pre-prompt the LLM but invited participants to freely a sk questions and follow up. Finally, we conducted a three-minute debrief about participants’ perceptions of the LLM. Data sources In this mixed methods research design study, we collected (1) audio recordings, (2) co-created stories on Google Docs (Design Fiction), and (3) paper and digital de sign sketches (Sketch). The audio recordings in bot h tasks included reactions to the LLM-generated outputs and explanations of writing or de sign ideation. Our analyses included qualitative analysis of the recording transcripts  to examine participants’  collaboration with the LLM s (generating ideas and co-writing in Design Fiction and brainstorming in Sketch) and scoring of the design artifacts.

## Participants

We categorized participants into two groups  (higher-scoring and lower-scoring) based on the quality of their products (e.g., design fiction s and sketches). These products were assessed using the same rubr ic that included four criteria (each scored on a scale of 1-5): novelty, usefulness, elaboration, and incorporation of human’s and AI’s ideas. Novelty and usefulness were key constructs to asses s creativity in product design (Runco & Jaeger, 2012). Evaluation described the depth with which pa rticipants detailed their solutions (Daly et al., 2 016). Additionally, idea incorporation reflected the stud y’s focus on human-AI collaboration and the creative outputs stemming from such collaboration (Rezwana & Maher, 2023). The score range was 5-18 (M = 13, SD = 4.5) for Design Fiction and 6-15 (M = 10.58, SD = 2.49) for Sketch. We used the median as a cutoff; Desig n Fiction: higher-scoring (n = 6, M = 16, SD = 1.55), lower-scoring (n = 4, M = 8.5, SD = 3.42); Sketch: higher-scoring (n =9, M = 12.39, SD = 1.5), lower-scoring (n = 8, M = 8.54, SD = 1.63). There was no substantial difference in roles (a mix of students and alumni) and gender between groups for each task. Analytical procedures We created a codebook for SRL processes  building on prior work  (Järvelä et al., 2016; Zimmerman & Moylan, 2009; Table 1). The SRL phases (forethought, performance, self-reflection) captured task understanding, planning, and monitoring of both individuals’ and L LMs’ progress toward the design tasks. We modified the definitions of cognitive and socioemotional interactions and added subcodes to consider LLM interactions. To answer RQ1 (engagement in SRL processes), we calculated the frequencies of SRL phases and subcodes. Cognitive interaction s included prompting, re-prompting, and evaluation  of LLMs,  while socioemotional interactions consisted of positive and negative reactions to LLM-generated text. We focused on participants’ think-aloud utterances at the sentence level as the main analysis unit. The authors reached substantial inter-rater agreement on 23.5% of the data (Cohen’s 𝜅 range.88-1). The first author coded the rest of the data and flagged cases of uncertainty to be resolved through two subsequent discussions. To answer RQ2 (linking SRL processes to design arti facts), for each design task, we first categorized participants into two groups (higher-scoring and lower-scoring), and then compared engagement in SRL between groups using Ordered Network Analysis (ONA; Tan et al., 2022). ONA highlighted the temporality in modeling learning activities, capturing their co-occurrences, sequences, and iterations within an an alysis window. This method allowed us to capture the order in which SRL  unfolded over time in interactions with LLMs. The ONA graphs can be interpreted as follows. The nodes ind icate activities (e.g., SRL phases and interactions), and the node sizes reveal activity frequencies. The colored circles inside the nodes suggest iterations of the same activities. The lines connecting tw o nodes represent co-occurrences, and the arrows suggest whether an acti vity more frequently precedes or follows another. We determined an analysis window size of three (code occurrences within three consecutive utterances). We selected this sho rter window size, as participants quickly moved through the tasks (e.g., context generation, information seeking, ideation). We constructed a subtraction network that showed the differences between the two groups and provided examples of individuals’ networks to illustrate the findings. ## Discussion

Examining the process of human-AI interaction  can illuminate ways to support and enhance  learning in AI-integrated environments. SRL processes in AI-enabled tasks  might differ from those in traditional learning environments. In these tasks, learners need to iteratively provide and refine p rompts to gain useful information, interpret and evaluate the AI’s responses, and selectively incorporate AI’s output  (Kasneci et al., 2023, Nguyen, A. et al., 2024; Tam, 2024). Our research contributes a codebook of SRL processes that not only looks at phases (e.g., Nguyen, H.  & Nguyen, 2024) but also finer-grained codes  (e.g., prompting, re-prompting, evaluation, positive/negative emotion) in interaction with LLMs. AI in education researchers have called for scholarship that examines how human-AI interaction not only replaces human efforts, but  also internalize s (e.g., supports selfcontrol in task performance)  and expands (i.e., add s new capacities to) human’s cognition (Cukurova, 2024). Researchers can apply our fine-grained codes to link human-AI interactions to these emerging AI capacities. In addition, findings  highlight the importance of SRL in learning interactions with emerging AI technologies. Across the two design ta sks, participants in the higher-scoring group showed higher frequenc y of SRL processes, particularly cognitive interaction (subcodes: prompting, re-prompting, evaluating LLM’s responses) in conjunction with metacognitive strategies  in the performance phase (subcodes: self-observation, self-control). In comparison, the lower-scoring group was more involved in forethought and performance. Further, as seen in the cases of John and Alex, participants  in the lower-scoring group less frequently built on LLM’s responses in their design artifacts. These findings  echo prior research that highlighted the importance  of metacognitive SRL strategies (such as monitoring, self-instruction, and evaluation) and their positive association with learning performance (DiBenedetto & Zimmerman, 2010; Tam, 2024). Our findings have design implications for creating SRL support in AI-integrated learning environments. For example, learners in the lowerscoring group might benefit from structured prompts  that  (1) clarify the task requirements (including how to leverage the LLM) and (2) encourage them to evaluate the LLM’s suggestions, in relation to the learning outcomes and task progress.  Additionally, those in the higher-scoring group could benefit from structured prompts  that facilitate active, ongoing cycles of performance and cognitive interaction to sustain performance and engagement. A limitation of the current work is that the  dataset is small and focuses on short tasks in a domain (UX design). Additionally, participants likely have different prior experiences with and perceptions of AI tools. Future research that involves more diverse learners and tasks might reveal addi tional SRL phases and subcodes, as well as potential differences in SRL across learner populations, tasks, and domains. Finally, future studies can investigate other models of human-AI collaboration, their intersection with SRL and learning outcomes, and longer-term implementation strategies to support and validate productive human-AI collaboration.

## Conclusion

We examined SRL in two AI-integrated tasks: co-writing a design fiction and brainstorming design s ketches. Findings illustrate the occurrences of cognitive and socioemotional interactions across SRL phases of forethought, performance, and self-reflection. Our results emphasize  the importance of metacognitive strategies, including monitoring, self-instruction, and evaluation, to increase learning performance with LLMs. Results also highlight the need for further research into how to support and assess different SRL phases in AI-integrated learning tasks.
