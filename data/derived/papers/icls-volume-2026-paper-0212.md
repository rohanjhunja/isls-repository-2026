# Learning Through Service: How Tutoring Underserved Students Shapes Volunteer Tutors’ Perspectives on Equity

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Tutoring provided by volunteers can help close the educational gap for underserved students. When accompanied by training or integrated into service-learning, tutoring can be educational and personally formative for tutors. In this study, 52 volunteer tutors provided online tutoring to underserved students for three months as part of a training program offered by a non-profit organization. Quantitative pre-and post-tests showed no significant changes in tutors’ sensitivity to injustice. However, qualitative reflections revealed a thorough understanding of educational inequity. Encounters with the lived realities of underserved students, such as a lack of parental support, left lasting impressions. Combined with guided reflections and exchanges during workshops, these experiences prompted the tutors to reflect on their own backgrounds and the emotions they evoke. Overall, volunteer tutoring, paired with workshops and written reflections, deepened knowledge on educational equity, fostered personal development through emotional engagement, and increased motivation to continue volunteering. ## Problem statement

Educational inequity is considered one of the most pressing challenges of today (Brenner, 2010). Students with lower socioeconomic status (SES) or those with a migration background are particularly disadvantaged within educational systems, as evidenced by their lower performance compared to their more privileged peers (Sälzer et al., 2013). To achieve educational equity, students would need instruction and support that acknowledge their individual characteristics and needs (i.e., the adequacy perspective; Dumont & Ready, 2023). Tutoring has the potential to address educational disparities by providing such individualized support (Topping, 2000). Tutors can tailor their sessions to meet their students ’ instructional needs and provide the most beneficial support. Importantly, tutoring often extends beyond the academic domain. Motivational and emotional support, for example, through the development of a strong tutor-student relationship, plays a central role (Cabezas et al., 2021). As commercial tutoring is often inaccessible to unde rserved students (Hille et al., 2016), there is a need for free tutoring, especially for this group, provided by volunteers (Ritter et al., 2009). Similar to commercial tutoring, volunteer tutoring yields positive effects on the students (Elbaum et al., 20 00). Volunteers are often driven by their sensitivity to injustice (Neumayr & Schröder, 2024). Sensitivity to injustice refers to an individual’s awareness of inequitable situations and their intense emotional or behavioral reactions to them (Schmitt et al., 2005). One-on-one tutoring offers volunteers the opportunity to gain insight into the everyday lives of underserved students. By adopting their students’ perspectives, volunteers can gain secondhand experiences of educational disadvantage, which may, in turn, shape their own attitudes and behavior. This potential is amplified when volunteer tutoring is integrated within workshops that provide theoretical input and offer opportunities for reflection and discussion. This approach reflects the core idea of service learning: integrating theory and practice to maximize learning while avoiding overwhelming learners (Heffernan, 2001). Combining both within a single course or training offered by a university or a non-profit organization allows them to inform and enrich one another mutually. Encouraging partic ipating volunteer tutors to reflect in writing can further deepen their learning. Beyond fostering conceptual understanding, such service-learning experiences and structured reflection can evoke emotions, making education more meaningful and personally relevant (Ángeles et al., 2023), particularly when the topic resonates with one’s own experiences.

## Research questions

The present study emerged from a purposeful partnership between a university and a non-profit organization, providing a service-learning opportunity that focuses on educational equity through support for underserved students. Analyzing quantitative and qualitative data, we address the following research questions: How does tutoring underserved students and attending accompanying workshops influence 1) the volunteer tutors’ sensitivity to injustice, 2) the volunteer tutors’ understanding of educational equity, 3) the volunteer tutors’ personal reflections about their  own privileges or disadvantages, and what emotions do they evoke? 1542

## Procedure

The data were collected as part of a research-practice partnership in collaboration with the German non-profit organization Lern-Fair e. V. that offers free one-on-one online tutoring to underserved students who cannot afford commercial tutoring. The tutoring is provided by volunteers who also participate in a training program. Students and tutors register on the non-profit’s website and indicate their subject preferences, after which they are matched into learning dyads following a short interview with a member of the non-profit organization. They are then free to arrange their tutoring sessions, usually once per week for one hour, via a dedicated online platform. The training program for tutors consisted of three two-day interactive workshops over four months (two online and one in-person), during which the volunteer tutors learned, among other things, about didactics, pedagogical attitudes, and digital tools. Besides preparing the volunteer tutors for tutoring, the workshops aimed to increase their awareness of educational inequity and encourage personal reflections. They were provided with materials, including research articles, online resources, and practical sug gestions, to deepen their knowledge in learning, instruction, and tutoring. Some of the volunteer tutors were part of an elective service-learning seminar in their undergraduate program in educational sciences at a German university. The volunteer tutors who decided voluntarily to participate in the study were sent a survey via email one day (pre-test) and ninety days after being matched with their students (post-test). Among other aspects, including demographics, SES, and migration history, the survey included a question about whether respondents considered themselves underserved (6-point Likert scale ranging from  fully agree  (1) to fully disagree  (6); pre-test) and validated questionnaire to assess their sensitivity to injustice (USS-8; Beierlein et al., 2021; pre-and post-test), which consisted of eight items (pre-test: Cronbach’s α =.74, McDonald’s ω =.76; post-test: α =.86, ω =.86). The items included the perspectives of victims, observers, beneficiaries, and perpetrators of injustice (example item: “It bothers me when someone has to work hard for things that fall into the laps of others.”). The volunteer tutors were asked to indic ate their level of agreement or disagreement on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree  (1) to  strongly agree  (5). In the post-test, they had to indicate whether they intend to continue volunteering. Moreover, the volunteer tutors who attended the university seminar were asked to write a final reflection at the end of the semester as part of their coursework. They received guiding questions and were encouraged to reflect on their understanding of, and feelings regarding educational inequity.

## Participants

The sample consisted of 52 volunteer tutors. However, only n = 50 filled in the demographic survey. These participants were between 19 and 43 years old (M = 24.2, SD = 4.19). The majority were students in educational sciences (n = 26) and preservice teachers (n = 17), who had been studying for between one and twelve semesters. The volunteer tutors’ SES was measured using the international socioeconomic index (ISEI; Ganzeboom, 2010), based on their parents’ occupations, and ranged from 18 to 89 (M = 62, SD = 20.6). All but two volunteer tutors were born in Germany, and 14 were second-generation immigrants. Students answered the question regarding their perceived educational disadvantage, on average, with 5.13 (SD = 1.35), indicating that most do not consider themselves underserved. Overall, only 15 volunteer tutors completed the relevant items in the post-test, and 15 volunteer tutors from the service-learning seminar agreed that their reflections could be used fo r research purposes. To determine whether the volunteer tutors’ sensitivity to injustice changed on average from pre-to post-test, a univariate latent change score model (LCSM) was conducted using the lavaan package in R (Rosseel, 2012). To account for missing data, we used the full information maximum likelihood method, assuming missingness was at random. Additionally, given the small sample size, we estimated the models using robust maximum likelihood. Moreover, the reflections were qualitatively analyzed by the first and second authors using Dedoose. To that end, a coding scheme was developed which differentiated between mentioning of volunteer tutors’ understanding of educational inequity (including causes and consequences), the description of their student’s individual sit uation, volunteer tutors’ own experiences with inequity or potential privileges, their emotions related to the topic, their previous knowledge of the subject, and measures to combat educational inequity (see https://osf.io/6puxf/overview?view_only=d81b0282572447bfbd3cbde527ec304a for the whole coding scheme). After coding, the excerpts for each code were downloaded and analyzed in more detail to identify patterns. 1543

## Quantitative results

The pre-test mean score for sensitivity to injustice was 3.93 (SD = 0.87), and it was slightly lower in the post-test, at 3.79 (SD = 1.13). Data from 49 volunteer tutors were included in the LCSM. The model had zero degrees of freedom and was thus fully saturated, rendering fit indices irrelevant (Kenny, 2024). The LCSM results indicated that the mean change was not statistically sig nificant (M = -0.18, SE = 0.24). However, the standard deviation of change was significant (SD = 0.93, SE = 0.17, p <.001), indicating variability in the individual change trajectories. The standardized path coefficients from pre-test to post-test were 𝛽𝛽 = 0.85, and from change to post-test, 𝛽𝛽 = 0.93. Moreover, there was a significant negative covariance between baseline and change (Cov = - 0.38, p <.05), suggesting that volunteer tutors with lower pre-test sensitivity to injustice scores showed greater change than those with higher pre-test scores.

Of those participants who completed the post-test, 83.3% indicated that they intend to continue volunteering as a tutor, and 91.7% reported that they are (very) likely to volunteer in general in the future.

## Qualitative results

Volunteer tutors’ understanding of educational inequity Some volunteer tutors reported learning about educational inequity in their university courses, and one person had already gained practical insights from working in the education sector. Overall, most volunteer tutors reported that the workshops and tutoring experience were eye-opening and insightful. When defining educational inequity, they mostly focused on the influence of SES or migration background on performance. To emphasize this point, they referred to the findings of recent PISA studies or to popular researchers in the field, such as Pierre Bourdieu. The volunteer tutors appeared to understand that the causes of educational inequity are often systemic and deeply rooted in structural issues. They described that specific sociocultural characteristics often influence the development of competencies, academic performance, and ultimately, the attainment of a degree and social advancement. In particular, the intense interaction during one-on-one tutoring enabled volunteer tutors to gain deeper insights into the living situations of underserved students. Most frequently, they mentioned that the students do not receive support from their parents. Furthermore, they reported that many students have unfavorable home learning environments, lacking relevant learning equipment and a quiet space for studying. The volunteer tutors provided several suggestions for addressing educational inequity. Of course, tutoring was often mentioned as a relevant measure that can compensate for academic difficulties. Moreover, adaptive instruction (e.g., tailoring the language  and difficulty of tasks to students’ needs) and the use of technology to make educational offerings more accessible were mentioned. Based on their own experiences, several volunteer tutors emphasized the potential of volunteering to support underserved students and make a genuine impact on society. Volunteer tutors’ reflections on their own situation and associated emotions Learning about educational inequity and getting to know an underserved student prompted the volunteer tutors to reflect on their own childhood experiences. Many became aware of the privileges they had often taken for granted, such as frequent parental support, favorable living conditions, access to private tutoring, and adequate technical 1544 equipment. A particularly eye-opening moment for the volunteer tutors was an activity during one of the workshops called the “race of life”. The workshop instructors read out statements, such as “I used to have my own room,” and participants took a step forward for each statement that applied to them. The further participants advanced, the more privileged their backgrounds were, which was true for the majority. At the same time, the activity prompted some participants to recognize challenges they had faced.  Both types of reflections evoked strong, mostly negative emotions. Volunteer tutors who realized they might be considered underserved described this as a shock, whereas more privileged tutors reported feelings of shame about having had greater opportuniti es than their peers. Many contemplated the role of luck in their circumstances and expressed gratitude in retrospect. Building on these emotions, several volunteer tutors reported increased motivation to continue tutoring. They emphasized that the service-learning experience made them realize that individuals can make a difference and that addressing educational inequity is, to some extent, a personal responsibility. Although volunteer tutors did not significantly change in their sensitivity to injustice (which was already relatively high at the outset), the qualitative analysis of their reflections revealed meaningful insights. The combination of practice experience i n tutoring underserved students and participating in interactive workshops deepened their understanding of educational equity. The experience also prompted authentic reflections on their own educational privileges and disadvantages, as well as the emotions associated with them. Given that most volunteer tutors came from relatively privileged backgrounds, their nuanced explanations of educational inequity, illustrated with individual examples drawn from their students’ lives, are striking. The level of detail in their descriptions of the causes and consequences of educational inequality demonstrates how practical experience can enrich theoretical knowledge. Moreover, their emphasis on the emotions evoked during the service-learning experience highlights the v ital role of emotions in the learning process (Tyng et al., 2017). This study thus illustrates that learning extends beyond intellectual engagement and can integrate the mind and heart, provided that learning environments create space for feelings and emotional reflection (Ángeles et al., 2023). The activities also fostered future action: most volunteer tutors indicated their intention to continue volunteering with the non-profit organization. This intention may be explained by the expectation-value model (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). Through their previous service-learning experience, the volunteer tutors recognized tutoring as an effective means of addressing educational inequity. Additionally, the personal relationships they built with underserved students, combined with their reflections on their own privileges, increased the value of volunteer tutoring to them, as they felt a sense of responsibility to help. The small sample size and participant attrition are limitations. Taking the time to fill in a survey in addition to volunteering might have been challenging for the volunteers. Overall, the present study highlights that integrating practical tutoring experiences with reflective and informative workshops candeepen volunteer tutors’ understanding of educational (in)equity and stir emotions that might lead to sustained volunteer engagement, ultimately contributing to a stronger civil society. As the results can be applied to non-profit contexts and universities, the study advocates for educational opportunities that extend beyond cognition alone, connecting learning to real-life experiences and fostering emotional engagement in the learning process.
