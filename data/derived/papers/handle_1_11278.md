# Connecting Higher Education Curriculum Practices and Learners’ Home Practices and Cultural Resources within an English Foreign Language Program

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research explores how the Funds of Knowledge and Funds of Identity frameworks uncover language instructors’ strategies for connecting students' home practices with higher education, aiming to improve foreign language learning. Specifically, it examines how English as a Foreign Language (EFL) instructors at a Costa Rican public university integrate learners' life experiences, skills, and local contexts into English for Specific Purposes lesson planning. Findings highlight how EFL instructors gather and incorporate students' household contexts and identities into the higher education ESP curricula, identify the challenges in bridging ESP curricula and learners’ household contexts, and demonstrate how a visualization tool helps EFL instructors reflect on and discuss FoK and FoI integration. The study underscores the necessity for higher curricular flexibility in higher education EFL programs to better align with language students' local backgrounds and identities.

## Introduction

This study examines  how English as a Foreign Language (EFL)  professors integrate undergraduates’ lived experiences, skills, and cultural resources into hi gher education curricula to  enhance EFL learning. It  explores how faculty identify and apply  students' Funds of Knowledge (FoK) and Funds of Ide ntity (FoI) in curriculum design and how network visualization tools help map connections between students' backgrounds and acade mic needs (Zhou et al., 2023). Conducted at a public un iversity in Costa Rica, where students typically have limited English proficiency before enrollment, the study centers on EFL instructors teaching English for Specific Purposes (ESP) courses vital to students’ academic and professional  growth. While  previous literature underscores the benefits of linking home practices to education  through FoK and FoI frameworks  (Mora & Rios-Aguilar, 2017; Neri, 2017; Kiyama et al., 2017),  and emphasize their role in creating immersive foreign language  learning experiences (Omer & Ali, 2011; Razı, 2016) and improving employability (Johns & Dudley‐Evans, 1991), there is limited specific research on their application in higher education EFL contexts (Kim & Lee, 2012). To address this gap, the study poses three research questions: (1) How do EFL instructors uncover students’ experiences and contextual factors before lesson planning for ESP courses? (2)  How are learners' experiences incorporated into course content and tasks? (3) How can an online vis ualization tool support EFL teachers’ conversations about integrating FoK and FoI in teaching?

## Theoretical framework and literature review

This study draws  on FoK and FoI  frameworks, which  promote culturally sustaining pedagogies to integrate learners’ contextual and linguistic capital into formal education (Paris, 2012). Introduced by Moll et al. (1992), FoK values learners' family practices, cultural resources, and  life knowledge, especially in marginalized communities. Empirical studies (Bensimon, 2007; Barnett, 2010) underscore Fok’s potential in higher education to link  learners' experiences with curricula, enhancing relevance. However, i mplementing Fok is demanding, requiring time-intensive ethnographic methods like home visits and extensive  data collection. T o address these limitations, the FoI framework (Esteban-Guitart & Moll, 2014) emphasizes leverag ing family and community resources for self-definition. M ultimethodological approach es such as  visuals, texts, interviews, s hoeboxes activity, identity texts, and autobiographical methods effectively capture identity complexities and make academic content more inclusive and engaging (Esteban-Guitart, 2012; Subero et al., 2017). Integrating learners’ contextual customs and resources makes foreign language learning relevant, reinforces cultural identity, and reduces barriers (Mahmud, 2019; Khan, 2014, 2016). Ling et al. (2022) showed that incorporating Costa Rican local and intercultural elements increased English learning engagement. Yet, most FoK and FoI research in higher education largely focuses on English-speaking contexts, leaving a gap in collecting and applying undergraduates’ FoK and FoI  in foreign language  learning. This study addresses this gap by examining how EFL instructors integrate their language learners’ household experience, identifying strategies and resources, and exploring how a visualization tool supports EFL educators’ discussion s and reflection. Research questions guiding the study are: (1) How do EFL instructors uncover students’ household experiences and cultural factors when planning ESP lessons? (2) How do instructors incorporate these experiences into course content, and what resources and tasks connect the curriculum wit h learners’ identit ies? (3) How can an online visualization tool support EFL teachers’ conversations about integrating FoK and FoI in teaching?

## Methods

This study involved five Costa Rican EFL instructors from the same foreign language academic unit  at a public university, each located in a different region, offering diverse perspectives on integrating contextual elements in ESP teaching. The small sample allowed for a detailed analysis of individual strategies. Participants were selected based on extensive ESP teaching experience and curriculum development expertise in higher education.  The researcher previously worked alongside these instructors in th e same foreign language academic. The researcher conducted one-hour Zoom interviews with  each EFL instructor, exploring strategies for in tegrating language students’ local c ontext knowledge and background, challenges in curriculum adaptation, and impacts on engagement. Using Net.Create (Craig & Danish, 2018)—a collaborative online network visualization tool —the researcher c onstructed a network visualization from interview data, highli ghting connections among identity categories (Esteban-Guitart & Moll, 2014) and instructional practices. This visualization tool facilitated instructor reflection on shared practices and challenges during a joint Zoom focus group. Interviews and focus groups were conducted in Spanish, recorded, transcribed, verifi ed, and translated. Using Braun and Clarke's (2006) thematic analysis approach, the researcher coded data to identify patterns of integration strategies and challenges. The focus group discussion served as member checking, validating initial interpretations and providing additional in sights. To protect participants’ anonymity, all names used in the results section are pseudonyms.

## Results

EFL instructors' approaches to finding learners' cultural and household contexts. Interviews with the EFL instructors revealed two contrasting perceptions about integrating learners’ personal and local backgrounds into ESP classes. T hree participants (Cameron, Davis, and Keyna)  stated they explicitly collected this information through in-class activities. For instance, Davis used a dice game as an engaging task to learn about learners' personal and cultural lives a nd identities. He designed categories for each side  of the dice, and each category had 5 to 7 questions. Learners th rew the dice and chose the question they wanted to reply to depending on the number it landed on. This strategy employed by the instructor seems to aid in eliciting learners’ identities, enhance learners’ speaking skills, build confidence in using English, and promote meaningful language practice. Cameron and Keyna  utilized introductory presentation activities. Cameron commented, “ Learners introduce themselves, I write down some questions on the board, so they can make their own presentation. And I always pay a lot of attention to the place they com e from, especially at the Atenas site, which is mul ticultural, right?” Keyna implements a sim ilar activity depicted by Cameron. “It's like a pre sentation activity, so it's like a warm-up in the class. They tell me their name, how old t hey are, what hobbies they like, and w hat they expect from the class. That is a space that I give to lear ners, and I tell them, you can say it in Spanish, E nglish, or Spanglish, right? I kind of give them that option so that they can express themselves at that moment.” Additionally, Keyna indicated that she uses recess time to connec t with and understand her students.  This student-centered approach, applied by these two participants, encour ages EFL students to engage in personal storytellin g, helping instructors better understand students' interests a nd local contexts. By creating informal opportuniti es for connection, this strategy aims to help  foster stronger relationships and a deeper understa nding of learners' contextual backgrounds.

In contrast, Mariana and Jorge valued connecting the ESP curriculum to language learners’ backgrounds but didn’t explicitly collect such information due to ESP curricular constraints. Jorge revealed that he previously collected learners’ local backgrounds and identities  through interactive activities but reported changes  post-COVID-19, emphasizing technical vocabulary and limiting perso nal interaction time. “I feel that before it was a little more interactive, so I invested more time in that part of getting to know my learners. Then, you have to teach more technical vocabulary, and we don’t have general topics.” Mariana also mentioned that obligatory diagnostic testing on the first day of class  reduced opportunities for such  activities. Participants’ perceptions cast light on how some EFL instructors must prioritize more  curricular demands to fulfill their role as English instructor s. Despite the constraints, both  instructors expressed that they  gathered student information informally. Jorge mentioned, “In a normal working day, I never sat at my desk; rather, I walk around while learners are working in small groups, and I am always talking to them. So, these informal conversations give me some insights about them instead of administering a formal task.” In the cas e of Mariana, she conducted a brief getting-to-know-you activity in the first class. “What I do in the first class is that they talk a little about themselves; I open the space; I do it one by one and I try to hear all of them beca use actually I am really interested in knowing wher e they are from, their preferences and at the same time I list en to their level of proficiency in English as a pa rt of the diagnostic test as well.” The i nference behind EFL instructors’ thoughts is that d espite technical challenges, knowing their learners’ local life experiences through informal interactions can be as effective as a formal method for understanding their EFL students in their formal learning environment. EFL instructors' strategies and challenges when connecting to learners’ experiences. This theme  explores EFL instructors'  methods and materials  for integrating  language learners’ cultural and household experiences with the ESP curriculum. Findings revealed that three participants actively employ varied approaches to connect students' identities with lesson content. Cameron and Keyna, for instance, use geographic information from language learners’ hometowns to contextualize curriculum topics. Cameron stated, “Depending on the topics, I am searching for examples of diffe rent parts of the country where they come from; it helps me to feel learners more identified as well as other clas smates learn and know about others’ environments.” This approach of using geographical examples recognizes learners’ local context and promotes inclusivity and grasping among learners. On the other hand, Davis attempted to connect language learners’ identities to the ESP syllabus, explaining, “If learners like video games, one day I bring in readings about video games, especially for Software learners, boys and girl s are fans of video games, right? Then, video games become a comm on topic in the classroom.” This EFL instructor’s  intentional customization suggests that incorporating topics that students ar e interested in enhances EFL learning engagement and customizes the educational content to specific interests. Additionally, findings revealed that EFL instructors use diverse educational resources to bridge learners’ local cultural backgrounds and home practices into ESP activities. For instance, Keyna and Davis use digital tools to facilitate the connection of their learners’ loc al contexts and syllabi. Keyna mentioned she uses digital tools such as Mentimeter, a presentation-based digital tool that allows educators to teach e ngaging lessons, attract the audience’s attention, or deliver vocabulary lists depending on the topic. Davis added that he employs Wordwall— an online tool for creating interactive activities —because it offers several options to create diverse  games and incorporate their lived experiences as vocabulary.” Other resources mentioned were physical materials such as photos, board games, worksheets, audio, and reading s. Keyna reported that everything depends on the lesson objective to decide what resource  might be more appropriate. Additionally, Cameron acknowle dged that sometimes he has to draw on t he use of Artificial Intelligence to connect learne rs’ home practices and syllabus because it is difficult to find concrete and specif ic materials that align with learners’ purposes. It was noticeable that interactive and diverse resources created enga ging and enjoyable learning experiences, fostered a deeper connection to students’ local identities and contexts, and supported inclusive classroom environments. Despite the benefits of integrating students' backg rounds into lesson planning, two EFL instructors pointed out that connecting learners’ local context s and identities with the ESP curriculum presents s everal challenges. One of the primary difficulties is rela ted to the specific demands of different fields wit hin the ESP curriculum. Jorge, who teaches technical vocabulary  in programming, noted that incorporating personal details about students' lives into lessons that focus on hi ghly specialized content like programming data is p articularly challenging. Nonetheless, he mentions that he makes an effort to learn more about his students' major curriculum to better connect the technical lexicon with Englis h lessons. Similarly, Mariana, who works with stude nts in a different sub-discipline, acknowledged that there are topics wher e bridging learners’ local backgrounds with the syllabus content is possible, but the ease of making these connections often depends on the specific major. Despite these drawbacks, both EFL instructors expressed openness to exploring ways to integrate their learners’ cultural identities and contexts into future ESP lesson planning.

## Sharing EFL participants’ insights by using a visualization tool

EFL participants engaged in a focus group to discuss how they surfaced their learners’ household life experiences, skills, and contextual factors and identities when they met their students  for the first time and how they included their language students’ data with the ESP curricul um. The facilitator (first author) started the focus group by requesting participants to look at the Net.Create visualization tool, to explore it, play with it, connect their nodes with their colleagues’ nodes to demonstrate perceiv ed connections, and include other aspects that were not discussed in the one-to-one interview with t he researcher. After participants engaged with the tool, they adde d new connections, built on shared themes with their coworkers, and refined existing ones. This process brought to light several key aspects of their teaching practic es, which were mediated and clarified through the o nline visualization tool.

One of the unfolded aspects was that Net.Create helped EFL instructors recognize that they all aske d EFL learners about topics such as hobbies, music, sports, and personal anecdotes, which are part of their language students’ lives and are integrated into their teaching practices. The t wo participants who had initially mentioned that they did not incorporate their learners’ personal preferences into their curriculum and hence didn’t have their nodes connected to the other participants’, realized upon exploring their colleagues' ideas represented in the form of corresponding nodes in the visualization tool that there were several common topics that they did indeed discuss with their EFL pupils in their classes. For instance, Mariana, who works at the main campus in Alajuela province, mentioned, “When I introduce myself and then I me ntion that I worked in  Puntarenas, it is highlighted, maybe some learners tell me that they are from there or they have famil y members there.”  Another crucial aspect was how the filtering feature of Net.Create allowed EFL instructors to isolate and focus on specific aspects of learners’ geographical and cultural backgrounds, making it easier to see how these factors contribute to understanding their local conditions and leveraging their lived experie nces in EFL  teaching environments.  For example, when the researcher asked why the FOI geographical  category was larger than others, some participants articulated that geographical factors significantly impact their tea ching as they shape students' perspectives and enga gement in class. For example, Davis indicated that even though all learners live in the  same regional place of the country, language learners always bring up their own local p lace of living, especially mentioning certain peculiarities of the place. This idea points out how geography impacts students ’ experiences and can be discussed within EFL formal ecosystems. On the other hand, Cameron under lined that at the Atenas regional site where he wor ks, language learners seldom mention their place of liv ing because they belong to diverse geographi cal areas of the country; nevertheless, that is why he asks their pupils’ hometown the first day of class because he takes this as an opportunity to raise this topic later in the EFL cu rriculum. This in fers that recognizing and leveraging their language learners’ geographical areas aids in creating a supportive and inclusive FL learning environment. Lastly, participants found that the visualization tool showe d connections related to social aspects rather than  purely academic ones. This captured the dynamic and evolving nature of student-teacher connections and interests, reflecting changes in their interactions and priori ties. Jorge, who remarked in his one-to-one interview with the researcher that he never applied an in-class activity to capture their EFL learners’ household practices and cultural identities, commented that he connected himself with the topics that he loved. “I connected myself with the topics that I loved, for example, video games. Actually, I  include this topic in dialogues as an in-class activity. I also added anime, which is something that learners love.” This shared interest empowers the participant to create more engaging and relatable learning experiences. Furthe rmore, Mariana expressed that she connected several  topics, such as family, hobbies, likes, and dislikes, among others on the network. “Maybe at the moment of the one-toone interview, I said, well no, perhaps in my case I try not to get involved much with them; however, I always take part of getting to know them at the end; then, I observe topics that I have covered and discussed with learners within the class.” This suggests that, although these two participants  initially felt they did not engage deeply in their language learners’ lived experiences and loca l identities, they later realized they had made imp licit connections through classroom discussions and inter actions. While these connections occur later in the  process, they still offer valuable insights into student int erests, which allows the instructors to adjust thei r teac hing approaches and better tailor the curriculum to meet learners’ needs.

## Conclusions and Implications

The study underscores the value of integrating language learners' cultural identities, household contexts, and lived experiences i nto foreign language (e.g., English) instruction, fostering inclusive, engaging, and personalized learning environments. Foreign language instructors can employ diverse methodological strategies and resources to creatively bridge learners' household experiences with curricular content.  Additionally, findings demonstrate the potential of  visualization tool s to facilitate instruction reflection, the identification of  shared method s, resources, challenges, and collaboration  within the teaching community. By honoring language learners’ contextual identities, household contexts, and lived experienc es, EFL instructors meaningfully connect local contexts to foreign language learning.

Within the Learning Sciences, this research contributes to grasping how the language learners' cultural identities, household practices, and lived experiences can enrich higher education curriculum design, specifically for EFL instruction in Costa Rica —where such  resource-based approaches (i.e., Fok and FoI)  remain underexplored. By  documenting various instructional strategies, resources,  and related challenges, this  study offers insights into balancing higher education EFL  curricular demands  with students' local experiences and identities, especially in specialized fields like ESP. F indings emphasize the need for  flexible, contextualized curriculum designs that maintain academic rigor, aligning with broader educational goals of creating equitable, meaningful learning by connecting academic content with learners' everyday realities.
