# Self-Regulated Learning with Generative AI: Student Strategies, Performance-Based Profiles, and Instructional Implications

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This mixed-methods study examines how students enact self-regulated learning (SRL) with GenAI in an authentic undergraduate chemical engineering course. Using an activity-centric SRL lens, we triangulated interviews with students (n = 12) and instructors (n = 3), a post-course survey (n = 40), course-tool interaction logs, and AI-supported student artifacts. Findings show that GenAI supported SRL across forethought, performance, and reflection by helping students initiate tasks, refine ideas through dialogue, verify outputs, and review their work. Across performance groups, three recurrent profiles emerged: Prudent Architects, Struggling Explorers, and Anxious GenAI functioned primarily as a learning-intention amplifier in SRL, strengthening deeper regulation when mastery goals were strong and reinforcing shortcut-oriented strategies when performance goals dominated. We discuss targeted instructional scaffolds for fostering critical and ethical AI-supported learning.

Generative AI (GenAI) is rapidly entering higher education, creating new opportunities for support, feedback, and task completion, but alsonew risks of over-reliance and shallow engagement (Baig & Yadegaridehkordi, 2024; Nikolic et al., 2023; Fan et al., 2025). Self-regulated learning (SRL), understood as learners’ active management of their thoughts and behaviors in pursuit of academic goals (Schunk & Zimmerman, 1994; Zimmerman, 2002), offers a useful lens for understanding this tension, as  students have greater autonomy but may also drift toward passive information gathering instead of deep engagement (Aleven et al., 2016; Broadbent & Poon, 2015; Ghaleb & Alshiha, 2023; Molenaar, 2022).

Prior work on GenAI-supported SRL has mainly examined purpose-built systems designed to scaffold planning, task execution, or post-task reflection, such as AI coaches, embedded scaffolds, and reflective dashboards (Mohan et al., 2020; Hew et al., 2023; Takagi et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2023). Less is known about how freely available GenAI, by lowering start-up barriers, providing immediate dialogic feedback, and externalizing intermediate reasoning, may reshape how regulatory activity is enacted and made visible across the learning cycle. We examine how AI-supported SRL was enacted in an undergraduate chemical engineering course. We ask: (1) How do students use GenAI to support SRL across the learning cycle? (2) How do AI-supported SRL strategies differ across performance groups? (3) What instructional implications follow from these patterns? Our contribution is an empirically grounded account of AI-supported SRL in an authentic course, and a performancelinked typology showing that GenAI tends to amplify learners’ existing intentions and regulatory orientations. Conceptual framing We draw on Zimmerman’s three-phase model of self-regulated learning (SRL)—forethought, performance, and reflection—as an organizing structure for examining how students work with GenAI across the learning cycle (Zimmerman, 2000, 2002). However, our aim is not to infer fine-grained internal psychological states from limited traces. nstead, following recent work that reorients SRL analysis toward concrete AI-supported learning activities (Jin et al., 2023), we adopt an activity-centric analytic lens suited to GenAI-rich settings, where planning, checking, and review are often enacted through interaction and can be corroborated through logs and artifacts. The framework was used as a sensitizing and coding structure. We focused on observable or clearly reportable AI-mediated activities, such as task framing, plan generation, iterative prompting, verification, review, and evaluative revision. Broader constructs such as motivation, goal orientation, and attribution were t reated as cross-phase interpretive themes that emerged through comparison across students and performance groups. This framing allowed us to ask how AI-mediated learning activities were enacted across phases, how these patterns clustered across learners, and what underlying orientations appeared to shape them. Methodologies This mixed-methods study was conducted in an 18-week undergraduate chemical engineering course at a top university in China, where students were explicitly encouraged to use GenAI for learning and assignments. In addition to freely available GenAI tools, students were provided with a course-specific chatbot intended to support course-related inquiry. The course also included targeted AI-related instructional activities, such as critical comparison of outputs across tools and an optional assignment involving chatbot construction. However, students were not taught any formal SRL theory or asked to follow an explicit SRL model. Data included semi-structured interviews with 12 students (3 0 minutes each) and 3 instructors  (60 minutes each) via Zoom, a post-course survey (n = 40), course-tool interaction logs, and AI-supported student artifacts. Student interviewees were selected through purposive stratified sampling across three performance groups based on final grades: Group A (high-performing), Gr oup B (mid-performing), and Group C (lower-performing). Interviews were conducted after grades had been calculated but before official release in order to reduce retrospective bias while preserving access to semester-long learning experiences.  The survey captured students’ AI use, perceived competence, ethical reflection, and future intentions. The scale showed strong internal consistency (α = 0.89; ω = 0.90), exceeding the commonly accepted threshold for reliability (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994). We then used correlation analysis to identify meaningful associations among key variables, following Cohen (2013). Logs and artifacts were used for triangulation, helping us corroborate students’ self-reported practices and strengthen the credibility of phase-based and cross-group interpretations. The activity-centric SRL framework was used as a first-cycle deductive coding structure. We coded for observable or clearly reportable AI-mediated activities within the three phases of forethought, performance, and reflection. Then, we conducted inductive coding to identify recurrent patterns, cross-phase themes, and differences across performance groups, which allowed broader constructs such as motivation, goal orientation, and attribution to emerge through cross-case comparison, rather than being imposed as primary coding categories from the outset. This section addresses RQ1 and RQ2, focusing on how students engage in AI-supported SRL and how such strategies vary across learners with different grade levels (Group A/B/C). AI-supported activities across the self-regulated learning cycle Students were observed to use GenAI in ways that mapped onto all three phases of the SRL cycle. We focus here on how AI-supported activities were enacted across forethought, performance, and self-reflection. In the forethought phase, students used AI to clarify task requirements, break down unfamiliar tasks, identify resources, and generate starting plans. AI also helped lower initiation barriers when assignments felt complex or overwhelming, by supporting goal setting, task analysis, and motivational regulation. A n example is the optional challenge-based assignment where students built an AI chatbot for polymer materials. Although the task required cross-disciplinary skills such as programming, students used AI to analyze the task and construct a learning blueprint rather than retreat from the unfamiliar domain. As students noted, AI could “ quickly show where to start,” turning an intimidating macro-task into manageable micro-steps. In the performance phase, students used AI not only to solve immediate problems, but also to support ongoing cognitive work. They asked AI to explain concepts, generate alternatives, test tentative ideas, and assist 2047 with subtasks, while monitoring their understanding through iterative prompting, response comparison, and verification against prior knowledge or external sources. AI also functioned as a n always-there help-seeking resource when students encountered uncertainty or gaps in understanding. In the self-reflection phase, students revisited AI outputs or interaction logs, used AI to critique drafts, reviewed their own reasoning, and identified weaknesses for revision. Students also reflected on the usefulness and limits of AI support and on how  their own effort interacted with AI in shaping outcomes. M any described a “use more, learn more” cycle: frequent, deliberate use improved their ability to ask better questions, judge value, and develop critical habits. In survey data, AI use frequency was positively correlated with evaluating AI answers, comparing them with one’s own understanding, and reflecting on academic integrity (all r > 0.30). S tudents also highlighted the value of a supportive and psychologically safe environment. Clear instructor signals that AI use was allowed reduced concerns and encouraged more open exploration, deeper evaluation, and revision. A supportive environment correlated with assessing relevance and revising before use (both r = 0.32). Differences in AI-supported SRL strategies across student groups Although AI-supported activities appeared across the dataset, their configuration differed systematically across performance groups. Consistent with prior work suggesting that learner differences matter for adaptive scaffolding (Bannert & Reimann, 2012; Jouhari et al., 2015), we identified three recurring profiles: Prudent Architects (Group A), Struggling Explorers (Group B), and Anxious Dependents (Group C). These distinctions became especially visible when examined across the three SRL phases of forethought, performance, and self-reflection. ## Method

Reflection Balance depth and efficiency Ask-and-think; evidence checking Tool-focused; shallow reflection In forethought, the key difference lay in how students framed the task before turning to AI. Prudent Architects typically formed an initial understanding first and then used AI to refine goals, decompose tasks, or structure a plan. For them, AI supported planning but did not replace initial task framing. Struggling Explorers moved back and forth between independent planning and rapid reliance on AI, especially when they encountered uncertainty or time pressure. They often wanted to understand the task for t hemselves, but their initial planning was less stable. Anxious Dependents  were more likely to approach AI at the outset and rely on it to generate the overall direction of the task. In this phase, AI primarily reduced uncertainty and lowered start-up barriers, but it also made it easier to outsource initial framing. Across group s, forethought was shaped mostly  by learners’ existing goal orientation and willingness to engage difficulty directly. In performance, the groups differed most clearly in the depth of processing, the quality of monitoring, and the role AI played in ongoing work. Prudent Architects used AI to extend and test their own thinking: they compared outputs with prior knowledge, checked logical coherence, and reconstructed ideas in their own terms. Their interaction with AI was dialogic and evaluative. Struggling Explorers also experimented actively and showed emerging awareness of productive practices, such as checking intermediate steps or comparing alternative responses, but their monitoring was often partial and inconsistent. They moved between genuine inquiry and expedient reliance. Anxious Dependents often appeared active because they consulted multiple tools or asked 2048 many follow-up questions, yet their use more often remained surface-level: they relied heavily on external validation and were less likely to transform comparison into independent judgment. In self-reflection, the differences centered on whether students could turn review into strategy adjustment. Prudent Architects engaged in confident but critical reflection: they revisited outputs, evaluated weaknesses, and used reflection to improve subsequent work. Struggling Explorers showed substantial problem awareness and sometimes adopted corrective strategies, such as drafting before consulting AI or revisiting key concepts after using it, but these adjustments were not yet stable. Anxious Dependents also recognized some risks of AI use, such as superficial understanding or reduced independent thinking, yet their reflections often remained aspirational rather than actionable. They could identify problems but were less likely to translate reflection i nto sustained regulatory change. Across groups, then, self-reflection marked the clearest divide between noticing problems and building durable strategies for future learning. Taken together, these phase-based differences suggest that GenAI tended to amplify the intentions and regulatory orientations students already brought into learning. Students with stronger mastery goals were more likely to use AI to deepen understanding and refine work, whereas students oriented more toward speed, completion, or external validation were more likely to use AI to bypass difficulty. This section addresses RQ3 by outlining instructional implications for supporting students’ AI use. I nstruction should help students develop the principles and habits needed for effective and critical learning with AI. Our take responsibility for their own learning while also supporting deeper thinking. Instructors in our interviews described some AI-assisted work as broad but not always deep, a concern that echoes prior studies suggesting that rapid AI-generated answers may reduce reflection and creativity (Fan et al., 202 5). Meanwhile, a supportive environment remains important: when AI use is openly permitted, students are more likely to move from covert use to more reflective experimentation (Albelbisi & Yusop, 2019; Chiu et al., 2022). In practice, one useful step is to establish clear expectations for AI use. Students can be guided to treat AI as a support rather than a substitute, while course rules can clarify acceptable uses and require disclosure of AI assistance where appropriate. AI-related instruction may include prompt design, evaluation of output quality, reflection should also be incorporated so that students consider the respective roles and boundaries of human and AI contributions. Task and feedback design can further promote deeper engagement. For example, students might first produce their own response, then compare it with AI output and reflect on differences as well as their prompting and revision process. More broadly, these changes suggest that instructors may increasingly act not only as content deliverers, but also as task designers, cognitive guides, and feedback providers who model disciplinary reasoning, scholarly judgment, and professional ethics (Jouhari et al., 2015). Given the differences across student groups, support should also be differentiated. Following adaptive scaffolding (Bannert & Reimann, 2012), instructors can adjust the form and intensity of support according to learners’ SRL needs. For Group C, support should focus on helping students see AI less as an authority and more as a cognitive aid, for example through routines such as “think first, then seek help” and explicit practice in questioning AI outputs. For Group B, more structured and concrete support may be especially helpful, including clear policies, labeling requirements, and illustrative examples that help turn emerging reflection into more stable learning strategies. For Group A, greater autonomy and more open-ended tasks may help extend their strat egic, creative, and ethical engagement with AI. Overall, the goal is to support students in developing the self-regulated and critical capacities needed to use AI productively and responsibly over time. This study has  several limitations. It was conducted in a single undergraduate chemical engineering course at a top university, which limits broader generalizability. Second, although we triangulated interviews with survey data, course-tool logs, and AI-supported artifacts, the logs did not c apture students’ full interactions with all GenAI tools. The findings are best understood as an analytically grounded account of how AI-supported SRL can unfold in an authentic setting, rather than as a full model of all student –AI interaction. In an authentic undergraduate course, students used GenAI in ways that mapped onto forethought, performance, and self-reflection, but these AI-supported activities differed meaningfully across performance groups. By retaining a phase-based SRL structure wh ile redefining its categories in more observable and reportable terms, this study offers a more defensible account of how GenAI relates to SRL  in practice. Our findings suggest that GenAI is best understood not as a simple catalyst of SRL, but as a learning-intention amplifier whose educational value depends heavily on pedagogy, learner orientation, and the quality of instructional support. 2049 Albelbisi, N.
