# Rotating Bodies Bulge Outward: A Novel Intuitive Resource for Rotational Motion

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Physics students routinely struggle to understand the Coriolis force and the centrifugal force. While investigating students’ prior knowledge resources in these domains, we observed the use of an intuitive element regarding the “bulging” demonstrated by rotating bodies. This element exhibits similar characteristics to a previously described class of intuitive knowledge resources for making sense of physics phenomena: phenomenological primitives (pprims). We document the attributes of the “bulging” resource and compare them to the expected traits of p-prims.

## Introduction

Inertial forces, such as the Coriolis force and the  centrifugal force, are notoriously difficult to te ach. Numerous errors about both can be found in the thinking of physicists and lay people alike (Grof et al., 2020). Our team has been working on a novel conceptual approach to teac hing the Coriolis force which utilizes the wealth o f knowledge that students have amassed about the physical world prior to their formal education (Minstrell, 1992). While cataloging the knowledge resources shared by our participants, we observed a pattern of responses which shared attributes with a previously documented clas s of intuitive knowledge elements. Here, we describ e the observed properties of this potential intuitive resource and make a case for its classification.

## Theoretical framework

Knowledge in Pieces (KiP) is a constructivist theory of knowledge and learning. It views knowledge as a network of individual elements, which are connected and activated through recognition. Naïve learners acquire elements to describe natural phenomena through their interactions with the physical world. These foundational r esources are called phenomenological primitives, or “p-prims ” (diSessa, 1993). P-prims provide “common sense” explanations for the causal mechanisms in the natur al world. They are activated when relevant features  of a problem-solving context are recognized by the learner, or when a closely related neighbor element is a ctivated. At times, learners may unwittingly create erroneous connections by missing relevant information or over-applying concepts. These faults can be the root of difficult-to-dispel “misconceptions” and can interfere with the learner’s construction of new understanding (Smith et al., 1993). As they advance from novice to expert, learners reorganize existing connections to better align with physics p recepts and construct higher-order knowledge struct ures to create interdisciplinary cohesion (diSessa, 1993; M instrell, 1992). This refinement repurposes p-prims  into a supportive role for the larger knowledge structures. By understanding the productive and counterproduc tive connections in their students’ knowledge, educators can improve their strategies to better address the incongruities and support the development of robust, reliable knowledge structures (Brown & Clement, 1992).

## Methodology

Eleven undergraduate physics students at a large university in the Intermountain West participated in 1-on-1 Zoom interviews, during which they thought out loud about phenomena influenced by the Coriolis force. Five students had not yet taken the upper-division course in which the Coriolis force is formally taught, while six students had completed this upper-division course. The participa nts were asked background questions before being gu ided through a set of thought experiments which visualized the earth as rotating and non-rotating, spherical and oblate, and from inertial and rotating reference frames. Th e students made predictions about the motion of an object moving across the surface of the earth in each scenario. The audio recording of the interviews was transcribed and student responses were analyzed to uncover the knowledge elements underlying their reasoning.

## Results

One element of knowledge, which emerged when the students discussed rotating objects, shared many similarities with p-prims. Nine of the eleven participants were aware that the earth’s shape deviated slightly from spherical. Most students explained that this is a consequence of the earth’s rotation. The ubiquity of this ass ertion prompted closer inspection. We named this el ement “bulging” and compared it to the expected attributes and identifying principles of p-prims (diSessa, 1993). There was a noticeable difference in the way the tw o groups of students spoke when explaining the phenomenon. The students who had only taken Introdu ctory Physics tended to offer rotation as a de fact o mechanism that needed no further dissection, which is an example of the “principle of impenetrability,” by which p-prims are self-sufficient for naïve explanations. In comparison, the students who had taken the upper-division course used more advanced terminology like “inertia ” and “centrifugal force” to describe the mechanism. This shift matches the predictions made by KiP: advanced physics instruction co-opts intuitive knowledge within larger frameworks to act as a supporting tenet to higher-order schema. The advanced students reorganized their thinking and used broader topics to explain bulging in a way the more naïve students had not yet discovered. The students mentioned bulging in multiple contexts which had little in common be yond the topic of rotation, such as pizza dough, pottery wheels, and carnival rides. This shows that the bulging primitive was not associated with any particular object or material p roperty, but rather with the rotation itself. This method of connection via an easy-to-observe abstraction demonstrates the “principle of ready availability.” It also supports the “principle of functionality,” as having an intuitive explanation for bulging would be useful to naïve learners. Students also utilized bulging to describe the common experience of feeling pushed to the outside edge of a turn while driving in a vehicle. This exemplifies the “principle of body,” which predicts that p-prims will likely have strong roots in physiological experience and will be described by novices using their bodily sensations, as well as the “principle of obviousness,” which expects p-prims to form around familiar or universal experiences. Finally, when asked to predict what would happen if the earth were to rotate faster than it currently does, all eleven students (including those who were previ ously unaware of the oblate shape of the earth or h ad not understood its connection to the earth’s rotation) instantly predicted that it would increase the size of the bulge. This was a consistent, reliable activation of Ohm's p-prim, in which a larger input predicts a larger result.  This could be evidence that the bulging primitive forms a strong syllogism with another wel l-established knowledge element. Additionally, students sometimes describe orbiting bodies as being trapped in place by the op posing gravitational and centrifugal forces. This explanation could be recontextualized as an interaction between bulging and the supporting or clamping p-prims. These interpretations showcase the “princ iple of diversity” and the subtlety that exists between primitive knowledge element activations.

## Discussion

Overall, it appears that bulging is a good candidate for a phenomenological primitive as it displays many of the key characteristics of p-prims and agrees with a wi de range of identifying principles. Ultimately, it will require more targeted scrutiny than this study was able to provide to make a definitive conclusion. Future explorations of bulging could endeavor to determine the origins of bulging, expand the list of contexts in which bulging is activated, clarify the interactions between bulging and other p-prims, and assess the role that bulging could play in physics instruction. Investigating bulging could shed light on the difficulties that many edu cators have experienced when teaching about the centrifugal for ce and rotational motion. Instead of fighting again st this primitive by labeling inertial forces as “imaginary” or “illusory” (which implies that the students are incorrect in their own perception and memory), educators could acknowledge and reinforce the bodily experience of students to situate the bulging primitive within a broader context. With careful r efinement, the bulging primitive could come to serve a unique and powerful role in the knowledge structures of intermediate physics learners.
