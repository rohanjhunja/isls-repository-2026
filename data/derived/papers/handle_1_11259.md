# Supporting Middle School Students to Learn AI Through Creative Machine Learning Projects

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To fill in the gap of K – 12 AI education, I designed and implemented a middle-school Machine Learning (ML) curriculum in a free online afterschool program. Students were engaged in hands-on ML projects for solving real-world problems. With the Teachable Machine platform, students took photos of themselves to train their own ML models that can automatically recognize if a person wears a mask or not in the context of COVID pandemic. Primary data sources were class recordings and students’ screen recordings. Interaction analysis of these recordings showed that, (1) students’ constant interactions with the learning tools helped them to test, revise, and improve their ML model; (2) students’ interactions with peers and teachers helped them to understand ML mechanisms; and (3) involving family members in data collection and model testing was helpful for students’ learning. The study sheds light on the learning design of AI/ML education for middle schoolers.

## Introduction

With the increasing popularity of Artificial Intell igence (AI), preparing the young generations for th e future AIdriven society becomes an important and urgent task (Tedre et al., 2021; Zhai et al., 2021). AI/Machine Learning (ML) courses have become popular in higher education for decades, but very few age-appropriate AI/ML curricula are available for K–12 students. ML refers to one group of computationa l algorithms that function as the “brain” of AI to support its intelligence (El Naqa & Murphy, 2015). Despite some recent explorations (Dwivedi et al., 2024; Gresse von Wangenheim et al., 2021; Norouzi et al., 2020; Touretzky et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022), much more research is needed to develop AI/ML curricula in K–12. Furthermore, very few existing AI/ML curricula include how to apply ML in addressing Socio-Scientific Issues (SSIs) — social issues with “conceptual or technological ties to science” (Sadler, 2004, p. 51 3). ML has been widely applied to solve SSIs in the  real world (WION news, 2020). A curriculum of applying ML to ad dress SSIs can engage students in authentic problem-solving (Breazeal et al., 2005; Famaye et al., 2023).

Therefore, to fill the gap in this field, I designe d and implemented a middle school AI curriculum to improve learners’ knowledge and practices in ML thr ough hands-on ML applications in the context of SSIs. The target grade level is middle school because i t is a critical phase for students’ STEM learning a nd identity development (Arnett, 2015; Potvin & Hasni, 2014). The current study focuses on students’ learning in one of the three modules in the curriculum, guided by the foll owing research question: How does the designed lear ning environment support students’ learning of ML through social interactions? Theoretical perspective of constructionism I followed the theoretical perspective of constructionism to guide my design of the curriculum (Kahn & Winters, 2021; Morales-Navarro et al., 2023). From the constructionist’s p erspective, students learn through constructing sharable and personally meaningful artifacts (Papert, 1980; Peppler & Kafai, 2007). Knowledge construction and learning cultures are the two key ideas in construc tionism (Kafai, 2006). In terms of knowledge constr uction, constructionism suggests that learning means building up knowledge structures based on what they already know (Kafai, 2006). For constructionists, objects play a significant role in the process of constructing knowledge, which could be either physical or di gital objects. Another key idea of constructionism is learning c ulture, which emphasizes the facilitating role of social context in the knowledge construction process. The curriculum is designed for a synchronous online  learning environment, where students can engage in hands-on practices of solving real-world problems with AI/ML technology. The real-world SSI context can connect students from what they already know to the  new AI/ML knowledge and practices. The learning environment is also designed to engage them in the social process of interacting with the computer too l (ML platform), physical tool, SSI context, and differen t people. These interactions provide students with the opportunity to build up new knowledge. Discussions and projects are included as key components that fo rm the constructionist’s learning environment. The projects allow students to create personally meaningful and shareable products. The discussions provide students with the opportunity of social interaction with peers and instructors.

## Methods

Implementations The current study is based on a larger design-based research (Barab, 2014) project, named Artificial Intelligence for Middle School Students (AIMS). The AIMS project includes two rounds of implementations. The curriculum and assessment tool were revised after the first iteration  in 2022. Then, the second iteration was implemented in Spring 2023 as a 16-hour online out-of-school extracurricular program, which is the focus of the current study. Throughout the Spring Program, I was the only instr uctor, occasionally assisted by a teaching assistant (TA). Participants were recruited mainly through distributing flyers on social media. This program was completed over four weekends, from 10:00 am to 2:30 pm on Saturday  or Sunday each week. Students self-selected to join the Saturday or Sunday cohor t. Ten students (Grade 6 –8) participated in the study and each cohort includ ed five students. Students’ pseudonyms and demographics (gender; race/ethnicity; location) are provided as follows:

• Saturday cohort: David (M ale; Asian/Chinese; Auckland, New Zealand), Feng (Male; Asian/Chinese;

New York, USA), Ting (F emale; Asian/Chinese; New York, USA), Benjamin (M ale; Asian/Chinese; North Carolina, USA), and Mei (Female; Asian/Chinese; New York, USA).

• Sunday cohort: Darsh (M ale; Asian/Indian; Texas, USA), Jack (M ale; Latino; Massachusetts, USA),

Carlos (Male; Hispanic; Florida, USA), Lily (Female; Asian/Chinese; Florida, USA), and Lucas (M ale; Asian/Chinese; Florida, USA).

ML-SSI curriculum design ML contents in the designed ML-SSI curriculum include the following five aspects: Definition, Data, Algorithm, Application, and Ethics. I selected the application of AI for automatic check of mask-wearing during the COVID pandemic as the SSI topic. Projects and discussions  are key components to form the constructionist lea rning environment. The curriculum includes three modules.  Module 1 focuses on supervised learning, with t he Teachable Machine platform (TM; https://teachablema chine.withgoogle.com/). Module 2 focuses on unsupervised learning, with the Orange Data Mining tool (Orange; https://orangedatamining.com/). Modul e 3 includes a brief introduction to reinforcement learning and a free-style debate on “Should AI technology be used to automatically check mask-wearing during the COVID pandemic?” Due to page limit, the current study focuses on students’ learning in Module 1. With TM, students collected data by taking photos o f themselves, used that data to train ML models to achieve automatic recogn ition of mask wearing, and tested and improved mode l performance. After individual work time on the ML project, they take turns to share their ML model with the class and discuss. Please note that both Saturday and Sunday cohorts completed Module 1.

## Data collection and analysis

Multiple sources of research data were collected, but the current study focuses on the recordings for the interactions among students and instructor/TA, including the Zoom recordings of all class sessions and some short episodes of students’ computer screen recordings (w hen they worked individually on TM or Orange). Supplementary data sources include pre-/post-tests and surveys, students’ learning journals, and instructor/guest’s observation notes. The recordings were analyzed with interaction analysis methods (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). The analysis focused on the class sessions of hands-on ML projects, during which: (1) how students interact with the ML tool/platform, materials or physical tools, and people; (2) how they draw on the SSI context; (3) if any evidence of their improvement in their ML knowledge  and practices; and (4) how those interactions may h ave supported their improvement. The analysis went thro ugh five major phases: Content logs, transcription, group view and discussion, individual researcher analysis, and detailed interaction analysis tables. More de tails about the data analysis and supplementary data sources can be found in Wang (2023).

## Discussion and conclusion

This study provides a successful example of middle school AI/ML curriculum that enga ged students in creative ML projects. Students worked on the Teachable Machi ne platform and created their own ML model that can automatically recognize if a person wears a mask  or not. Interaction analysis of students’ learning process in the ML projects showed the following findings. (1) Students had many interactions with the learning tools, including the computational tool TM platform and the physical  tool mask. They voluntarily referred to the SSI context, when they iteratively tested and revised their ML m odel. During the iterative process, they detected i ssues and improved their model performance. Aligned with prio r research of constructionist learning environment, both computational tools and physical tools can effectiv ely engage students  (Berland, 2016; Wilkerson-Jerde et al., 2015). (2) Students’ frequent interactions  with peers and teachers were critical to help them  understand ML mechanisms. This finding aligns with those in recent explorations of students’ learning of ML (e.g., Tseng et al., 2024). (3) Students involve d family members to help with enriching data and test ing ML models, which added fun to their project and contributed to their learn ing. Different from the way parents monitor kids’ attention and their task completion (Ribeiro et al., 2021), this new format of family AI/ML learning has great p otential and deserves further research.  This study also shows great potential for applying interaction analysis methods to investigate students’ learning process of AI/ML and inform the learning design for K–12 AI/ML education.
