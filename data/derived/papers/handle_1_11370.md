# The Influence of Colonial Legacy on Science Teachers’ Perspectives of Real-World Science Issues

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Science education has become increasingly linked to social justice, as global challenges like climate change and public health crises reveal underlying societal inequities. Scholars emphasize that science educators must engage with social and political movements to prevent reinforcing harmful ideologies that sustain these inequities. This submission explores the influence of colonial legacy on science teachers’ perspectives of science issues. Using narrative interview data about how secondary school science teachers in Guyana perceive and address socio-political dimensions of science, we explore in what ways the view of land and natural resources as commodities shows up in their perspectives. Findings reveal that, while teachers express concerns about sustainability and environmental health, their views often reflect colonial attitudes toward land and resources, treating them as opportunities for economic gain. This submission raises critical questions about how science education can authentically integrate social justice and challenge colonial ideologies in both curriculum and teaching practices.

## Introduction

Given global challenges like climate change, environmental degradation, and  public health crises (e.g., COVID19), science education scholars have increasingly advoca ted for the importance of embedding social justice imperatives in the teaching and learning of science phenomena. These global challenges that have a clear scientific connection have underscored societal inequities that shape  and are shaped by  related decision-making and problem-solving efforts. As such, scholars like Bazzul and Tolbert (2019) have argued that science educators must align themselves with social and political movement s as there is no true n eutral ground – a point made over 50 years ago by Paolo Freire (1970) who emphasized tha t any attempts to be “neutral” in education perpetu ates dominant and harmful ideologies.

While there is some scholarship that articulates examples of successful  attempts to intentionally embed social justice ideas with science phenomena (e.g., Upadhyay et al. 2019) and unsuccessful attempts (e. g., Tobin, 2002), Morales-Doyle (2024) has recently published a book entitled Transformative Science Teaching: A Catalyst for Justice and Sustainability  that offers an extensive discussion of teaching science for justice. He provides an argument for interrogating the problematic ideologi es teachers will otherwise perpetuate  in traditional science teaching, the kind of learning teachers must engage in within their communities in order to identify relevant social justice science issues, and examples of how he has done it and lessons he has learned along the way. His explicit examples illustrate how to modify science curricula such that “the science” is not compromised  in offering students a more  social justice-oriented approach that appropriately historicizes scientific  principles. MoralesDoyle emphasizes that this way of teaching would allow students to see sci ence more authentically as both part of the problem and of the solution for issues like global crises earlier mentioned. Through this submission, we would like to surface a consideration mentioned by Morales-Doyle (2024) that affect achieving these goals of teaching science for justi ce: the influence of colonial legacy on the ontolog y of science. For example, in a book that examines  pollution through the lens of colonialism, Liborion (2021 highlights the invisible ways that colonialism has influenced our approach to environmental problem-solving, that is, there is an assumption of un inhibited access to land to “produce value for settle r and colonial desires and futures” (p. 11). In the case of replacing fossil fuels for green technologies, for instance, land is still exploited for extraction, and waste management and storage. Liborion (2021) makes the important distinction that colonialism is the grounding problem -- not capitalism as commonly thought -- because it inspires Marxists and capitalists alike to view land and natural resources as commodi ties, rather than something we are in relationship with and have an obligation to. He proposes an alternative way forward for science:  anticolonial science i.e., science that does not reproduce entitlement to land and resources.

Using narrative interview data collected for a stud y exploring influencing factors for what and how science teachers in Guyana teach about socio-political dimensions of science issues, we explore here the ways colonial and anticolonial thinking shows up in teacher perspectives on science issues. As Guyana w as formerly colonized first by the Dutch and then by the Britis h for over three centuries, we wondered how teacher s in this postcolonial nation conceive of science issues and in what ways the colonial legacy informs their thinking on how these issues should be problem-solved.

## Methodology

This study was designed to explore (1) how science teachers situate science as a socio-political discipline and (2) in what ways science teachers position themselves a s socio-political actors in their teaching work. Guided by a theoretical framework that used the “figured worlds ” construct (Holland et al., 1998) and positioning theory (Davies and Harré, 1990; Van Langenhove, 2017), we aimed to surface how teachers position themselves i n relation to sociopolitical forc es and moral space (Van Langenhove, 2017) that constitute their figured worlds of science and science teaching. This framing allowed us to step in to the world as seen by teachers to learn the context that shape their thinking and decision-making relevant to their teaching. The first author conducted a  three-part semi-structured interview series in person and on a one-on-one basis with 25 secondary school science teachers across seven secondary schools within a single city  in Guyana (Seidman, 2013). Interviews in this tradition engage participants in  exploring their life experiences related to a relevant topic, exploring the concrete details of their lived experience with the topic, and reflecting on the meaning of those experiences (Seidman, 2013). Questions wer e designed as pre-set open-ended questions so that participants could reconstruct experiences relevant to the question (e.g., How did you decide to become a science teacher?) and so that “unexpected leads” could be followed (Glesn e, 2011, p. 134). ## A note about context

Guyana’s colonial history began 350 years ago when the na tion was colonized by the Dutch (Merrill 1992). Though the Dutch initially expressed interest only in trading with Indigenous people, they quickly transitioned to bringing enslaved people from Africa to work on plantations. This arrangement persisted for a century before the British took over as the colonizing entity. Under British rule, the importation of enslaved Africans continued until the abolition of slavery in 1834. A system of East Indian indentureship replaced African enslavement  in 1836 whereby Indian people were recruited, often under false pretenses, to work on plantations (Bahadur, 2014). Guyana gained its independence in 1966. T oday, its population largely consists of descendant s of enslaved Africans and indentured Indians, who together make up 69% of the population (MoE Guyana, 2021). In the nearly 60 years since independence, Guyana has become one of the fastest growing econom ies globally after recently integrating large-scale oil mining into its economy (Mackenzie, 2020).  Once among the lowest GDP nations in South America and the Caribbe an, Guyana saw a remarkable 62.3% increase in GDP i n 2022 alone (IMF, 2023). This new wealth has been funneled into several development projects around the country (e.g., Department of Public Information, 2024)  and has drawn interest from other nations for inves tment opportunity (e.g., Duncan and Marks, 2024).

## Findings

When sharing perspectives on real-world science issues, participants generally expressed concern with improving the economy of the country for the wellbeing of Guyanese people. In fact, one participant explicitly points out that Guyana’s main economic activities are “science based ” e.g., mining (oil, gold, silver) and large-scale agriculture. (2021) would call a colonial relation.

## Conclusion and implications

Science teachers in this study largely framed science issues important to Guyana’s context in a way that Liboiron

(2021) would classify as colonial, though they were all interested in ways to preserve the health of the environment

too. This tension in perspective is unsurprising. Given the legacy of the colonial relations Guyana has had  with other countries in which they have pillaged Guyana for wealth (Galeano, 1974) coupled with the lived reality of residing in a country that has not previously experienced economic growth, we considered most participants to be thinking practically about how Guyana can finally (and only) benefit Guyanese. Their perspectives align with the avenues that the wealthiest nations in the world have taken to accrue their wealth. In light of our findings, we propose that regardles s of any modifications to curriculum, there are dee p seated values and barriers that will guide how teac hers think about, treat, and deliver science ideas such as colonialism as we see here, which has inculcated th is idea of land as resource. We end with a two questions: (1) How can we prepare science teachers to talk about social justice  authentically in tandem with science when the way we currently address science issues reproduce colonial relations ? (2) In what ways should science teachers be trained in the sciences to disrupt our colonial relations that influence science problem-solving?
