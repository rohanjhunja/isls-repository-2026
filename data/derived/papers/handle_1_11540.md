# First-year STEM Students’ Learning Strategies: Status Quo

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates the learning strategies of 98 first-year STEM college students through the lens of information processing theory, self-regulated learning, and Dunlosky’s 10 learning strategies. Through analyzing open-ended survey responses, the study identifies six prevalent strategies for academic learning. The findings suggest that integrating foundational cognitive theories with current student practices can provide valuable instructional insights for supporting STEM learning.

## Introduction

Since the U.S. National Science Foundation (NSF) of ficially included engineering and technology in the  K-12 science and mathematics curriculum in the 1990s (NS F, 1998), STEM education has received unprecedented attention. However, learning STEM subjects often presents challenges due to the complexity of the discipline and the high cognitive load it imposes, particularly on students with limited prior knowledge (Gupta & Zheng, 2020). Consequently, supporting STEM students to foster su ccessful learning outcom es is crucial.  While many researchers and practitioners strive to construct supportive learning environments in STEM education, undergraduate STEM education is often taught in large, lecture-based formats, especially in introductory courses that enroll hundreds of students (Owens et al., 2017). Many undergraduate STEM dropouts occur within the first two years when students struggle with introductory courses du e to insufficient prior knowledge, cognitive skills, and effective study habits (Cromley et al., 20 16). In these settings, learning strategies such as note-taking play a crucial role, yet they remain underexplored in terms of their impact on student success. Grade point average (GPA) has emerged as one of the  critical predictors of achievement in these programs, highlighting the importance of identifyin g the strategies that correlate with academic succe ss. Thus, this study aims to examine the learning strategies most used by first-year STEM students and explore how these strategies relate to academic achievement. Specific ally, the research questions are: (1) What are the prevalent learning strategies of first-year STEM college students? and (2) How are these l earning strategies connected to academic achievement?

## Theoretical framework

This study utilizes three theoretical foundations f or qualitative data analysis: (a) Ten Learning Tech niques by Dunlosky et al. (2013), (b) Information Processing Theory (Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1968; Shiffrin, 1969),  and (c) Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) Theory (Zimmerman, 1990). Dunlosky et al. (2013) synthesized prior research to identify ten learning strategies that improve stude nt learning. Practice testing and distributed practice offer the highest utility across diverse learners, materials, and tasks. In contrast, keyword mnemonics and imagery use for text learning have lower utility due to their limited applicability. The selection criteria for these strategies included ease of practice without additional supervision and strong empirical support (Dunlosky et al., 2013). Information Processing Theory explains how new information is p rocessed into knowledge. According to Shiffrin and Atkinson (1968), memory consists of three component s: sensory register, short-term store, and long-term store. A stimulus is initially registered through sensory input, briefly held in short-term memory, and, if retained, stored permanently in long-term memory. The short-term store serves as a working memory where informa tion is manipulated and retrieved from long-term storage (Shiffrin, 1969). This process facilitates effectiv e learning through storage and retrieval practices (Swanson, 1 987). SRL theories describe how learners manage the ir learning via self-monitoring, self-evaluation, and self-reflection (Zimmerman, 1990, 2002; Pintrich, 2004). Zimmerman’s model includes three cyclical phases: forethought (goal-setting), performance (strategy execution), and self-reflection (evaluation). Pintrich’s model integrates motivational and cognitive factors, emphasizing goalsetting and strategic learning (Pintrich, 2004). Broadbent and Poon (2015) identified cognitive and metacognitive strategies, including note-taking, as essential for academic success in online learning. Russell et al. (2020) found that educators who model self-regulated behaviors enhance students’ learning strategies, particularly note-taking.

## Method

This study employed an exploratory, cross-sectional survey to examine study strategies among first-year STEM college students in the U.S. Using Prolific, a conv enience sample of 98 students (M = 23.19, SD = 8.11) was collected, with 40% identifying as female, 56% as male, and 4% as non-binary or transgender. Data was gathered via an online Qualtrics survey from March to Septem ber 2024, using open-ended questions on study habits. A two-stage coding process analyzed responses. Open codin g generated 23 codes, later refined into six categories using axial coding. The analysis was guided by thre e theoretical foundations: Dunlosky et al.’s (2013)  learning techniques, Information Processing Theory, and Self-Regulated Learning Theory  (Table 1). Key strategies included distributed and interleaved practice, alongside metacognitive thinking. The final coding scheme mapped responses to these frameworks, achieving a high inter-rater agreement (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.94). ## Results

We counted the frequency of each code per category to answer the posed research questions. While the a verage GPA did not greatly vary across the categories, som e discrepancies emerged in the frequency. For examp le, ‘repetition’ was the most reported learning technique. Such a report indicates that regardless of note-taking styles, students agree that repetitive learning, such as re viewing notes, is essential in benefiting from note-taking activities. Especially students who reported they r eviewed their notes (3.70) had higher GPA than thos e who reviewed textbook or course material (3.66). Review ing what was once organized through their own cogni tive process could be more beneficial compared to extern al sources. Another interesting observation emerges  from imagery from the text; although literature (e.g.,  Lerchenfeldt & Nyland, 2016) says ‘imagery for text’ is a highutility learning technique, it was less reported than other learning techniques.

## Discussion

This study analyzed open-ended responses from first-year STEM students, identifying common learning strategies: ‘attention,’ ‘retrieval,’ ‘self-regulated learning,’ ‘pattern recognition,’ ‘sensory input,’ and ‘elaborated rehearsal.’ The findings highlight the continued relevance of cognitive learning theories, particularly for students navigating large lecture-based STEM courses that require complex information  processing. Despite advances in technology and instructional methods, traditional l earning theories remain valuable for understanding student learning and informing effective educational suppor t. A key area for future research is the role of AI  in learning. Many students mentioned AI tools like Notion AI and  Google Gemini, but the survey lacked detail  on their specific use, complicating coding. Future studies could incorporate targeted questions to explore how AI supports student learning. This study also faced limitations due to its open-ended survey format, which lacked the depth of interviews and did not distinguish between effective and ineffective strategies. Some students reported using rote copying and cramming, which Dunlosky et al. (2013) classify as ineffective, suggesting the need for fu rther research on their potential cognitive benefits. Additionally, exploring how students balance effortful but effective strategies with motivation could offer insights into fostering persistence in STEM learning.
