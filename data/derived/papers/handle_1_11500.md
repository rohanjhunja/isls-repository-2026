# Promoting Learning and STEM Interest with Serious Games

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Using a robust experimental design, the research explores how game-based interventions can augment traditional fraction instruction and promote STEM interest among a diverse group of elementary school students, including students with disabilities. The curriculum, grounded in Scheme Theory and Learning Trajectories, significantly improved students’ fraction understanding and STEM interest. The findings highlight the potential of integrating game-based learning into mathematics education to address foundational STEM concepts and advocate for further research to explore scalability and broader applicability.

## Introduction

Most 21st-century jobs require a college degree. Consequently, United States education reform in mathematics over the past 70 years has primarily aimed at ensur ing students successfully complete Algebra (Nationa l Mathematics Advisory Panel (NMAP), 2008), considered a "gatekeeper" course (Bailey, Hoard, Nugent, & Geary,

2012) for college and career readiness. However, research indic ates that many students' struggles with Algebra,

including students who experience mathematics diffi culties (MD), may stem from a limited understanding  of foundational concepts and skills. A significant lin k exists between algebraic concepts and an understa nding of rational numbers, especially fractions (Booth & New ton, 2012; Brown & Quinn, 2007; NMAP, 2008; Siegler, Duncan, Davis-Kean, Duckworth, Claessens, Engel, et al., 2012). A lack of conceptual understanding of fractions, in particular, can significantly impact students' a bility to perform and apply computational procedure s with fractions in advanced mathematics contexts (Hackenburg, 2013; NMAP, 2008). In this research, we examine how a game-enhanced supplemental fraction curriculum grounded in STEM careers (Hunt et al., 2025, 2024) impacts student engagement, fraction performance, a nd STEM interest in inclusive elementary school mathematics classrooms.  The curriculum (Figure s 1 and 2; see also Author, year) address 3rd through 6th grade US fraction curriculum standards and was created with a series of design experiments with elementary school students and their teachers across a period of 3 years (Brown,  1992). The research questions are: (a) Are there statistically signific ant differences in overall performance (i.e. the nu mber of correct responses) on a curriculum-based measure of fractions and on a measure of frac tion schemes for students with and without disabilities who do and do not particip ate in a game-enhanced intervention?  and (b) Are there statistically significant differences in STEM Interest for students with and without disabilities who do and do not participate in a game-enhanced intervention?

## Participants, data sources, and analysis

An experimental pretest-posttest design with mixed methods was used in this study. It occurred with six 4th grade teachers and their students (n = 86) in two different schools in the western United States. Full demographics will be given in session. The intervention occurred in T ier 1 (i.e., inclusive general education) settings,  which commonly includes 15-25 students with one teacher. The interventions in each school took place over 12 weeks, which is best practice in terms of time period for technology-based interventions (Gersten & Edyburn, 2007). Classrooms were randomly assigned to either the stu dy or the comparison group using matched pairs base d on the classroom means scores from students’ prior year state mathematics test scores. Fraction knowledge was measured before, during, and  after intervention using the Trajectory Aligned Fraction Assessment (TEMA), hereafter called “FractionCBM”. The test is a 26-item curriculum-based measure of the game-enhanced intervention. The test was group administe red and paper and pencil based. Test-retest reliability was 0.86; internal consistency reliabil ity for the test was 0.75. The Test of Fraction Schemes (Wilkins et al., 2012)  was used to address long-range fraction learning (called “FractionFA R”). The test is a 12-item measure of students’ partitioning and iterating pro cesses along with their mental schemes for fraction s. Internal consistency reliability and criterion validity for the measure are 0.70 and 0.58, respectively. Finally, the measure has The Upper Elementary School (4-5) and Middle High School (6-12) Student Attitudes Toward STEM (S-STEM) was used to measure changes in students’ self-reported STEM interests before, during, and after t he intervention. Cronbach’s α of the S-STEM ranged from 0.84 to 0.86 for the grade 4-5 subscales.

To address the first research question, we performe d a 3 (Time) × 2 (Condition) 2-way multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) on two dependent v ariables: FractionCBM and FractionFAR, after controlling for disability status. The independent variables were time (Pre -, Mid-, and Post-Test) and condition (Experimental and Control groups). Follow up analys es were performed using univariate ANOVAs. To addre ss the second research question, the team performed a 3 (Time) × 2 (Condition) 2-way analysis of covar iance (ANCOVA) on one dependent variable: STEM interest, after controlling for disability status. The indepe ndent variables were time (Pre -, Mid-, and Post-Test) and condition (Experimental and Control group s). Results of evaluation assumptions of normality, homogeneity of  variance-covariance matrices, and linearity were tested using Box’s method. Follow up analyses were performed using univariate ANOVAs.

## Results and discussion

For research question 1, there was a large, significant time*condi tion interaction effect (Wilks’ λ  =.71, F(4, 80) = 8.88, p <.001, ηp2 =.295, Observed power =.998).). To investigate t he impact of the effect on each DV, we performed univariate F-tests using an alpha level of.05, while controllin g for disability status. For the within subject analysis, the interaction effect was signif icant for both FractionCBM (Greenhouse-Geisser F(1.646, 135.579) = 11.36, p <.001, ηp2 =.12, Observed power =.98), and FractionFAR (F(2, 166) = 6.41, p =.002, ηp2 =.072, Observed power =.898). Figure 2 Student Fraction Outcomes For research question 2, there was a significant time*condition interaction with large effect sizes (Wilks’ λ  =.78, F(2, 45) = 6.55, p =.003, ηp2 =.225, Observed power =.89). Between-subjects effects revealed, while controlling for disability status, there was a main effect of condition on STEM interest (F(1, 46) = 11.39, p =.002, ηp2 =.199, Observed power =.91). Pairwise comparisons  indicated a significant meandifference on STEM interest score between the experimental and control groups (p =.002). Students in the experimental condition had significantly higher scores on the STEM interest su rvey (MSTEMExp = 6.022, SE =.332) compared to stude nts in the control condition (MSTEMCont = 4.571, SE =.262).

The findings highlight the potential of integrating  game-based learning into mathematics education to address foundational STEM concepts and advocate for  further research to explore scalability and broade r applicability (Hunt et al, 2025). The study also underscores the efficacy of innovative educational strategies in enhancing learning outcomes and fostering interest in STEM careers among diverse student populations. Results will be further unpacked and discussed in session.
