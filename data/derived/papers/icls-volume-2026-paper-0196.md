# From Tool to Encounter: Generative AI as a Catalyst for Ethical and Epistemic Transformation in STEM Teacher Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines how generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) reconfigures teacher learning within STEM education through a posthumanist and diffractive lens. Drawing on an empirical study involving preservice and inservice teachers engaged in AI-integrated science and mathematics classrooms, the paper reconceptualizes GenAI not as an instructional tool but as a pedagogical encounter and an active participant shaping teachers’ epistemological and ethical becomings. Using data from microteaching sessions, collaborative lesson design, and reflective narratives, the study maps how teachers negotiate distributed learning and ethical response-ability as they design and critique AI-mediated learning activities. Findings reveal three entangled processes: emergent agency in classroom dialogues, distributed learning between teachers and AI tools, and ethical reconfigurations of teacher becoming. The paper proposes an onto-epistemological framework for GenAI integration that emphasizes relationality, reflexivity, and culturally responsive practice, contributing to lea rning sciences scholarship on technology-mediated teacher education.

Recent debates within learning sciences have highlighted the urgency of rethinking how emerging educational technologies shape teaching, learning, and ethical practice. Generative AI (GenAI), with its capacity to produce text, code, and visualizations, has introduced not only new affordances but also ontological tensions within science, technology, mathematics, and engineering (STEM) education. While much of the public discourse frames AI as a productivity enhancer or cognitive aid, this paper reframes GenAI as a pedagogical encounter: a relational and transformative presence that reshapes how teachers come to know, act, and respond within their professional worlds.

This study situates itself within a science and mathematics teacher education context that foregrounds posthumanist understandings of agency and relational ethics. Rather than treating AI as an external instrument, the work explores how teachers co-constitute meaning and pedagogy through entanglements with AI syste ms, peers, and material contexts. The central question guiding this inquiry is: How does generative AI reconfigure teachers’ epistemological and ethical becomings within STEM education? By engaging with AI as a learning partner, the teachers in this study reveal shifts in professional noticing, identity negotiation, and moral imagination. Their experiences extend beyond skill acquisition, pointing toward a reorientation of what counts as learning, thinking, and teaching in AI-mediated environments. This paper aims to contribute to ISLS dialogues by offering an empirically grounded and theoretically rich account of how AI integration can cultivate ethical, reflexive, and epistemically transformative forms of teacher learning.

## Theoretical framework

The study is anchored in posthumanist and diffractive theoretical perspectives that challenge anthropocentric assumptions about knowledge production. Drawing from Barad’s (2007) concept of intra-action, Haraway’s

(2016) ethics of response-ability, and Dele uze and Guattari’s (1987) notion of assemblage, the paper

conceptualizes teaching and learning as emergent from entangled relations among humans, technologies, materials, and discourses.

From this stance, GenAI is understood not as a neutral artifact but as a co-participant in the pedagogical process, an actor whose presence reconfigures what teachers attend to, how they reason, and how they engage ethically with uncertainty. The framework of diffractive noticing (Jeong & Steele 2023) is used to analyze how teachers’ attention shifts when AI participates in meaning-making. Rather than focusing on accuracy or efficiency, diffractive noticing invites attention to difference, tension, and multiplicity: the productive disruptions that generate new understandings.

In classrooms where AI systems produce outputs that reflect cultural or epistemic biases, teachers are called to act not only as interpreters but as ethical agents capable of mediating and reframing the human– AI relationship. The framework thus aligns with learning sciences work on sociomateriality, epistemic justice, and design for equitable participation, emphasizing how technological entanglements can become sites for ethical lea rning rather than mere tools for delivery.

## Context and methodology

The study was conducted in a large public university located in a n Eurasian  national context where teacher education reform has emphasized digital transformation and interdisciplinary STEM learning. Within this setting, a semester-long professional learning course was offered to preservice and inservice teachers exploring generative AI (GenAI) integration in science and mathematics instruction. The course, facilitated by two researchers with expertise in science education and learning sciences, one of whom was visiting the university on a prestigious fellowship from the United States to conduct international and cross-cultural research, invited teachers to co-design inquiry-based lessons that positioned GenAI as a thinking partner rather than a content generator. Participants included six teachers, each identified with pseudonyms to preserve confidentiality: Doubleforce, Alice Detroidi, P eter Parker, Tony, Walter White, and Tweety. They represented a diverse range of teaching experience with AI technologies ranged from none to emerging familiarity through informal experimentation. The group composition was intentionally heterogeneous to support peer scaffolding and to encourage cross-disciplinary dialogue about technology-mediated pedagogy. Data were drawn from multiple sources that collectively captured the dynamic interplay of design, enactment, and reflection in AI-mediated teaching. These included microteaching videos documenting how participants implemented GenAI-supported lessons; reflective journals and design notebooks that chr onicled their evolving ideas, uncertainties, and ethical reflections; collaborative planning artifacts such as AI prompts, lesson outlines, and chat transcripts; and post-session interviews in which teachers elaborated on their perceptions of GenAI’s affordances, risks, and pedagogical value. All sessions were video-recorded, transcribed, and treated as multimodal artifacts for analysis. The analytic process followed a diffractive inquiry approach (Barad, 2007; Jeong & Steele,  2023; Lenz Taguchi, 2010), viewing data not as representations of discrete events but as material-discursive entanglements where human and nonhuman actants co-shaped knowledge. Each transcript and artifact was examined through theoretical diffraction gratings, mapping  how GenAI reshaped teachers’ epistemic stance, relational ethics, and decision-making. synthesis linking these to constructs such as entangled agency and ethical response-ability, and recursive cross-reading among the research team to refine insights. Researcher reflexivity was integral to the analytic process. The facilitators maintained analytic memos and reflexive journals to account for their positionalities and interpretive lenses. Ethical clearance was obtained from the host institution, and participants provided informed consent. They were invited to review case narratives and analytic summaries to ensure interpretive resonance and to affirm that their perspectives were represented authentically. Rather than seeking generalizability, the study aimed to illuminate how teachers, GenAI, and pedagogical contexts co-emerged in the act of learning, foregrounding the situated, relational, and ethical complexities of teaching with AI. Findings Entangled agency in classroom dialogues Across early microteaching sessions, teachers entered AI-supported lessons with expectations of control, often viewing GenAI as an assistant that could deliver pre-formulated answers. However, as they interacted with AIgenerated responses, new forms of dialogic tension emerged. Teachers found themselves drawn into exchanges where the AI’s phrasing, questions, or unexpected analogies reshaped the trajectory of classroom discussions. This entanglement blurred distinctions between who or what was steering the conversation. In one instance, a teacher reflected that she felt as though the AI was “thinking with me rather than for me,” signaling a shift from viewing AI as an external aid to recognizing its co-constitutive role in meaning-making. These encounters exemplify what Barad (2007) describes as intra-action moments where agency is not possessed but enacted through relation. Teachers’ epistemic orientations began to shift from directive questioning toward collective exploration. Rather than filtering AI outputs through evaluative authority, they leveraged them  to provoke curiosity, open interpretive space, and decentralize authorship. The resulting classroom dynamic was less about commanding the AI’s capabilities and more about attuning to the discursive rhythms emerging between human and machine. (Figure 1).

In alignment with Haraway’s (2016) notion of response-ability, these interactions required teachers to remain critically engaged with how knowledge was being co-constructed. By externalizing their thinking through GenAI, teachers developed meta-awareness of epistemic processes often invisible in routine practice. Their collaboration with AI tools thus r eframed learning as a socially and materially extended phenomenon, an assemblage that redefined what it means to “understand” in the act of teaching.

Through iterative practice, they encountered situations where AI outputs reproduced gendered assumptions, omitted marginalized perspectives, or distorted culturally nuanced examples. These moments of dissonance sparked sustained reflection and dialogue among participants, leading teachers to question the moral contours of algorithmic assistance. One teacher noted how such experiences “made me realize my role isn’t just to teach content, but to mediate how AI shapes what students see as valid or possible.” Such realizations mark what Deleuze and Guattari (1987) would call lines of flight—ruptures through which new forms of becoming e merge. Teachers began to recognize ethical reflection as integral, not peripheral, to pedagogical design. They reframed their role from users of technology to stewards of human– AI ecologies, cultivating sensitivity toward whose voices and values become amplified or silenced through AI mediation. This movement toward ethical response-ability reflects a broader epistemological reorientation: knowledge is no longer a fixed entity to be transmitted but a living, negotiated relation within more-than-human collectives. Figure 3 Entangled Trajectories of Ethical Response-Ability The study contributes to ISLS scholarship by advancing an empirical account of how GenAI can function as a pedagogical interlocutor that transforms the nature of teacher learning. It demonstrates that when teachers engage AI through curiosity and ethical awareness, they cultivate forms of reflexivity crucial for navigating the sociotechnical complexities of modern classrooms. First, conceptually, the paper proposes that STEM teacher education should move beyond instrumental training toward onto-epistemological design, designing environments that allow teachers to experience AI as relational and agentic. Second, methodologically, the study models a diffractive analytic approach suited for examining emergent learning phenomena where agency is distributed across human and nonhuman entities. Third, pedagogically, it argues that ethical reflection should not be treated as an add-on but as a constitutive aspect of AI integration, embedded in everyday design, dialogue, and classroom practice. These findings call for reimagining teacher preparation as a site for cultivating ethical attunement and epistemic humility. In reframing AI from a tool to an encounter, this study aligns with ISLS commitments to understanding learning as situated, mediated, and relational.
