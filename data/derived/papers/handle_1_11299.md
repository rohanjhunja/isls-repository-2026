# BarrelBots: Measuring Self-efficacy and Puzzle-based Computational Thinking in Minecraft

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Computer science education continues to be a factor in the leaky pipeline for students in STEM. To support the development of computational thinking (CT) skills and self-efficacy (SE) in computer science, we introduce BarrelBots, a puzzle-based block coding activity in Minecraft designed for middle school participants. We conducted an initial investigation on the context of a summer camp where participants (n = 15) solved researcher-designed puzzles that incorporated basic coding skills including sequencing, loops, conditionals, and functions. They also had the opportunity to design their own puzzles and share them with a partner and the class. In this paper, we investigate to what extent the BarrelBots activity promoted CT and SE development. In addition, we analyzed log data to examine correlations between in-game behaviors and participant post-test scores. We found that there was a significant improvement in STEM SE and some large significant correlations between the individual problem-solving log data behaviors and the CT post-test scores.

## Introduction

STEM education, and specifically Computer Science, continues to suffer from a “leaky pipeline”, a metaphor that characterizes the loss of STEM interest in children  as they grow older (Maltese & Tai, 2011). The prob lem is particularly problematic for groups who are current ly underrepresented in STEM (National Scienc e Board & National Science Foundation, 2024). For computing e ducation, Quigley et al. (2023) identified three fa ctors in developing underrepresented student interest: expos ure to CT activities that support agency and identi ty expression, provide an adequate level of challenge to promote self-efficacy, and allow for collaboration. Clearly, there is a need for continued research into creative ways to introduce computer science to young learners.

In this paper, we introduce BarrelBots, a computati onal thinking (CT) activity in Minecraft with collaborative and creative features. Based on the r ecommendations by Quigley et al. (2023), participan ts in the activity are given agency in how they utilize the CT knowledge that they acquire, have the opportunity to express themselves, and are able  to share their ideas with their peers. BarrelBots are modeled after the Minecraft barrel block (see Figure 1). They feature arrows on top indicating the direction that  they are facing and will move, if instructed to move forward (see Figure 1). At the t ime of the study, they had three ways to move: move forward one block, rotate clockwise 90 degrees, or rotate c ounterclockwise 90 degrees. There were also loops, conditionals, functions, and several actions (e.g. place a block) blocks. The puzzle modules are organized by the CT construc t to be learned and difficulty level so that participants candevelop SE while learning CT. Participants are given a set number of specific b locks per puzzle and are tasked with moving the BarrelBots from thei r starting position to the green block, representin g the end state (see Figure 1). Participants add blocks to th eir program by right clicking on the barrel and pla cing blocks into the BarrelBots, which will then execute the bl ocks in a simple linear fa shion. Unlike previous CT interventions in Minecraft, the code is displayed above the BarrelBots as it runs so learners can better identify errors, learners can hover their cursor over blocks  to get information about the certain block, and li ve log data is automatically collected. Once participants finish all the puzzles in a module, they can express themselves through building their own puzzles and sharing their work with their peers. Figure 1 The First Puzzle (left) and First Module Series, Movement and Loops (right) Computational thinking in Minecraft Minecraft has become one of the most popular games in the wor ld, with over 170 million monthly users, and 21.21% of daily user traffic originating from the U.S. (Kumar, 2024). Researchers have shown that even  with little experience, learners master controls quickly  and can effectively engage content when Minecraft is used in STEM learning environments (Lane et al., 2022). Given the ubiquity of the game, as well as ample opportunities to conduct research on learning and motivation (Lan e & Yi, 2017), Minecraft deserves attention regarding its effectiveness for promoting positive student outcomes in formal and informal learning environments. Minecraft has been previously used to teach CT, starting with fa n-built mods and associated informal curriculum like ComputerCraft over a decade ago (Mo nk & Monk, 2014). More recently Minecraft Education (formerly Minecraft Education Edition) has established itself as a formal platform for computer science education, including standards-aligned curriculum for several age levels, hosting events like Hour of Code and even finding adoption in schools worldwide (Minecraft Education, n.d.). Bile (2022), Kumral and Çam (2023), and Kutay and Oner (2022) used Minecraft Education  (ME) to teach participants either block programming  or python programming. Zorn et al. (2013) instead used a rese archer-designed block coding environment in Minecraft, CodeBlocks, where adult participants solved puzzles by placing blocks in the overworld. Although the researcher-designed BarrelBots intervention is similar to CodeBlocks described by Zorn et al. (2013), BarrelBots make use of several novel designs, use r experience, and data-collection approaches that suggest it may be a more effective platform for beg inning coders. There also has not been a study to l everage Minecraft for the potential to support underrepresented groups in CT. As such we seek to extend the work of Zorn et al. (2013) with the following research questions: RQ1: How does an informal learning experience utilizing collaborative, puzzle-based block programming in Minecraft influence computational thinking ability among underrepresented middle school learners? RQ2: How does the same experience referenced in RQ1 influence individual learners’ self-reported feelings of self-efficacy?

RQ3: How do variables derived from logged gameplay behaviors relate to self-efficacy scores and CT ability scores at post-test?

## Participants

BarrelBots was deployed as part of a large week-long summer camp series that reaches over 100 middl e school learners. Participants came from partner public schools that operate in some of the most underserved regions of a large midwestern city in the Unite d States. Our study included a subsection (single week-long class) with a total of 15 learners who were able to attend the entire w eek. The demographic breakdown of our learner sampl e is as follows: 80% African American, 20% White/Caucasian,  13% identified  as female, average age of just over 12 years old and all participants qualified for free a nd reduced lunch. The majority of learners came fro m schools where there is limited computer science curriculum, and many were beginner programmers. Materials Participants were all provided with a laptop, mouse, and an individual loaner (anonymous) account to p lay Minecraft: Java Edition on a server with the BarrelBots data pack described  above. They used the same account for each session. Log data regarding button presses, block interactions, puzzle completions, and playe r locations were automatically and anonymously logged using Minecraft plugins and stored in a remote, secure database. This is a notable difference between BarrelBots and  nearly every other introductory code interface in Minecraft: it is possible to capture data on learner interactions as well as real-time information about their code status. For pre-and post-tests, we used the 25-question competent Computational Thinking test (cCT t), a CT puzzle assessment developed by experts through use of focus groups and validated with substantial lear ner samples (El-Hamamsy et al., 2022). The measure showed strong mo del fit for  6 latent factors representing CT concepts (e.g. while statements, loops) across all participants (a ges 7-9; 𝝌2(260) = 551, p <.001, CFI =.978, TLI =.974, RMSEA =.027, SRMR =.052). Overall int ernal reliability for the measure was high for the full sample (ɑ =.85).

We also used Likert type 7-point scaled 5-question pre-and post-test SE measures for STEM and coding SE that were adapted from questions used by van Aal deren-Smeets et al. (2019). The measure showed adequate model fit for STEM intention, general beliefs, STEM beliefs, self-efficacy, and achievement across all participants (ages 14-19; 𝝌2(395) = 984.64, CFI =.973, TLI =.971, RMSEA =.056). Internal reliability for the measure was high with ɑ between.84 and.93 for each factor. We used parallel que stions for both the STEM and coding SE surveys (e.g. “I cando even the hardest work in STEM classes if I  try” and “I cando even the hardest work in Coding classes if I try”) and the responses ranged from fully disagree (scored as a 1) to strongly agree (scored as a 7).

## Procedure

Camp consisted of five days based in a classroom. O n the first day, campers tried activities related t o all of the camps offered. They ranked their first and second choices and BarrelBots was overwhelmingly the preferred firstchoice among all learners, emphasizing its overall appeal and immediate accessibility. On the second day of camp, the participants that ranked the BarrelBots activity highest (or only) were selected to take part in the sessions and were given the cCTt, STEM SE, and coding SE pre-tests. All participants were given as much time as neede d to complete all three measures, with everyone finishin g within 20 minutes. After completing the pre-tests, the participants had 1 hour to finish the puzzles in mo dule 1 and create their own puzzle using loops. The y then had 30 minutes to share the puzzle they created with their partners and the instructors. After a recess break, participants had additional time to work on module 2 (conditiona ls) and module 3 (sequencing) puzzles and create th eir own puzzles for each module. The remaining time was spent sh aring the puzzles that they created with their part ner and class.

On the third day, participants had an hour to work on continuing puzzles from the previous day and work their way towards the final module (functions). Aft er recess, they had time to continue the function p uzzles and had collaborative open build time if they finished early. The remaining time was spent sharing the puz zles that they created.

On the fourth day, participants worked on a habitat building challenge. In small teams, they were tasked with collaboratively designing a feasible Minecraft base on Mars. Teams had to include a form of Barrel Bots automation in their bases that used computational thinking concepts they had learned (conditionals, loops, nesting, etc.).

On the final day of the camp, participants were given the cCTt, STEM SE, and coding SE post-tests. All participants were again given as much time as neede d to complete all three measures, with everyone fin ishing within 20 minutes. The remaining time was spent finishing up their habitats and sharing them with all participants from the entire camp series as presentations in an auditorium.

## Data analysis

To answer the first research question, participant pre-and post-test cCTt scores were used to determine whether the BarrelBots intervention had an effect on participant CT knowledge. To answer the second research q uestion, participant STEM and coding SE surveys conducted before and after the BarrelBots activity were used. The sum of the self-reported answers given by the participants was calculated for the surveys. Given the small sample size, the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess changes in scores between pre-and post-test. To answer the third research question, measuring in-game participant individual puzzle solving behaviors was guided by previous literature in the field. Isr ael-Fishelson et al. (2021) used total solution attempt s, number of correct solution attempts, and completion time to assess CT skills from log data in a puzzle-based block coding environment. To operationalize analogous BarrelBots log data to match the dimensions that Israel-Fishelson et al.

(2021) discussed, the total number of times that th e BarrelBots was run (total solution attempts), number of tota l

solves (number of correct solution attempts), and n umber of attempts before solving a puzzle for the f irst time (completion time) were all counted by frequency.

Giving underrepresented students in STEM opportunit ies to express agency and identity is important in developing STEM interest (Quigley et al., 2023). Th us, investigating whether the in-game behaviors of the participants during the participant-designed puzzles were related to outcome measures was deemed im portant. Two metrics from log data associated with effort spent on the activity were gleaned: the number of white concrete blocks and the number of unique types of Minecraft blocks. The number of white concrete blocks interacted with was used, because participants were instructed to use this material to build the path; the number is used as a proxy for puzzle length. The number of unique blocks interacted with was used as a proxy for participant personalization of their own puzzles. A block interaction is defined as when a participant either places or destroys a block in their puzzle. Due to the small sample size of participants, Spearman correlations were used to examine the relationship between behaviors of interest and outcomes.

## Results

With regards to RQ1, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test was run to assess whether there was a significant difference on cCTt pre-and post-tests. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test revealed a non-significant difference in pre-and posttest scores,  z = 0.60, p =.57. The BarrelBots activity did not have any not iceable effect on participants’ CT assessment scores.

With regards to RQ2, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were also run to assess whether there was a significant difference on the STEM and coding SE pre-and post-tests. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test found that the effect of the BarrelBots activity on STEM SE was statistic ally significant, z = 2.67, p =.01. The measured effect size using Cohen’s d, revealed a moderate effect, d = 0.42. The BarrelBots activity did likely contribu te to improvements of general STEM self-efficacy feelings. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test found that the effect of the BarrelBots activity on coding SE was not statistica lly significant, z = 1.23, p =.23. The BarrelBots activity did not seem to have any noticeable effect on coding SE.

Lastly, with regards to RQ3, Spearman rank correlat ions were run to assess the relationship between participant in-game behavior and participant cCTt, coding SE, and STEM SE post-test scores. There were large significant negative correlations between the avera ge BarrelBots runs and participant cCTt post-test score, r(13) = -.67, p <.05 and the average attempts before puzzle completion and participant cCTt post-test scores, r(13) = -.54, p <.05.We, however, did not find any other significa nt correlations between the log data and the outcom e measures. It is worth noting the predominantly nega tive correlations between knowledge and self-efficacy measures and log data. As knowledge and self-efficacy tend to increase, attempts tend to decrease, suggesting overall increases in efficiency with greater knowle dge and efficacy. Between log variables a significa nt positive correlation was found between the number  of puzzles solved and unique blocks used when const ructing puzzles r(13) =.68, p <.05. This might indicate greater Minecraft knowledge corresponds to greater ability to solve i ngame puzzles. The rest of the correlations were non-significant.

## Discussion

BarrelBots is intended to be a friendlier, more inclusive puzzle-based block coding CT activity in Minecraft, with the goal of providing more engaging opportunities f or underrepresented and low-SES middle school participants to develop CT skills and build self-efficacy in coding and STEM more broadly. With rega rds to RQ1, we found that the intervention did not significantly affect pre-to post-test scores on the cCTt measure. The  duration of the experience was relatively short, and therefore the engagement time with BarrelBots may have been inadequate to realize any large changes in CT skills.

With regards to the second research question, we fo und that participants reported significantly higher levels of STEM SE after completing the BarrelBots a ctivity. SE has been shown to be linked to well-developed interest (Renninger, 2010). Thus, this result is es pecially encouraging for the potential for the inte rvention to trigger STEM interest with young underrepresented and low-SES participants and promote future STEM retention of the participants. We, however, found that the in tervention did not signifi cantly affect pre-to post-test scores on the coding SE test. As with CT skills, we hypothesize that the engagement time with BarrelBots was inadequate to realize any large changes in coding SE.

For the third research question, we found that two out of the three individual puzzle solving log data behaviors were significantly correlated with cCTt p ost-test scores. The average number of runs and the ave rage number of attempts before first solving a puzzle bo th had large significant negative correlations. Int uitively both of these findings make sense, since participants who have a greater grasp of CT skills would most likely struggle less on the puzzles and would thus run the BarrelBots puzzles less. Similarly, these participants would also solve the puzzles quicker. Thus, a higher number of puzzle runs may be indicative of participants struggling. Additional scaffolding/support when introducing new CT structu res could be beneficial in lowering the total numbe r of participant puzzle runs and the number of runs before first completing a puzzle. With regards to the participant-designed puzzle log data behaviors of interest we f ound that the use of unique blocks was significantly correlated with the  number of puzzles solved. This indicates that ther e may be knowledge linkages between Minecraft and computational thinking. A player who is more familiar with Minecraft might be aware of the function of a greater range of blocks, and in turn spends less grasping Minecraft mechanics when solving puzzles. Also, it is possible that the  more puzzles a player  solves the greater knowledge of block types and functions they develop and implement in their own puzzles. Interviews could be combined with artifact analysis to better recognize and interpret student learning outcomes and comparative analysis to simil ar blockbased Minecraft programming environments (in ME) could help to distinguish the impact and benefits of specific curricular and learning environment design decision s. Overall, the results demonstrate the potential f or a researcher-designed intervention in Minecraft for promoting inclusion of underrepresented and low-SES students in STEM. We are optimistic that future redesigns will show greater effects of BarrelBots.
