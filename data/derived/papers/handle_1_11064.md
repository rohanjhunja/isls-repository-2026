# Developing a Rubric to Evaluate the Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion Statement for Teaching Professors

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Issues of representation for faculty continue to be a challenge for institutions of higher education to address. Even within emerging faculty positions such as teaching professor/professors of teaching, there is a lack of diversity that is similar to research focused positions. To address this issue, we examined the hiring process for teaching professors/professors of teaching by designing an evaluation tool that promotes an equitable search process. More specifically, we designed a rubric that evaluates the work that has historically been undervalued. To design and construct our rubric, we utilized different aspects of design based research and faculty learning communities to ground and contextualize our rubric within communities of practitioners. From the design process, we present our validation process and key insights from our learning communities that informed our rubric.

## Introduction

Higher education has traditionally leveraged two fa culty positions, research-focused faculty who are evaluated primarily based on their scholarship, and adjunct l ecturer who typically focuses solely on classroom i nstruction. Institutions, particularly those with a research focus, have begun to embrace teac hing-focused faculty positions, such as the teaching professors or professors of te aching (TP/PoT) position at the University of Calif ornia (UC) system (Harlow et al., 2020). These faculty focus o n classro om instruction like the adjunct lecturer but, like research-focused faculty, also have scholarship responsibili ties including discipline-based education research, professional development facilitation, curriculum development, and/or program assessment (Harlow et al., 2020). Also similar to research-focused faculty, TP/PoTs have issues of representation (UC Workforce, 2023). Previous studies have examined hiring process for f aculty, including strategies that search committees use to evaluate candidates equitably; these can inc lude criteria that historically have been underutil ized, such as service or equity work in the Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI) statement submitted in applicatio ns (Bhalla, 2019). However, these studies have not examined the hiring process in the context of TP/PoT faculty, despite the increasing number of these positions. We developed a rubric that focuses on evaluating the DEI stateme nts that TP/PoT candidates submit. We build on the work by Culpepper et al. (2023), which examined how to construct a more equitable faculty hiring process by examining different aspects of the search process. We describ e here the development process of our DEI rubric. We integrate  aspects of design based research (DBR) (McKenny & Reeves, 2013) and faculty learning communities (FLCs) (Cox, 2004) to design our rubric, drawing from i nsights of our participants and creating a space where participants can learn from one another.

## Methods

We use DBR to develop a rubric with practical applications and inform our understanding of how to better foster inclusive faculty search processes for TP/PoTs (McK enny & Reeves, 2013). Since DBR requires collaborat ion, we utilized FLCs from 2021-2023 across four R1 emerging or current Hispanic-Serving Institutions to discuss literature, gather the collective insights, and co-create faculty search rubrics  on equitable hiring practices. As outlined by Cox (2004), FLC sessions focused on distinct phases of the hiring process that could be supported by rubrics (McConnel et al., 2013). Additionally, the FLCs enabled participants to learn about and strategize on how to implement equitable hiring practices in their local departmental and campus contexts.

## Results and discussion

We developed our rubric as outlined in Reddy & Andr ade (2010) by integrating evaluation criteria, exam ples of the criteria, and a scoring strategy. We added an introduction that prefaced the intended purpose of the rubric. The introduction draws on sociocultural views of assess ment and states that the rubric is context dependen t to the department where the rubric is applied (Panadero & Jonsson, 2020). To determine the number of criteria  and performance levels, we adapted the recommendations set out in Humphry & Heldsinger (2014). An important first step when developing a rubric is defining the construct of interest (Wilson, 2005). Key insights derived from FLC participants helped defin e inclusive hiring practices for TP/PoTs that became the foundation for our rubric. For example, a participa nt noted that in evaluation of DEI statements, the knowledge that candidates have on issues faced by members of minoritized communities should be considered. Participant 1: “I think there is an element of… is this person going to vibe with students, inspire students, fit well in the student community, and connect with students? Insights such as these contributed to our construct  and content validity by allowing us to incorporate perspectives of our intended users into the rubrics. We develop the rubric to measure two key themes: what DEI activities candidates describe engaging in  and what role they take in that activity. By showca sing previous experiences and leadership roles in DEI initiatives, candidates demonstrate what they can bring to an institution.

## Conclusion and ffuture directions

Our process focused on incorporating collective ins ights from participants. We identified key themes t hat can assist in evaluation of DEI work in the TP/PoT search process. Our rubric supports an inclusive search by requiring users to attend to criteria including knowledge of and participation in DEI issues, engagement in DEI activities, and taking on a leadership role in DEI activities. Our rubric contributes to the learning sciences by providing a tool that can serve as a point of discussion for in stitutions to initiate conversations as to what DEI  initiatives are truly valued. These conversations can assist schola rs in learning how to further develop ways in which  DEI initiatives can further strengthen campus activities, such as the hiring process. Our next step is to validate the rubric by having search committees implement it, which will allow us to address criterion and internal structure validity. This description of our validation process has impl ications for practitioners and scholars. For pra ctitioners, we provide a rubric that outlines key e valuation criteria for DEI statements. For scholars, we provide a basis for future work regarding TP/PoTs, which is currently understudied.
