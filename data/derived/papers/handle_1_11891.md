# Stories, Printing Press, Internet, and now ChatGPT: Examined via the SMART Framework

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Artificial Intelligence (AI) represents a transformative leap in education technology, with profound implications for policy, classrooms, teachers, and students worldwide. This paper explores AI’s impact using the proposed SMART framework (Source, Medium, Agency, Reach, and Transformation), comparing it to three pivotal milestones in educational technology: storytelling, the printing press, and the internet. Building on discussions of existing educational frameworks, AI literacy, and systemic approaches, we highlight how earlier technologies integrated into human-driven systems, while AI functions as an adaptive agent that is capable of generating knowledge, influencing relationships, and shaping learning experiences in real-time. By addressing the philosophical debates surrounding AI’s human-like intelligence and the practical implications of its widespread adoption, this critical-technocentric perspective recognizes both its transformative potential and its risks, including inequities and ethical concerns. Ultimately, we call for proactive regulation and equity-driven strategies to navigate AI’s promises and challenges in education.

## Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has surged into public and academic domains with remarkable momentum, particularly following the launches of  ChatGPT. Scholarly interest has grown exponentially, with over 1,368 peer-reviewed articles mentioning “Artificial Intelligence” in their titles published in 2024 alone—even outpacing pivotal terms like “Equity.” Unlike earlier technologies, AI’s growth is marked by rapid and compounding advancements, including the ability to generate human-like language and solve complex problems, capabilities unimaginable a few years ago (Vaswani et al., 2017). This rapid expansion raises critical questions about its broader implications, particularly in education, where AI can influence a ccess, learning outcomes, and societal inequities. AI’s penetration has also spurred two dominant discourses: one philosophical, questioning the nature and limits of AI’s intelligence, and the other practical, focusing on its applications and societal consequences. Philosophically, scholars like Chomsky  et al. (2023) argue that AI lacks genuine understanding, dismissing it as a pattern-matching tool devoid of comprehension. Similarly, M arcus and Davis (2019) critique AI as “stochastic parrots,” mimicking language with out reasoning. At the other extreme, figures like Elon Musk and Stephen Hawking warn of AI’s existential risks, suggesting its unchecked development could surpass human oversight, with potentially catastrophic outcomes. Despite this spectrum of views, the reality remains  that AI is already shaping human systems and societies, compelling us to address its consequences. Beyond philosophical debates, scholars increasingly  emphasize AI’s practical impacts. Critics like Bender et al. (2021) warn against “AI hype,” arguin g that apocalyptic narratives often obscure pressin g issues, such as AI’s reinforcement of existing power structures. Others, like Miao et al. (20 21), liken AI literacy to “classic literacy,” which historically created binaries that privilege Western norms. Environmental concerns also loom large, with AI’s resource-intensive development exacerbating climate change (Strubell et al., 2019). These critiques stress that AI’s applications often ampli fy societal inequities, raising ethical questions a bout its role in education and beyond (Miao et al., 2021).

This paper adopts a critical-technocentric lens, recognizing AI as an important but ultimately a humandriven tool whose impact depends on how it is shape d and used. Influenced by thinkers like Freire (197 0), Benjamin (2023), and Philip & Sengupta (2021), this perspective advocates for a critical approac h to understanding AI’s societal implications. Building on scholars such as Logan (2024)  and Feenberg (2010) who call for critical examination of AI ’s impact, this paper explores AI through a proposed S MART Framework (Source, Medium, Agency, Reach, and Transformation). By situating AI within this framework, we aim to explore its transformative potential while addressing its risks and inequities. In this paper, the SMART framework is used to analyze how AI, particularly generative models l ike GPTs, represents a fundamental shift in educati onal technology. The terms 'ChatGPT' and 'AI' are used interchangeably to denote modern generative AI technologies that exemplify this transformation.  Next, we introduce the theoretical framework that u nderpins this analysis, providing a structured lens to examine AI’s transformative role in education.

## Theoretical background

Existing educational frameworks like Diffusion of Innovations (DOI), Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK)  Framework focus on how technologies integrate into society and education (Davis, 1989). DOI examines the spread of innovations, TAM emphasizes user acceptance based on perceived usefulness, and TPACK explores t he interplay of pedagogy, content, and technology. While these frameworks provide valuable guidance for unde rstanding how technology  integrates into educational contexts, they predominantly assume technology’s in herent benefit and fail to critically address its p otential harms. As Logan (2024) argues, "What might be required is a more heterogeneous approach to generative AI, one that makes space for learning with, about, and against it" (p. 362). Scholars (Bender et al., 2021; Miao et al., 2021) have highlighted the need for deeper critiques of AI’s societal impact, moving beyond simple integration-focused frameworks.

If existing frameworks fall short, why not simply e ducate users about AI? This approach, rooted in the concept of AI literacy —which is defined as the ability to  evaluate, communicate, and collaborate with AI — emerged from digital literacy (Long & Magerko, 2020). However, as Logan (2024) ar gues, narratives about AI literacy are often shaped by powerful stakeholders, such as corporations, which influence public perceptions. For example, the Common Sense Media AI literacy curricu lum, funded in partnership with OpenAI, illustrates  how these narratives may serve corporate interests rath er than fostering critical examination (Logan, 2024, p. 364). Scholars have called for critical AI literacy, urgi ng analyses of AI’s role in perpetuating societal i nequities and environmental harms (Bali, 2023; Miao et al., 2021). Yet, critical AI literacy remains limited, often overlooking broader socio-political and economic factors that influence AI’s design and deployment, highlighting the need for a more direct framework that can address the inhere nt potential and limitations of the technology itse lf (Kafai et al., 2024; Morales-Navarro et al., 2023).

Building on these critiques, Logan’s (2024) topolog ical approach provides a compelling alternative by mapping the social, technical, and political-economic facets of AI, revealing power dynamics and environmental costs. His analysis of ChatGPT exposes inequities i n labor practices and resource extraction (p.365). While Logan’s findings bring critical insights into the d ynamics shaping AI, this approach remains broad, wi th an emphasis on systemic factors like politics and econ omics. The proposed solution of a "Luddite praxis,"  which suggests resisting or abandoning AI technologies, m ay not be feasible in an era where technological re liance is deeply ingrained in everyday lives. Moreover, this framework leaves underexplored the question of agency—not only who has the power to shape AI use, but also how agency is enacted in practice by educators, learners, and AI systems themselves. Thus, while Logan’s topological approach contributes valuable perspectives, it falls short in addressing how to practically engage with AI in ways that empower educators and learners, especiall y from vulnerable populations.

SMART framework Existing models like TAM and TPACK focus on integrating technology, while Critical AI literacy and systemic approaches either narrow in on skills or overly broaden in critiques. This paper proposes the SMART Framework (Source, Medium, Agency, Reach, and Transformation) to examine AI’s unique characteristics at a more direct level of technology itself. By understanding AI’s foundations—its information sources, its existence mediums, its agency, its reach, and its transformative power—SMART goes beyond integration-focused and scope-limiting approaches. Situating AI alongside past technologies, such as storytelling and the internet, this framework emphasizes proactive research into AI’s educational impact. Source Storytelling, as the earliest form of knowledge exchange, played a central role in fostering social cohesion. Shared myths and values unified communities, creating a co llective identity and purpose (Harari, 2015). The p rinting press revolutionized this dynamic by enabling wides pread dissemination of information, transcending lo cal boundaries and allowing ideas to influence global audiences (Eisenstein, 1979). The internet further democratized information, introducing an interactive dimension where users could both access and contribute content (Castells, 2010). Yet, these technologies relied on humans as the primary creators and curators of knowledge. AI fundamentally alters this paradigm by synthesizing vast datasets to generate responses independently. For instance, ChatGPT aggregates and presents cohes ive answers without human intervention, drastically accelerating the process of accessing knowledge. Th is represents a major departure, shifting the sour ce of information from humans to a dynamic, algorithm-driven entity.  AI’s ability to centralize and synthesize knowledge brings clear advantages. Students and professionals can save hours of manual research, leveraging AI tools to obtain instant, cohesive answers tailored to their needs. For example, learners can query ChatGPT to gain structured explanations of complex topics in real-time, enhancing productivity and comprehension. However, this centralization carries risks, particularly in foste ring over-reliance on AI-generated information. Users may increasingly trust AI’s outputs without verifying its accuracy, leading to potential misinformation and intellectual complacency (Bender et al., 2021). Moreover, the ri sk of confirmation bias looms large, as AI algorithms may reinforce users’ pre-existing beliefs by tailoring responses to their queries (Pariser, 2011). These issues challenge the critical thinking skills that traditional metho ds of knowledge acquisition foster, raising concern s about the long-term impact of AI as an information source.

Medium Historically, mediums of information have evolved t o enhance accessibility and interaction. Oral story telling requires the physical presence of both storyteller and liste ner, confining its reach to local communities. Writ ten texts, and later the printing press, transcended th ese limitations, allowing knowledge to spread acros s time and space (Eisenstein, 1979). The internet further tran sformed the medium by enabling instantaneous, two-way interaction, granting users the ability to engage w ith content and contribute at great speed (Castells, 2010). AI represents a new medium that transcends these traditional constraints. Unlike books or static web pages, AI exists as an omnipresent digital entity embedded across pl atforms, from smart assistants like Alexa and social media like Instagram and YouTube to educational tools lik e Khanmingo and Grammarly. This fluidity allows AI to interact dynamically with users, adapting to various contexts and delivering personalized experiences. The dynamic nature of AI as a medium holds tremendo us promise. By offering tailored, interactive support, AI can address educational inequities, rea ching underserved communities with limited access t o traditional resources (Woolf et al., 2013). For ins tance, a student in a remote village with internet conn ectivity could use AI to receive real-time tutoring or clarification on complex concepts—something static resources cannot achieve. Evidence from India further supports the se promises of adaptive learning technologies in improving outcomes, particularly for low-performing students (Ganimian, 2023; Muralidharan e t al., 2019). However, this adaptability also raises concerns about equitable access. Unlike books or freely available internet resources, highquality AI tools often require premium subscriptions, advanced devices, or reliable internet connectivity, creating barriers for underprivileged groups (Bali, 2023; Mi ao et al., 2021). For instance, Khanmingo, a Khan Academy chatbot, costs $44 yearly, which raises a question about accessibility for students around the world. Without proactive measures to ensure inclusivity, AI’s role as a medium may exacerbate existing educational inequalities, benefiting those with resources while marginalizing  those without. This duality underscores the need f or careful management of AI’s role in education to ensure it bridges, rather than widens, access and performance gaps. Agency In the past, agency in knowledge creation and disse mination rested firmly with humans —storytellers, authors, educators, and institutions. Even with the advent of transformative technologies like the printing press, such tools were often positioned as passive conduits for human-authored, static content. Digital platforms l ike YouTube extended the reach and speed of dissemination yet still relied on human actors for content production and updates. However, research in the Learning Sciences and Scie nce and Technology Studies (STS), such as Latour’s actornetwork theory, reminds us that agency is not exclu sively human. Rather, it is distributed across soci o-material networks, where technologies, texts, and tools co-participate in the production and circulation of knowledge. From this perspective, the emergence of AI does not represent a cleandisruption but rather a reconfiguration of existing socio-material relations. Large Language Models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT participate in the educational process not merely as tools but as generative systems that adapt to user input and produce content in real time. Yet, while such models are often described as co-participants or collaborators, this characterization remains contested. Critics argue that these systems, trained on large and ofte n biased datasets, may reproduce dominant ideolog ies, flatten epistemic diversity, and obscure the human labor and data that make their outputs possible. Thus, the role of AI in learning demands critical interrogation —not only of what these tools cando, but also of th e values, assumptions, and power relations embedded in their design and use (Burgers et al., 2020). AI’s generative capabilities empower learners in unprecedented ways. Tools like DALL-E and AlphaFold enable students to explore creative and scientific frontiers, fostering innovation and engagement (Jum per et al., 2021). Yet, these same generative capacities also introduce profound risks. From a sociotechnical perspective, the affordances of AI are not inherently benevolent; their impact depends on the values, incentives, and infrastructures that shape their development and use. A stark illus tration lies outside the classroom: Facebook’s AI a lgorithm, designed to optimize user engagement, inadvertently  fueled real-world violence during the Myanmar crisis by amplifying divisive and inflammatory content (Mozur, 2018). Although programmers in California did not explicitly design the algorithm to harm an ethnic g roup, the AI’s decisions to maximize engagement evi dently contributed to the large-scale genocide of Rohingya Muslims. In education, s imilar missteps could occur if AI systems prioritize efficiency over fairness or inclusivity. For instance, AI-driven grading algorithms, such as those used during the UK’s COVID-19 exams, disproportionately disadvantaged students from lower-income areas by relying on biased historical data (Hern, 2020). The se examples underscore the dual nature of AI system s—not just as tools for learning but as powerful actors w ithin broader socio-material networks that can produce both educational value and social harm. As such, integra ting AI into learning environments demands critical  scrutiny of the assumptions and power relations embedded in these technologies, particularly in education. Reach The evolution of communication technologies has con sistently expanded the reach and depth of human connection. Storytelling unified small communities;  the printing press catalyzed global intellectual r evolutions; and the internet enabled instantaneous connections across continents (Harari, 2015; Eisenstein, 1979).  However, these technologies primarily served as platforms for human-to-human connection. For instance, a YouTube video can evoke strong emotional reactions, but the conte nt is ultimately a product  of human creativity and intent, transmitted through a digital platform. AI, however, introduces a fundamentally new dynamic. Tools lik e ChatGPT can simulate empathy and nuanced understand ing, creating interactions that feel inherently hum an but are generated by non-human intelligence (Welivita & Pu, 2024). This represents a shift from technology merely facilitating connections between humans to becoming  an active agent  in shaping emotional and intellectual experiences. AI redefines the boundaries of connect ion, blending alien intelligence with human relatio nships in unprecedented ways.

The reach of AI presents significant opportunities and risks. On the positive side, AI’s ability to provide empathetic, context-aware responses can enhance educational support (Wo olf et al., 2013). However, its ability to show fake empathy raises ethical concerns. Over-reliance on AI for emotional or academic support may erode human connections, fostering depen dence on non-human entities for validation and guidance. This co ncern is amplified by the ongoing loneliness epidemic, which  has left many seeking connection in digital intera ctions. Instances like the Google engineer who was fired fo r claiming AI had gained sentience underscore the g rowing confusion around AI’s simulated potential to blur b oundaries between human and artificial relationship s. This dependency risks altering the fabric of social relationships, particularly for vulnerabl e populations like adolescents.

Transformation Education and information technology tools have con tinually transformed human life, enabling adaptatio n and survival. Storytelling allowed early humans to coll aborate in large groups, fostering societal cohesio n (Harari, 2015). The printing press revolutionized knowledge dissemination, unifying masses through texts like t he Bible and Principia Mathematica, though it also reinforce d dominant ideologies (Eisenstein, 1979). The inter net and computers accelerated these changes, transcending space and time with instantaneous, interactive communication (Berners-Lee & Fischetti, 2000). Today, AI marks an even greater transformation, reshaping how humans interact with and understand the world in ways previously unimaginable. AI’s transformative potential in education lies in redefining what it means to learn and teach. Just as the internet deprioritized rote memorization in favor o f critical thinking, and computational devices minimized the need for automaticity training, AI is poised to shift the focus again. Skills such as prompt engineering are emerging as critical, exemplifying how interactions with AI tools shape new forms of expertise (Jang et al., 2024). Recent research highlights a surge in prompt engineering a s an essential skill, with studies showing its posi tive impact on argumentative writing (Jang et al., 2024). At the latest ISLS conference, Jang and coll eagues (2024) demonstrated that well-crafted prompts significantly enhance students’ ability to articulate arguments. However, these shifts also increase reliance on AI, raising concerns about skill redundancy and the ethical imp lications of overdependence (Ahmad et al., 2023). As ChatGPT and similar tools reshape education, their integration must be carefully guided by equity-focused approaches to ensure they complement, rathe r than replace, critical human capacities.

The SMART framework reveals how AI marks a fundamen tal shift in educational technology, moving from passive delivery to real-time, adaptive engagement. By analyzing AI’s Source, Medium, Agency, Reach, and Transformation, we can better understand both i ts immense promise and its risks. As AI rapidly evo lves, a critical and equitable approach is essential to harness its power without deepening existing inequalities. AI’s future in education will be defined not by its capabilities alone, but by how we choose to shape and govern its use. This moment demands deliberate equity-centered action to ensure AI advances educational justice ra ther than merely optimizing efficiency.
