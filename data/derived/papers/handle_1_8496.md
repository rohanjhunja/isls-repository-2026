# Instructional Landmarks: Describing a Novel Intermediate Knowledge Structure for Physics Learners

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Mapping the intermediate stages of developing knowledge structures is a critical endeavor for educational researchers. The influence of formal education on knowledge structures is particularly relevant to instructional designers. During an exploration of intermediate physics learners’ knowledge of the Coriolis force, a novel knowledge structure was observed as the students discussed isolated systems, frictionless surfaces, and the law of inertia. We call these structures “instructional landmarks” as they appear to emerge from classroom instruction and serve to orient students in conceptual spaces and resolve conflicts during problem-solving. These structures, if found in other physics domains, could be useful for describing and modeling the cognition and development of intermediate physics students.

## Introduction

Participating in formal instruction can help students construct more elaborate mental models for making sense of the physical world. In response to educational inte rventions and experience, novices reorganize their prior knowledge to construct higher-order structures that can meet the increasing functional demands of more complex problem-solving (Vosniadou & Brewer, 1987). During this restructuring, novices can build intermediate structures which act as a sort of conceptual bridge between their previous, naïve understanding and the rigorous, methodical, mathematical operations that are exemplified by experts (Niedderer, 2001). Identifying and describing these intermediate structures can help instructors develop alternative teaching approaches, which can further develop these intermediate conceptions into productive expertise (Brown & Clement, 1992). In the course of developing a conceptual teaching approach for the Coriolis force for undergraduate physics, our team conducted a series of task-based interviews with intermediate physics students in or der to observe how their prior knowledge, both intuitive and from their introductory physics education, played a role in their sense-making. We observed patterns in student  reasoning, which may implicate a new category of intermediate knowledge structure in physics learning. We call this category of knowledge instructional landmarks. The role of instructional landmarks in learning cou ld inform instructional design across multiple doma ins of physics.

## Theoretical framework

This work builds on a theory of knowledge and learn ing called Knowledge in Pieces (KiP; diSessa, 1993). KiP postulates that knowledge is a complex web of atomized knowledge elements with context-dependent connections. The connections which exist within the knowledge structures of novices are highly localized and sparser in general than those employed by experts. Novices also tend t o lack the higher-level, abstract schemas that gove rn largescale cognitive organization in the minds of expert s. Part of the transition from novice to expert inv olves reorganizing and repurposing existing knowledge elements to align with accepted scientific understanding. As the knowledge network becomes more reliable, clusters of related elements fuse into more developed structures which can be used productively in sense-making. By examining learners at various stages of development and across a broad range of topics, researchers can catalogue the various available resources, understand the relationships and interactions between them, and explore their evolut ion and development over time. This would allow for  the creation of adaptive educational materials, which utilize the students’ cognitive resources to their fullest extent.

## Methodology

In order to get a sense for the conceptual resource s undergraduates bring to their learning of the Cor iolis force, our team interviewed eleven undergraduate students from a large university in the U.S. Intermountain West. All participants had finished the university’s Introductory Physics course, which covered basic physics concepts that were necessary to understand the Coriolis force. Si x of the participants had also completed the Classi cal Mechanics course that included traditional instruction about the Coriolis force. During each one-on-one interview, students were presented with a series of thought ex periments, which explored the motion of a puck as i t moved across the surface of the earth under various circumstances. Specifically, we used a microanalysis to identify participants’ resources f or reasoning about Coriolis force phenomena. Knowle dge Analysis is a grounded approach, meaning it allows patterns and interpretations to emerge from the dat a rather than looking at the data to find evidence of existi ng components of a theory (Corbin & Strauss, 2008).  After transcribing the recorded audio from the interviews, the students’ responses were analyzed to identify  the ideas they featured. Similarities in student ideas were t hen compared to previously documented knowledge ele ments and structures to identify the existence of previously documented resources and highlight ones that were novel.

## Results

Among the data, three patterns in student reasoning  stood out. They shared enough similarities with ea ch other that they appeared to be instances of a common stru cture and the three together offered enough detail to form a rudimentary outline of the structure. They appeared to be heuristics that the students relied upon as they interacted with intermediate physics concepts, which had strong roots within the students’ formal education. Their primary function seemed to be orienting the students within  a conceptual landscape during problem solving. We named these structures “instructional landmarks” and begandocumenting their common traits:

1. Central Node: Instructional landmarks have a focal point that is a common physics term, phrase, or rule

(e.g., “Newton’s 2nd Law”). The highlighted term is one that would have little importance to a lay person but is instantly recognizable to anyone with an introductory level of physics education. This demonstrates that instructional landmarks are not intuitive knowledge elements, but rather are constructed in the same environment where novice physics learners are introduced to the technical terms of physics.

2. Loose Organization: Concepts which share an association to the central  node are cued when learners

activate the instructional landmark. The strength of cuing is proportional to the frequency at which t he concept was relevant to problem solving involving t he central node during physics instruction. The connections surrounding the central node are still unrefined; students may mistakenly use the wrong term when describing the central node even when their understanding of the central node is correct.

3. Authoritative: Instructional landmarks form around central nodes which were taught in an axiomatic

manner: within a specific context, the rules imposed by the central node are assumed to be of the highest priority. Due to this assertion, students grant the se structures an enormous amount of trust, rarely questioning them. The reliable nature of the centra l node also allows students to confidently and repetitively cite the central node to demonstrate the validity of their own arguments.

4. Orientation: When exploring the cognitive space around a landmark, students will use the central node

to orient themselves as they attempt to determine t he range of acceptable solutions and weed out irrelevant ideas. Before brainstorming, students wi ll try to verify that the problem space is within t he central node’s conceptual boundaries. Once confirme d, they branch outwards from the central node, working through related concepts while maintaining their view of the central node. The students may reiterate the central node during the cognitive exploration to assure it has not been neglected, and if they get lost or become unsure, they will try to reestablish their view of the central node before continuing.

5. Conflict Resolution: Students will measure potential solutions against the central node to determine if

the suggested answers are acceptable; in cases where multiple instructional landmarks are relevant, the rules of all visible central nodes will be applied.  If the solutions disagree with the postulates of a ny relevant central node, they must be relinquished (e ven if the solution is supported by the students’ intuition). When multiple ideas clash, preference will be given to whichever best aligns with the nearby landmarks. If the students fail to discover a solution which meets these requirements, they will abandon the search rather than suggest an answer which knowingly conflicts with the central node. Some landmarks exhibited certain traits more strongly than others, and in some cases did not exhibit a trait at all (though this was usually because the interview did not provide an opportunity for the trait to be demo nstrated). During our observations, we noted three distinct instructional landmarks built around the central nodes: isolated systems, frictionless environments, and the law of inertia. The isolated systems landmark At the start of the interview, each participant was asked to give a simple description of the laws of conservation of energy and conservation of angular momentum. The se concepts were covered as part of the Introductor y Physics curricula and are fundamental to describing  the Coriolis force. As part of their explanations,  six of the eleven participants stated that these conservation laws were only applicable in isolated systems. The repetitiveness of the statement ind icated that the participants viewed the rule as ext remely important, one that must not be neglected when interacting with any conservation problem. The participants were practicing a routine of delineation which is taught to every introductory physics student; it is imperative to clarify the boundary of your system at the start of most ph ysics problems. Without this step, it becomes impos sible to know which rules or precepts will apply to which objects. Our team was unable to resolve the finer details of  this landmark, primarily because the conceptual approach did not incorporate scenarios in which the isolated system of the earth is intruded upon by outside forces. Without those conflicts, there was no reason for the students to utilize the landmark beyond its initial mentioning when explaining the general rules of the conservati on laws. Further study would be necessary to confid ently identify the internal structure of this landmark, explore other contexts in which the landmark might be cued, and determine the manner in which students rely upon it during conceptual problem-solving. The frictionless environments landmark Our conceptual approach, like many thought experiments in physics, made use of an idealized frictionless surface. The participants were familiar with frictionless su rfaces as they play an integral role in Introductor y Physics. Throughout the intervention, several participants regularly stopped to verify with the instructor that the frictionless environment was still in effect, asking questions l ike “You said it’s frictionless, right?” for clarif ication. As the participants began to vocalize their own prediction s, they commonly cited the frictionless environment  before they offered their answer. This manifested in statements like “Because there’s no friction, there’s not going to be a force that inhibits the motion,” and “With the planet being frictionless, the rotation underneath shouldn’t matter.” This habitual reiteration seemed to a) ensure that the frictionless state was not being neglected or forgotten and b) lend credence to their proposals by showing that they were obeying the rules of the scenario. It was clear that the participants were treating th e impositions of the frictionless state as an essen tial component of the problem-solving process, but the g reatest utilization of the landmark came when the s tudents realized that their current intuition was at odds with the frictionless assumption. The students would immediately abandon their idea and search for an alternative that would adhere to the frictionless ruleset: Interviewer: Why would the puck be moving…?

Student: Because the earth is rotating … and because the puc k is on the earth... Actually, if we're still negating friction, I would say the puck still is in the same stationary spot. With very little pushback, the participants discarded ideas that were incongruent with what they had been taught about frictionless surfaces. These real-time  corrections exemplify one of the primary functions  of instructional landmarks: because the rules associated with each landmark can be reliably assumed to be true within a conceptual space, they can serve as a litmus test  for detecting flawed ideas or uncovering contradic tions. Interestingly, the frictionless landmark encompasse s a ruleset that does not exist in the real world, but the usefulness of an instructional landmark is specific to the problem-solving contexts in which it can be applied. The students appeared to have a great deal of experienc e navigating using the frictionless landmark, and w e predict that the Introductory Physics classroom would be a perfect environment for exploring students’ use of the frictionless landmark in greater detail.

The inertia landmark Throughout the interview, the participants were asked to make predictions about the motion of a puck subjected to a variety of initial conditions. Within these pr edictions, the participants expected the puck to mo ve if it was pushed and to remain stationary otherwise. At this stage in their instruction, the participants were well acquainted with kinetic physics problems: a significant amount  of the introductory curriculum involves enumeratin g and analyzing the forces acting on an object in order to calculate the resultant motion. We refer to the structure which arises from these practices as the “inertia landmark,” although the central node of this landmark had a far weaker association with proper terminology than any other observed landmark. In fact, across all eleven students, not one used the word “inertia” when predicting the motion of the puck (even though they were using the concep t to inform their predictions). Despite the weaker jargo n, the landmark still proved decisively useful: the  students tended to vocalize which forces were (or weren’t) p resent as they started to formulate their predictio ns, saying things like “there’s just gravity pointing down, there’s no friction keeping it in place,” and “there’s no force that is changing where the puck is, other than gravity a nd the normal force.” Even though the word “inertia ” wasn’t being offered as a part of these propositions, it w as clear that the students had formed a habit of en umerating forces as a preliminary step when predicting motion during their introductory physics education. Interestingly, the inertia  mentioned in these contexts activates the same landmark as before: the students recognized that a state of mot ion will remain unchanged according to an inertial observer unless acted upon. The strong contextual associatio n of inertia with the centrifugal force (which is c overed in Classical Mechanics but not Introductory Physics) demonstrates that the connection has been developed further during the advanced instruction; the same conceptua l framework that applied in kinetics is now relevan t in the new territory of inertial forces. To put it another way, the conceptual landscape from which the inertia landmark was visible had been increased as a direct result of additional instruction. This is a prime example of the continuous nature of knowledge as it is refined from a naive s tate to expertise: existing knowledge elements and structures are repurposed and expanded to facilitate complex understanding and enable more advanced problem-solving.

## Discussion

All three landmarks had a strong central conceptual  node, though the association of that node to speci fic terminology or neighboring concepts varied. The connections displayed by all three landmarks bear a significant resemblance to traditional instruction methods and practices, suggesting that the refinement of these structures rely on student-teacher interactions. These landmar ks occupy an elevated status in the students’ minds, where they are given priority over other intuitive or instinctual knowledge. Once built, the landmarks serve the students during conceptual problem solving, providing a yardstick with which the students can measure possible solutions, eliminate erroneous suggestions, or differentiate a mong competing hypotheses. The landmarks display a wide range of applications which vary in specificity from broader physics topics to specific problem-solving scenarios. Given the similarity in traits between the trends d ocumented here, the construct of instructional landmarks could provide education researchers with an interesting perspective from which we candecons truct and analyze intermediate knowledge structures in ph ysics learners. The three landmarks documented here  are merely those which were relevant to discussions about the Coriolis force and are not at all a comprehensive list. We expect to find similar landmarks built on other conceptual nodes throughout physics. The nodes will  most likely be those which have high experimental confir mation (which are taught in an axiomatic way) or wi dereaching conceptual utility (such as idealizations or simplifications). The high degree to which context determines activation will mean that instructional landmarks will most easily be identified by examining students’ cognitive decision-making within specific conceptual neighbor hoods. Instructional landmarks should also develop into more refined models as the students pursue deeper and more complex guidance from physics experts throughout their formal education. Ultimately, these instructi onal landmarks could prove to be a precursor to lar ger, more systematized structures that provide support across domains for experts. Describing and investigating intermediate knowledge structures such as instructional landmarks will be a critical step in advancing toward a computational model for conceptual physics understanding. Expanding the vocabulary for describing intermediate structures w ill not only empower the communication between stud ents and teachers, but among educational researchers as well. The intermediary stages that exist between novices and experts are among the most crucial to understand, as they have the potential to redefine priorities and revolutionize strategies within classrooms, workspaces, and academic communities.
