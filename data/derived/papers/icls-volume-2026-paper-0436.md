# Mapping the Mess: A Visual Approach to Leveraging Strengths to Address Struggles in Universal Learning Design

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study analyzed learner experiences in a graduate course on universal design. Using network analysis on reflections from 15 students, we identified two distinct engagement patterns. One cluster emphasized transformation and identity development while s truggling with technical tools. The other valued structured learning theory and community. Findings suggest that courses preparing learning designers should intentionally support a complex diversity of learning experiences.

Graduate programs preparing learning designers must help students develop professional identities and ways of thinking aligned with expert practice (Reimann & Markauskaite, 2018). Human-centered design (Story et al.,

1998) presents the challenge of consistently analyzing and responding to diverse learner needs. This study

examined learner experiences in a graduate course on universal design to understand how different students engaged with the same learning environment designed around student learning of hu man-centered principles. The design of this course was grounded in human-centered design theory principles of always analyzing and designing for the margins (Story et al., 1998). This principle was supported in the course design through principles from cognitive constructivist learning theory (Piaget, 1952) and constructionist learning theory (Kafai & Resnick, 1996), focusing especially on knowledge construction through the construction of authentic real-world artifacts. To go beyond knowledge, we leveraged transformative learning theory (Mezirow, 2000) to engage learners in identifying, problematizing, and reframing their own assumptions, as well as the assumptions of others in the context for which they were designing learning experiences. In this way, we were seeking to help students develop not only knowledge and skills, but “ways of knowing” and “ways of doing” aligned with those of experts. Therefore, we also designed for situated learning theory (Lave & Wenger, 1991) to engage students in a process of “becoming” (Donaldson & A llen-Handy, 2023) within the community of practice of learning experience designers grounded in the learning sciences.

This study was conducted on data from an online graduate instructional design course. The main source of data was collected by asking students to write an in-depth course analysis of what worked well, what did not work well, and how they felt about the ove rall course. Reflections from the 15 students were collected and then analyzed. To analyze this data, we wanted to identify patterns instead of generalizing, so we used learning experience network analysis (LENA; Donaldson et al., 2024). This allowed us to transform qualitative data into a network map, allowing us to visualize all the connections and patterns in the learner’s experiences. We used the software MAXQDA to separately code each line of data into one of the three categories: alignments with a learning theory, parts that worked well, and parts that did not work so well or that they struggled with, and 55 codes were applied to 284 coded segments. We calculated Pearson correlations and uploaded this data into UCINET to produce a network map to visualize the relationships and connections between each of the codes. We used the Girvan-Newman clustering (Girvan & Newman, 2002) to produce a network map with two significant clusters.

A clustered network map was created as a result of the analysis (see Figure 1 below). There are two main clusters on this map showing a collection of different student experiences throughout the course. This visual map was developed using the learning expe riences correlated at p < 0.01. The two clusters have a robust modularity of Q = 0.417. For the student experiences represented by the purple square cluster, the learning experience was centered on personal and professional growth. Students in this group talked about building confidence, strengthening their skills, and feeling more capable overall. Many also described changes in how they think and approach problems, showing a deeper awareness of themselves as learners and future professionals. A major part of this experience was developing a human-centered design mindset, which encouraged empathy and a better understanding of 2737 different perspectives. Students felt that the course helped them see the real-world impact of their work and gave them new ways of thinking about learning and design. The main challenges they faced were connected to technology and technical tools.

Figure 1 LENA Map of Learning Experience in a Universal Design Course These struggles, however, also opened space for growth. The same mindset that supported their transformation could be used to help students overcome their technical frustrations. Reframing technical skills as part of being empathetic and professional designers could make these challenges feel more relevant. Instructors might create short discussions or small activities that ask students to think about how poor technology design affects real users. Connecting technical skills to the bigger goal of designing accessible and effective learning experiences can help students use their confidence and motivation to push through difficulties. Collaborative, scaffolded tech workshops could also help students learn together, turning technical challenges into shared opportunities for practice and problem-solving.

While the purple square cluster focused on transformation and mindset, the salmon circle cluster reflected a more structured and steady learning experience. Students with experiences in this cluster were focused on understanding how learning happens and how the course design supported it. They were engaged in building on what they already knew like applying new concepts and reflecting on their own thinking. Many found motivation in having a clear purpose and an authentic audience for their work, as well as being part of a supportive community. The practical parts of the course, for example the organization of assignments, pacing, and open This cluster showed how strong course design and learning theories can work hand in hand. The structure and timing of assignments gave students the scaffolding they needed to connect new ideas with prior knowledge, which reflects principles of cognitive constructivism. The emphasis on creating meaningful work supported constructionist learning, giving students a sense of purpose and relevance. The ongoing discussions and collaborative environment also built a strong community of practice, which is at the heart of situated learning. Within that space, students learned by participating, reflecting, and sharing ideas with one another, turning the course into both a personal and collective learning experience. The network visualization revealed two distinct patterns of engagement with the course. Students with experiences in the transformation cluster connected course activities to identity development and professional growth but struggled with technical tools. Students with experiences in the structure cluster aligned with learning theory and valued the course's organization and community aspects. This suggests that courses preparing learning designers should honor a diversity of experiences. Rather than choosing between transformation-focused or structure-focused approaches, courses should intentionally build in both. This study also demonstrates how network miss.
