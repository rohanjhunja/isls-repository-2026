# Surfacing Dilemmas of Responsive Facilitation of Teacher Collaboration in the Context of a Video Club

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Routine opportunities for teacher collaboration are often central to school improvement initiatives. However, limited opportunities for teacher learning during collaboration have led to calls for skilled facilitators. This study extends emerging research on responsive facilitation of teacher learning by investigating the dilemmas facilitators face in the moment of facilitation. Five teacher collaboration facilitators participated in a video club, which was anchored in video clips from their facilitation of elementary school grade-level team meetings. Qualitative analysis of five video club sessions revealed the dilemmas that facilitators face regarding three responsive facilitation practices. Findings suggest facilitators must navigate competing commitments, including building on teachers’ existing expertise towards goals for teacher learning, conferring professional dignity, sharing authority, and cultivating a collegial community. Implications for facilitator professional development are discussed.

## Collaboration in the Context of a Video Club

Christina Kimmerling, University of California, Irvine, ckimmerl@uci.edu Abstract: Routine opportunities for teacher collaboration are often central to school improvement initiatives. Routine opportunities for teacher collaboration are often central to school improvement  efforts, with research suggesting that collaboration has a positive impact on student achievement (Gallimore et al., 2009; Moosa et al., 2024; Vescio et al., 2008). Moreover, sustained opportunities for collective participation aligned with larger professional development (PD) initiatives are increasingly considered core to robust learning (Desimone, 2009). However, participation in teacher collaboration does not guarant ee teacher learning (De Jong et al., 2022; Horn et al., 2017; Vangrieken et al., 2017), prompting calls for a skilled facilitator to support generative discourse (Lefstein et al., 2020).

Emerging scholarship is just beginning to focus on developing facilitators, including identifying the knowledge and practices they need to support teachers (Krainer et al., 2021; Perry & Booth, 2024). To that end, scholars have unpacked facilitation practices to support collaborative learning opportunities for teachers that are generative and responsive to their expertise and needs (Ebby et al., 2024; Gibbons & Neiman, 2024; Onrubia et al., 2022). This work suggests that responsive facilitation during teacher collaboration is demanding and requires in-the-moment meaning-making to respond to teachers’ thinking in ways that build towards goals for teacher and student learning. Further, recent research has documented how facilitators' decision-making impacts PD implementation and, in turn, teachers’ experiences and uptake of learning (Russell et al., 2020; Warner et al., 2022), suggesting that investigating facilitators’ thinking during collaboration is imperative to understanding the learning opportunities created for teachers.

In this paper, I investigate the dilemmas that facilitators encounter as they strive to lead teacher collaborative groups that are responsive to the needs of participating teachers and supportive of their learning. Through a multi-session video club, facilitators shared and discussed video clips from recent experiences facilitating elementary grade-level team meetings. All participating facilitators supported teacher learning in mathematics and were responsible for l eading conversations anchored in students ' mathematical work or mathematics curriculum materials. By analyzing video club conversations around video clips of facilitator practice, I gain insight into facilitators' in-the-moment thinking and decision-making. In this paper, I investigate the following research question: What dilemmas surface for facilitators as they support teacher collaboration that aims to be responsive to teachers? The findings contribute to limited research on facilitator sensemaking (Amador et al., 2025; Lesseig et al., 2017; W arner et al., 2022) and have implications for the design of future facilitator learning opportunities.

## Theoretical framework

Responsive facilitation of teacher learning In this study, I view learning through situative perspectives (Greeno, 2005; Lave & Wenger, 1991). During teacher collaboration, teacher learning is accomplished through interaction with colleagues and the facilitator, who serves as a more knowledgeable ot her. Facilitators support the co-construction of shared understandings and insights about teaching among participants, which requires active and responsive facilitation. At the same time, facilitators are themselves learners who develop their understanding of facilitation through practice and professional learning. 310 Recent scholarship has begun to define and unpack responsive facilitation of teacher learning opportunities, both during pull-out PD and ongoing teacher collaborative groups. Responsive facilitation requires taking an asset-based view of teachers and their contributions (Cavendish et al., 2020; Richards, 2022; Talafian et al., 2025). F acilitators elicit teachers’ thoughts and concerns, consider potential responses, and then act to address teachers’ needs in ways that are tailored to their contexts (Inouye & Gunshenan, 2024). The emphasis on responsiveness as continuous adjustment  (Inouye & Gunshenan, 2024) suggests that responsive facilitation is analogous to responsive teaching. Like teachers who must build from what their students raise during classroom artifacts, as they frequently do not have access to these items in advance (Ebby et al., 2024). However, responsive facilitation among adult learners  in informal collaborative setting s likely presents particular challenges. When facilitating teacher collaborative groups, facilitators must navigate their dual roles as experts and conversation leaders (Harvey & Nilsson, 2022).

Recent scholarship has focused on the facilitation of teacher collaborative groups at the discourse level, recognizing teacher learning as co-constructed through interaction. Ebby and colleagues (2024) suggest four types of facilitation moves  to support productive discourse: structuring the conversation, eliciting teachers’ participation, cultivating a trusting and connected community, and connecting participant talk to build understanding surrounding instructional decisions. Gibbons and Neiman (2024) also explore specific facilitation moves that create learning opportunities during teacher workgroup conversations, specifically by supporting teachers’ sustained attention to students, mathematics, and teaching. Together, this work suggests that facilitators must navigate in-the-moment interactions to engage teachers as a community of learners, while still guiding the group’s conversation toward goals for teacher learning. Video clubs as an opportunity to support facilitators’ learning Video is widely used in teacher learning settings as an anchoring artifact to build images of particular practices and create opportunities to reason about teaching and learning with evidence (Brouwer, 2022). As a PD tool, research has shown that video cli ps candevelop teachers’ noticing competencies (Santagata et al., 2021) and support in-depth, productive conversations about teaching and learning (Borko et al., 2008). Video, when used as a mirror, can serve as a catalyst for teachers’ thinking (Clarke & Esther Chan, 2018). As such, v ideo clubs are a popular form of video-based PD. In these settings, teachers watch and discuss clips from their own classrooms and from colleagues' classrooms. Research suggests that video club participation informs teachers’ thinking and practice (van Es & Sherin, 2010; Perry et al., 2020).

In this study, video clubs were designed as a setting for facilitators’ learning. Prior research suggests that video can support facilitators in gaining insight into leading teacher learning (Lesseig et al., 2017). Drawing on Prediger and colleagues’ (2019) model for content-related PD research, the design of the video club was lifted from research on video-based teacher PD, including clip selection and facilitation practices (Sherin et al., 2009; van Es et al., 2014). Video clips surfaced dilemmas of facilitation practice, which became openings for facilitators to investigate and refine their practice, supporting their learning. Video club conversations provided rich access to facilitator thinking. By investigating these conversations, I unveil the complexity of being responsive to elementary school teachers during collaboration designed to support their mathematics teaching.

## Context

This study takes place in the context of a research-practice partnership (RPP) between a university researcher and a team of educators from a county office of education on the West Coast of the United States. The county office team supports district-wide mathematics instructional improvement efforts at the elementary school level through offering a coherent system of support for teacher learning (Cobb et al., 2018). They design and lead learning opportunities through pull-out PD, principal and district leader training, and grade-level collaboration. To support grade-level collaboration facilitators, Amy (all names are pseudonyms), a member of the county office team, and I co-designed and led a facilitator video club.

Teacher collaboration facilitator video club The video club consisted of six sessions. During the sessions, participants read and discussed short articles and analyzed video clips (see Table 1). The readings for the first two se ssions were selected in advance, and the remaining readings were chosen in response to conversations from prior sessions. The initial sessions emphasized positioning teachers as sensemakers, in which teachers should be engaged “as active, capable contributors to developing insight into the complex practice of teaching” (Kazemi et al., 2024, p. 26). Before each session, a 311 facilitator shared a video recording from a recent teacher collaboration meeting they facilitated. I watched the entire meeting and selected two or three clips that could be used to stimulate conversation. Drawing from Sherin et al. (2009), video clips were chosen because they showed windows into teachers’ thinking, evidenced by their discourse in the clip, and the exchange had some depth, that is, it focused on a substantive topic where there was potential for a variety of facilitator responses in the moment (e.g., a teacher asks a pedagogical question). Each session, I reminded participants of our norms for watching video clips: video is a means to discuss teaching and learning, as well as facilitation practice; we are not evaluating individuals shown in the clip; and we commit to maintaining an asset-based stance on the facilitator and participating teachers. Before viewing a clip, the facilitator whose clip was the focus of that session (i.e., focus facilitator) shared their goals for the particular teacher collaboration meeting, in terms of math content  or instructional practices they intended to develop, and how they wanted teachers to experience collaboration. After viewing the clip, the discussion prompts included: What did you notice about teachers’ thinking? What are opportunities for teacher sensemaking?  What do you notice? What are you wondering? And, for the focus  facilitator: Were you curious at times about teachers’ thinking? (drawn from Kazemi et al., 2024; van Es et al., 2014). ## Participants

Participants in the video club included five facilitators and a director from the county office of education, who did not facilitate teacher collaboration. Based on interviews I conducted at the onset of the video club, facilitators share a consistent vision of high-quality mathematics instruction (Munter, 2014). They were all committed to positioning teachers as sensemakers during professional learning. However, facilitators had a range of experience leading teacher learning, with Bella in her first year and Amy with more than 15 years. At the time of the video club, participating facilitators worked in two districts, supporting two different protocols for teacher collaboration at the elementary school level. In both districts, facilitators led grade-level weekly meetings embedded in teachers’ daily work. In one district, facilitators supported teachers in analyzing student work and planning the next instructional steps. In the other district, facilitators helped teachers make sense of and leverage curriculum materials towards mathematics learning goals. Facilitators employed a range of strategies to support teacher learning during collaboration meetings, including lesson simulations and modeling questioning techniques. In both districts, teacher participation in collaboration was mandatory. Teachers understood that facilitators were there to support their learning and were responsible for planning the activities that occurred during their meetings. However, at times, this conflicted with what teachers wanted to achieve during their weekly meetings.

Data The data consist  of audio recordings of the first five video club meetings (range: 85-113 minutes). The sixth meeting was not recorded because additional participants who were not part of the RPP  were invited. I cleaned transcripts of the audio recordings after they were generated by software. From these transcripts, segments where the focus facilitator framed the clip (5 segments; range: 2-4 minutes) and discussion segments following the video clips were selected for analysis. Across five video club sessio ns, 12 video clips were shown (mean: 4.67; range: 1-8 minutes). Subsequent discussion of each clip lasted between 6 and 32 minutes (mean: 13 minutes).

## Analytic method

To explore dilemmas that facilitators faced in leading  teacher collaboration, I conducted an iterative qualitative 312 segments where the discussion focused on a single topic. I identified 36 episodes across 12 discussions. I then drew on literature (e.g., Inouye & Gunshenan, 2024) to identify episodes that focused on responsive facilitation by coding episodes as a discussion of teachers’ discourse or  facilitators’ response or potential response. These were not mutually exclusive: eight episodes focused solely on te achers' discourse in the clip, eight on facilitator responses without discussing what the teachers said, and 15 on both. Five episodes did not receive a code because, rather thandiscussing teacher thinking in the clip or in-the-moment facilitation practices, the episodes focused on structural or systemic issues (e.g., the need for a district-wide plan to support mathematical fluency). The 31 episodes that focused on responsive facilitation, along with the five transcript segments in which focus facilitators framed their video clips, were then analyzed in further detail. In the next round of analysis, to begin to understand the dilemmas facilitators face when leading teacher collaboration, I read all clip framing segments and responsive facilitation episodes, noting when a participant raised a question, wondering, or issue. I then grouped these questions, wonderings, and issues together when they stemmed from a similar facilitation practice (e.g., pressing teachers’ thinking). In a meeting with all facilitators after the last video club meeting, I shared these practices and related issues as a form of member checking. This initial inductive analysis of responsive facilitation episodes, along with facilitators’ feedback, informed a coding framework to identify transcript segments in which  participants discussed responsive facilitation practices that appeared challenging and worthy of discussion during the video club (see Table 2). A dilemma arises when two or more commitments or perspectives conflict or are at odds. As I read each episode, I noted what facilitators considered noteworthy or problematic in the video clip and what that revealed about their values and obligations, that is, their commitments. Then, I wrote analytic memos to describe the commitments that undergirded  each facilitation practice, noting when commitments were in tension with one  another, thereby revealing dilemmas that surfaced for facilitators. I shared these dilemmas with an external researcher to vet and triangulate findings (Nowell et al., 2017). Through this process, it became apparent that facilitation practices often co-occurred, which increased their complexity. Navigating authority when responding to teachers’ questions Facilitators also faced a dilemma in sharing authority with teachers during collaborative conversations. By role, they were deemed to have expertise to share with teachers. However, as responsive practitioners, facilitators aimed to recognize and build on the existing assets among the group of teachers they worked with (Ebby et al., 2024). 315 This meant they had to navigate responses to teachers' questions without assuming traditional facilitator-teacher hierarchies, in which  facilitators are solely responsible for holding expertise and thus providing answers to teachers’ questions.

To be responsive, they felt that they needed to provide teachers with concrete suggestions to their questions so that teachers would feel heard and valued. However, they also wanted to shift away from traditional fac ilitator-teacher hierarchies toward relationships that recognized the valuable expertise of all participants (Kazemi et al., 2024). This requires both a change for the facilitator, including a willingness to let teachers share their ideas, and a change in the teachers themselves, who must see themselves as having expertise to draw from. Once facilitators have selected a goal, they need to make decisions as they facilitate in the service of that goal. Celia decide d she needed to ask Nikki to repeat the content because she had a goal around building fraction understanding. Amy was  bothered by leaving the conversation as she had with the teachers, because she had a goal of teachers supporting their students in using precise mathematical language. Relatedly, during several video club discussions, facilitators wondered when and if they should pause to make explicit particular instructional moves or mathematical knowledge for teaching. This often occurred when discussing video clips of lesson simulations. After watching one of Celia’s clips, Diane shared: Something I wrote down was about … when you said, ‘oh, and we're spending time here because we know that there's going to be questions.’ So it's like making a pause for teachers to anticipate alongside with you, I think is something important that it's lik e so hard when you're up there and facilitating and trying to chart and listen right? Diane’s comment highlights the complexity of being responsive to teachers and the nested nature of leading learning experiences for teachers (Prediger et al., 2019). In simulating a lesson, the facilitator needs to create an experience that attends to math ematics, students’ thinking, and instructional practices. Then, they need to pause and step out to create meaningful experiences for teachers to analyze those three aspects, considering specific goals or focuses for that analysis.

Teacher collaboration is often regarded as a crucial component of professional development initiatives (Cobb et al., 2018). Skilled facilitators are recognized as essential to supporting teacher learning (Lefstein et al., 2020). This study extends emerging research on facilitation practices (Ebby et al., 2024; Gibbons & Neiman, 2024; Onrubia et al., 2022) by investigating the dilemmas that surface during responsive facilitation. Findings present three facilitation practices that surfaced dilemmas for facili tators. Underlying these practices are facilitators’ commitments to be responsive to teachers, including creating opportunities to engage substantively with the mathematics, maintaining teachers’ focus on aspects related to particular goals for teacher learning, and making sure teachers feel heard, valued, and responded to in ways that build on their existing knowledge and expertise. For facilitators, holding these commitments became complicated by  other meaningful yet competing commitments, such as conferring professional dignity, cultivating a collegial community, and sharing authority. These findings shed light on parallels between responsive teaching and responsive facilitation. Responsive teaching is inherently relational work, embodied by the joint interaction and engagement of teacher and student (Lampert, 2010). The results of this study suggest that responsive facilitation is also relational. More than just a set of practices, being responsive to teachers involves being attuned to and participating in the cocreation of shared learning. At the same time, findings also highlight differences. In line with Harvey and Nilsson (2022), findings suggest that responsive facilitation of teacher collaboration requires navigating professional relationships with adults. Facilitators showed deference to teachers to foster safe learning experiences, yet tensions emerged because they still had specific teacher learning goals to address. similar facilitation practices (e.g., Ebby et al., 2024; Gibbons & Neiman, 2024), this paper’s findings illuminate the complex decision-making required to enact them. PD designers tasked with supporting facilitator learning may consider designing experiences that explicitly attend to dilemmas. Similarly, facilitators may use the dilemmas reported here as a tool for reflecting on their practice. If they tend to prioritize collegiality and rapport, they may consider whether and to what extent that interferes with teacher learning goals. Moreover, these findings raise questions about what it means to support teachers in taking risks to move beyond their current practices. Facilitators may be well served by protocols  and norms to help them build relationships, feel comfortable addressing teachers' questions, or navigate their positionalities. Future research might examine how protocols and norms help facilitators navigate potential tensions between content and social goals for teacher learning. Additionally, findings suggest that the video club was a productive setting for facilitators from diverse contexts to engage in rich discussions of their practice. Future research might consider other artifact-rich PD settings to support facilitator learning and how participation in PD settings relates to facilitation practice.
