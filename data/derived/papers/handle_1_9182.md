# Quantifying Differences in Students’ Participation Patterns in Classroom Discussions

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research has shown that student participation in classroom dialogues is associated with learning gains, and initiatives to encourage more dialogic forms of learning and teaching are abundant. Yet, less is known about how different students may experience, participate in, and what they may gain from dialogic classroom activities. In the current work, we explore potential differences in participation of high and low-achieving students (from 6 different classrooms) in upper elementary Hebrew lessons of teachers who participated in a professional development program on academically productive dialogue. We used Epistemic Network Analysis to identify differences across twelve lessons. Findings reveal that the network model of low-achieving students is characterized by simpler talk moves, reduced connectivity, and repetitive loops. In contrast, high achieving students’ network model is more interconnected, and the strongest connections formed among codes there are indicative of a reasoned argumentation and critical stance. Analyses of selected excerpts further explored the dynamics that may have led to these different patterns.

## Introduction

Academically Productive Dialogue (APD hereafter) has been shown to foster c ritical thinking skills in students, teach them to formulate arguments to support their own claims, as well as pr obe others to better understand or refute opposing views (Alexander, 2018; Reznitskaya 2009 Webb 2009). Teachers and re searchers report that through APD in the classroom, students improve both their learning achievements a nd further strengthen their cognitive skills (Resnick et al., 2015; 2018), their content knowledge understanding (Wilkis on et al., 2016) their reading comprehension (Wilkinson et al., 2015), writing (Applebee et al., 2003) and argument ation skills (Kuhn & Zilmmer, 2015).

A central tenet of APD research and theory is that all students, regardless of background and capability, are expected to participate actively (e.g., Resnick et al., 2015; 2018; Clarke, 2015) a nd to benefit from this participation (Stack, 2013). Yet, this assumption is rarely tested. Thus far, most of the quantitative research efforts on associations between APD and students' learning gains are tested at the a ggregated classroom level (e.g., on average), treating the classroom dialogue as a whole, without distinguishing between different subgroups of students and how they perceive, experience and participate in it. This comm on research practice could possibly mask uneven participation distribution among students with different academic backgrounds and abilities (Langer, 2001; Tengberg & Olin-Scheller, 2016). Accumulating findings from whole classroom dis cussions (e.g., Applebee et al., 2003; Reznitskaya et al., 2009; Sedova et al., 2019) as well as sm all group, peer-led talk (e.g., Asterhan & Schwarz, 2009; Webb et al., 2014) seem to suggest that individual learning gains fro m APD may be conditioned by active (as opposed to passive) student participation and specific ty pes of participation patterns. Yet, studies in countries with separate tracks for low-achieving and high-achieving students show that APD is, in fact, rarely encountered in lower tracks (Davies & Meissel, 2016; Ha rris & Williamson, 2012; Pauli & Ruesser, 2015). Additionally, in an in-depth exploration of student experiences in two academically heterogeneous classrooms, low-achieving students reported feeling they get fewer opportunitie s to participate in high-quality dialogue (Sedova & Navratiolva, 2020, see also Clarke, 2015). Taken together, the picture that emerges from the combination of these different findings is troublesome. It suggests that, notwithstanding the ideals and norms of APD-based pedagogy and in tensive professional development efforts, in practice, low-achieving students may be perceived as  less capable of participating in and/or gaining from dialogic pedagogy and thus receive less opportunities to meani ngfully engage in dialogic learning activities.

There is in fact ample quantitative evidence from an adjacent field  of research that supports this conjecture. Research on teachers' differential behavior, also known as the Pygm alion effect (Rosenthal & Jacobson, 1968) or the Golem effect (Babad, 1993), suggests that teacher perceptions  and expectations about a student's academic abilities affect the opportunities they create for stu dent learning: Students that are perceived to be highly capable are allocated the stage much more often and for longer periods of time, are allotted more wait time, and are asked more challenging questions (Babad, 1992; Brophy, 1983; Harris & Rosenthal, 1985; 1986; de Boer et al., 2018).

When student behavior in classrooms is assessed at all, it is limited to the fr equency and length of students’ turns, not their content or the quality of participation (see also Sedova & Salamounova, 2016 for similar arguments). Most importantly, data collection efforts did not target designated classroom dialogue activities, nor distinguish between APD and more traditional IRE/F types of traditional teacher-student interactions. In the present work, we aim to explore potential differences in high and low-achieving students' participation patterns in designated teacher-led, APD-based classroom dialogue a ctivities in heterogeneous, mainstream classrooms. In addition to focusing on length, number, and types of questions or responses, we use a methodology for investigating the form and content of student utterances in these  classroom activities. We used a coding approach for classroom dialogue quality that captures both the main cha racteristics of academically productive dialogue, agreed upon in the literature, as well as frequently appearing elements of everyday classroom talk, reoccurring naturally (or traditionally) in the lessons (Authors, 2022). We th en use Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA, Shaffer, 2017) to model co-occurrences of previously coded aspe cts of classroom dialogue. We explore whether depicting these co-occurrences of coded elements in addition to their frequencies will allow a more in-depth look into classroom dialogue participation patterns and specific differences between low and highachieving students in the same classroom. To do so, we first use the ext ensive APD literature to identify what productive vs. everyday classroom dialogue may look and sound like. Characteristics of academically productive classroom dialogue The APD literature is rife with examples of how the complex, sought after and rarer expressions of academically productive classroom dialogue patterns look like. For one, when a student offers his  opinion, shares the reasons of his thinking, or otherwise explicates the inner thinking process that brought him/her to a conclusion, it is often linked to the academic quality of the dialogue (Mercer et al., 1999; O'Connor &  Michaels, 1993; Reznitskaya et al., 2009a; 2009b). The use of external evidence and knowledge sources is also considere d one of the defining features of APD (Kuhn & Udell, 2003; Rapanta, 2019; Resnick et al., 2018). As stude nts learn to evaluate the strength of evidence they candistinguish between stronger and weaker support for their  arguments (Osborne, 2010; Osborne & Patterson, 2011; Reznitskaya et al., 2009). Finally, sharing a personal e xperience or emotion that resonates with the story discussed is often considered desirable, particul arly in discussions around narrative texts in language arts education (Hardman, 2019; Hennessy et al., 2016; Murphy et al., 2009). Another feature of APD is transactivity, when learners engage with and interact around the contributions, ways of thinking and ideas that others have added to the dialogue space (We bb et al., 2014). This is viewed as a key element in most APD frameworks, such as Accountable Talk (Michaels et al., 2008; Resnick et al., 2018), Alexander's dialogic teaching (2018), and Exploratory Talk (Mercer et al., 1999; M ercer & Littleton, 2007). Finally, APD is also characterized by interlocutors critically exploring or que stioning the epistemic status of a particular idea, solution, or claim. This can take many forms, ranging from subt le challenges to more direct and articulated oppositions, counterarguments, and rebuttals (Asterhan & Schwarz, 2009; Wilkinson et al., 2015). Characteristics of everyday classroom discourse Since the 1970s, scholars have meticulously documented the common features of everyday classroom discourse. Questions and invitations to respond are a prominent aspect (Coulthard, 1992; Galton et al., 1999; Howe & Abedin, 2013). These invitations are often answered by participants using short turns, represented by the 'R' (response) in the IRE (Mehan, 1979) or IRF model (Coulthard & Sinclair, 1975), or answers to closed teacher invitations (Howe & Abedin, 2013; Peled-Elhanan & Blum-Kulka, 2006). In response, teachers often offer direct feedback or judgement on student responses (Berry & Kim, 2008). Frequent evaluative feedback provided by an authoritative figure can suppress participation by students with different view points and/or with less academic self-efficacy (Scott et al., 2006; Segal & Lefstein, 2016), as well as unintentionally signal students that only those with the 'right answer' can participate (Clarke et al., 2016). In addition to this now infamous cycle of invitation, (simple) response and evaluative feedback, another element that is common in everyday classroom discourse is repetition, instanc es where a participant reiterates something that was already said in the discourse. The repetition may be of an idea previously introduced by the repeater, or by someone else, and it can be nearly verbatim or in a rephrased form (Bleicher et al., 2003; Molinari, 2008). Finally, a frequent feature of existing classroom discourse are instances where the participant (usually the teacher) contributes more information on an idea, a solution, or a propositio n, by extending, explaining, expanding, or clarifying it, with the intention of making sure everyone understands (e.g., Alexander, 2018; Howe et al., 2007; 2019; Reznitskaya, 2012; Webb et al., 2009). Unlike the IRE/F cycle, both elaborations and repetitions CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 149 are not necessarily indicative of unproductive discourse. In fact, they are bot h crucial elements in rare, complex and sought after APD talk and facilitation moves.

Current study aim and research questions The current study's aim is to explore differences in dialogue participation patterns across subgroups of students, based on their perceived academic status. The research questions we focus on are: 1) Do high and low-achieving students in heterogeneous, upper elementary classrooms show different partic ipation patterns in dialogic whole classroom activities?; and 2) If the answer to RQ1 is affirmative, what characterizes these differences?

## Method

The data for this research was collected in the context of a large-scale, RPP-based project aiming to embed APD activities in upper elementary Hebrew language arts lessons in four urban and suburban residential areas in one Israeli school district. Hebrew language arts teachers participated in 60-120 hours of professional development about dialogic pedagogy in the upper elementary language arts discipline. Together with research team members, they designed and implemented APD-infused lesson plans around narrative texts from the curriculum. Participating teachers and students The data in this study was obtained from six participating teachers and a tota l of 145 students for whom active parental consent to participate in the study was obtained (ranging from 25 to 34 students per classroom). For each classroom, we video and audio-recorded two different lessons (approximately 45 min each) in which teachers led classroom discussions around literary narrative texts. Each of these lessons  was observed by a research team member and transcribed verbatim. Active parental consent forms for video a nd audiotaping were collected from at least 80% of the students in each classroom. There were several (N < 6) students in each classroom for whom no parental consent to participate was obtained (a total of 30). They participate d in the classroom activities but were seated outside of the camera's scope and their contributions were excluded from data collection efforts. After the data collection, each teacher was shown a screenshot of the vi deo recordings from her classroom, in which all consenting students were visible, and was asked to identify the students whom she considered as low achievers (LAs, N = 33) and high achievers (HAs, N = 22) in her Hebrew language arts lessons. The remaining students were considered medium achievers (MAs, N = 90). Teachers were not asked to 'fill a quota' of either category, merely to identify each student based on their a cademic level in the Hebrew language arts discipline as they saw it.

Coding classroom dialogue Transcribed classroom lessons were coded at the speaker turn level (5,975 turns in total, students and teachers combined) with the Dialogue Elements to Compound Constructs Approach (DECCA; Author s, 2022). The ten coding categories are: Reasoning (explicit use of logical, reasoned, and conditional thinking), Evidence (use of facts, data, specific quotes from the text or an outside knowledge source), Per sonal POV (speculations, imaginative extensions, reflections, memories or feelings) Refer (connecting, linking, addressing an idea that was already suggested), Challenge (explicit disagreement with an idea suggested, offer ing an opposing view), Invitation (questions, requests to participate, probing), Simple (relevant yet short, unexpe nded or procedural turns), Evaluation (judging the accuracy, relevancy or correctness of previou s turns), Repetition (iteration of an idea or a claim that were already proposed with little or no alteration) and f inally Elaboration (extensions, expansions or additions to an idea proposed with the aim of clarifying it). Interrater reliability indices for the ten DECCA coding categories ranged from Cohen’s κ =.65 to κ =.85 (κ >.7 for 8 of these categories). DECCA allows coders to code eac h turn for none, one or more codes. This way, in addition to capturing these 10 aspects of classroom discourse, it also allows coders to combine more than one code per turn, in relevant cases, thus capturing complex and rarer teache rs’ as well as students' talk or facilitation moves (not further discussed or used in this paper, Authors, 2022).

## Method of analysis

Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA hereafter), specifically the ENA1.7.0 web to ol (Marquart et al., 2018), was used to model the structure of connections and patterns among coded categories w ithin and across turns in the transcribed conversations (Shaffer, 2017). The unit of analysis was the stude nt, and students were grouped by their perceived academic abilities in Hebrew language arts. The network models are graphed from the connections computed among the coded categories within a single turn and across the six tu rns after it (termed a moving stanza, see Siebert-Evenstone et al., 2007, for more information). For each unit, an adjacency vector is created in a multidimensional space, whose spatial location is based on the coding categories  that were most often used by CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 150 the student. Finally, a dimensional reduction is conducted, based on the units' subgroups’ va riance (in our case the LA vs. HA students).

## Results

We first present, compare and interpret the network models of low and high-ac hieving students' participation patterns in dialogic whole classroom activities. We then present one excerpt of a classroom conversation between a LA student and his teacher and peers to further delve into the dynamic s that could have contributed to the participation patterns.

Interpretation of the ENA models The main comparison plot, shown in Figure 1, depicts the three subgroups of stude nts based on their perceived academic abilities: LAs are depicted in pink, MAs in purple, and HAs in green. The mean position of each group in the orthographic space is determined by the coding categories most freque ntly coded among its' members' speech turns. Each mean is surrounded by a box that represents the variance of position along the Y axis (height) and along the X axis (length). As can be seen, these boxes do not overlap, me aning that the differences found among these subgroups are significant and thus answer the first research question presented in this study. The lines connecting them are termed edges and they represent the co-occurrences of two nodes in a turn or across turns in a six-turns moving stanza. The thickness of the edges is derived from the frequency of its co-occurrences across all students. At the bottom of the y-axis, there is a clustering of the nodes Repetition, Invitation and Simple. These correspond to two of the three prominent aspects of more every day, traditional and naturally occurring classroom discourse, as embodied in the IRE/F cycle (Mehan, 1979; Sinclair & Coulthard, 1975). In ENA, the clustering of nodes together in the projection space indicates that they were frequently coded among the turns of the specific units of analysis subgroups under inspection. As can be seen in Figure 1, the units (represented by colored dots, separated by subgroup) clustered around these nodes are mainly pink and purple dots (i.e., LAs and MAs). At the upper end of the y-axis, the nodes Personal POV, Reasoning and Refer are found. These  nodes correspond to three of the five prominent aspects of APD. If certain categories were coded more of ten across a specific group of students, these nodes (i.e., the coding categories) will cluster at the vicinity of the units of analysis (i.e., the students). Along the x-axis, the Challenge node, situated on the far right, represents  a coding category that is considered a typical APD move, capturing disagreements, refutations, and querying, actions that open the dialogue tonew and unexplored aspects. The node Evaluation is situated on the opposing left side. This node represents a didactic teacher move aimed to control and regulate the talk, closing up the dia logue, and channeling it to the CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 151 desired result. The tension between the didactic and dialogic types of classroom discourse is considered a central aspect of APD (e.g., Scott et al., 2006; Wegerif, 2008). An independent samples t-test on the mean position of the three groups and respective confidence intervals on both dimensions showed that the participation patterns of low-achieving students' (M =.16, SD =.23, N = 33) are significantly different from those of high-achieving students (M =.27, SD =.26, N= 22) along the xaxis, t(41.24) = 6.4, p <.001, d = 1.81. Mid achievers' participation patterns (M =.001, SD =.23, N = 90) were also significantly different from the HAs, t(29.18) = 4.47, p <.001, d = 1.16, and from LAs, t(69.88) = -3.71, p <.001, d =.69, along the x-axis. No significant difference was detected among the groups along the y-axis. Thus, the participation patterns of the three subgroups varied mainly on the didactic to dialogic axis. Yet, the model explained only 6.6% of the overall variance detected. Moreover, a calculation of the goodness of fit showed that these were low for the x-axis (Xr =.49, Yr =.92). To address this, we removed from the model nodes that did not contribute to the explained variance (i.e., those th at are positioned closest to the origin point of the axes, meaning that their frequency was similar across groups). The network changes and shifts around the existing nodes with the removal of each category. We conducted four removals  of codes, each time choosing one node that was closest to the origin points of the axes. The order of removal was Elaboration, Refer, Personal POV and finally Simple. The reduced network model's goodness of fit found f or the x-axis was acceptable (Xr =.94, Yr =.8) and the model, still significant, now explains 19.2% of the detected variances across the groups along the x-axis and 21.6% along the y-axis. Comparing low and high achieving students' network models Each subgroup's individual network model, depicted in Figure 2, shows the networks of connecti ons formed separately for LAs and HAs across the six different classrooms and 12 lessons coded and analyzed. As can be seen, the network of connections among LAs on the left-hand side (in pink, Figure 2a)  can be characterized by low connectivity between nodes and its most prominent category (visually expressed by the corresponding node's size) is Repetition, which is not considered specific to APD per se. In addition, the strongest and practically only connection formed in the network is between Reasoning and Repetition (v =.11). Taken together, both the overall shape of the network, its focal point and the one connection formed in it, indicate tha t even when low achieving students do participate, they tend to express ideas in simpler talk moves, the ir turns are either not related to the ongoing discussion or are unreciprocated by their peers and also they possibly share a specific problem of 'getting stuck' in repetitive loops in the discussion.

An in-depth glance into one exchange to illustrate the dynamics of LA participation patterns CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 152 We turn to a qualitative exploration of one such conversation to better convey the spe cific situations that contributed to the formation of the resulting network's shape. The limited space here does not allow us to go into more examples, and admittedly we have cherry-picked an interesting, rich, a nd relevant example. However, as indicated by the aggregated network model result, these types of participation patterns were prevalent among lowachieving students across all participants, regardless of their school or teacher. The most prominent edge found in the low-achieving network model is the co-occurrence of Reasoning and Repetition. In the chosen example, depicted in Figure 3, teacher Ruth led her students in a discussion around the narrative text 'The donkey's advice' and the question: Would you like to have a friend such as the donkey? In the discussion that led up to this selected exchange, a student named Yael B. had said she thought the donkey was a bad friend, lying to the ox and stealing all of his food. A different student na med Yael A. had said she thought the donkey was a good friend, because his initial advice to the ox to fake an illness was kindly meant, to offer him a day of rest. The focus in this exchange is Omri, a low-achieving student, Yael A. and Zohar mid-achievers, and Yael B. a high-achieving student.

Figure 3 An illustrative example of a LA's participation in classroom dialogue In the excerpt, there are many things that contribute to the overall sense of confusion: Omri relates to the opinions of two different classmates who share the same name (Yael), but who voiced opposite opinions about the topic under discussion. It is not clear whose opinion he is referring to or challenging at a given moment, and thus, what his own stance is. In addition, the different participants in this e xcerpt do not use names for the characters they refer to but rely on deixis (he, him, his). Finally, there are many unallocated turns of students who want to participate in the discussion but try to 'steal' the stage allocation from Omri. Aside and on top of all that, the communication between Omri and his teacher is not progressing, as she str uggles to understand what he is trying to contribute, and asks for clarifications or reasoning. He desperately trie s to explain and clarify himself but instead of adding new information or explaining his original meaning, he end s up repeating the same few phrases again and again.

The focus of the coding scheme used, and analysis methods employed were ac ademic and epistemic aspects of the discourse and those alone. In this excerpt, there are ma ny other forces at play that get little or no representation in the network models. One of these forces is the immense so cial pressure Omri is under. It is embodied in the way that other students keep trying to 'go over his head' and explain his intentions to the teacher, or insert their own claims, without giving him a true space to speak his mind. These constant interruptions might be the cause of the network's lack of connectivity because we did not code for stage bids. Another dimension that was not included in our assessment, and therefore not captured in the network model, is the social-emotional one. At one point during this exchange, Omri is even reduced to crying out of frustration. CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 153 This excerpt was chosen despite its lengths and the shortcomings described above, because it embodies within it some of the tensions of APD practices in classrooms: The struggle of LA students to be heard and heeded, the undercurrents of social status (and even bullying) and their effects on the dialogic atmosphere, and the struggles teachers face when conducting a whole classroom discussion in a regular, heterogeneous classroom. The teacher allocates the stage to the LA student several times. In addition, we counted three instances in which she intervenes on Omri's behalf to allow him to finish his contribution without interruptions. Though this is the most common recommendation for teachers in APD, looking at the excerpt raises que stions on whether in this case, this was productive, for the LA student Omri, as well as for the classroom as a whole. We would also like to highlight the specific contribution of a network model approach as opposed to the standard frequency analysis methods: Omri received the stage eighteen time s in this excerpt (see his name highlighted in figure 3). In fifteen of the turns, the code 'repetition' was us ed (see blue frames in figure 3). We have also coded three reasoning attempts (note that only when a new infer ence of reasoning is introduced it is coded thus, once, see yellow frames in figure 3), one challenge attempt (How can he not be loyal to his own advice?), one text-based evidence (The donkey said to the ox: don't eat your food. He didn't say that he wouldn't eat it) and also refers to what others have said before him. However, when looking at what makes up the network's form in terms of frequencies and connections, these few codes of APD, are  buried under the most prominent aspect of repetition. Interestingly, when we compare this to frequency counting ana lysis, Omri had received the stage (during this observed lesson) more than any other student in the classroom. Some of his turns are rather long, not necessarily in terms of words but in terms of utterance time because of the way he speaks (i.e., repeating himself, losings track of what he was saying, answering people who are trying to take his turn). Therefore, in this case, analyzing the number of turns or their length, as sole indicators of the qua lity of exchange, would not only cause us to miss important information, it would have made us reach an opposite conclusion, namely that this exchange was productive and informative for learning.

## Discussion

One question asked in this study was can significant differences in particip ation patterns of high and lowachieving students be discerned? A second question was, assuming we find differences, can a richer analysis (i.e., not only in the number of stage allocations and length of turns) help pinpoint specific hurdles that will allow practitioners and scholars to devise more adapted solutions? In addition to the  numbe r and length of students’ turns, we have coded for content and form or participation, to show that they us e simpler, more everyday classroom talk moves in comparison to high-achieving students. The added ben efit of computing the cooccurrence of codes across and within turns was that it helped illuminate how the low-achieving students’ network model is less interconnected, possibly indicating the students’ struggles to engage in the expected manner, or that their peers do not engage with or reciprocate their turns. Another specific c ommunication hurdle spotted among low-achieving students was repetitive loops. These hurdles were found across six different teachers, as prevailing among this subgroup of students.

Contribution of the network analysis The main advantage we see of using new technologies such as ENA (Shaf fer, 2017) to interpret and analyze the data. The network models allowed us to compare dozens of students, on multiple and c omplex variables, across different teachers, classrooms, and schools. Though the COVID-19 pandemic obstructe d us from collecting a much larger database, we hope that this effort still illustrates the power of network analysis to allow more indepth and richer representations of what had transpired in the classroom, wh ile still allowing quantifiable and graphic ways to depict differences.

## Limitations and future research

One limitation of the current work is that we were unable to corroborate th e teachers' perceptions of the participating students' academic status with outside sources, such as standardized test scores in language tests. We also could not further test to see if and how the observed differences in pa rticipation patterns translated into different learning gains. The reason was that the data collection efforts w ere halted due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Future studies should incorporate a way to validate the teachers' perspective on the students' academic status, as well as check for correlations with learning gains. Another limit ation of the current study is that ENA models only show a specific presentation that is both dependent on the aspects previously coded and on the given context. In our case, the qualitative analyses of the selected transcript e xcerpt showed that it may be worthwhile to develop and incorporate ways to code for the socio-emotional aspects of par ticipation in classroom dialogue. Finally, the network models only inform us of the differences found among the groups, not where they stem from. While it is probable that teachers' differential behavior influence students'  participation, other factors heavily CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 154 affect it as well. A competitive or competence-based classroom atmospher e candeter LAs from participation (Clarke, 2016; Sedova & Navratilova, 2020). It is also possible that LAs students in  a heterogeneous classroom may experience APD as more challenging and difficult to follow (Echevarria & McDonough, 1993), consequently requiring perhaps a different design, more attunement from their teachers or added mediations (Munoz-Hurtado, 2020).
