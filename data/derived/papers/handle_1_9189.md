# Towards Recognition of Students’ Epistemic Emotions in a Student Knowledge Building Design Studio

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
When students build knowledge and apply critical thinking to real ideas and problems around them, their expressed emotions are important to recognize as drivers of knowledge acquisition of themselves and the world. These epistemic emotions are also critical for knowledge generation and cognitive performance. In this pilot study, we attempt to examine and recognize students’ epistemic emotions in an informal learning environment, student Knowledge Building Design Studio (sKBDS), that was designed for cultivating collaborative knowledge creation and enhancement of student agency. A sensing module was developed to collect students’ facial and Heart Rate Variability (HRV) data, before using machine learning algorithms and models that are trained on students’ data to recognize the different types of epistemic emotions that students exhibit during an empirical knowledge building study. Initial findings are promising and shows the possibility of recognizing students’ epistemic emotions in real-time.

## Introduction

Within the field of education, studies of achievement emotions have long been a central point of focus, due to the related performance effects, such as failure and success being linked with anxiety and pride (Pekrun, 2018). This is evident when high stakes are often involved in summative assessment and evaluation. However, there are also other mental states within the process of students’ exploration and inquiry that manifest cognitive characteristics, which are key to complex learning and are often referred to as epistemic emotions (Pekrun & Stephens, 2012). These are related to the idea-generation and knowledge-creation qualities that have  garnered increased attention (e.g., D’Mello et al., 2014), along with emergent studies on how seemingly negative emotions such as frustration and confusion may positively affect cognitive processing underlying learning (Pekrun & Stephens, 2012). In order to obtain, analyze, and better understand students’ epistemic emotions, an informal le arning environment, known as the student Knowledge Building Design Studio (sKBDS), was de signed, implemented, and guided by knowledge building principles (Scardamalia, 2002), as part of coherent e fforts over four years to initiate students into a knowledge creating culture (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). The sKBDS was designed to be used with the Knowledge Forum (Scardamalia, 2004), serving as a platform for students to knowledge build and apply critical thinking to real ideas and authentic problems around them, and to recognize their expressed emotions as drivers of knowledge acquisition of themselves and the world. With th e increased prevalence of emerging technologies such as machine learning, this study, in a way, reflects our effort to initiate students into a knowledge building culture and to better understand student expressed emotions during lear ning. We ask and attempt to address the following research question: How c an students’ epistemic emotions be recognized and understood in an informal learning environment such as the student Knowledge Building Design Studio? Conducive environment for studying epistemic emotions The need for a student Knowledge Building design studio Different from achievement emotions that are often tied to achievement activities (e.g., studying for success), epistemic emotions relate to the perceived quality of knowledge and the processin g of information (Pekrun & Stephens, 2012). Both processes are inherently important within a knowledge building envi ronment that can support idea development in both education and workplace situations (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2003). The existing classroom setting, with its underlying focus on academic performances, m ight hinder the manifestation and effect of emotions before actual academic-related emotions. Hence, there is a need to create a more conducive environment to study epistemic emotions.

CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 190 In addition, as part of the commitment to create purposeful collaborative re search design and to elevate students and teachers’ awareness of knowledge building as a need to understand and handle critical discourse (Lee et al., 2022), the design of the sKBDS environment can be initiated without the c onstraint and limitations of curriculum, type of assessments, duration of a regular classroom lesson, and still be able to maintain the authenticity of the learning environment through the engagement of students from different schools and agegroups (Teo et al., 2022a). This setting also allows the study of authentic engagem ent and potential of students using different forms of engagement and types of analysis for sense-making (e.g., use of multimodal approach; Teo et al., 2022b), which some students feedback as being instrumental in providing the utmost relevance and usefulness to students’ own understanding. For example, one student reflected tha t “this [creation of conducive environment] makes so much sense to me, we are all figuring out the same big problem in class, this [knowledge building] is always a part of our work… [while in classrooms] we have to stop when the bell rings.” Hence, the creation and design of the student Knowledge Building Design Studio (T eo et al., 2022b) aims to engage students in open inquiry with real-world communities that build knowledge on authentic problems (e.g., sustainability) and a motto to act (i.e., save the planet, save lives), and to also cultivate collaborative knowledge creation and enhancement of student agency. Students are provided with res ources and support to identify problems and ideas that are meaningful to them (e.g., renewable materials to combat fast-fashion). They are then encouraged to use scaffolds to improve and share their ideas wit h existing real-world communities that are tackling similar problems. Existing examples of how students manifested ideas and actions include the curation of one-minute videos that are meaningful to causes, or proposal submissio ns with concrete evidence to governmental agencies in order to obtain start-up grants for beginning student-owned and ground-up initiatives. Recognizing epistemic emotions and their importance We posit that students in the sKBDS who are engaged in exploration and inquiry, are more likely to display cognitive characteristics that are key to complex learning. In such a settin g, their expressed emotions become important drivers of this knowledge exchange between students and the world. Within the  sKBDS, the understanding of students’ epistemic emotions was conducted using a variety of methods including self-reporting surveys, video analysis, and physiological measures, but in this study, we foc us on the video analysis of facial recordings for the identification of epistemic emotions, due to the methods that we deploy which predominantly recognizes and analyzes expressed facial features.

As there is no definitive list of epistemic emotions, hence, when students  are presented with tasks that engage their individual beliefs about knowledge and knowing, frequently occurring epistemic emotions often include surprise, curiosity, enjoyment, confusion, anxiety, frustration, and boredom (Muis et al., 2015). In this study, we also considered states that students align closely with the epistemic emotions that they often feel within a sKBDS, such as activeness and interest. For example, being active ref ers to the condition of things happening or being done, whereas interest as a psychological concept closely relates to explanations of motivation to learn, attend to a task or an activity (O'Keefe & Harackiewicz, 2017). This in clusion resulted in a final list of widely recognized emotions and states that we attempt to recognize in this study, c omprising of surprise, curiosity, enjoyment, confusion, anxiety, frustration, boredom, active, and interest.

## Method and pilot

This study used supervised learning methods as shown in Ong et al.’s study (2020) with a different set of parameters and data that were obtained from the virtual learning environment  of sKBDS. A browser-based web application used a web camera to record facial data before uploading to a c loud server, while a Heart Rate Variability (HRV) sensor (Scosche heart rate monitor armband) collected students’ HRV readings as logs that were downloaded from the armband after the sKBDS ended. The resulting data set therefore consist of videos of students’ faces and synchronized HRV data. Feature engineering was then conducted to extract features from the images, audio, and HRV data, before facial data analysis was conducted with a “Human Sensing Software” (Ong et al., 2020) that programed a model (with an 80-20 training to testing ratio) which detects movement of eyebrows, eyelids, mouth, and cheeks to match a list of states and emotions. This model was  initially trained using expertlabelled data, coded by two researchers with a good to excellent inter-rater re liability score, and is poised for further fine-tuning with more data. The model can store predictions with numerical weights assigned for various scenarios and choices of modalities, allowing for the model to be deployed, tested, and validated with future data. A brief report on the model’s performance allows for benchmarking and comparison of the model’s accuracy against others. For this study and comparison purposes, we trained and tested two mode ls using different combinations of data: 1) One model uses only facial data, and the 2) other model uses both facial and HRV data. We are able to report some key findings using preliminary data. By compar ing human coding with the results from the model trained and tested using only facial data, the accuracy was about 64.3% when tested on three most frequent classes of emotions and states (i.e., active, boredom, interest) with cross-validation. These are three of the most frequent emotions and states that were detected over three days of knowledge building activities. The accuracy rate was deemed acceptable at this stage of development and exploration, although not  as accurate as human observers (i.e., 75%) as reported by Dupré et al (2020), but still on pa r and slightly better than several other commercially available classifiers reported in Dupré et al’s paper, albeit with lesser classes (three classes) being identified in this study. Next, another model that was trained and tested using both facial and HRV data achieved a lower accuracy rate of 45%, due to higher-than-expected noise in the HRV data, resulting in poorer performances as compared to the first model that uses only facial data.

## Discussions and conclusions

Overall, we are concerned with the recognition of epistemic emotions that a re important to students and the relation with the ongoing activities held during the sKBDS. The former takes pr ecedence in this paper, as we investigate how epistemic emotions were identified using machine learning algorithms and models that are trained on students’ data to recognize the different types of epistemic emotions that students exhibit during a knowledge building study. Initial findings are promising and shows the possibility of resear chers and teachers being able to conduct recognition of students’ epistemic emotions in real-time. Through this study, the presence of three dominant classes of emotions, that is, active, boredom, and interest, are intr iguing indicators of what students commonly express in an authentic learning environment such as the sKBDS. Apar t from the social meaning of boredom from literature (Barbalet, 1999), understanding when and why students are ac tive and interested at specific temporal junctures of a sKBDS may also reveal insights into  the innate thinking and consequent actions of students as they build knowledge in future iterations of the sKBDS. CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 192 In summary, although most researchers and practitioners agree that epistemic emotions are increasingly important in the holistic assessment and evaluation of students’ growth in a knowle dge society, existing works are still far and few, especially studies that attempt to place quantitative values to abstract and conceptual entities such as emotions. In this paper, we have described how an informal learning environment such as the sKBDS was designed and implemented based on increasing needs to recognize students’ epistemic emotions. We also reported how affordances from emerging technologies such as machine learning has aided our pilot attempts at discerning epistemic emotions within an informal learning environment, potentially allowing fur ther investigations on a larger-scale. In essence, we feel that this study presents a promising start towards how we can significantly contribute to the existing literature, and further build on existent work that researchers can reliably tap on to recognize epistemic emotions in different scenarios and contexts of learning in collaborative environments.
