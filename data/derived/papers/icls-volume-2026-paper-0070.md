# Exploring Spatiotemporal Data Through a Space-Time Cube Interface: Affordances and Struggles

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Spatiotemporal (ST) data, data that vary across space and time, are at the heart of pressing, socially relevant issues, such as climate change, disease spread, and population trends. Adolescents regularly encounter such data in both school and everyday lif e, and they must develop the data acumen and habits of mind to successfully interpret them. However, ST data are inherently complex and challenging, even for experts, and research on how to best design ST visualizations to foster interpretation is limited. In this study, w e explore how the affordances of two data exploration tools, the CODAP interface and a space-time cube (STC), both benefit and hinder students’ exploration of ST data. We found that students identified the value of being able to view ST data in the STC, pointing out how the STC contained more information, allowed for easier exploration of ST data, and allowed them to see time trends more clearly.

Spatiotemporal (ST) data, or data that vary across space and time, are integral to understanding phenomena across a wide range of disciplines (Yang et al., 2020). For example, ST data can track movement or migration of endangered species, contributing to our understanding of their behavior and supporting their preservation and recovery. Weather events such as tornadoes and hurricanes can also be represented across both space and time to understand changes in weather patterns and help prepare for future weather events and disasters. ST data can also visualize the location and timing of socially relevant issues such as socioeconomic inequities or political events. It is crucial that adolescents develop the expertise required to successfully investigate and int erpret the ST data representations that they encounter in school and in the world around them (NASEM, 2018). In a world increasingly steeped in and run by data of all kinds, it is vital that students develop the data literacy skills to navigate this datafied world (Dorsey et al., 2025; Lee & Wilkerson, 2018). While more attention is being paid to bringing data practices into classrooms and the types of data that students are exposed to (Delany & Lee, 2024; Rosenberg & Jones, 2024), ST data is under-represented in students’ engagement with data practices (Israel-Fishelson et al., 2024). ST data is prevalent across  disciplines in school and in daily life, yet tools for analyzing and interpreting it are few, and those that do exist are designed for industry and not for adolescents (Humble, 2023, Yang et al., 2020).  Even at a professional level, the analysis of large  ST datasets has been impractical until only recently as it requires the ability to search through multivariate data where patterns may be disparately spread across multiple variables in ways that are difficult to represent (Moosavi, 2017) and frequently involve complex patterns that interlink coupled temporal and spatial variables (de Oliveira & de Souza Baptista, 2012; Li et al., 2018; Shekhar et al., 2015; Zhong et al., 2012). External representations are key to supporting learners in complex spatial cognition operations and have been shown to aid learners in acquiring, managing, and analyzing spatial data (Kolvoord et al., 2019; Jant et al., 2020). However, the addition of a temporal component complicates the nature of any external representation. Recent research has made clear that novel interface designs hold meaningful promise for enabling in-depth exploration and analysis of time-based geospatial data (Fan et al., 2024; Lie et al., 2023). However, these technological interfaces have remained in professional and expert spaces and have not yet been explored with adolescent learners. To support students effectively, technology systems should support the inquiry process, provide a rich environment for problem solving, and be accessible, flexible, and intuitive for teachers and students while remaining developmentally appropriate (Zacharia, et al., 2015). A key aspect of this study’s use-inspired research approach is the exploration of how using a space-time cube for ST data exploration both helps and hinders students’ search for meaning in ST data. This study sheds light on specific affordances of the space-time cube as identified by students. We use these findings to identify opportunities for future research into the design of technology-based supports for ST data visualizations.

The fundamental concepts guiding this research are the complex nature of ST data and the ways in which technology can provide affordances to support learning. Spatiotemporal data While prior research with adolescent learners has explored how they understand and interpret spatial and temporal data, these two aspects have been studied individually. It has been shown that when working with spatial data, students’ existing spatial skills (Sinton et al., 2013) and their prior knowledge (Cook, 2006) of the context have the highest impact on their thinking. Additionally, students’ interpretation of the spatial data representations is influenced by the format of the representations, such as  how many representations are shown, what the connections between them are, or whether the representations are stationary or animated (Shipley et al., 2013). The format and type of representation can also influence how students interpret temporal informati on (Lee et al., 2011), however, the greater impact with temporal data is the scale. Students better understand temporal information that is within the scale of human experience than information that is on much larger or smaller scales (Cheek et al., 2017). Additionally, people tend to better comprehend temporal data that is related to specific events (e.g. lightning strikes, elections) thandata that measures over continuous passage of time (e.g. weather patterns) (Shipley & Zacks, 2008).

ST data is uniquely challenging because it combines two already challenging components of spatial and temporal patterns and requires students to integrate information from multiple data representations and identify patterns across both space and time simul taneously (Kozma, 2003). The combination of spatial and temporal aspects within time-based spatial datasets magnifies the complexities of managing, understanding, and analyzing them in manifold ways. In large part because of these challenges, even at a professional level, the analysis of large datasets of these types has been impractical until only recently (Yang et al., 2020). Analyzing time-based geospatial data requires the ability to filter and search through large, multivariable datasets represent a wi de variety of data types, which must often be reconciled for purposes of visualization and analysis (Atluri et al., 2018; Shekhar et al., 2015) and are inherently rooted in specific scientific, social, or technical domains such that understanding demand a command of both data analysis skill and domain knowledge (Shekhar et al., 2015). However, research has made clear that spatial thinking is a generalized skill that can be taught to and learned by students (Sinton et al., 2013; NRC & GSC, 2005) and can be effectively supported by technological tools (Muñiz Solari et al., 2015; National Research Council & Geographical Sciences Committee, 2005). As such, there is a pressing need for age-appropriate combinations of technologies and approaches that can help learners become fluent with the skills and habits of mind necessary to deal with ST data. Subtle specifics in how representations depict elements, map spatial relations, and convey abstract connections play outsized roles in their ability to support quality thinking effectively (Tversky, 2001). In particular, understanding how spatiotempor al representations can support learning and analysis effectively is a key domain (NRC & GSC, 2005). Affordances of technology Spatio-temporal thinking, which can be facilitated by several technologies and modern advances in spatial thinking, are intimately connected to these technologies (Bearman et al., 2016; Jant et al., 2020; Kim & Bednarz, 2013; Metoyer et al., 2015; Uttal & Sheehan, 2014). Successful support systems aid learners in acquiring, managing, and analyzing data, and supporting and guiding them by tracking and representing the results of exploration and analysis (Ainsworth, 2008). To support ST thinking in particular, systems must be able to portray spatial data (or connect data to geographic coordinates), create spatial representations, and enable transformations, operations, and analysis of these data, including analysis of patterns, structures and modeling of ST pr ocesses (NRC & GSC, 2005).

Student inquiry using ST data visualizations requires simulations that allow them to “manipulate, test, explore, predict, question, observe, [and] make sense of” scientific phenomena (National Academies of Science, 2019, p. 34). However, the spectrum of data visualizations is large and diverse, and not all materials are effective at fostering science learning through inquiry (Zacharia et al., 2015). The key to a data visualization’s effectiveness in supporting inquiry is its design. Every data visualization offers unique affordances that offer possible and 594 productive action with that tool (Podolefsky et al., 2010). Affordances can be broken down into three main categories. The first is interactive affordances, which are the actions users cando and how they interact with the tool. An example of an interactiv e affordance includes the manipulation and customization of the data visualization. The second is representational affordances, which are what a user can see and how that information is represented. Examples of representational affordances include multiple and multimodal representations and dynamic linking across representations. Lastly, structural affordances are how the interactions and representations are made available to the user. In this study, structural affordances refer to the two-dimensional and t hreedimensional design of the data visualizations. This study represents one phase of a larger exploratory study on how high school and undergraduate students explore, interact with, and interpret ST data across different contexts and within different visual representations and interactive interfaces. The initial phase of the project explored students’ strategies for understanding visualizations about different types of ST data (Pagano et al., 2024). Based on this initial round of research, combined with interviews and discussions with experts in the field of data visualization, a tool was designed and developed explicitly to support the exploration of event-based ST data in which events exist in one specific place at one specific time, such as human encounters with a wolf, or protests across a country or st ate. This tool was then combined with and compared against an existing data analysis tool with capabilities to explore data spatially and temporally, though not in the same representation.

The interactive tools The existing tool is the Common Online Data Analysis Platform (CODAP). Designed with learning in mind, it is widely used in K-12 classrooms across the country and globally (https://codap.concord.org/). CODAP candisplay any data containing latitude and longitude spatially on a built-in map (See Figure 1) as well as any data on an x-y graph. By placing a time variable on the x-axis of the graph, a time series graph can be generated (See Figure 1). This allows a user to compare two representations, one spatia l and one temporal, on the same screen, within the same tool. CODAP representations are linked, such that selecting a data point or points in one representation highlights those same data points in all other representations. Figure 1 Tornados Dataset in CODAP While CODAP’s linked representations enable comparison between a spatial exploration and a temporal one, they do not allow users to view both variables on the same representation. The Space-Time Cube (STC), which consists of a three-dimensional (3D) repres entation, was designed specifically to allow the possibility of visualizing spatial and temporal patterns on the same representation (See Figure 2). 595 Figure 2 Four Seals Dataset in the Space-time Cube The STC is built on top of the CODAP interface as a plug-in, allowing it to maintain most of the functionality of CODAP, including the linked representations. The STC also presents the user with additional control over the time component of the data with a “time slider” (See Figure 2), which allows the user to explore and play through the time components in multiple interactive modalities.

## Context, data sources and participants

In order to understand how the tools were supporting adolescents in exploring ST data, individual think-aloud interviews were conducted virtually with undergraduate students from a U.S. midwestern university. Interviews were conducted with 15 students, each lasting approximately 40 minutes. In the sample of students, 2 identified as female, 12 identified as male, and one chose not to respond. Four students identified as white, four as Asian, two as Black, two as multiracial, and three chose not to respond. All students were recruited from an intro to psychology class being offered that semester.

The interviews presented students with different scenarios in which to explore ST data. In the first scenario, students were provided a dataset in CODAP of four seals in the Pacific Ocean. The data records the time and position of the four seals over eight  months in addition to many other attributes, such as water temperature, chlorophyll levels of the water, and speed of the seal. Students are prompted to interpret a 2D map of the seals’ paths and select data attributes to plot on a graph with questions such as, “What do you notice about the dataset?”, “Tell me a story about the speed patterns of the seals in this dataset.”, and “What are you still wondering?”  Next, students were presented with the same dataset on seals in the STC (See Figure 2). Students are prompted by the interviewer to add attributes to the STC and are asked how the visualization compares with questions, including “Anything different or new from the previous visualization?” and “Can you add something to your story? Or tell a different story?” In the second scenario, students were provided a dataset containing information on tornadoes in the US from January 2019 to December 2022. Again, the students were first shown the data in CODAP with a pre-loaded map and graph representation (See Figure 1). They were again asked what they noticed and wondered and to tell a story about the data over time. They were again provided with the same data in the STC, but this time, the STC plugin was loaded directly into the CODAP file they were already using, allowing them to see and explore the 2D and 3D representations on the same screen and to utilize the linked representation functionality of CODAP if they chose to. With this dual display, students were asked which representation they preferred and why.

## Data analysis

596 We focused our analysis on identifying students' struggles and successes in using the two data visualization tools as they relate to the tools’ affordances. After the interviews with students, the transcripts were transcribed and coded by the first two authors. The coding process combined inductive and deductive coding schemes and focused on individual thoughts as the grain-size for applying codes. We began thematic coding by evaluating the conversations and interactions with CODAP and the STC through the lens of the three categories of affordances: representational, interactive, and structural. Representational aspects of the affordances offered by the two data visualizations include the size and color of data points, the location of axes, and the spacing of data in the visualizations, among other aspects. Interactive aspects include the ability to select different attributes for comparison on a graph or map, the option to click on specific data points to access more information, and the ability to modify th e visual aspects of the graphs or data points. Structural affordances refer to the layout of the data and the 2D versus 3D nature of the graphs. We initially sorted all students’ comments about the use of the tools into these three categories. Once sorted, we grouped related comments and themes together within each category. We report these inductive themes below in the framework of the interactive, representational, and structural affordances below.

We organize the findings first by tool type (ie. CODAP or STC), next by affordance type (i.e. interaction, representation, and structure), then by how the affordance either served as a benefit or hindered students’ exploration of the data, and finally by the emergent themes. We use quotes from students to elucidate their opinions and identifications of the affordances of each tool and how it helped or hindered their interpretation of the provided data sets.

CODAP Interactive affordances Throughout the protocol, four beneficial interactive affordance themes emerged from student conversation while investigating data using the CODAP tool. These include 1) The ability to zoom in and out on the 2D map of the data, 2) The ability to select and drag specific data attributes to the graph axes, 3) The ability to change the visual properties of the data, including the color of the data points and the color gradient of the data trends, and 4) The ability to click on a specific data point and have it highlight in multiple locations, including on the map, in the graph, and in the data table of data. This ability allows the student to discover additional information about the selected data point.

In terms of struggles with interactive affordances, we found that some students had trouble with selecting and dragging attributes to the graph area and the tables. One student wished that there were “fewer buttons to get through everything and dragging stuff in.” While this affordance allows for open exploration, it can also make it more difficult for students to use the tool.

Representational affordances Three beneficial representational affordances emerged from the conversations. Many students felt more comfortable working with the 2D visualizations and graphs without articulating what exactly they felt more comfortable with. One student said, “I'm more comfortable looking at this type of data in a 2D setting, I guess.” Another beneficial affordance students found was working with only two attributes at a time to find a relationship. For example, when commenting on the tornado data, one student said, “...a  benefit of the previous [CODAP] is highlighting rare data points like the level four intensity. It's much harder to see them on the 3D diagram versus 2d.” A third representational affordance is the 2D map visualization with the data points overlaid on top of the map. Students indicated that they like to see the data distributed across the US, and it makes it easier to see certain attributes of the data, such as in which locations the most intense tornadoes occur. In terms of struggles with the CODAP representations, three themes emerged. The first was the large number of data points on a single representation. Students observed how the number of data points made it difficult to interpret anything from the map visualization because “there’s a lot going on.” Students also pointed out how the color gradient used to indicate different values of an attribute in a graph made interpretation more difficult. The way the data was arranged on the graphs also threw some students for a loop. For example, when students first load the tornado data in a graph, it is a stacked distribution plot with each tornado intensity (on a scale of 1-5). Due to the size of the dataset, this led to misinterpretations of the maximum versus the most common intensity (see Figure 1).

Structural affordances 597 When discussing the CODAP tool, students made few comments about the structural benefits of the 2D maps and graphs. Given that students were likely most familiar with this representational structure, it is not surprising that it didn’t elicit many questions. There were also no emergent struggles in terms of structural affordances. Space-time cube Interactive affordances Six interactive benefits were identified by students when using the STC: 1) The ability to click and hover over data to find more information, 2) Increased versatility in making comparisons, 3) The ability to zoom in/out, 4) The ability to rotate the 3D cube, 5) The ability to highlight different points in time with the time slider, and 6) General ease of use. The ability to zoom in and out, as well as the ability to click and hover over a data point to find more info, are also identified as benefits of the CODAP tool. The rotational ability of the 3D STC was found to be particularly useful by students, one of whom identified how it could be used to show the same view as the CODAP map, “if I flip it, flip this 90 degrees that way, it just looks like the graph that we had earlier, right, with the summer months here.” Another additional interactive affordance, the time slider (see Figure 2), was helpful to students because it “allows me to scrub through time and play it through time because I think it just helps limit the amount of data that I need to process in my brain at once so I can focus on patterns at each point in time.” However, the time slider was also an interactive hindrance for some students with two students commenting on how it was difficult to understand its purpose.

Representational affordances We identified two beneficial aspects of the representational affordances of the STC. The first is that students articulated how being able to see the timeline of data points in addition to other attributes, such as latitude and longitude, helped them make sense of the data. One student reported that “it just helped me link together the timeline of what they were doing.” Another student described how the STC helped them understand the tornado data: “So yeah, I mean the main pattern that this makes really clear, the 3D compared to the 2D one is just, it shows how many more tornadoes are happening during the summer months compared to the winter months.” The other representational benefit is that the STC contains more information, in general, than the typical 2D  plot or map. For example, one student describes how the STC made it easier to understand the four seals data, saying, “one thing that's a little easier with this one is you don't really need the animal ID at least not as much because the 3D space makes it pretty easy to distinguish the paths from one another.” There were three aspects of the STC representation that made students struggle. Two of the struggles, both shared with the CODAP visualization, are the large number of data points and the size and color of the data points. One student noted how the STC is “very cluttered, and if you want to see something over time, I think it would just be better to make separate graphs.” The third struggle students had was confusion with the layout of the axes and data in the STC. Some students thought the z-axis represented depth or elevation rather than time. In describing their confusion with the four seals data, one student remarked, “I thought what this was representing was the depth. Obviously they weren't in the sky, but the underwater, something like that. Like their paths in 3D space instead of just on a 2D plane. So I was a bit confused at the start.” Students had trouble understanding the STC visualization when first coming upon it. For example, one student reported, “it's in 3D format, which is kind of bizarre to digest that first. I'm not really sure what I'm looking at.” Structural affordances The structural benefits of the STC are deeply intertwined with the representational benefits, given that the 3D structure enables the 3D representation of ST data. However, there are two emergent beneficial themes that align with the structure more than the representation aspect. The first is that more information can be represented in the STC than in the 2D graphs and maps. One student said, “I feel like by being able to interact with a 3D model, you can see things you wouldn't see with the other one.” Additionally, the 3D STC allows the user to square up the axes to only look at two axes at one time in a 2D view. One student remarked, “I liked playing around with this so I could see it in the traditional format that I had earlier, just the 2D if that's easy to visualize at first. And then once I understand the data cells, I could move around with it.” However, other students struggled with the 3D structure and did not connect that they could rotate the cube to only see two axes at a time. These students preferred the 2D representations, saying, “I guess in this case it would be helpful to have it a little bit more 2D, just to see a bird's eye view of where it starts.” 598 Prior work provides insight into the challenges that learners might face when engaging with spatial and temporal data visualizations (e.g., James & Goldman, 2020; Humble, 2023, Mayer, 2005; Sanchez & Wiley, 2014). To make sense of such visualizations, students first need to learn to interpret the various forms of representation, such as graphs, tables, models, diagrams, and visualizations (e.g., Ainsworth, 2008; Lemke, 2004). Different structures of representation serve different functions, follow different  norms and conventions, provide unique learning opportunities, and vary with respect to the information they can convey (Ainsworth, 2008; Lie et al., 2023; Mayer, 2005; Schnotz & Bannert, 2003). While this study focuses on visual representations of ST data in particular, it builds on and adds to work done on data practices for visualizations of other types of data (e.g. Kahn & Jiang, 2021; Shapiro et al., 2022) In this study, we witnessed many students’ initial confusion with the STC representation. Many people think the upward trend of data points represents depth or elevation, assuming the cube represents a parcel of 3D space. However, many students were able to move past this initial conception and begin to productively interpret data in the STC. Students reported how the STC made it easier to interpret the timeline of movement in the four seals data. The time axis allowed students to easily identify the patter ns and changes of the color gradient of the data, representing another attribute such as speed or temperature. One student remarked that the STC allowed them to essentially view four aspects of the seals data at once, the four elements being time of year, latitude, longitude, and speed of the seal. A major breakthrough for some students came when they realized they could rotate the STC to view on two axes at once. For example, students can rotate the STC so that they are only viewing the x-y axes, looking top down on the spatial aspect of the data. In this configuration, the view of data on the maps is exactly the same as the view of data in the 2D CODAP visualizations. The STC inherently contains more information than any 2D map of the same data, because it  allows users the ability to view the data in 2D as well as 3D. In this way, the STC affords more possibilities for data exploration. Another affordance of the STC was the time scrubber tool, which allows students to shrink or expand the time period of dat a visualized in the STC and control a plane of focus, which they candrag up and down the z-axis to identify specific time points.  For some students, this interaction helped limit the amount of data needing to be processed and allowed them to focus on patterns at each point in time. Others described this tool as helping them see trends over time, rather than just on a graph.

This work has implications for how researchers, educators, and technology and tool developers move forward with supporting students in interpreting and analyzing ST data. ST data is becoming more prevalent across more fields and within society as data collection and storage capacities increase and students must be prepared to recognize, understand, and work with this type of complex data (Dorsey et al., 2025; NASEM, 2018). As researchers and developers seek to prepare students for existing in a world awash in complex ST data, it is vital to understand how specific novel interface designs can support that learning  (Kolvoord et al., 2019; Fan et al., 2024; Lie et al., 2023). While this work is a first small step in understanding what affordances novel user interface data analysis tools can provide for grappling with ST data, it is an important first step towards building supports that can be used in classrooms to increase adolescents’ understanding and analysis of complex spatiotemporal data.
