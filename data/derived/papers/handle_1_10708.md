# Transitioning an Active Learning Physics Course Between Professors was More Successful with a Third Person Acting as a Bridging Agent

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this study, we discuss how the presence of a self-study critical friend acted as a bridging agent to aid the transition of a Physical Science in Contemporary Society active learning course design between physics professors at a major Central Canadian research university. We also examine the roles the participants took, how the background of the participants may have aided the transition, and how these characteristics may assist transitions of active learning course designs in the future. Given the propensity of active learning courses to lose their active learning characteristic during the transition between instructors, techniques to retain the design of these courses are critically important.

## Introduction

Active learning activities and designs are thorough ly represented in the International Journal of the Learning Sciences (Wiebe et al., 2021). However, more research is needed on how to make these interventions stable across instructors. To this end, this study focuses on transitioning a successful active-learning-focused design of a Physical Science in Contemporary Society course from a professor with extensive exper ience in active learning pedagogy to a professor with more limited experienc e in active learning. Active learning pedagogy has been recognized as a more effective instructional style when compared to teach er-centered instructional styles (Freeman et al., 2014). Further, active learning is likely  critical to improving the retention of knowledge by students (Chi et al., 2018).

However, it is common for active learning classes t hat are transitioned between professors to lose the ir active learning components (Dancy et al., 2016; Khatri et al., 2016). These co mponents may be lost due to the unfamiliarity of the new professor with active lear ning techniques and/or lack of support for the transition. If a new professor is unfamiliar with active learning pe dagogy, they may transition to superficial active learning components while moving the focus of the class back to themselves (Dancy et al., 2016; Song & Looi, 2012). We proceeded to answer three questions: In this successful transition, what roles did each participant play, and how did those roles influence the result? How did the professors' backgrounds influence the outcome of the transition? Finally, what does this imply for future active learning instructional design transitions between university physics instructors?

## Methods

This case study uses a self-study design methodology (Loughran, 2004), which necessitates the presence of a “critical friend” whose role is to provide neutral feedback to the instructor of the course  under study. Self-study methodology focuses on the pedagogical problems of the instructor. The instructor addresses and analyz es the problem with the assistance of the critical friend to improve the validity of their reflections on the  changes made to the course to address the pedagogical problem. The previous self-study of this course, initiated in Fall 2022 (Sealfon & Burron, 2023), focused on the course des ign of Sealfon and examined some pedagogical challenges Sealfon was experiencing. The present case study was based on facilitating the transition of the course from Sealfon to Sinervo. Sinervo subsequently taught the course starting in Fall 2023. The critical friend (Burron) and the class format remained consistent between the two case studies. Data was collected through planning, reflection meetings, and observations of each class iteration. These meetings generally lasted about one hour, during wh ich Burron took notes of the instructors' planning and reflection. There were five planning meetings per course iter ation. Observations were conducted of both class iterations and captured one-third of each course ’s in-class activity. The activity was recorded in time-stamped notes recorded by Burron. Each course comprised twelve class meetings of two hours. The dataset is 16 hours of class observation and 10 hours of planning and reflection meetings.

## Participants

Burron acted as the critical friend in both case studies and is a PhD candidate at a large educational research institution in Central Canada. His research focuses  on active learning and applying self-study methodology to address instructor’s concerns with active learning pedagogies. Sealfon was an instructor in the Department of Physics at a large research institution in Central Canada. She has over a decade of experience applying resear ch-based teaching approaches in physics and across a w ide variety of undergraduate science courses.  This includes serving as a critical friend aiding c ourse transformations at a prestigious U.S. university with faculty across both the sciences and engineering. Sinervo is a professor of 30 years in the same department a s Sealfon. He has extensive experience in traditional instructional techniques (e.g., lectures, labs, and tutorials) but little experience in the  active learning techniques used in the course. However, he also use d some student-centred instruction (e.g., student-led presentations and student feedback) as part of a Scientific Communication graduate-level course.

## Results

As discussed in Sealfon & Burron (2023), the first case study with Sealfon resulted in a course that focused on active-learning activities facilitated by students. Each student-led class addressed a selected topic related to physics in contemporary society (e.g., racial and g ender bias in physics). These facilitations were pr eceded by course readings and followed by reflective writing for the first two-thirds of the course. Sealfon’s design explicitly required student facilitations to use active-learning pedagogy to encourage student engagement i n practicing clearly articulated learning goals. This facilitation component was also heavily weig hted in the course’s grading to avoid students opting to simply lecture to the class. The remaining third of the course was reserved for students to work on final projects and to engage their peers in some of the learning goals from their final projects. Students’ final projects were designed to allow them to go deeper into relevant topics of their choice. The course design also featured ungrading (Blum, 2020)  as the assessment method  for the students’ reflective writing and in-class presentations.

At the end of the 2022-2023 instructional year, Sealfon left the institution, and Sinervo was selected to instruct the course for the 2023-2024 year. As described in her reflections on this transition, Sealfon provided Sinervo with their course plan and met with Sinervo once to explain the course design. However, Sealfon was concerned that the course may lose its active learn ing and interactive components (as predicted in the  literature) during the transition and suggested that Sinervo continue the self-study of the course. Sealfon hoped that the selfstudy research would allow Sinervo to continue building on the benefits and learning gains produced in the course. Sinervo, while a dedicated instructor, was more familiar w ith “traditional” instructional approaches. As described in a reflection, Sinervo found the Physical Science in Contemporary Society course format to be completely different from his previous experiences. Sinervo found the use of Burron, in his role as a critical friend, to be effective in smoothing out any transitional hurdles. In reflective meetings, Sinervo frequently related the design of the course to his scientific communication course because he felt the two had some similarities. In later reflective meetings, Sinervo reaffirmed his desire to continue with the course’s  active learning design in subsequent offerings. He expressed a desire to doub le down on some aspects of the active learning design, such as meeting with students twice, rather than  once, prior to their in-class facilitation and enhancing the reflective writing component.

In the role of critical friend, Burron engaged with both Sealfon and Sinervo during the initial transition and then focused on supporting Sinervo’s transition. Burron answered any questions, provided feedback when requested, and offered to stay on between the two c ourses. Before the course began, Burron was also able to address any concerns Sinervo had about the active l earning pedagogies the course used and explained th e pedagogical thinking that had gone into those desig n choices. As part of Self-Study Methodology (Loughran, 2004), Burron focused on creating a collegial and non-judgmental relationship with both co-authors. While this transition may have been possible without Burron’s assistance, the presence of the critical friend made the process immeasurably easier.

In Sinervo’s iteration of the course, Burron observed a lack of the issues that the previous self-study with Sealfon aimed to solve (such as a tendency of some students  to lecture regardless of Sealfon’s encouragement to use active learning facilitation techniques). While there is some variability in the success of third-and fourth-year physics students to facilitate using active learning pedagogies (as expected in novice p edagogues), none of the students engaged in lecture-focused activities. Instead, the groups focused on active learning activities such as class debates, mock parliamentary committee hearings, and small group discussions.

## Discussion

This study discusses the successful transition of a n active learning course design from one instructor  to another.

Using a critical third person may have helped avoid the t raditionally rocky transitions the literature predi cts. If this is the case, then using a third bridging person may help avoid the repeated  development and subsequent loss of active learning pedagogy -- which is described in the literature (Dancy et al.,  2016; Khatri et al., 2016). While some instructors may be dedicated enough to prevent this expected loss, it more generally results in si gnificant wasted effort and may encourage instructors to shift to more traditional instructional methods (Dancy et al., 2016). Interestingly, issues addressed in Sealfon’s self-study design did not re-emerge in Sinervo’s course. While Sinervo was unaware of these issues, the design of the course that was carried forward has allowed these issues to remain controlled for, regardless of whether Sinervo was aware of them. Fullan (2007) predicted that the facilitation of new pedagogies b y a mentor figure would result in their implementation by teachers. This study assists in the confirmation of that expectation by providing an example of its successful implementation. The presence of the critical friend, in a mentorship role,  appears to have assisted the course transition  between instructors. This transition has included adopting student-facilitated instruction, ungrading, student-selected and implemented projects, and the change o f the role of the professor from the focal point of the classroom to that of the facilitator. Though Sinervo was unfamiliar with these techniques, he has become enthusiastic about their use.

It is also important to acknowledge Sinervo's history as a recipient of an active learn ing course design. While Sinervo was more seasoned in traditional course design, he did have experience with student-led presentations and student-provided feedback as part of his scientific communication graduate course. As predicted by Burron (2022), active learning pedagogy appears to be easier to conceptualize for instructors who have been exposed to a successful model of the technique in t he past and where active learning pedagogy has suff icient environmental support to flourish. It is possible that Sinervo’s earlier experience made the transition to a more radical, but fundamentally similar, design easier.  Further, Sinervo’s position as an internationally w ell-regarded researcher and tenured professor allowed him the freedom to engage in whatever pedagogical activity he deemed appropriate in his classroom.

The design of the present course is dissimilar to m ore traditional physics course designs. However, several institutions, such as the University of Colorado (Otero et al., 2010), have been successful in implementing active learning instructional designs in mathematic s-focused physics classrooms in first-year courses that are integrated into subsequent years of study.  This department initiated the use of  learning assistants as bridging persons to maintain the integrity of their physics instructional pedagogy across instructors and years. These learning assistants were trained in the active learning pedagogy of the course’s design, and they may also serve in the role for multiple years and across professors. Caravez et al. (2018) produced a six-year longitudinal study of these learning assistants that showed they helped maintain the active learning pedagogies  of the course’s professors. A learning assistant program at Widener University also helped with faculty development in studentcentred pedagogy (McHenry et al., 2010). Successful  learning assistant programs may provide analogous examples with similar characteristics to the present study, that effectively transition active learning course designs between instructors.

## Conclusion

Providing a third person, who is familiar with the active-learning instructional methods of a course, may be  an invaluable aid to improve the continuity of active-learning pedagogy across instructors. Fullan (2007) argued that mentorship over an extended period of time is criti cal to the development of pedagogical techniques. F urther, he argued that developing instructional techniques cannot be left to short pr ofessional development opportunities (such as meetings or email). In a parallel vein, th e transition of an active-learning course between instructors appears to benefit from a third person who can bridge the pedagogy between professors over an extended period. This presence of a bridging person may also aid in the continuous development of active learning cours es by reducing the requirement of new instructors to reinvent a course with each transition. While the learning sciences advocate for research-based instructional techniques, it is rare for the continued health and longevity of these instructional techniques to be addressed. If the learning sciences intend to have a lasting impact on instructional change (and to advocate for innovative instructional designs), then it is also critical that we consider the transitions between i nstructors and how to maintain our works across pra ctitioner transitions.
