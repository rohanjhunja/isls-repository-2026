# Exploring How Teacher Educators Critically Reason with Data

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Data literacy is now required for civic participation and engagement in a society embedded in datafication (Gebre, 2022). Teacher educators must integrate statistics and data science across the pre-service curriculum to equip students with the tools to be data literate. This paper reports on how teacher educators in a public university system integrate data investigations into their courses and examines the conceptualization of data literacy across interdisciplinary contexts. We found that these teacher educators 1) framed data as text to bring light to the ideologies at play behind data collection and analysis, 2) negotiated the disconnect between lived experiences and statistical reasoning to claim data authorship, 3) presented data in ways that enable richer understandings of diversity and complexity in systems. Our findings suggest that in classes where data is not a core stated learning goal, it illustrates the complexity and diversity of the content they teach.

## Introduction

With the ascendance of datafication, concerns about  its potential social and political ramifications h ave risen (Pangrazio & Sefton-Green, 2019). Students and educators encounter data  daily, for example, from social networking sites, smartphones, gaming de vices, and websites (Gould et al., 2017). Civic dat a (e.g., municipality demographics) and environmental data (e.g., air qua lity benchmark data) are also collected by devices multiple times daily (Lee & Wilkerson, 2018; Wilkerson & Pol man, 2020). However, these datasets containing sensitive information about ourselves, including our behavior, can be exploited (Pangrazio & Sefton-Green, 2019). For example, targeted advertisements stem from individu al preferences gathered from website interaction or clickstream data (Wilkerson & Polman, 2020). Concerns about data privacy continue to rise as students interrogate how, why, and from whom the data is collected (Phil ip et al., 2013). To critically explore data infras tructure, students, educators, and teacher educators must develop data literacies (Raffaghelli & Stewart, 2020). Data is pertinent for active citizenship and inform ed decision-making, and this recognition in research and policy recommends incorporating data literacy across various disciplines (Gebre, 2022). Data science is also inherently interdisciplinary, therefore  knowledge of the dataset’s context is necessary for  analysis (De Veaux et al., 2017; Wilkerson & Polman, 2020). To enable students to re ason with data, educators must provide opportunities to conduct data-based investigations across disciplines (De Veaux e t al., 2017; Ellerbrock et al., 2016; Konold & Higgins, 2003; Vahey et al., 2012). For educators to accomplish this goal, teacher prepar ation programs must provide pre-service teachers with the skills to integrate stati stics and data science content in the classroom (Bargaliotti & Franklin, 2021). Teacher e ducators can provide professional  development and instruction that addresses this gap in data literacy for K-12 educators (Raffaghelli, 2019; Shreiner & Dykes, 2021). However, information on the professional developmen t of educators’ data literacy remains scarce despit e their need to develop these literacies (Mandinach & Gummer, 2013; Raffaghelli & Stewart, 2020), and we know little about how they conceptualize data (Gould, 2021). To address this gap, we explore the following questions: RQ1: What types of data engagements, through the lens of literacy, did teacher educators construct for  the future teachers with whom they work?

RQ2: In what ways did teacher educators, especially thos e for whom data was not already a significant component of their courses, leverage data as text?

## Literature review

Data literacy Data-literate individuals have the skills to participate in a society embedded in datafication (Gebre, 2022; Konold & Higgins, 2003). A wide array of literature define s data literacy as developing the skills to manage and work with data (Raffaghelli & Stewart, 2020). A commonal ity among the definitions is the focus on developin g the technical skill set to transform data into information. Raffaghelli and Stewart’s (2020) systematic literature review investigating gaps in faculty development and data framing indicates that most papers discussing data literacy focus on instrument framing and the development of technical skills. Another central theme across the litera ture was the emphasis on teachers’ data literacy to prod uce evidence (Raffaghelli & Stewart, 2020). Recent studies involving teachers’ data literacy skills focus on u sing classroom data for assessment and improving le arning outcomes (Miller-Bains et al., 2022; Mandinach et al., 2015). Formal  data literacy pedagogies often focus on helping students work with data for research and so metimes include discussion on social data and priva cy (Pangrazio & Sefton-Green, 2020). Although these competencies are necessary, they do not support learning how methods of data collection impact communities (Gebr e, 2022). However, constructs such as racial data l iteracy can challenge racialized ideologies when interpreti ng data visualizations and decenter statistics-based reasoning in STEM learning environments (Philip et al., 2016). Nevertheless, there still needs to be a consensus  on what competencies constitute data literacy (Mandinach & Gummer, 2013). Researchers have conceptualized data literacy in a variety of contexts. For example, Maybee and Zilinski

(2015) introduce d ‘data-informed learning’ as a framework for instructors t o embed data literacy in higher

education curricula. The framework comprises three key components: “guides data-related course content, encourages coursework relevancy, and supports lifelong learning (using data in the context of learning and doing prepares learners to use data to learn in professio nal and personal settings)” (Maybee & Zilinski, 201 5, p. 3). By contextualizing how data is used and communicated a cross different disciplinary contexts, the framework shifts the traditional focus from skills acquisition to en abling students to learn how to use data within the ir discipline (Maybee & Zilinksi, 2015). Additionally, data liter acy frameworks have emerged for teacher preparation programs. Mandinach and Gummer (2016) argue d that existing definitions of data literacy are inad equate for integrating into teacher preparation programs. To mitigate this gap, they introduced the conceptual framework of data literacy for teachers (DLFT) to equip pre-service teachers with the capacity to use data in their future practice. DLFT comprises seven critical components of the data inquiry process: “content knowledge; general pedagogical knowledge; curriculum knowledge; pedagogical conten t knowledge; knowledge of learners and their characteristics; knowledge of educational contexts;  and knowledge of educational ends, purposes and va lues'' (Mandinach & Gummer, 2016, p. 369). These component s stem from their review of other conceptual frameworks, state licensure and certification records, and scholars' varied definitions of data literacy. Furthermore, there have been overt conversations on  the explicit integration of equity and social just ice in data literacy. Philip et al. (2013) shared a framework grounded in sociocultural theories of learning and critical pedagogy to enable democratic participation through  learning about big data in public schools. This fr amework outlines five objectives to develop the technical c apacities to use data science tools for collecting,  analyzing, visualizing, and interpreting data. These objective s help us understand if incorporating Big Data in a  classroom promotes democratic participation. However, this fr amework is specific to the school’s context and for  civic engagement. From the various frameworks shared, it is evident that the lack of clarity on what compete ncies constitute data literacy feeds into a disarray of c onceptualizations that affect educational policies and practices. However, the consensus in data literacy exists in the field's communal desi re to produce citizens who can reason with data across the education and workforce ecosys tem. Accordingly, as the prevalence of big data gro ws, scholarship continues to expand on what it means to be data literate (Louie, 2022). Pre-service teachers’ data literacy skills and training An empirical overview of educator data literacy ski lls reveals that pre-service teachers do not receive adequate training to be data literate, nor can they facilitate discussions on how data can impact commu nities without marginalizing minority students in the process (Bow en & Roth, 2005; Gould, 2021; Shreiner & Dykes, 202 1). Henderson and Corry (2020) suggest that the lack of clarity in the literature on the construct of data literacy may affect the development of teacher preparation progr ams that adequately equip teachers with data litera cy skills and knowledge. Mandinach and Gummer (2016) suggest that this obscurity has led the field to perceive assessment data to be most paramount for educators, thereby im pacting the quality and content of training provided by teacher preparation programs. In additi on, the rise of data-driven decision-making in education has led researchers and educators to conflate data literacy with assessment literacy (Cui et al.,  2023; Mandinach & Gummer, 2016). For instance, Mandinach et al. (2016) comprehensive review of how schools of education prepare pre-service teachers to use data reveals that assessmen t data (e.g., diagnostic data) was a more prominent part of course content than non-assessment data (e.g., attendance data). Furthermor e, data literacy interventions for preservice teachers focus on classroom data for assess ing and improving student learning and data-driven decisionmaking for instruction (Carey et al., 2018; Reeves & Honig, 2015; Reeves, 2017). While assessment literacy is a component of data literacy, it does not consider the social and critical dimensions of data that are integral for preservice teachers to understand (Cowie & Cooper, 2019; Mandinach & Gummer, 2016). Perhaps as a result of this over-emphasis on assessment data literacy, several studi es suggest that pre-service and in-service teachers lack confidence in their ability to critically interpret data and use data for practical application prior to receiving  targeted professional development (Carey et al., 20 18; Reeves & Chiang, 2018; Shreiner & Dykes, 2021).  Professional development is one way to address these issues. For example, Carey et al.

(2018) found that pre-service teachers were not confident about their ability to interpret and analyze data critically.

However, after taking a data literacy course, survey results indicated that participating teachers felt they acquired basic data literacy and could interpret student data to support their learning. Research suggests similar patterns in teachers’ confidence  in teaching their  students about data. For example, Shreiner and Dyke s’s (2021) study examining social studies teachers’ practices, belie fs, and knowledge about data literacy found that 97 % of 262 social studies teachers had no instruction that wou ld prepare them to teach data literacy. Their findings sug gest inadequate preparation leads to a lack of confidence in teaching data literacy. Overall, research about pre-service teachers’ understanding and application of statistical content knowledge in their lesson plans remains scarce. Gould (2021) argues that providing adequate professional development will ensure teachers are prepared to support their students’ effective development of data literacy and to facilitate constructive discourse about data. However, to cultivate meaningful learning experienc es for non-STEM pre-service teachers, working with data exposure to a dataset in their relevant context is necessary.  Indeed, prior work has shown that educators can apply mathematical concepts to modeling and reasoning in real-life situations (Konold & Higgins, 2003). Vahey et al.

(2012) found that cross-disciplinary curricular materials can increase stud ent data literacy and understanding of

mathematical disciplinary content. Additionally, Finzer (2013) posits that history coursework can use census data to explore migration patterns, and science classrooms can use data to interrogate deviations from their hypothesis. Ellerbrock et al. (2016) also describe examples of how teacher educators can teach data literacy through culturally relevant pedagogy across disciplines. They highlight, in particular, Vasquez (2014), who collaborated with h er pre-service teachers to investigate data in the context  of their school, such as data on their school demo graphics and neighborhood. Data related to the pre-service teachers’ social and cultural contexts supp orted them in going beyond basic statistics as they developed awareness  of how the inequitable distribution of resources c ontributes to educational disparities. As such, schools of education can inte grate relevant data constructs across disciplines in their teacher preparation programs (Mandinach & Gummer, 2013; Mandinach & Gummer, 2016). Literacy as a paradigm for understanding data The conventional notion of literacy focuses on read ing and writing; thus, adopting a literacy paradigm  for data requires identifying the ‘text’ to be interpreted (Pangrazio & Sefton-Green, 2020). Pangrazio and Sefton-Green

(2020) posit that data literacy r equires the skills to read the symbolic codes and g rammar inscribed in data and,

by relation, the ability to construct data inscript ions. Literacy provides a heuristic through which i ndividuals can make sense of the patterns underlying the relations hip between critical theories and functional symbol ic codes (Pangrazio & Sefton-Green, 2020). Yet, they also confront that statistical and mathematical rea soning, which requires knowing operational functions for mathemat ical processes, forms the foundation for how data o perates at a functional and critical level. Whether this can still be classified as literacy rather than mathematical reasoning remains. This may challenge conceptualizing literac y as a paradigm for understanding data. As such, Pa ngrazio and Sefton-Green (2020) suggest a need for more discourse on the relationship between oral communication and written symbolic codes and the consequential implications for conceptualizing data literacy. Drawing upon New London Group’s multiliteracy pedag ogies, datasets can be read as ‘texts’ that, similarly, are subject to an individual’s interpret ation and transformation (Cazden et al., 1996). Eve ry individual is a creator of patterns and conventions of meaning and a recipient of them. As designers of meaning, individuals influence the future of social change, citizenship, workplace culture, and our communities. In a classroom context, teachers design the environment and their students’  learning processe s (Cazden et al., 1996). Teachers can leverage syncretic pedagogies to help students negotiate data as an additional literacy with their lived experiences (Vogelstein et al., 2023). In addition, when students juxtapose different languages and approaches, they attain the capacity to critically reflect and challenge existi ng systems and norms (Cazden et al., 1996). For exa mple, Vogelstein et al. (2023) discuss a project that illustrated how students considered their experiences when reasoning with data that was at odds with statistical reasoni ng. Students’ daily lived experiences have epistemologi cal significance, as they have a more complete view of their learning progression and knowledge construction across multiple contexts (Nielsen, 1990). Addressing the mismatch between student realities a nd the data presented candeepen the development of data literacy, offering students the agency to author data that holds personal relevance (Reigh et al., 2022; Van Wart et al., 2020). Hence, adopting a syncretic approa ch to literacy can address the disconnection between everyday experiences and data  science practices and disrupt the conventional not ions of literacy (Gutierrez, 2014).

In proximal disciplines, such as science, the conceptualization of literacy has evolved over the years, and it has yet to be determined what orientation is app ropriate to address the challenges of the twenty-first century. Saija et al. (2022) describe sci entific literacy as an individual’s knowledge and u nderstanding of scientific ideas and processes necessary for decision-making and engaging in social, cultural, and economic affairs. Recent studies in science education research emphasize that scient ific l iteracy in today’s classroom should focus on social activism (Valladares, 2021). However, the ability to think critically in science, or broadly in STEM, is not enough to understand the challenges confronting science ed ucation. Critical thinking may address the goals of science education in the twenty-first century, but it does not consider the gaps in  the learning process (Bang & Medin, 2010). To mitigate scientific literacy's shortcomin gs, utilizing socio-scientific issues in classrooms have become a mechanism to make scientific learning more accessible and relevant (Saija et al., 2022). Despite the communal understanding across scholarsh ip on the need for data literacy instruction across disciplines, a theoretical approach to classroom im plementation is lacking (Vahey et al., 2012). With limited evidence on teacher pedagogical practices in teachi ng data literacy in cross-disciplinary contexts, we look to science education research to draw parallels to inform this work. Similar to integrating social activism in scientific literacy, scholars have emphasized the importance o f perceiving data  as more than just numbers (Konold & Higgins, 2003). It is necessary to interrogate the data collection process – specifically investigating the ‘who, what, why, when, and where?’ of the given dataset (Rubin, 2022). With statistical thinking at the hear t of data literacy, there are calls to action for teacher edu cators to include relevant context when teaching st atistics to preservice teachers (Bailey et al., 2020).

## Participants

In 2023, we designed and enacted a multi-week “Summer Camp” for teacher educators (TEs) on Zoom in a large, public university system. The camp was part of the university’s efforts to include computing experienc es within the pre-service teacher programs. It was one of many offeri ngs participants could choose from to integrate computing experiences into their planned coursework  for pre-service teachers. Twenty TEs enrolled, nine consented to participate, and six had previously in tegrated data-based activities into their coursework. Participants’ courses included Biology, Multilingualism, Child Development, Probability and Statistics, and Special Education. A pre-survey gauged teacher educators’ confidence in integrating the technical, social, and ethical aspects of data in their coursework.

## Procedure

Participants conducted their data explorations on C ODAP (a common online data analysis platform). CODA P is a valuable tool for teacher educators who are novic es at reasoning with data because it is user-friendly, does not require prior programming experien ce, and enables novice learners to begin data explo ration promptly (Frischemeier et al.,  2021). Participants were provided asynchronous modu les to acclimate to the CODAP environment before the workshop. The first day of t he workshop involved data exploration tasks in whic h participants had the same dataset and instructions to explore, interpret, and visualize the data. The following days included office hours to provide CODAP support and guidance on constructing data-based activities. Through the workshop, participants created data stories and activities to embed into their curriculum. We collected all CODAP files, Zoom audio and video transcripts, and other artifacts outlining their ideas or data engagements.

## Data analysis

We use Stake’s (2013) multicase study approach to e xamine the data engagements teacher educators devel oped and discussed during and after the workshop. The mu lti-case approach allows us to examine the phenomena of framing data as text across the teacher ed ucators’ various disciplinary contexts. The cases w ere constructed through data collected and triangulated from worksh op audio transcripts, CODAP files, and artifacts ou tlining their data engagements. We pull from the literacy p aradigm to reveal how the pa rticipants incorporated data investigations into their coursework and the types of data engagements they plan to prescribe to their  students. Lastly, we use ideologies from syncretism, socio-scientific issues, and Pangrazio and Sefton-Green’s (2020) conceptualization of data literacy to structure our analysis (Gutierrez, 2014; Saija et al., 2022).

## Findings

A preliminary survey indicated that K-12 teacher educators reported high or low confidenc e in the technical aspects of data and moderate confidence in the soci al aspects. They cited student voice, critical reas oning, and data fluency as motivations for data integrative coursework. The teacher educators also leveraged data to illustrate variability, complexity, and social dimensions of core concepts across their domains. The followin g case studies detail how teacher educators framed data as text during the workshop and through their artifacts: Participant a case study: Data storytelling for COVID-19 health equity by race Participant A, an instructor of a Biology course, hoped to use data to highlight health inequities by  race to their students. Participant A did not have prior experience in using a classroom-derived dataset. In the pre-survey, they expressed a lack of confidence in recognizing data limitations, making inferences, exploring data, and identifying appropriate data visualizations to perform statisti cal analysis. They also shared uncertainty about us ing data to examine hidden narratives and advocacy due to limited prior experience. However, they expressed confidence in using data to investigate relevant social issues through their course.

Using a syncretic pedagogical approach to frame data as text, Participant A enlisted students to juxtapose their experiences with their interpretation of the dataset and the associated data visualizations to 1) address the disconnect with their lived experiences and 2) tran sform the data to author a story that reflects thei r realities (Vogelstein et al., 2023). To accomplish this, they saw it fit to incorporate data investigations in their coursework, explicitly looking at racial disparities in health outcomes. Participant A provided students with a dataset on ‘ life expectancy’ for COVID-19 from the CDC through the NYTimes, the latter of which provided t he data visualization for interpretation. They constructed an assignment of data engagements to enable their s tudents to interrogate and explore a dataset in ways that indi vidualize their learning of fund amental concepts around race and health equity. The data engagements included as signments that supported students in understanding the symbolic codes and grammar of data through graph creation, interpretation, analysis, and contextualization; asked students to make personal connections to the data and prop ose additional ways to use the data; and informed students to use CODAP for creating a data story for advocacy. To develop and build upon their students' technical  fluency, Participant A determined the following learning objectives: interpreting two-and three-dimensional plots, selecting and graphically repres enting the appropriate data to identify racial disparities in health outcomes, discussing the limitations and biases of the data, and using CODAP to create a data story for health a dvocacy. The questions were designed to enable stud ents to understand how the graph was made, how to interpret the graph, and comprehend the context of the data, fostering comprehension of the symbolic codes and grammar needed to reason with data. Participant A also provided guided questions that offered students to make personal co nnections to the data and propose additional ways t o use the data. Building upon this 'unplugged' exercise and i ntegrating the social considerations of the data, s tudents then were to use CODAP to create a data story that shows  how to use data for advocacy to answer a self-generated question. Furthermore, Participant A used a socio-scientific approach to support students in identify ing how to mitigate health disparities through selection, visualization, and critical analyses of health intervention data points. Focusing on more recent global eve nts (i.e., COVID-19), Participant A’s assignment topic was the pande mic’s varied impact on the health outcomes across Black, Latinx, and white individuals. Attending to a topic that is personally relevant to themselves and their classro oms and connected to their disciplinary context, Pa rticipant A makes data literacy more accessible (Saija et al., 2022). This accessibility also allows students to take authorship of the dataset through the data engagements that pr ovoke a critical reflection on CDC’s and NY Times narrative on COVID-related health disparities to create an individual data story that promotes health advocacy.

## Participant B case study: Multilingualism-diversity of language

Participant B is an instructor for a Multilingualism course, in which they hope to have students analyze institutional survey data on language use. In the pre-survey, Participant B expressed moderate confidence around integrating data's social and ethical aspects in their work because they lacked experience analyzing data while considering ethical issues and historical context. They shared that previously, they relied on others to frame the data or point out biases. Participant B found it essential for their students to be able to understand and participate in data storytelling critically. In particular, the y suggested creating professional development opportunities tha t provide a richer sense of bilingualism, which can be conceptualized as more th an a double monologue. They expressed that there is a fundamental need to under stand the ideology at play behind the symbolic code s and grammar (e.g., graphs, inferences, proportionality)  inscribed when consuming published statistics and how to collect, analyze, and visualize data (Pangrazio & Sefton-Green, 2019). Additionally, Participant B leveraged a syncretic a pproach to analyzing the institutional survey data, juxtaposing the complexity and differences between spoken/heard/read/written languages and society's t houghts about what defines home and dominant langua ges. Through this exercise, Participant B frame d data as text in interrogating the repeated marginalization of langu ages that do not align with white mainstream Englis h when critically comparing how language diversity is lived and its relationship to white mainstream English.  By taking authorship of data interpretation from their instit ution, Participant B discusses moving away from lab eling languages as ‘home’ or ‘dominant’ and instead autho ring them Languages 1, 2, 3, and so on to deconstru ct language hierarchy. They also shared concerns about  how institutions count languages and the contradic tory narrative of valuing language diversity but not interrogating what language diversity means. While the participant did not explicitly discuss language diversity through the lens of statistical reasoning, we can map their ideas about the different dimensions of language back to the ge neral concept of variability and classification. As  such, Participant B used socio-scientific ideologies in their discussion of how da ta is more than numbers and, consequently, in their intent to have stud ents within linguistics classrooms critically exami ne datasets for any omission of multilingual learners’ experiences. The  relevant disciplinary context of the dataset offer s the agency to transform the dataset to author a story that changes the narrative scarcity of the multilingual experience to one of narrative plenitude (Lee, 2021).

## Participant C: Child development

Participant C is an instructor for courses in Child  Development and hopes to help their students under stand the complexity of how multiple factors influence child behavior and functioning at any given time. In the pre-survey, Participant C expressed confidence in understanding data's social, ethical, and technical aspects and the ability to understand data conceptually. However, they also sh ared that they are not good at performing mental ma th or working with numbers. They noted experience working with mixed methods data and a preference for leveraging video analysis for research with children and class rooms. Participant C shared that across their stude nts, there is a vast range of academic, intellectual, and socio-emotional capacities and challenges among them. Mos t have been directly impacted by racism/gender bias and ec onomic injustice, and most will also serve children  and families in similar situations.

Considering their students’ diverse contexts and de mographics, Participant C took a syncretic approach to embed socio-scientific ideologies within statistical reasoning to their course design, expressing their goal to have their students look at patterns over time to e mpower themselves to become aware of how different factors of their lives and the lives of their communities intersect and produce realities. For example, Participant C’s data engagement instructs students to look at a graph in CODAP, articulate what patterns or relationships they see, and consider additional variables to include that could help them understand their case study child and development better. Furthermore, Participant C expressed a desi re to learn how to frame data as text through the w orkshop to support their students in using data analysis in a manner that helps them recognize patterns of information to tell stories at varied levels. In their data engagement,  they prompt students to reflect on how the data is  different or similar to their descriptive review, giving them authorship of their interpretation and the data story.

## Participant D: Science methods

Participant D is an instructor for a Science Methods course. In the pre-survey, they expressed the least confidence in their statistical knowledge and had never includ ed data-rich materials or activities in their coursework. However, they conveyed confidence in numerical reasoning, such as creating  graphs, selecting appropriate numerical summaries (e.g., categorical vs. measurem ent), identifying variability and measures of cente r from a graph, and identifying patterns  or trends from a scatter plot. Participant D also s hared that they were mostly confident in several dimensions of integrating the social and ethical aspects of data, including the c onsideration of ethical issues that surface from patterns or trends, what perspectives are highlighted or obscured in the selection of data analysis or visualization methods, and creating datasets to explore or highlight issues of social relevance. Participant D constructed their data engagement aro und a socio-scientific issue, integrating a data story that guides students in examining the relationship between temperature, precipitation, and wind from data collected for 10 years from Mt. Washington Observatory.  Through this exercise, Participant D frame d data as text by walking through observation data, which was  depicted through tables and scatter plots on CODAP, to share the relationship between temperature and snow, temperature and rain, and temperature and wind. T hey scaffolded the walkthrough; each relationship is observed separately first, then paired (wind and snow, wind and temperature), and lastly, all three relationships a re shown together. This engagement allows students to individually interpret the relationships, famili arize themselves with the data, and then compare the different relationships. Participant D’s data engagement depi cts how one can take authorship when exploring data. However, they did not take a syncretic approach to construct their activity, but their goals as instructor align with leveraging syncretism in their course. As such, Participant D recognizes that their students are both motivated and scared to learn technology and science. They intended to participate in the workshop to acquire the skills to support their students in expressing themselves and who they are from their stories and experiences and give them a voice that will include their story as part of the overall story.

## Discussion

The four case studies illustrate how teacher educat ors integrated data activities across different dis ciplinary contexts. Overall, the TEs incorporated data investigations to help their students negotiate the disconnect between their lived experiences and the methods of statistical reasoning to author thei r interpretations of the dataset. The juxtaposition brought to light inequities caused by  current data practices in the participants’ cross-disciplinary contexts. There is also evidence of syncretism, transfer of authorship, and leveraging social issues to reason with data critically in their pedagogical approaches. We detail our findings from the initial analysis below. To begin, Participant A contextualized the data eng agements to relevant issues within their discipline and then scaffolded tasks in their pre-service coursework to move from using basic to more  advanced statistical reasoning. These tasks also lead students  to generate an original question from the dataset. At the end of the assignment, using the knowledge they have generated  thus far, Participant A directs students to use da ta tools to create a data story through which the student contr ols the narrative and engages in health advocacy. In contrast, Participant B did not detail data engagements for their pre-service coursework like Participant A. In conversation with one of the workshop facilitators, Participant B verbally interrogated the current process of c onstructing institutional datasets on language and expressed th eir desire to change the narrative and perception o f multilingualism and language hierarchy in the class room. In particular, Participant B outlined their g oal for their pre-service course: to help pre-service teachers develop data skills to create a richer sense of multilingualism and language diversity, consequently shifting data authorship from the institution to the student. Through the workshop session, with guidance from the facilitato r, Participant B begins with primary data explorati on of the institutional dataset and moves to more advanced reasoning. Through this gradual process, their data interrogation manifested, leading them tonegotiate their experiences with the findings from the data. While verbally authoring discipline-related data to include lived experiences, Particip ant B deepened their data literacy and ability to address social issues within their discipline (Van Wart et al., 2020; Reigh et al., 2022). Like Participant A, Participant C developed a discipline-related data engagement that integrated tasks to build their students’ fluency and capacity to understand the technical and social aspects of child development data. The scaffolded tasks support students in building the technical skills necessary for statistical reasoning with data. For example, the activities enabled students to int erpret graphs, articulate observed relationships be tween variables, and identify additional variables needed  to analyze their individual case study child and d evelopment. Through this last step, Participant C frames data a s text and shifts the data authorship to the studen ts so they can connect their data interpretations to their case st udy and decide which variables are ne cessary for constructing their specific data story on child development.   Furthermore, Participant C employs syncretism in th eir pedagogical approach. In particular, they discuss their instructional goal to have students identify patterns across data and connect to their and their communities’ lived experiences to understand how these intersections influence their realities. Comparable to Participants A and C, Participant D also constructed a data engagement that allows students to familiarize themselves with the wind, temperature, and precipitation data separately, then in pairs, and lastly, altogether. Through these scaffolds, Participant C builds students’ ability to statistically r eason with data and transfers data authorship to students through activities that allow multiperspective observations and relational comparisons between wind, temperature, and precipit ation. In contrast to Participants A, B, and C, Par ticipant D does not employ a syncretic approach to developing their data engagement. However, their goal for the course aligns with syncretism as they hope that through the workshop, they can build the skills necessary to support their students in expressing their realities and ensuring their stories are heard and included.

In conclusion, the workshop demonstrated how the pa rticipants framed data as text distinctively across their respective disciplines. Despite the variation across the data investigation s, all participants emphasized the role of technical fluency when framing data as text  to help their students comprehend data's symbolic codes and grammar, critically assess data, and examine the mo tivations behind data collection and presentation. These findings suggest that using data related to the disciplinary contexts of teacher educators supports them in moving beyond learning the technical aspects of data to de veloping awareness of how data can revea l inequities (Ellerbrock et al., 2016). Furthermore, all partici pants shared their intentions to employ syncretism in their pedagogical approach and emphasize the social and e thical considerations of data in their coursework. They discussed shifting data authorship to their students, allowing them to create a data story that reflects their narrative. However, while the findings reveal the TEs' capacit y to critically author data nonverbally, there is m inimal evidence of whether the TEs can author data in a t echnical sense through nonverbal and verbal formats. Future work may require knowing the TEs' technical and cri tical reasoning skills to establish a baseline. Thi s baseline will help assess the TEs' verbal and nonverbal data  authorship to examine their cri tical and technical abilities. Therefore, by leveraging literacy as a heuristic, this work provides new insights into framing data as text.

## Implications

Statistical reasoning with data is now a requisite for participating in today’s increasingly data-and digitally-driven world. In response to this datafication, there is a national call to action to prepare students with the skills to reason with data across disciplinary contexts before they leave secondary school (Finzer, 2013; Bargagliotti & Franklin, 2021). To successfully address this call, teacher preparation programs must prepare pre-service teachers to be data literate. However, despite the increasin g attention toward cultivating statistical skills t o reason with data, state requirements on data literacy for teacher licensure  still need to be clarified, impacting schools of e ducation that seek to integrate it into their curriculum (Henders on & Corry, 2020). Therefore, scholarship must cont inue to address the absence of empirical evidence on pre-service teachers’ skills in critical reasoning with data and reach a consensus on what competencies constitute data li teracy. Furthermore, as the construct of literacy h as evolved to include multimodalities, which are not exclusive  to no nverbal and verbal communicative practices, scholar s must also re-examine how pre-service teachers communicate about data and where t hose practices fit into measuring an individual’s data literacy (Cazden et al., 1996; Drewry et al., 2020). Thus, further work  on data literacy is necessary to provide more specific reco mmendations to address the societal need for develo ping dataliterate citizens.
