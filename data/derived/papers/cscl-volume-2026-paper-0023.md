# Farm Harvests and Airplane Engines: Contextualizing Database Exercises in Relatable Contexts with a Learnersourcing Workflow

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Developing learning materials that are relatable to students with varied backgrounds poses substantial challenges for instructors. This paper introduces a learnersourcing workflow designed to empower students as creators of personalized educational content, specifically through generating and peer-reviewing worked examples (WEs) that connect computing concepts to real-world scenarios. We empirically evaluated this learnersourcing approach within a database programming course and collected 127 student-generated WEs. The WEs contextualized database problems within diverse and novel scenarios, ranging from agricultural management to aircraft maintenance. Although some WEs had incomplete problem statements or incorrect solutions, most were complete, clear, and educational. In addition, student peer assessments of the WEs are aligned with instructor evaluations. We discuss the lessons learned regarding how a structured learnersourcing workflow can help students contextualize computing concepts and support learning.

Learning materials that resonate with students' diverse backgrounds are crucial for building foundational programming knowledge  (Bouvier et al., 2016;  McKenna & Laycock, 2004). However, effective contextualization relies on contexts that are authentic and relatable to the learners  (Griggs & Cox, 1982). Developing such learning materials requires substantial instructor effort and sensitivity to understand diverse student backgrounds.

We propose a learnersourcing  (Kim, 2015) workflow that considers students as experts in their own guides students to create and peer-review worked examples (Renkl, 2002) as a channel to communicate how the computing concepts can be applied to address real-world problems relevant to them. The workflow was empirically evaluated in a database programming course with 45 junior and senior students. Students completed learnersourcing-based assignments covering different computing concepts. While some student-generated examples occasionally had vague problem statements or incorrect solutions, most were clear, relevant, and educational within the course contexts. We identified 19 context themes from the student-created WEs and found close alignment between peer assessments and instructor evaluations. This experience report contributes (1) a learnersourcing workflow and instructional design that enable students to contextualize computing concepts and collaboratively generate high-quality, relatable learning materials; and (2) empirical insights into the  effectiveness of learnersourcing in producing student-centered worked examples and supporting student learning.

## Related work

Relatable examples reduce student dropout rates  (Guzdial, 2010), facilitate skill transfer  (Bouvier et al., 2016), and support autonomous behavior aligned with self-determination theory (Assor et al., 2002; Patall et al., 2010). While the outcomes are beneficial, perception of relatability varies by prior knowledge  (Wu, 2021), learning preferences (Smith & Renzulli, 1984), and cultural backgrounds (Fisher et al., 2012). This aligns with Culturally Responsive Teaching (CRT)  (Scott et al., 2015), which values students’ cultures and promotes inclusivity. However, contextualized learning materials by personal background require substantial instructor effort and sensitivity to diverse student experiences (Wyatt, 2015; Scott et al., 2015). Learnersourcing in computing education Learnersourcing, rooted in crowdsourcing and participatory learning (Kim, 2015), has the potential to address these challenges by transforming students from passive consumers to active contributors and co-creators of knowledge (Abdi et al., 2021; Singh, Brooks, & Shayandoroudi, 2022). Notable implementations of learnersourcing in programming education include generating explanations of coding misconceptions (Guo et al., 2020), instructional hints  (Glassman et al., 2016), and SQL exercises  (Pirttinen et al., 2023). Platforms like 129 PeerWise (Denny et al., 2008), RiPPLE (Khosravi et al., 2019), AXIS (Williams et al., 2016), and ExPeerience (Zhou et al., 2026) further facilitate the broader application of learnersourcing across various courses. In line with the principles of culturally responsive teaching (Scott et al., 2015), we view students' diverse are structured explanations that clearly present problem statements and detailed solution steps (Collins et al., 1991; Vieira et al., 2015).

Research testbed We developed and refined our learnersourcing workflow within the context of an advanced database programming course offered at a large public university. The class covers PL/SQL (P rocedural Language extension for SQL), Oracle’s procedural extension for Structured Query Language. The majority of students enrolled in this course are junior or senior college students majoring in Information Technology or related fields. Enrollment in the course requires prior completion of an introductory SQL course. Class sizes range from 20-25 students in the spring semester to 40-50 in the fall semester, consistently maintaining a 1:4 female-to-male ratio that reflects the college’s broader demographics. The preliminary study occurred in Spring 2022, and the evaluation study was conducted during Fall 2022. The course covers three main themes. PL/SQL Fundamentals: PL/SQL blocks, variables, conditional control structures, exception handling, and integration with SQL statements. Functions and Procedures: userdefined stored programs designed to encapsulate SQL and PL/SQL logic. Triggers: stored programs that execute automatically in response to defined database events.

Learnersourcing workflow design We employed a design-based research approach structured around a three-phase design process. In Phase 1, we developed the learnersourcing workflow grounded in constructivist learning principles  (Gance, 2002) (Figure 1, top). Phase 2 involved a preliminary study to identify key leverage points for scaffolding students. In Phase 3, we refined and evaluated the workflow design.

Initial design and theoretical grounding Our design of the learnersourcing workflow was guided by the constructivist framework proposed by Gance (2002), which emphasizes: (1) engaging learners, (2) enabling hands-on interaction with task-related materials, 130

(3) situating learning within authentic problem-solving contexts, and (4) facilitating meaningful human

interactions throughout the learning process.

The initial workflow includes four components. First, Activate Prior Knowledge connects learners to existing course structures (e.g., lectures, labs). Second, Contextualize Computing Concepts  prompts students to propose scenarios rooted in their personal interests. Third,  Construct Worked Examples guides students to create step-by-step examples based on those scenarios. Finally,  Collaborative Assessment  fosters peer and self-evaluation, aligning with the constructivist emphasis on social learning. Preliminary study and key leverage points We implemented the workflow as bonus point opportunities within an online quiz and an assignment. Students were asked to design two practice questions, one easy and one medium, for classmates to practice PL/SQL functions and procedures. Students were required to outline relevant scenarios and database schemas and provide solutions with self-assessments. Each submitted WE underwent peer evaluations and independent instructor assessments using a rubric evaluating Difficulty, Clarity, Topi c Relevance, and Educational V alue. From a preliminary study, we collected 23 WEs and 100 peer reviews. Detailed elaboration and results are presented in another paper (Li et al., 2025).

Leveraging points for creating worked examples The majority of students successfully created WEs relevant to course topics. Some problem statements did not provide a clear task regarding what should be done. Another challenge is the lack of details  about the contexts, including the example  data. In addition, many students did not come up with new contexts but rather reused or adapted the instructor's examples. The  quality of the solutions  created by students also varied by levels of detail and clarity. Comments from peer assessments also confirmed that the above-mentioned challenges would be confusing when engaging with WEs created by their peers.

## Leveraging points and implications for peer assessment

We compared the self, peer, and instructor ratings using the Friedman test  (Friedman, 1937). Results indicated statistically significant differences in Difficulty, Clarity, and Relevance ratings (p  < 0.001). However, calculated effect sizes using Kendall’s W were negligible (w < 0.1), suggesting the practical differences between groups’ ratings were minimal despite statistical significance. Inter-rater agreements among peer reviewers were low, potentially due to varied interpretations of the evaluation criteria. Nonetheless, aggregating peer ratings across multiple reviewers provided sufficient reliability when compared with instructor ratings. Revised workflow Drawing on the lessons learned from the preliminary study, we improved the instructional design and assessment rubrics into the final design of the learnersourcing workflow (See Figure 1). The workflow is implemented within homework assignments.

Activate prior knowledge The first task (pink arrow in Figure 1) is to answer two review questions, revisiting the key concept (Step 1.1). After that, a WE created by previous students in the preliminary study is presented as an example (with permission, Step 1.2). The opportunity to be featured in future classes also served as an extra source of motivation. Contextualize computing concepts and construct worked examples The second task (blue arrow in Figure 1) is to create the WE through five sub-steps. The first sub-step is to identify a database problem from the students' lived experience, where PL/SQL can provide a solution. In this sub-step, students are asked to explain the problem context in a way that is understandable to someone unfamiliar with the topic (Step 2.1). Next, students are tasked to define the problem formally and specify the expected answer (Step 2.2). The third sub-step asks students to provide the data needed to solve the problem, with a minimum of two data tables, each with at least five attributes and 100 rows of dummy data (Step 2.3). The final two sub-steps ask students to provide their solutions in a step-by-step manner, with at least three steps, each accompanied by a clearly labeled subgoal and explanation (Steps 2.4 and 2.5). Collaborative assessment The third task (yellow arrow in Figure 1) is to evaluate the student-generated worked examples. This task served both as a quality control mechanism and as an additional learning opportunity. By reviewing peers’ worked examples, students engaged in evaluating different problem contexts and solution strategies, which can support 131 deeper conceptual understanding through explanation, comparison, and reflection. In addition, this process exposed students to how their peers approached the same programming concepts, allowing them to observe different problem-solving mindsets and ways of connecting abstract database concepts to real-world contexts. Based on the pilot study results, we expanded the assessment rubrics to nine dimensions to define more specific and clear evaluation criteria (See Table 1). We also switched to a 7-step Likert scale, with 1 indicating Not at all, 7 for Very much, and 4 as neutral.

## Procedure

We deployed the learnersourcing workflow on all three course themes in the form of homework assignments. Homework 1 focused on PL/SQL blocks, Homework 2 focused on functions and procedures, and Homework 3 focused on triggers.  Given that these homework assignments represent a novel learning activity still in its developmental stages, their grading was intentionally weighted relatively low (each homework is 4% of the final grade), with an emphasis placed on effort and engagement. The homework assignments were released one week after the concepts were introduced in the lecture and practiced in the lab sessions. Students had two weeks to complete and submit each homework. Homework instructions are described in Table 2.

Table 2 Structure of The Homework Instruction and Corresponding Tasks Part Task Description A Task 1.1 Answer two review questions related to the course topic. B Task 1.2 Review an example contextualized worked example (WE). C Task 2.1 – 2.5 Follow the steps shown in the example to construct their own WE on the topic. D Task 3.1 Self-assess the quality of the WE developed. The homework has four parts (first column). The task numbers in the second column are aligned with the step numbers in Figure 1.

Students were asked to submit their answers to parts A and C of the instruction document within a single text file. The self-assessment (Part D) was administered through Circuit, an online peer-review platform developed at Purdue University that supports anonymous and asynchronous peer assessment, using 7-point Likertscale questions for each metric listed in Table 1. After the due date of submitting the WE, the Circuit tool randomly assigned three students to review each WE anonymously (Task 3.2 in Figure 1). Each peer review includes the same questions in self-assessment, as well as an optional free-text comment.

## Data analysis

To identify themes in student-selected contexts, each author independently analyzed 10 randomly selected WEs from each homework. They then compared and synthesized their findings, resolving any disagreements. One author used the agreed-upon coding scheme to analyze the remaining WEs, and another reviewed a new random sample of 10 WEs. This process was repeated until nonew disagreements arose. To assess context integration, one author annotated justifications for the problem's necessity beyond a generic " given certain inputs, produce specified outputs. " This involved noting whether the  WEs mentioned reasons for solving the problem, such as addressing a business question or achieving a personal goal. Another author randomly selected 10 WEs from each homework to identify disagreements, repeating the process until nonew disagreements arose.

In this work, we designed and evaluated a learnersourcing workflow to involve students in co-creating relatable learning materials. This work opens up exciting possibilities for leveraging the learnersourcing approach in the empirical classroom settings, with implications for both teaching and system design to support learners. Eliciting diverse and relatable contexts from students The revised learnersourcing workflow guided the students to produce WEs that are not only contextualized but also highly relevant to the specified course topic. The workflow successfully elicited more varied contexts, moving beyond the instructor’s examples and reflecting diverse student interests. The majority of the WEs from the final evaluation were reasonably contextualized and meaningfully integrated the context with the computing concepts.

This study introduces a compelling avenue for instructors navigating the intricate task of tailoring learning materials for diverse students, which often requires an in-depth understanding of each student's unique students can contribute to enriching the variety of contexts within learning materials.  Over time, this process can build a repository of contextually rich examples that instructors can curate and reuse in future course offerings. Such repositories may help instructors continuously update course materials while maintaining relevance to students’ evolving interests.

Learnersourcing as an artifact to implement social learning theories Grounded in constructivist principles, the learnersourcing workflow transforms students from passive recipients to active agents in knowledge creation, merging computational concepts with their real-life experiences. The WEs demonstrate this blend, with students applying theoretical knowledge to varied real-world contexts. In addition, 135 our results resonate with the principles of the protégé effect  (Chase et al., 2009; Roscoe & Chi, 2007), which posits that students deepen their own understanding and skills by teaching their peers. This is manifested in the high-quality WEs as well as the active participation and enthusiastic peer assessment comments. The learnersourcing approach also has the potential to shift the classroom dynamics. Empowering students to contribute content and insights alters the traditional power dynamic, positioning students not just as learners but as active participants and contributors in the educational process. For instructors, the contexts created by students, along with any errors in the creation and assessment phases, provide deep insights into students' learning progress and interests. Student-created materials provide indirect feedback on instructional efficacy, suggesting areas where students may struggle or lack interest, thus informing necessary pedagogical refinements. Although this study focuses on database programming, the learnersourcing workflow is not specific to PL/SQL. The core design principles, eliciting students’ lived experiences, structuring  the creation of worked examples, and integrating peer evaluation, can be adapted to other computing domains  that involve problem solving and conceptual transfer.  Instructors can modify the scaffolding prompts and evaluation criteria to align with different technical topics while preserving the overall workflow structure. Associating educational values to quality control of learnersourced materials Our preliminary study reveals several quality control challenges, and the revised workflow demonstrated effective mitigation of those challenges. Similar to crowdsourcing, quality control of learnersourced materials in our studies primarily relied on scaff olding instructions and self-assessment. Step-by-step guidance to describe the problem context and develop worked examples effectively assisted students in contemplating and explaining their contexts. The nine reviewing criteria served as additional guidance for students to understand the learnersourcing tasks and expectations on the contextualized worked examples to create. In addition, the self and peer assessment can be regarded as an additional learnersourcing process embedded in the workflow. As students worked on and reviewed their peers' WEs, they not only practiced applying computational concepts in various real-world contexts but also gained insights into how their classmates structured solutions, thus broadening their understanding of applying computing concepts tonew situations. Our learnersourcing workflow has great potential to be combined with prior successful learnersourcing methods (Glassman et al., 2016;  Guo et al., 2020) to elicit richer peer feedback, such as hints and debugging suggestions on the WE solutions.

## Limitations and future works

Our evaluation assessed the effectiveness of a learnersourcing workflow in eliciting diverse and relatable contexts from students. While promising, future research is needed to examine students' perceptions, workload concerns (Kyndt & Lydwin Bulckens, 2014),  and engagement, as well as use controlled studies to formally quantify learning outcomes. Given variations in students' cultural, social, and academic backgrounds, long-term evaluations are needed to understand how these demographic factors influence the workflow’s effectiveness across different course levels and subjects. Finally, despite students' valuable contributions, not all WEs are ready for direct integration into course materials. More rigorous quality assurance to address errors, formatting, and solution accuracy is required before student-generated WEs become standard course resources. This paper introduces and evaluates a learnersourcing workflow that empowers students to create Worked Examples (WEs) based on their experiences and interests. Our evaluation showed that students produced highquality, relatable learning materials across various real-world contexts. The learnersourcing approach leverages students' inherent expertise in their own backgrounds and interests to co-create relatable learning materials. In addition, peer assessment can serve as a reliable quality assurance mechanism for learnersourced materials. While most WEs were clear and valuable, maintaining consistent quality is important.
