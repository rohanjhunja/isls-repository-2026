# “You're too cool for that”: Analyzing Kosova Youth Learning Opportunities in/through Discourses of Adolescence

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This ethnographic study examines Kosova youth participation and learning experiences in a community-based empowerment program through a sociocultural learning and critical youth study lens. The initial findings demonstrate how adolescent participants resist deficit discourses of Kosova youth that position them as “functionally illiterate,” how youth club leaders both reify and expand notions adolescence/ts, and how ideas of adolescence/ts simultaneously constrain and enable opportunities for participation and learning.

## Introduction

There is an ever-growing body of research illuminating youth onto-epistemologies, literacies, and activism in many contexts around the world (Mirra et al, 2015; Vogel, et. al., 2022), however, there remains littl e to no exploration of Kosova youth and their ways of being and doing in the world. Kosova and its youth, including the wider region of western Balkans, have been marked by the historical trauma of a war-torn region and a struggling socioeconomic status (Malcolm, 1999; World Bank, 20 18). Currently, Kosova adolescents are being framed as “70% functionally illiterate” in public discourse b y government officials and local media. These deficit notions come as a result of high school age students’ perfo rmance on the international standardized test, PISA, which conceptualizes literacy in quite rigid ways—print-based modalities and written word skills. This paper speaks back to these dominant, diminishe d discourses by illuminating a group of Kosova youth’s knowledges, expansive language and literacy practices that go unrecognized by standardized print-based literacy tests.  Moreover, this study seeks to understand how adoles cence/ts discourses shape (afford and/or constrain) youth participation and learning in a yo uth empowerment program, and how discourse of youth  and age are taken up by the leaders of the program.  We seek answers to these questions as educational r esearch thus far has contributed significantly to uplifting marg inalized voices at the intersection of learning sci ences and critical race theory, disability studies, queer the ory, etc., (Esmonde & Booker,  2016). However, critical youth study (CYS) perspectives have largely been absent in  the literature. Thus, theoretically, this study aims to contribute to narrowing this gap by situating youth  as a social category and interrogating how systems  of reasoning such as age, adults, adolescence/ts shape youth participation in learning contexts.

## Theoretical framework

Theoretically, this study is situated at the inters ection of sociocultural critical learning theory and critical youth studies (CYS).

Sociocultural critical learning theory posits that learning is relational,  emerging through the various contexts we participate in (Vygotsky, 1978). By participating in various contexts (families, community, etc.) we learn the cultural practices of the context, its va lues, how people identify, and make meaning. However, participation and learning are constrained due to issues of power permeating contexts and relations (Esmonde & Booker, 2016).

On the other hand, we draw on CYS as a way to criti cally interrogate the construction of youth, adolescents, and age, and the discourses authoring them (Lesko, 2012). In order to better interrogate power issues pertaining to learning opportunities for adolescence/ts, we draw on CYS in order to illuminate how discourses of youth shape learning opportunities. Furthermore, CYS helps us to situate youth as a social category and examine how discourses of adolescence/ts are taken up by Lumi (all names are pseudonyms), and the participation patterns and practices afforded and/or constrained by Lumi. It is through this lens, an intersection of sociocultural critical learning theory and critical youth studies that we examine the intricacies of how learning is happening at Lumi.

## Methods, data, analysis

This paper reports the initial findings of a qualit ative study focused on a group of 10 Kosova youth a s part of their engagement in a youth empowerment program “Lu mi.” The data for this study was collected through an ethnographic approach throughout May and June 2022 in an urban area in southeast Kosova. Data sources include observational notes of youth a ctivities and informal talks with youth participants and group leaders, memos, pictures of the spaces, literacy artifacts produced and disseminated by the group, and three semi-structured interviews with the young participants. Data was analyzed through an inductive open coding approach whereby the authors generated codes to ref lect the youth’s emic perspectives on public/popula r discourses of youth, their participation, learning, schooling, language and literacy practices. After the codes were collapsed, the following themes were revealed: 1) youth participants resist deficit perspectives of adolescence; 2) leaders both reify and expand notions of adolescenc e; 3) ideas of adolescence hinder potential opportu nities for participation as well as create new ones.

## Findings

The First set of findings demonstrates how youth participants  resist deficit perspectives of adolescence. Lumi context facilitated youth’s critiques and theorizing of sch ooling, teacher, pedagogy, and the very purpose of education. For example, the youth pointed out how at schools “you had to understand the teacher but the teacher does not have to understand you as students. At LUMI it was different, the leaders looked how we are as students and then to get together with us.” Moreover, the yo uth participants highlighted how through their coll aborative activities with local organizations they were able to, as one says, “contribute something valuable to their own city and community,” which they assert was not the case at/through their public schools. The Second set of findings highlights how the leaders of the y outh club both reify and expand notions of adolescence. While the leaders at Lumi facilitated youth expansive language and literacy practices, disrupting learner and teaching identities, and the very purpose of education, the leaders of the youth club also reified deficit adolescent perspectives in their activities with yo uth. For instance, in a discussion around the three  Rs (Reduce, Reuse, & Recycle) of environmental protection, participants elaborated concretely on how they reuse old clothes. However, the leaders dismissed them by stating that teenagers are “too cool” and “too embarrassed” to reuse someone else’s clothes. In this way, while leaders provided youth  space and opportunities to showcase their expansive ways of being and doing, deficit notions of adolescents were still reified by the leaders. The Third set of findings point out how ideas of adolescence hinder potential opportunities for participation as well as create new ones. The very premise (idea) of Lumi is built around providing youth the opportunity to participate in community-based activities for social change. In this way, ideas of  adolescence afford youth opportunities to participate and learn by providing them with resources that fac ilitate opportunities for change in their own communities  and beyond, but at the same time, hinder potential opportunities for participation such as the three Rs example illustrates. The overall finding of the study demonstrates that discourses o f adolescence simultaneously constrain and enable opportunities for participation and lear ning. While participation in community-based learning opportunities are made possible by the youth empowe rment organization, deficit discourses of adolescen ce/ts also function to disempower them.

## Educational significance

Through a sociocultural and CYS lens, we were able to illuminate how youth empo werment programs can reify deficit notions of youth and adolescence/ts and at the same time provide opportunities for particip ation and learning. Moreover, the findings point out that discourse s of adolescence/ts permeate  learning context, shape participant and leaders power relations and with th at participation and learning opportunities. Furthe rmore, our study leads us to question the very notion of ‘yout h empowerment’ by asking why youth are disempowered  in the first place, by whom, when, and under what circumstances.
