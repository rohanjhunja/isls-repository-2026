# Applying Sequence Mining to Explore Students’ Problem-Solving Practices Using an Interactive Simulation-Based Task

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Extensive research has been done to capture and understand human problem solving across different research methods and traditions. This study examines how interaction data generated in an open-ended, interactive task could be used to identify patterns of effective and ineffective practices for solving problems in science and engineering domains. We collected data from 80 undergraduate students on their solutions and problem-solving processes as interaction data logged in the task environment. The log data was parsed to reveal the sequence of test trials conducted by individual participants when solving the problem as well as the pauses in-between test trials. Results of the clustering analysis showed that distinct trial sequence patterns were associated with different levels of performance and provided evidence for specific problem-solving practices. These results have implications for how to assess a complex construct like problem solving and how real-time feedback could be provided to improve students’ problem-solving practices.

## Introduction

Problem-solving situations arise as scientists investigate and build models and theories about the phe nomena in the natural world and engineers design and build sy stems (NGSS, 2013). Authentic problems like these need to be incorporated into science and engineering classrooms to help students develop expertise in systematic practices of scientific inquiry and problem solving (Krajcik,  2015). One efficient and cost-effective way of eng aging students in authentic problem solving is through technology-based, open-ended learning environments, such as interactive simulations. In this paper, we employ a n interactive task embedded in a physics simulation  to elicit undergraduate students’ problem-solving performance. Furthermore, we explore how different patterns of students’ interactions in the task environment as r ecorded by the log data are associated with effecti ve and ineffective problem-solving practices.

## Background

Student-centered, open-ended learning environments (OELEs) provide opportunities for students to engag e in authentic scientific inquiry and problem solving (H annafin et al., 1994; Land & Jonassen, 2012). In co ntrast to traditional end-of-chapter exercises in science textbooks, interactive tasks embedded in OELEs can be designed to better mimic authentic problems by situating the task in an engaging, real-world context and providing minimal data upfront. One example of OELEs is educational simulation, where students explore scientific phenomena and solve problems in an authentic, safe, and cost-effe ctive manner (Wieman, Adams, & Perkins, 2008). The interactive task environments uniquely allow students to decide what information they need to solve the problem, how to collect that information, and what conclusions they candraw from it. These decisions are integral parts of the practices adopted by scientists and engineers to solve problems encountered in their work (Price et al., 2021). In the process of students working on the problem e mbedded in OELEs, large volumes of log data are generated in the backend of the task environments. How to analyze the log data and extract insights about students’ cognitive and metacognitive processes is an evolving area of inquiry, especially in the fields of learning analytics and educational data mining (Fischer et al., 2020). Approaches and techniques adopted to process and model log data in previous research include directly hand cod ing log data to create a training set for classifyi ng students’ inquiry skills (Gobert et al., 2013), engineering features based on raw data and using them to identify patterns of productive actions in a circuit simulation (Fratami co et al., 2017), and conducting sequential pattern  mining analysis to group students based on their behavior sequences (Eichmann et al., 2020). One promising ap proach for feature extraction is to incorporate human knowledge gained through domain expertise or direct observation of students solving the task into log data processi ng, creating a set of semantically meaningful featu res. These features can then be used as inputs to either supervised machine learning algorithms to predict performance, or to unsupervised algorithms to identify differentiating  patterns among students. In this paper, we applied  such approach to analyze the log data generated in an in teractive problem-solving task in a physics simulat ion. Our goal is to identify patterns of students’ actions that reflect specific problem-solving practices and understand how these patterns are associated with different levels of problem-solving performance. ## Methods

The Mystery Weight problem embedded in the PhET Bal ancing Act simulation was used in this study (phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/balancing-act). Students were first introduced to a scenario of needing to measure the weight of a gift to be mailed without access to a weighing scale. They were then presented with the task of figuring out the weight of the gift using a seesaw and a few bricks of known weights in the Balancing Act simulation. Figure 1 presents the user interface of the task. Students can place bricks and the gift at various marked locations on the beam in the “Setup” mode and observe the outcome of the trial (i.e., how the beam would rotate or stay balanced) in the “Test” mode. The simulatio n does not allow bricks to be stacked on top of eac h other, which makes the problem less intuitive and more dif ficult. Furthermore, the weight of the gift was del iberately chosen to be unsolvable using a single brick. Balancing the beam thus requires a combination of different weights placed at different locations.

Figure 1 The Mystery Weight problem with the gift randomly placed on the beam 80 undergraduate students in STEM majors across the US (47.5% female) were recruited via an online research crowdsourcing platform, Prolific (www.prolific.co). Students received $4 for participating in the 30-min online study and up to $4 additional bonus for correctly solving the problems. During the study, participants first worked on a Mystery Weight problem and were then randomly selected to view one of the two versions of worked examples demonstrating how to solve the problem. Pa rticipants worked on a second Mystery Weight proble m afterwards. In this paper, we focus our analysis on  participants’ performance in the first problem pri or to any interventions.

Data collected to capture participants’ problem-sol ving performance include 1) their solutions for the mystery weight (the gift’s weight in kg); 2) their responses to a post-task question probing whether t he torque formula was used when solving the problem; 3) log d ata recording participants’ actions within the task environment in JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) files as they worked on the problem. The log data was parsed using a Python script to reveal a sequence of test trials conducted by individual participants and the pause time after each test trial. The TraMineR R package (Gaba dinho et al., 2011) was used to visualize and analy ze the sequential data. In the following section, we first  discuss participants’ overall problem-solving outc omes as measured by their solutions to the problem. We then present the features extracted from the log data and results of a cluster analysis on the sequential features.

## Results

Problem-solving outcome Participants’ problem-solving outcomes were determined by their solutions for the weight of the gift. We coded the correct solution and near-correct solutions (17-19kg) both as “correct” and solutions outside the range (< 17kg or > 19kg) as “incorrect.” Four participants were excluded from data analysis due to extremely low time-on-task (< 1 min). Among the remaining 76 participants, 45%  reached a correct or near-correct solution while 5 5% did not.

Once they set up a trial, they can toggle to “Test” mode to observe how the beam might tilt based on the weights and positions of the objects. A test trial is thus characterized by the number and type of objects used (both the gift with unknown weight and bricks with known weights) as well as their positions on the beam. Guided by qualitative observation of students solving the problem in a pilot study, we focused on the test trials instead of discrete actions and abstracted sequences of low-level actions into test trials conducted by individual participants, a long with timestamps recording when the trials took place during their problem-solving processes. Participants were instructed to solve the mystery w eight in 15 test trials or less and stop working af ter reaching 15 trials. One exceptional participant conducted 106 test trials in 26 minutes. The mean number of trials conducted excluding this participant was 17, with a minimum of 4 trials and a maximum of 46 trials.

Figure 2 Two sample test trials conducted when solving the mystery weight problem; the left trial used two weights and the right trial used six weights Pause after a test trial While the number and nature of test trials conducted represent how participants actively went about solving the problem, the pause times, or periods of inactivity in the simulation after each test trial, potentiall y constitute an important signal on how participants managed their problem-solving processes. Based on our previous research (Wang et al., 2021), we define a meaningful pause as one where the time between the action of toggling to “Test” and the next action recorded in the simulation bein g longer than 10 seconds. This definition allows us  to differentiate between longer, meaningful pauses ind icative of underlying cognitive processes and short er, mechanical pauses that are approximately the time required to view the outcome (i.e., how the beam will tilt) of the test trial. A few extremely long pauses (> 2 mins) were excluded from the analysis as they were likely incurred due to off-task behaviors. Among all participants, six did not have any meaningful pauses throughout the problemsolving process. The number of meaningful pauses range from one to eleven for the remaining 70 participants. Qualitative interviews of students’ working through the mystery weight problem in our previous research revealed that the underlying cognitive processes during a meaningful pause may include 1) pause to und erstand the nature of the problem and devise a solution plan; 2) pause to record and interpret the data observed from the trial using relevant physics knowledge; and 3) paus e to evaluate and revise their strategies after uns uccessful attempts to solve the problem.

## Clusters of participants’ test trial sequences

As shown in Figure 3, a test trial sequence is characterized by its length (no. of trials conducted), simplicity (no. of weights used in each trial), and pace (whether the trial was followed by a meaningful pause). Given the large variance in the number of trials conducted by individual participants, we truncated the sequences of all participants to the first 15 test trials to be used as inputs to cluster analysis. This decision ensures that the sequence length will not be the dominant factor for clustering and allow s us to explore how we might be able to efficiently  identify different problem-solving profiles with only a portion of students’ interaction data. A matrix of pairwise distance between individual participants’ test trial sequences was computed using an Optimal Matching algorithm. The Ward hierarchica l clustering method was then applied to cluster the sequences into distinct groups based on the distance matrix (Murtagh & Legendre, 2014). The average silhouette width was used to select the appropriate number of clusters (Rousseeuw, 1987). ## Discussion & Conclusion

Despite the recognition that students need to learn science and engineering practices useful for solving authentic problems, valid and efficient assessments of these practices remain scarce (Quellmalz et al., 2013). Researchers in the learning analytics and educational data mining communities have explored how to model student abilities and knowledge states using large volumes of interaction data generated in open-ended learning environments. In this study, we extend the research efforts and seek to understand how the log data of students solving a problem in a physics simulation could be used to capture and evaluate specific problem-solving practices of co llege students in science and engineering domains.

The results indicate that there exist distinct patterns of test trials conducted by effective and ineffective problem solvers. Effective problem solvers set up s imple trials and paused frequently after conducting  a trial, while ineffective problem solvers either switched to increasingly complex trials by using more and more weights or kept the trial set-up simple yet quickly moved the weights around many different positions. There were minimal pauses in-between test trials among both clusters of ineffective problem solvers. These differentiated interaction patterns were closely related to how well participa nts adopted specific problem-solving practices, inc luding collecting data, recording data, interpreting data using domain knowledge, and reflecting on their progress towards a solution. Specifically, the difference in pause p atterns provided microscopic behavioral evidence fo r how effective and ineffective problem solvers differed in their monitoring and management of problem-solvi ng processes as noted in Schoenfeld (1981). While effe ctive problem solvers were deliberate in considerin g and accommodating new information gained from each test  trial, ineffective problem solvers largely resorte d to unstructured exploration.

The finding that the absence of meaningful pauses d uring problem solving is associated with poor performance is consistent with previous research th at used OELE-based tasks to study students' higher order thinking skills. For example, Amerish and Conati (2009) found that low-performing students paused significantly shorter on average than high-performing students when interacting with an OELE presenting artificial intelligence algorithms. Perez et al. (2017) reported that a str ategic use of pauses during an inquiry activity in a circuit simulation is associated with productive learning o utcomes. Wang et al. (2021) found that the occurren ce of meaningful pauses can predict problem-solving perfo rmance above and beyond students' domain knowledge. Instances of pause during students' work process ma y foreground key cognitive and metacognitive proces ses involved in problem solving and inquiry and should be explored more fully in future research. This study has important implications for how we assess students’ proficiency in science and engineering practices, as well as for how to provide real-time feedback to help students improve their problem-sol ving practices in simulation-based tasks. As shown by the results of the cluster analysis, differentiating patterns already emerged in the first few minutes of students workin g on the task. Understanding which pattern a studen t’s sequence of operation falls into would allow us to support and guide them with targeted feedback, potentially in real-time in the task environment. This would afford students the opportunity to iteratively adjust their problemICLS2022 Proceedings 438 © ISLS solving approaches and acquire expert-like thinking and practices. In future studies, we will also explore whether students’ problem-solving practices as captured by the brief simulation-based task are connected to their broader academic performance.
