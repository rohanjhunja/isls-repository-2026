# Investigating Cultural Signatures in Game Designs of Middle School Students from Argentina, India, and the United States

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Cultural signatures can offer insights into the ways that learners draw on their cultural backgrounds and experiences as they engage within learning environments. Our work aims to understand how learners within game design learning contexts use cultural signatures to express their cultural identities within game elements. We analyzed game designs from students in Argentina, India, and the United States and described how cultural signatures reflect specific cultural contexts and connect to game elements and mechanics. Our work highlights the value of creating computing learning experiences that support and engage learners’ rich cultural identities, particularly within game design-based learning environments.

## Introduction

As computing increasingly becomes integrated into K-12 education (Kafai & Proctor, 2021) and with the diversity of cultural backgrounds and identities among young learners, there is a growing imperative to foster culturall y sustaining and responsive computing education learning environments. Culturally-responsive education respects, values, and incorporates learners’ diverse cultural backgrounds, experiences, and perspectives to create inclusive and empowering learning environments (Gay, 2015; La dson-Billings, 1995). Within culturally-responsive learning experiences, cultural signatures—actions, behaviors, belief systems that shape cultural norms, practices, and perspectives—deeply inform the ways that students engage with and interpret educational content, shaping their learning journeys and interpretations of the world within the tapestry of their own cultural contexts (Bednar et al., 2010). In this paper, we identify and describe the cultural signatures within the game design products created by middle school students from three countries: Arg entina, India, and the United States. Stude nts participated in activities where they designed games by creating na rratives, drawings, and other game play mechanics t hat illustrate cultural objects, practices, and context s, which students drew from for their game designs.  Examining these can offer i nsights into how students’ cultural identities shap e their creative expression in game design contexts, which can inform the design of future cul turally-responsive pedagogies that use game-based learning technologies. Our work is thus guided by the follow ing research question: How do middle school students from Argentina, India, and the US incorporate cultural signatures in their game design products?

## Background and related work

Culturally-relevant pedagogy and cultural signatures Pusch (1979) defines culture as “the sum total of w ays of living, including values, beliefs, aesthetic  standards, linguistic expressions, patterns of thinking, behav ioral norms, and styles of communication which a gr oup of people has developed to ensure its survival in a sp ecific physical and human environment.” This highli ghts the intricate interplay of various cultural dimensions in shaping human experiences and emphasizes the cri tical role of multicultural perspectives in communication and educational practices. This underpins the need for culturallyrelevant learning experiences that empower students  by connecting their education to their real-life experiences (Ladson-Billings, 1995). Within these learning experiences,  cultural signatures (Bednar et al.,  2010) can help us explore and understand the ways in which students’ cultural identities shape their learning through th eir interpretations of the world and their environments, and their interactions with their peers. Furtherm ore, this understanding can help us meaningfully reflect on t he ways that our pedagogical approaches and designs  for learning environments and technologies can enable and empower students from diverse cultural identities. Game design for learning Educational game design has gained traction as a po werful method for engaging learners in creative com puting (Kafai & Burke, 2015) and developing their problem-solving, computational, and critical thinking skills (Plass et al., 2015). In computing education, prior work has shown that game design can improve learners’ unders tanding of programming concepts compared to just learning t o write code (Basawapatna et al., 2011). Within our  work, middle school students designed educational math games by brainstorming and creating narratives, drawings, and representations of game rules and mechanics on pape r booklets, which they later implement on a visual programming platform. These narratives, drawings, a nd symbolic representations reflect cultural signat ures that communicate ideas, concepts, and practices drawn fr om their own cultural backgrounds and identities. T hese cultural signatures become part of th eir ideation of game elements such as player roles,  the use of their physical environments as a game-playing space, use of game props, and game activities (e.g., throwing a ball, solving math problems). In this way, game design becomes an enga ging avenue for personal and creative expression that also makes space for cultural expression. Our work explo res the affordances of game design-based learning environments in empowering learners to engage in cr eative computing activities that also enables them to meaningfully express their cultural and lived identities within their creations.

## Study context

We ran learning activities with middle school stude nts in schools in Argentina, India, and the United States. The learning activities were based on the six-stage WearableLearning (WL) curriculum (Castro, 2022), which engaged students in designing educational math games starti ng from a paper-based design to its programmatic implementation in the WearableLearning platform (Castro, 2022). In summary, the curriculum  stages are as follows: (1) play a pre-made game on WL, (2) collaboratively design a game on paper, (3) use finite-state machine diagrams to represent the game design, (4) program the game on the WL platform, (5) play-test the game, and (6) iterate on the game design to address issues. Each curriculum stage corresponds roughly to a 60 to 90 minute learning activity, spread across several days. Our work focuses on Stage 2 of the curriculum, where students, in small groups, brainstormed math game ideas using pa per booklets where they drew sketches, described ga me elements, and wrote game narratives (samples in Fig ure 1). The learning activities for Argentina and I ndia were implemented as in-class activities; the United States ones in after-school programs.

## Participants, data, and analysis

We collected data from a total of 257 students across Argentina (153 students), India (43 students), and the United States (61 students). The paper booklets with the s tudents’ game designs is our primary data source, w hich we analyzed to identify cultural signatures that students used within their designs. The student groups created a total of 68 game designs (40 from Argentina, 13 from Indi a, and 15 from the United States). We focused our analyses on two game designs from each country, using conven ience sampling to select games with multiple instances of symbols, narratives, and drawings that reflected cultural signatures. We analyzed the cultural signatures in the six student-designed games using Pusch’s (1979) multicultural education framework as an initial coding scheme. The original framework identifies eight cultural dimens ions: values, beliefs, behavioral norms, aesthetic standards, linguistic expressions, patterns of thinking, communication styles, and ways of living. The first author deductively coded the data using this framework. We then discussed the coding to consider our interpretations of the data and resolve conflicts or misalignments in interpretatio n. We found considerable overlaps between the origi nal categories and thus consolidated the codes and their def initions into three overarching themes: (1) Values and Beliefs, (2) Aesthetic Elements, and (3) Communication Styles. ## Results and discussion

In designing their games, students created narrativ es, drawings, and other gameplay mechanics. These d esign elements reflect cultural signatures that illustrate cultural objects, practices, and contexts that students drew from. Values and beliefs Students created game elements that reflected cultu ral values, beliefs, and meaningful traditions. The  “Om” (Figure 1.e), an important Indian cultural and reli gious symbol (Madhavi et al., 2005), was drawn repe atedly within the Indian students’ game design booklets, suggesting its significance within their belief systems. Cricket, a nationally beloved sport in India, was also a recurring theme in the Indian students’ game designs, using cricket elements within the core game mechanic and scoring system (Figu re 1.d) —teams compete to gain points by striking cricket balls through hoops, and lose poin ts when they miss. Similarly, Argentinian students’  design for the N2G3 game was inspired by soccer, a sport with a rich hi story in the country (Noguera, 1986). The multi-player, multi-team game challenges players to solve math square r oot problems (Figure 1.b), which determine their movements on the soccer field; the multi-team design of the game also echoes the team-based mechanics of soccer.

Aesthetic elements Students used visual elements such as drawings of people, places, and things, character representations, and other symbolic designs or patterns in their games to express cultural and creative ideas or influences such as ones from digital trends. Figure 1.c shows a game created by Indian students about calcu lating long distances that featured drawings of trains, train stations, and players as passengers. In an informal conversation between the  first author and the students, the students revealed that they drew inspiration for their game from their own daily commute to school where they used trains, showing how students  encode their lived experiences and local contexts into their game designs.

Communication styles We found that the La Mosqueteros game from the US students (Figure 1.a) extensively used emojis (e.g., hearts and mushroom emojis), which may suggest students’ p revalent exposure to, or engagement with, popular digital media. In our examination of the other game design booklets from US students (beyond the ones we analy zed specifically for this paper), we also noticed repea ted use of emojis. In comparison, the game designs of students from Argentina and India featured a markedly less f requent use of emojis.  We are unsure if the usage of emojis in the game designs are integral to the game play m echanics, or for some other reason, such as if they  were sketched out of boredom or were doodles that studen ts drew during the learning activities. In the Hackers and Indian Boys game designs by the Indian students, the use of sports-specific language in the game (i.e., cricket bat, ball, bowler) (Figure 1.f and 1.g) demonstrates how  language from traditional sports can shape student s’ communication of game instructions within their game designs.

## Conclusion, limitations, and future work

In this work, we examined the game designs produced  by middle school students from Argentina, India, a nd the United States to identify the cultural signatures that students used in their designs and how these relate to the game elements they created within their games. Within a similar line of research, prior work by Gattupalli et al. (2022) found initial indicators of ways that learners incorporated cultural expressions in game designs. We build on this work through descriptions of how cultural signatures reflect students’ cultural contexts and backgrounds and their connections to the game elements and mechanics arti culated within students’ game designs. We also crea ted a preliminary categorization of these cultural signat ures, adapted from Pusch’s (1979) m ulticultural education framework. Our findings reinforce the value of game  design-based creative computing learning experiences that support and enable learners to also draw on their c ultural identities and personal experiences while l earning computing. Drawing from this, designers of learning  environments and technologies should intentionally  think about meaningful ways to honor and actively engage learners’ rich cultural backgrounds to create meaningful and culturally-sustaining learning experiences. Going forward, we plan to go beyond the document analysis in this work and further engage learners through focus grou ps and interviews. This will help us to better teas e out and understand learners’ intentions, thinking and design processes, and reflections on their selection and use of cultural signatures when designing games. For example, we wa nt to better clarify the intentions and motivations  around students’ drawings (e.g., emojis and doodles) and understand the meanings that learners attribute to them and how these shape the designs of the games that they create.
