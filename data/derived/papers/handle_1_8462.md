# Revisiting Revoice: An Inductive Method for Capturing Revoice in Classroom Discussions

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Within the scholarly field of academically productive classroom dialogue, several elusive, yet rich and potent discourse moves have received special attention. However, their rarity and complexity also poses significant challenges to meeting interrater reliability thresholds, and they are often omitted from quantitative research efforts. We propose a different approach for coding that circumvents these issues, called DECCA. In this presentation, we showcase this methodology by focusing on teacher revoice. We demonstrate how it is possible to deconstruct this complex phenomenon into smaller and simpler elements. We then code each turn in the corpus for the existence or absence of each element (which we term Dialogue Elements, DEs). Adding a post-coding, pre-analysis stage allows us to extract turns which contain the specific combination of DEs relevant for revoice and distinguish it from similar teacher dialogue facilitation moves. Theoretical and practical implications are discussed.

## Introduction

In academically productive dialog (APD hereafter), teachers engage their students with open ended, aut hentic questions or tasks, encourage them to share their i deas and thinking processes, to build on each other ’s contributions, to (critically) explore alternative viewpoints and to use external knowledge sources to  evaluate them (Asterhan & Schwarz, 2016; Howe & Abedin, 2013; Howe & Mercer, 2017; Kim & Wilkinson, 2019). This form of classroom dialogue has been identified as b eneficial for learning and scholars of dialogic tea ching and learning have identified the foundations and characteristics of classroom talk that promotes student learning and cognitive development (Resnick, Asterhan & Clarke, 2015; 2018). Expert teacher facilitation has been proven crucial for APD in classrooms (e.g., Pauli & Reuser, 2015; vander Pol, 2010; Webb, 2009) and research has documented how specific dialogue moves can stimulate (or stymie) intellective student engagement. One teacher facilitation move that stands out in terms of the scholarly attention it has received is “revoice”  (Michaels & O’Connor,  2015; O’Connor & Michaels, 2019; 1993; 1996). Revo ice occurs when a teacher reiterates a particular stude nt contribution in her own words, while acknowledgi ng the student’s ownership of the original contribution, e laborating on its content, and finally asking for t he same student's confirmation on her inferences. The phenomenology and affordances of revoice have been documented extensively (e.g., Alozie et al., 2009; Ferris, 2014; Fitzgerald & Palincsar, 2019; Glenn et al., 1999; Hmelo-Silver & Barrows, 2006; Kwon et al., 2008). Yet, despite t his attention in conceptual, descriptive, and quali tative research, revoice has been largely absent from quantitative research efforts on APD. A possible reason for this absence is that revoice is a collateral victim of common top-down coding practices, while adhering to strict standards of scientific measurement. Quantitative research on dialogue requires the systematic categorization of selected characteristics, that is: coding (Hennessy et al., 2020; Sha ffer & Ruis, 2020). Yet, when attempting to capture a phenomenon  such as revoice with a standalone, dedicated codin g category, coders encounter two challenges: First, “ true” revoice is not a common speech event in class room discourse (Enyedy et al., 2008; Inan, 2014). When a particular feature is rare, reliability thresholds for the coding category that intends to capture it cannot be met. Second, revoice is a high-inference construct that is a particular composite of different speech moves and can serve multiple purposes. The more multi-faceted the phenomenon is, the less likely it is for the judges to agree.

Some tools give only ambiguous information on this front (see Thurlings & den Brok's 2017 review) whereas others deal with rare and nuanced dialogic constructs by either omitting them from analysis, o r by clustering them together in more inclusive categories (Chiu, 2015). Either way, the intended aim of pr oviding a comprehensive and sensitive account of classroom dialogue, is sacrificed at the altar of interrater reliability, and analysis is limited to common and/or coarser dialogue features. Instead of searching for higher-order discourse con structs (such as revoice) in a top-down manner, we suggest adopting a bottom-up approach according to which we first code for a small and reliable set of  basic dialogue elements (DEs). The DEs were systematically identified by comparing seven renowned and well u sed coding schemes for quality of classroom discourse. This is then followed by a post-coding phase, in wh ich we search for co-occurrences of particular combination s of these elements that represent higher order, co mpound discourse constructs (such as revoice). In a recent paper (Authors, under review), we presented the reliability and validity of this approach which we call Dialogue Elements to Compound Constructs Approach (DECCA). This  approach to coding has an added benefit, since it also captures approximations of revoice, that can be of particular interest to researchers looking into emerging processes and implementation efforts of new practices. Whereas in traditional coding tools the turns that fail to meet the full requisite list of the full construct are left uncoded.

## Method

Data was collected from a pedagogical development effort aiming to encourage APD in upper elementary Hebrew Language Arts lessons. Over the course of 1.5 school years (2018-March 2020, start of the Covid 19 Pandemic) 18 classroom discussion recordings were collected f rom seven teachers (4 teachers experienced in APD a nd 3 teachers who were novices). Each transcript involve d the teacher and her students (20-34 per class) and was collected by a research team member. Students without active parental consent were seated outside of the scope of the camera and their answers were not transcribed. Four lessons were collected from the veteran teachers in the first year of the project (4) and additional two lessons from each of the seven teachers (14) were collected in the second year (18 lessons total, approx. 45 min each). The lessons, conducted in whole classroom format,  were video-and audio taped and then, transcribed (parsing on level of speech turn). Each dialogue protocol was then coded with DECCA (nine DE coding categories in all, each turn coded for the existence of one or more of the 9). Revoice deconstructed The facilitation move of Revoice can be deconstruct ed into several components (O’Connor & Michaels, 1993). The teacher gives the original speaker ownership of the content. (in DECCA, the DE Refer). Another component is 'retroactive contextualization' (Lemke, 1990): A  combination of repeating the student's contributio n (DE Repetition), while elaborating on its meaning (DE E laborate). Finally, the teacher gives the student a  chance to affirm or deny her inferences, captured with DEs In vitation and Evaluation. Interrater reliability for  these five DEs ranges from κ = 0.7 to κ = 0.85. Then, we extra cted only turns with specific combinations of these  five DECCA elements. Finally, we compared those turns to  search for similarities and differences among them  and common constructs from the APD literature.

Only seven of these were identifie d as instances of “true” Revoice, all occurring in three of the four veteran teachers' lessons. Due to shortage of space, we will only discuss one example in depth. The students and teacher had just read the story ‘Fly, eagle, fly!’ a tale about a farmer who finds an eagle nestling and raises it with the rest of his chickens, on the ground, until a friend comes and convinces the eagle to fly. The question under discussion was about the sort of change that occurred in the story. The student speaking in this example is Nicolay (all names were changed to protect the identity of our participants), for whom Hebrew is the second language, his mother tongue is Russian. The farmer, Nicolay says (to the class), and tell me if I understood you (to Nicolay), tried to change the eagle and turn him into a (chicken). In the fourth and final turn in the transcript the teacher's utterance begins with a word-by-word repetition of the student's answer, therefore, the DE Repetition will be marked with 1. Next, the coders indicate the DE Refer here because the teacher refers the idea to Nicolay by using his name (and writing it on the board next to his contribution). The DE Evaluation is present also as a standalone since the teacher gives Nicolay explicit positive evaluation (that’s great!) as well as implicit positive evaluation (by putting his response up on the whiteboard).

Throughout her response, and by inserting few key words into her repetition, the teacher manages to elaborate and elevate Nicolay's answer. For example, she adds the word 'tries' before the phrase 'changed the behavior' that Nicolay used, thereby stressing the rigorous and not necessarily successful process this entails. Finally, she invites Nicolay’s evaluation on her interpretation, asking him to correct her if he does not agree with her interpretation. Therefore, the coders will mark 1 for the Invitation DE and Evaluation DE. ## Discussion

This paper uses the construct of revoice to showcase how a slightly altered coding procedure paves way to both keep the inter-rater reliability indices at acceptable levels and still be able to capture compound and less frequent ICLS2022 Proceedings 1267 © ISLS dialogue moves. Moreover, using the same DEs we hav e been able to distinguish revoice from close approximations moves, closely related yet qualitatively different teacher moves (Repeat, Reformulate, Reframe). For the sake of simplicity, this paper has only focused on the 5 dialogue elements that in combination create the compound construct of Revoice.  DECCA currently includes only nine DEs yet is able to distinguish amon g 52 qualitatively different coding categories, taken from renowned, existing coding frameworks. One challenge that should be further explored in future research is the finding that out of the nine cases that were deductively identified with DECCA as “Rev oice”, in two of these, a teacher had acknowledged the student’s ownership of the repeated content, but sh e then evaluated it herself (instead of inviting th e student’s evaluation) and she did not elaborate on it but, in stead, invited the original speaker to do so. Altho ugh this is indeed a compound and potentially valuable teacher facilitation move, it is not a classic instance of Revoice, at least not as described in the literature. Future research on larger data sets will show whether these instances were particular to this study’s setting, and if not, whether these subtle differences will prove to be important vis-a-vis their impact on student learning and other correlates.

In any case, we believe that the advantages of the DECCA framework outweigh its limitations and hope it will prove to be an important first step toward creating a bridge between the richness and validity afforded in qualitative research methods, and consistency and reliability offered by quantitative research methods.
