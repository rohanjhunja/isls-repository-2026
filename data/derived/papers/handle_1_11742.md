# Imagining Inclusive Makerspaces Through a Creative Process with Tangible Materials and AI-Generative Arts

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The educational maker movement promotes design with innovative technologies, encouraging community collaboration. However, exclusionary settings can hinder participation. The participatory design offers opportunities for creating inclusive makerspaces, allowing young people and other community members to shape their learning environments and foster socio-emotional learning, such as respect for diversity. This study explores opportunities for co-designing inclusive makerspaces for broadening participation and fostering socio-emotional learning by combining AI-generated art and tangible tools.

## Introduction

The educational maker movement reflects a global shift toward hands-on, interactive, and design-focused learning approaches, often integrating innovative technologi es (Peppler & Bender, 2013). Makerspaces provide un ique opportunities to support Global Citi zenship Education (GCE), such as socio-emotional learning and respect for diversity, especially when they include community-focused engagement (Halverson & Peppler, 2018). However, makerspaces can unintentionally reproduce exclusion ary norms (Calabrese-Barton & Tan, 2020), for example spaces and objects contribute to the construction of racial and class-based literacies, influencing how youth learn to interpret the world (Thiel & Jones, 2017). To foster inclusivity, it is essential to rethink and reshape spaces that convey messages of domination, opening up new possi bilities for pedagogy and literacy (Thiel & Jones, 2017). This process involves recognizing youth as active creators of their own worlds, giving them opportunities to shape their learning environments (Calabrese-Barton & Tan, 2020). Therefore we asked: How can we support youth to reimagine makerspaces as a means to foster socio-emotional learning and respect for diversity? Building on research in maker education (Peppler & Bender, 2013; Halverson & Sheridan, 2014; Peppler et al., 2016; Halverson & Sawyer, 2022) and principles of socio-emotional learning from GCE, the study explores how makerspaces can be reimagined by integrating di verse community perspectives with teachers contribu ting by mapping the space and identifying challenges, re sources, and cultural elements present in the schoo l’s makerspace. Design meetings revealed insights into the diversity of makerspace resourc es, activities, cultural practices (e.g., woodworking, textiles, electronics, clay modeling, crafting, and outdoor activities),  and user groups (e.g., school classes, teachers, student aut onomous projects, and after-school programs). It also showed that activities are needed for designing inclusive makersp aces. The integration of crafting tangible materials and AI-generated art emerged as an innovative approach  to help students envision their makerspace (Keune e t al., 2024).

AI generative tools are valuable for socio-emotional learning. For example, AI has been used to promote empathy through VR simulations that allow youth to engage in perspective-taking exercises, immersing them in different viewpoints to foster a more profound sense of interconnectedness and appreciation for diversity (Slater & Sanchez-Vives, 2016; Riva et al., 2019). Both tangible and AI-generated designs will be utilized to explore inclusivity in visual representations, culminating in a gallery exhibition where feedback will be collected to refine the students' concepts. This iterative process high lights how technology and creativity can converge t o develop inclusive, community-focused learning environments that address diverse needs while enhancing socio-emotional learning.

## Methods

This study was conducted in a school-based makerspace in southern Germany, involving two educators (female, with 7 and 21 years of experience). We held meeting s with the makerspace educators to envision an educ ational program fostering student participation in shaping the space. Following these discussions, we conducte d ethnographically inspired observations within the m akerspace. This approach involved close collaborati on with teachers, gathering insights from their past experiences as a foundation for design considerations, and shadowing two classes (4 hours total). These observations all owed us to gain a deep contextual understanding of the school environment. Additionally, we conducted a video-recorded guided tour (4 hours total), documenting a ll equipment, materials, and activities. During this visit, w e carried out semi-structured interviews with educators, exploring cultural forms and arts, materials, practices, and learning processes. These interviews also  provided insights into the needs and interests of 11th-grade students, as well as the challenges faced within the makerspace. Further data collection included 33 high-quality photographs that captured resources, materi als, and accessibility. This visual data facilitated an analysis of the competing interests and aspirations tied to the school’s creative space. In addition, we mapped the material s present and examined their associated cultural fo rms, drawing on UNESCO’s (2009) framework. This mapping process provided foundational insights into the cultural and material landscape, informing strategies to des ign more inclusive learning environments within the  school setting.

Reflection notes and observations from regular co-design meetings (10 hours total) with the educators were also documented. These meetings played a cruci al role in shaping a vision for reimagining learnin g spaces in ways that foster socio-emotional growth and respect for diversity.

## Findings

The semi-structured conversations revealed that the makerspa ce serves as a hub for multiple communities, each bringing diverse perspectives that must be consider ed in its design and use. For instance, the makersp ace accommodates scheduled classes led by teachers, stu dent-initiated projects as part of the coursework, and a fterschool programs. Moreover, students’ parents volunteer during courses, enhancing the sense of community within the space. School administrators also used the spac e, especially to showcase it as a model of innovati ve technologies. Educators highlighted diverse perspec tives and uses for the makerspace. This variety of activities underscores the importance of a thoughtful redesign  of the makerspace to reflect on the perspectives t hat come together in the space. Such a redesign is essential  in enhancing student engagement, broadening partic ipation, and fostering socio-emotional learning.

The makerspace was equipped with a wide array of ma terials, as well as high-and low-tech tools, that can be grouped into cultural forms such as woodwork ing, textiles, electronics, clay modeling, crafting, bicycle repairing, gardening, E-sports, ceramics, photography, LEGO Robotics, podcast journalism, and  collaborative building projects. The diversity of cultural forms in the makerspace provides an opportunity to envisi on a space for learning that considers multiple perspectives. Each cultural form attract s distinct learning communities, and integrating these can foster inclusivity and creati vity in reimagining the makerspace. Examples of how  these cultural forms contribute include: Crafting: This activity often appeals to female participants and involves materials like felt, decorative elements, cork pieces, buttons, paints, and fabrics. While distinct from traditional construction materials, crafting inspires prototyping with everyday items, leading to innovative structures and designs. Textiles: Parents, particularly mothers, often bring sewing a nd garment creation expertise to the makerspace. This knowledge can inspire novel, organ ic designs for construction by integrating patterns  and techniques from textiles into architectural concepts.

Teachers highlighted that the space cannot be easil y changed due to budget and space limitations. As a result of these discussions, we see AI as a valuabl e tool for reimagining learning environments, expanding perspectives, fostering respect for diversity,  and supporting the development of inclusive and pea ce-oriented spaces.

As a result of the reflection sessions, we develope d a set of activities to assess the impact on GCE learning. Mapping different groups, workstation usa ge, cultural forms, and points of overlap within th e makerspace emerges as a pedagogy to foster connecte dness and awareness of diverse communities and cult ural practices. Additionally, tangible models of the mak erspace and AI-assisted visuals serve as tools to represent concepts that integrate multiple perspectives and promote respect for diversity. In this approach, students take on the role of makerspace designers, using AI to enhan ce their creativity, generate realistic representat ions of learning spaces, and integrate reflections on diver sity and Global Citizenship Education (GCE). Feedba ck from the school community during a visual exhibit will refine these concepts to better align with diverse needs.

## Discussion

This work emphasizes the importance of designing ac tivities that empower young people to reimagine makerspaces as inclusive, collaborative environment s reflecting diverse cultural perspectives. The var iety of materials and cultural forms within makerspaces  offers a foundation for integrating generative AI w ith tangible resources, enabling the creation of innovative desi gns that merge physical artifacts with digital visu alizations. Future directions involve scaling this iterative de sign process by blending t angible materials with AI-generated art tools and integrating the space transformation activity into school curricula. This approach could  provide a powerful framework for enhancing socio-emotional learning by fostering empathy, collaboration, and respect for diversity.
