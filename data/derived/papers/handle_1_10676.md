# Mapping a School-based Ecology of Learning In and Through Music

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Within the small amount of existing music-centric learning sciences research, the majority of these studies overtly center the outcomes of singular pedagogical interventions. However, extant (and foundational) research also shows that music learning occurs across multiple, distinct experiences and environments. Music-centric learning sciences research therefore needs to expand to consider how individuals learn in and through music beyond individual educative experiences. In response, I consider the following research question: how do early childhood educators and musicians conceptualize the process of learning in and through music within schools? Drawing on interviews with teachers and musicians from a multi-school embedded music program, I provide an emergent framework for categorizing music-centered learning that incorporates four themes: music as skill set, cultural artifact, pedagogy, and context. This framework presents an opportunity to structure future learning sciences research into learning in and through music across multiple encounters with the art form.

## Introduction

As Halverson & Sawyer (2022) recently noted, the learning sciences has only just started to explore the processes of learning embedded within the arts. This assertio n proves especially true for music.  Although it does not represent a perfect metric for the field, the fact the Journal of Learning Sciences has only ever published one article on music learning (Kupers et al., 2017) and the collected proceedings for the ISLS Annual Meeting  only include 26 publications on music  speaks to this lack of attention. In those instances when lea rning scientists do explore learning in and through music, they often focus on singular interventions where learners develop musical skills (Black, 2008; Cawelti et al., 2023), new forms of expression (Sawyer, 2014), musical i dentities (Woods, 2022), and community formations (Kenny, 2016; Tahirbegi, 2023). Yet well-known publications on the cognitive (Bamberger, 1991) and sociocultural (Green, 2002)  elements of music education have shown that developing musical knowledge stretches across multiple contexts and encounters with music. To this end, focusing solely on one single intervention or site of interaction fail s to encompass the entirety of how individuals learn in musical contexts and through music-based pedagogies.

In response to these  areas of need, I use this p aper to present emergent findings from a culturally embedded music program in England. This currently ongoing program invites professional musicians to perform in multiple settings within primary schools (classrooms, hallways, assemblies, lunch halls, etc.) during the entire school day. These interactions occur one day per week throughout the academic year. Musicians also work with teachers to develop and implement interdisciplinary learning activities and occasionally facilitate traditional music education. This program therefore provides an opportunity to explore what and how students learn when engaging music in formal education context s across multiple different kinds of interaction. In turn, I respond to the following research question:  how do early childhood educators and musicians conc eptualize the process of learning in and through music within schools? Throu gh my analysis, I provide a framework for mapping this learning across multiple interactions, thus providing a tool for learning scientists to conduct  further analyses of music-based learning.

## Methods

Data for this paper comes from the initial 30 to 45-minute interviews I conducted with the eight generalist teachers and five musicians in the program described in the introduction. The teachers work at four primary schools in the UK (two in the North, two in the Midlands), with one or two musicians paired with one school during the entirety of the year-long program. Following Seidman’s (2005) three-interview structure, this initial interview focused on the experiences teacher s and musicians have had with music, music education, and th is program specifically. I also asked the participants questions about their h opes for the upcoming implementation and any barriers they anticipate. As the research project continues, I will intervi ew teachers, musicians, and students about this year’s implementation, conduct observations, and collect artifacts for f urther analysis.  To analyze the interview transcripts, I employed an open and iterative approach to thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2012). This process began with a first round of descriptive coding (Saldaña, 2015) where I categorized the data into broad topics that emerged from the data itself. I then conducted a second round of pattern coding (Saldaña, 2015), grouping my now coded data into themes related to (a) types of music-based learning experiences and (b) learning outcomes of these experiences. I then used the themes from this analysis (see Table 1) to construct a framework for musiccentered learning shared by the participants.

## Findings

In describing their experiences of engaging with music as students, musicians-in-training, and educators, the participants conceptualized learning through four broad lenses: music as skill set, cultural artifact, pedagogy, and context. Learning in music Regarding the two themes related to learning in music,  the music as skillset theme represents the category most aligned with traditional school-based music education. In describing this theme, th e teachers and musicians discussed students developing instrumental and voca l performance skills along with the ability to compose their own music. As one of the teacher s explains, this primarily involves broadly learnin g introductory performance skills for students at this age: “every child learns to play musical instruments [at our school]. We also have private piano lessons and we have choir rehearsals at lunchtimes. We have a recorder club as well where children can learn to play the recorder and learn to read music.” Beyond these instrumental performance skills, the participants also discussed the process of learning how to compose music or improvise. One of the musicians touched on this when describing his own teaching practice: with my kids at school, I've occasionally got them t ogether to do, for instance, a Greek tune, which is essentially written down but they have a l ittle improv section. But since I've been teaching, I've sort of taken the students who aren't in other ensembles, and I had to try and make something with them, which is very time consuming but ends up being very good. Although collaborative in nature, this quote speaks  to the goal of this educational experience being t he ability to both play and compose music for oneself.

Outside of writing and performing, the teachers and musicians also stressed the impo rtance of learning about music history and contemporary music culture under the music as cultural artifact theme. Within this aspect of learning in music, students develop cultural capital related to understanding different types of music (including classical, jazz, and pop music)  and knowing about influential composers or pieces. This often stemmed from an assumed lack of exposure to music in childrens ’ lives. As one teacher explains, “knowing that they haven't been exposed to a range of music, like classical music for example, it is less likely that our children wil l have had that experience. I just want to make sure that, with the  cultural capital, that we are addressing t hat.” Speaking to the idea of cultural capital directly, another teacher linked knowing about popular music to cultural participation: When it would have been John Lennon's 80 th birthday, I thought, “they need to know who he was and the impact that he's had.” So if somebody makes a reference to “Imagine” or “Give Peace a Chance,” they can say, “Oh yes, I've heard about that,” because they're exposure to music and to performances and that kind of information would be very limited. When they hear somebody singing at a football match or somewhere else, one of his songs, they will be able to join in. And being part of those big cultural events, this is an amazing feeling and we want them to be able to join in. Additionally, this aspect of learning about music i nvolves gaining an understanding of  what musicians do  as professionals. One musiciandescribed this aspect when discussing his teaching experience: “ we've been in schools and some of the younger children don't know  what instruments are.  The way that kids consume media and are exposed to stuff is so different. Unless it's kind of very clearly presented, they mig ht not just pick up on that through the ambient culture.” Rather than learning how to play music themselves, this quote speaks to the idea that students learning what being a musician entails can contribute to the development of cultural capital and knowledge described by the teachers.

Learning through music In the final two themes, the participants focused o n using music as a tool or medium to facilitate oth er kinds of learning. While this process inevitably resulted in  learning about music, the ultimate goal shifted towards other types of knowledge. In discussing the theme of music as pedagogy, teachers and musicians positioned music as a tool for learning within a wide range of other disciplines.  In one example, a musiciandescribed using his instruments as a way to illustrate the physics of sound: “one week, I brought a load o f different instruments. [As I played, I asked] is this low? Is this high? How is that achieved? On a wind instrument, it's the length of the tube. And my partner was able to demonstrate how that's s imilar on a [violin] string.” By showing the studen ts how pitch relies on the physical nature of the instrume nt, the students can engage with the physics of sou ndwaves through a musical practice. One of the teachers also described an example from their English curriculum: We wanted to use music in our other curricula and w e made a decision that we wanted it to enhance our writing throughout the school. The musicians came into each class and did six really short pieces of music. And as they played the music, all the children created a storyboard of what they heard in each piece and then wrote a story inspired by the music that they'd heard. Through this process, the students engage with live  musical performances but do so in order to develop  their writing skills as opposed to musical knowledge.

Finally, the participants also discussed the educative value of providing ambient music within the school. This included performances happening in the classro oms, hallways, lunch halls, playgrounds, and more.  In describing this notion of music as part of the learning context, the participants focused on the value music has for socioemotional forms of learning and emotional regulation in particular. A teacher succinctly defined this theme when he described his expectations of the program: “to see music as a way of [improving] their own mental health. Can they use music to calm themselves? Express thei r feelings? I think it's going to be fantastic, th at full school culture where it's integrated into the day-to-day life of the school rather than being a subject. ” In describing the program in this way, the teacher highlights the val ue of music in deescalating a student’s heightened emotions within school contexts. One of the musicians provided a similar example in the following story: So my colleague and I were playing in one of the ha llways during the lunch period and there was a child having a breakdown. He was basically cr ying in the corner and shouting and everything. And there was a teacher with him who wa s trying to calm him down. So we went over to them and played a really calm duet, Mozart or Handel. And he basically just listened, and he stopped crying. Then after we finished, he got up and they went to the classroom where he was supposed to go. The teacher came back afterwards and said, “I didn't realize that music had that effect on him.” To this end, the participants in this study framed music as a means for engaging students in other kinds of learning activities by providing a tool for emotional regulation. Music  can therefore assist in learning other disciplinary subjects within school settings.

## Discussion

Taken together, the four themes described above present a framework for music learning within schools that stretches across multiple sites of engagement. Within both music education contexts and any other space in the school that involves music, students have opportuni ties to learn about and from music, its role within their own lives, and music’s place within the broader culture. Although previous research has often taken a more a tomized approach by focusing on singular interventions or learning contexts, this framework still holds resonances to these extant studies: the importance of using music in em otional regulation described by Tahibergi (2023), the collaborative process of developing song writing ab ilities outlined by Cawelti et al. (2023), and the process of musical knowledge building through listening that Black (2023) highlights in his study of jazz music can all fit within this analysis. By drawing out how learning processes  connect across multiple  pedagogical and social interactions, this study provides a tool for learning scientists to more fully examine how individuals learn in and through music. Future research within this project will continue to refine this framework  based on analyses of upcoming observations and student interview s, providing an opportunity to understand how students conceptualize and experience this learning as well.
