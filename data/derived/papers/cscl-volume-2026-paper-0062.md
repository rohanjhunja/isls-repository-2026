# Multi-Level Knowledge Building Engagement: Pathways to Collaborative Problem- Solving and Academic Excellence in Higher Education

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaborative problem-solving (CPS) is increasingly vital for 21st-century learning, yet research on innovative pedagogy for cultivating these competencies in higher education remains limited. This study evaluated knowledge building (KB) as an innovative pedagogy to foster CPS in a university research methods course with 46 students. By analyzing actual online discourse on Knowledge Forum (KF), the study examined five levels of students’ KB engagement—behavioral, cognitive, thematic, methodological, and pro duct-based—in relation to their academic achievement. Results revealed high levels of active participation, with 54.10% of contributions involving higher-order cognitive activities. Furthermore, multi-level analysis of KB engagement significantly predicted 43.6% of the variance in academic achievement. These findings provide empirical evidence that the KB environment on KF facilitates the practice of CPS while enhancing academic excellence, offering a validated framework for university curriculum innovation.

In the contemporary knowledge economy, the cultivation of core competencies such as Collaborative Problem Solving (CPS) has become a vital driver of national development and academic success (Tan et al., 2021; OECD, 2017). While higher education research methods courses are traditionally conducted in isolation, Knowledge Building (KB) pedagogy provides a transformative framework that shifts the focus from individual information acquisition to the collective advancement and iterative improvement of ideas (Ber eiter & Scardamalia, 2014). Despite KB’s potential to foster higher-order cognitive engagement, its application in university research methods remains underexplored, with limited evidence on how specific online discourse quality and multi-level engagement relate to academic outcomes. To address these research gaps, this study investigates the impact of KB pedagogy through the following research questions:

• RQ1. How do students participate in KB discourse during the course, and what is the effectiveness and

quality of their contributions?

• RQ2. To what extent does  students’ multi-level engagement in online KF discussions predict their

academic performance?

## Literature review

KB engagement and CPS In the contemporary knowledge economy, education must shift from individualistic information transmission toward collective knowledge creation through Knowledge Building (KB). This pedagogy is fundamentally oriented toward the collaborative generation and "idea improvement," a central principle emphasizing that all ideas are improvable through continuous iterative processes that align classroom inquiry with professional knowledge work (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 200 3, 2006). Such collective progress relies on Knowledge Building Engagement, which is conceptually defined as an intellectually energized focus and deep commitment to the community’s problem-solving and idea-refinement processes. Scardamalia and Bereiter (2021) emphasize that this shift from individual learning to community-level knowledge advancement is a critical transformation that promotes both innovation and students' academic performance. CPS and online discourse Collaborative problem solving (CPS) is a vital 21st-century competence involving the capacity to effectively participate in processes where multiple agents work together by sharing understanding and pooling skills toward a solution (OECD, 2017). Beyond a simple skill set, CPS is a complex socio-cognitive competence encompassing dimensions such as social regulation, perspective-taking, and collective knowledge building (Hesse et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2019). Knowledge Building (KB) is recognized as an optimal environment for CPS development 409 because its emphasis on collective cognitive responsibility and iterative dialogue aligns with the socio-cognitive demands of collaborative work (Scardamalia, 2002). Online environments, particularly platforms like Knowledge Forum (KF), further facilitate these interactions by providing digital spaces for continuous idea improvement and community knowledge construction (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006; Soliman et al., 2021). Research further underscores the link between high-quality online discourse and academic success, with studies showing that both participation volume and higher-level discourse moves —such as synthesizing and integrating ideas —are significantly correlated with improved domain knowledge (Tong & Chan, 2023). Additionally, the quality of interaction and feedback received in online discussions has been shown to be positively correlated with academic achievement (Jiang et al., 2023). Meta-analytic evidence confirms that online collaborative learning has an overall positive effect on learning performance (Tian & Zheng, 2024). By engaging in iterative processes of idea improvement on KF, students not only deepen their subject-matter expertise but also cultivate the essential collaborative skills required for modern professional knowledge work. The present study The present study addresses these critical research gaps. First, while Knowledge Building (KB) is established in K-12 settings, its validation in higher education research methods courses —which naturally require collective inquiry—remains limited (Chen & Hong, 2016; Zhang et al., 2011). Second, while general platform participation is linked to achievement, the predictive influence of qualitative discourse content on academic success necessitates deeper study (Tong & Chan, 2023). Therefore, this study examines the impact of KB pedagogy in a university context to clarify how multi-level online engagement patterns facilitate collaborative skills and academic excellence.

## Research design, participants, and context

A mixed-methods design was employed to examine students' multi-level learning engagement. Participants (N=46) were second-and third-year undergraduate students, primarily from the Department of Education, enrolled in a compulsory Educational Research Methods course (3 credits) as preparation for education-related graduate programs. The 18-week course combined i n-class collaborative activities with online interactions via KF, a computer-supported collaborative KB  platform. The course fostered an open, supportive environment emphasizing class discussions and knowledge sharing without rigid structures, aiming to develop a collaborative KB community among students with no prior background in educational research methods. Course design The course was designed to integrate collaborative KB pedagogy into this Educational Research Methods course and it was structured in two main parts: Part 1--Theoretical Instruction. In one part, the instructor delivered lectures, performed regular quizzes (10 times in total), assigning students assignments, and conducted Q&A sessions. Students were introduced to essential concepts in the educational research textbook, including such as research problem formulation, literature review, research design, data collection, qualitative and quantitative analysis, and academic writing etc. Ten class quizzes were administered after the teaching of each essential concept. Part 2--Collaborative Knowledge Building: In this part, students were required to collectively work on their research proposals  from scratch, by translating the conceptual knowledge learned in Part 1 into practic al, feasible proposals. Collaboration was community-based and idea-centered, meaning students can interact with any idea or any classmate during online discussion as long as they are interested in the ideas posted in the form of notes on KF through iterative idea interactions to improve their research proposal ideas. As illustrated in Figure 1, the course created a Knowledge Building (KB) Environment where students engaged in idea-centered Knowledge Building Engagement (KBE) to foster collective discussion and knowledge advancement, ultimately leading to enhanced perceptions of Collaborative Problem-Solving (CPS) and fostering the development of Academic Achievements in this course of "Educational Research Methods." This KB engagement is operationally defined by five ascending levels of student participation: (1) Level 1: Behavioral Engagement refers to students' online interactions as reflected in their quantitative behaviors on the KF, as reflected in the total number of words generated in KF as derived  from specifically note-contributing, note-reading, and note-modifying activities; (2) Level 2: Cognitive Engagement relates to the complexity of students' online contributions, which are evaluated using the six Bloom's cognitive activities: remember, unde rstand, apply, analyze, evaluate, and create; (3) Level 3: Thematic Engagement focuses on whether the students' online discussed topics for their research proposals 410 show a focus on "future learning competencies" such as team-working, collaborating, and communicating; (4) Level 4: Method Engagement assesses whether students' online discussion is highly related to the textbook content about "educational research methods" in this course, indicating a deep engagement with the course's subject matter;

(5) Level 5: Product Engagement refers to the quality of the students' final draft research proposals, serving as the

culminating artifact of their collective knowledge advancement efforts. The successful navigation of these engagement levels in the KB environment is theorized to foster both students' CPS perceptions and their overall academic achievements in the course.

## Methodology & Study Context

Data were collected from multiple sources and analyzed to address the research questions. The primary data source was the interactions and discussion content in KF. Students' KF contributions were analyzed across five levels using keywords from KF notes as the unit of analysis: (1) Level 1 (b ehavior engagement): Descriptive statistics examined students' foundational participation through three activities: note-contributing (creating content), notereading (understanding content), and note-modifying (enhancing quality), using total word count produced as a predictor. (2) Level 2 (c ognitive engagement): Bloom's taxonomy of six cognitive levels —remembering, understanding, applying, analyzing, evaluating, and creating—was used to classify students' cognitive engagement as either lower-order or higher-order. (3) Level 3 (thematic engagement): Students' research proposal as communication and discussion skills, inquiry skills, and teamwork/collaboration.  (4) Level 4 (method engagement): Given the course's emphasis on educational research methods, students' discourse was coded for content knowledge, focusing on elements such as variable definition and operationalization, literature review and theoretical framework, and research design and methodological approach. (5) Level 5 (product engagement): The final research proposals, which were required to be submitted to KF, were assessed for quality based on educational research criteria (Aga, 2023).

In addition, data concerning students' academic achievements were gathered. Academic achievements were determined by final course grades on a 100-point scale (M = 73.73, SD = 8.44), which comprised the average scores of 10 regular class quizzes, all class assignments, and the final exam. Correlation and multiple regression analyses were conducted to examine how students' five-level KF performance indicators —online engagement (word count), cognitive engagement, content engagement (educational research methods), proposal engagement (future learning competencies), and outcome engagement (research proposal quality) —predicted final academic achievements.

## Result

KB engagement on KF Students exhibited high levels of engagement in KF discussions. This was reflected across multiple levels of participation and cognitive contribution as detailed below. Level 1 (behavior engagement). At the foundational level, each student produced an average of 3,574.19 words (SD = 2,628.12) for KF discussion through three activities: (1) Note-contributing (M = 21.21; SD = 16.12)—This activity primarily involves creating  discussion content; (2) Note-reading (M = 148.24; SD = 247.14)—This activity enables deeper understanding of the discussion content; (3) Note-modifying (M = 82.40; 411 SD = 229.20)—This activity enhances the quality of the discussion content. Then, at higher levels, we also coded keywords from the discussion content to identify “cognitive e ngagement”, “thematic engagement”, “method engagement”, and “proposal engagement”.

Level 2 (cognitive engagement). We assessed the level of students' cognitive engagement in KF related keyword contributions. In contrast, lower-order engagement (remembering, understanding, and applying) accounted for 45.90% of related keyword contributions. This finding suggests that KB practices fostered substantial engagement in deeper cognitive activities in KF discussions. Level 3 (thematic engagement). We analyzed the level of discussion focus concerning the direction of students' research proposals. Specifically, we examined how discussions related to future learning competencies. The discussion showed that the most prominent dimensions were: (1) Communication and discussion skills (24.78%); (2) Inquiry skills (21.37%); and (3) Teamwork/collaboration (18.81%). This indicates that students' online proposal discussions regarding the research issues were highly relevant to future learning competencies, which represent an important research trend.

Level 4 (method engagement). We also examined the level of content richness in students' KF frequently focused on three areas: (1) Variable definition and operationalization (24.56%); (2) Literature review and theoretical framework (17.97%); and (3) Research design and methodological approach (17.08%). These Level 5 (product engagement). At the final and highest level, we assessed the scores (M = 72.60; SD = 11.00) of students' final draft proposals. These proposals were uploaded to the KF platform and served as a culminating outcome of students' online KF discussion efforts. Correlations between KF performance and academic achievements Correlational analyses  showed that academic achievements were significantly associated with the five engagement levels of online KF discussion. Regression analysis further demonstrated important findings. The combined 5-level factors/predictors accounted for 43.6% of the varianc e in academic achievements (with Adjusted R² =.382, F(5, 52) = 8.04, p <.001). Findings suggest that students' active engagement across multiple levels of KF discourse significantly predicted academic performance, accounting for 43.6% of the variance. This study validates the effectiveness of Knowledge Building (KB) pedagogy in a higher education research the multi-level analysis of Knowledge Forum (KF) discourse demonstrate that students exhibited robust involvement, with 54.10% of contributions representing higher-order cognitive activities such as analyzing, evaluating, and creating. Furthermore, this multi-level engagement across behavioral, co gnitive, thematic, methodological, and product dimensions significantly predicted 43.6% of the variance in final academic achievement.

Crucially, the findings underscore that the KF environment served as a vital arena for promoting Collaborative Problem Solving (CPS). By providing an asynchronous digital space for sustained "idea improvement," KF allowed students to manifest core CPS dime nsions—such as social regulation, perspective-taking, and collective knowledge building—through their online interactions. The thematic analysis of discourse revealed that discussions were highly relevant to future learning competencies, particularly communication and teamwork skills, which are fundamental to effective CPS. This demonstrates that the KB environment on KF successfully transformed traditionally individualistic learning into a collaborative community responsible for collective knowledge advancement.

Practically, these results suggest that university educators should deliberately design online environments like KF to scaffold multiple levels of engagement, ensuring that collaborative tasks are aligned with both disciplinary mastery and 21st-century competencies. Although limited by its single-course scope, this study provides a validated framework for curriculum innovation in higher education. In conclusion, KB pedagogy, facilitated through the KF environment, offers an empirically grounded approach to fostering students' collaborative problem-solving (CPS) capabilities while simultaneously enhancing their academic excellence.
