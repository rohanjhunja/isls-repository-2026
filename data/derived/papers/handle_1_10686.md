# How AI Evaluates Learner Comprehension: A Comparison of Knowledge-Based and Large Language Model (LLM)-Based AI Approaches

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigated two AI techniques for evaluating learners’ summaries and explored the relationship between them: the SMART knowledge-based AI tool, which generated multidimensional measures representing knowledge models derived from learner summaries, and a Large Language Model (LLM) fine-tuned for summary scoring. The LLM model incorporated both the summary and source texts in the input sequence to calculate two component scores related to text content and wording. Summary revisions from 172 undergraduates in English and Biology classes were analyzed. The results of linear mixed-effects models revealed that both AI techniques detected changes during revisions. Several SMART measures were positively associated with an increase in the LLM’s Content scores. These findings support the notion that the LLM model excels at broad and comprehensive assessment, while SMART measures are more effective in providing fine-grained feedback on specific dimensions of knowledge structures.

## Introduction

Reading comprehension can be regarded as the intric ate process of constructing meaning from text  (Singer & Alexander, 2017). Learners achieve deep comprehension by developing a cohesive mental model of a text (Kintsch, 1998). Learners are frequently tasked with developing an appropriate understanding of the content in read-it-on-your-own learning contexts, such as online or pre-classroom learning activities.  Read-to-write tasks, like summaries, can aid learners in comprehending a  text more effectively. These tasks are frequently employed in various educational settings as a pedagogical st rategy to assist learners in developing reading com prehension and as a means of evaluating their understanding of  the text (Graham et al., 2020).  Unfortunately, learners often lack support in writing a quality summary, leaving them ill-prepared for subsequent activities (Bernacki et al., 2021). This is generally because instructors face time and resource constraints when monitoring learners’ reading, assessing summary quality, and providing prompt, personalized feedback. Summarization is not a simple task. Even many proficient learners experience difficulty in making good inferences from text (McNamara et al., 2019).  Artificial Intelligence (AI) can help both learners and instructors become more resilient to these difficulties by providing automated assessment and formative support for summary writing. There is, however, ongoing doubt about AI’s ability to evaluate complex tasks such as summaries as accurately as human experts (Dede et al., 2019) indicating a need to validate different AI techniques deployed in summary assessment.

In this study, we explored two AI techniques  to evaluate and assess learners’ progress in summar y writing: a knowledge-based AI systems and transformer-based Large Language Models (LLMs). The knowledgebased AI tool utilizes computational models to comp are a learner's summary with an expert model, while LLMs are fine-tuned using expert ratings of summaries.  Specifically, we employed the knowledge-based SMART tool and a fine-tuned Longformer LLM for cross-validation.

SMART is an AI-augmented summary assessment system where learners engage with learning materials and then summarize their understanding. SMART evalu ates the quality of learners’ summaries by comparing them to a reference model (model summary) to assess content coverage and the underlying mental model. Previous studies have shown that feedback provided through S MART assessments aids learners in constructing a so lid understanding of the texts (Kim & McCarthy, 2021). In a recent study (Kim et al., 2023), SMART measures were compared with rubric-based human evaluations. The results indicated weak  to moderate-level correlations, suggesting that knowledge-based AI and human evaluations assess somewhat diff erent aspects of summaries, complementing each other.

AI can be used to automatically score summaries using LLMs (Botarleanu et al., 2022; Morris et al., 2023). Morris et al. (2023) developed a summary scoring LLM based on Longformer that was fine-tuned on over 3,000 summaries written on over 100 source texts. The summaries had expert scores related to the content and the wording of the summary. The LLM accounted for  79% of the variance in  the summary Content scores and 72% of the variance in the summary Wording scores found in a test set. The current study seeks to investigate the extent to which AI-driven evaluation measures from SMART and the LLMs developed in Morris et al. (2023) are predictive of potential changes in learner summaries during the revision process. Additionally, we examined how changes in LLM measures are explained by the knowledge-based SMART measures.

## Context and data source

We analyzed a total of 553 summary revisions written by 172 learners participating in English and Biology classes. The English classes were offered in three consecutive semesters, and the Biology classes were offered across two semesters. In the English classes, 58 learners contributed 196 su mmary revisions based on four reading s. In the Biology classes, 114 learners generated 357 revisio ns across seven reading s. The English class materials were sourced from journal articles, while the Biology class materials were based on an OpenStax online textbook.

## LLM-based measures

The Longformer  model includes both summaries and reading texts in the input sequence to generate two normalized scores  which predict principal components derived from hum an ratings on an analytic rubric. The Content scores refer to the component which distils details, main points, and the cohesion of the summaries, while the Wording scores refer to the component which distils the analytic scales for paraphrasing and language beyond the source.

## Data analysis

This study utilized linear mixed-effects (LME) models to explore the capacity of the measures in tracking quality changes in summaries resulting from revisions and the strength of SMART  features to predict the Longformer Content scores. LMEs enable the accommodation of varying numbers of revisions performed by learners, while controlling for random effects attributed to individuals, assignments, and classes.

Six SMART measures that were not strongly collinear were used in a sec ond LME model to predict Longformer Content scores. The SMART features explained 20%  of the variance in Content scores, which increased to 68% when including the random effects.  The coefficient values indicated that SMART measures from three dimensions were included in the model (see Table 4). For example, Content scores in the LME model increased by 1.286 standard deviations  per increase in Concept Matchi ng similarity, providing evidence that Content scores generated by the LLM help explain the overall quality of learner summaries (Morris et al., 2023), while the SMART measures delineate various facets of the knowledge structure derived from these summaries. In contrast, Recall-C did not show predictability concerning the Conten t scores. Given that Recall-C is an index related to the similarity of key concepts between s ummaries and the expert models, the Content scores could be more strongly associated with the overall content overlap than similarity among particular key concepts found in the summaries and expert models. This suggests that  when evaluation and feedback focus on targeting ke y concepts, it may be advisable to refer to Recall-C measures as a supplement to the Content scores. ## Conclusion

This study employed two distinct tools: the SMART k nowledge-based AI tool, which enables multidimensional measures representing knowledge structures derived from lear ner summaries, and a Longformer LLM model, which considers both summary and source texts for s ummary scoring. Both tools were pre-validated through comparison with human-rated scores in prior studies. We investigated how these AI-driven measures tracked changes in summary revisions and the relationship between the SMART AI measures and LLM-based measures. Both sets of measures effectively detected quality changes in the summaries during revisions. The LLM Content scores demonstrates the potential to assess the ove rall progression in the summaries, whereas the know ledgebased AI measures offer more specific indices for v arious facets of knowledge structures extracted fro m the summaries during revision.
