# When Interdisciplinary Groups Succeed: The Role of Direct Critiques

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines the dynamics of dialogic argumentation among physics teachers with different disciplinary backgrounds in a professional development program. The discourse of seventeen teacher triads participating in structured argumentative activities was analyzed using detailed coding schemes for dialogic moves and epistemic practices. We found that heterogeneous groups of teachers were on average more successful in engaging in deliberative and epistemically-rich dialogues compared to homogeneous groups. However, the success of heterogeneous groups varied widely. Further analysis showed that the heterogeneous groups differed in the nature of their interactions: participants in deliberative groups tended to challenge each other directly, whereas in less deliberative groups direct challenges were often avoided. Qualitative analysis reveals how direct challenges act to create space for deliberation in heterogeneous groups. This study challenges the assumption that interdisciplinary collaboration is always beneficial, pointing to the crucial role of direct, cross-disciplinary challenges in stimulating productive dialogue.

## Introduction

Dialogic argumentation has been recognized in the learning sciences and specifically in science education as a powerful means for learning. Researchers point to the importance of engaging learners in deliberative argumentation, where competing perspectives, explanations or models are proposed and critically examined, and mutual knowledge is constructed (Asterhan & Schwarz, 2016). This idyllic aim meets traditional classroom culture and institutional logics (Zafrani & Yarden, 2022) that impede such interactions. A major challenge for dialogic learning is the compartmentalization of disciplines (Schwarz et al., 2024), where each school discipline focuses on specific ways to construct knowledge and on  narrow sets of epistemic practices (Kelly, 2016). Already Dewey pointed out the pitfalls of disciplinary learning, arguing that it “stifles every question” (Dewey, 1916, p. 139). Bateson (1980) similarly contrasted confined disciplinary learning with the ability to raise doubt or engage in creative  inquiry that integrates multiple perspectives to address real-life problems. Drawing from this literature, we conjecture that deliberation in science education can be supported by interdisciplinarity, mirroring  how actual scientific issues require collaboration across disciplines  involving diverse epistemic practices, reasoning styles and inquiry methods (You, 2017). While research on interdisciplinary learning highli ghts the importance of dialogue (Nikitina, 2005)  and pioneering efforts have been made to elicit design guidelines for interdisciplinary dialogic argumentation activities (Koichu et al, 2022), very little is known about the actual emergence of interdisciplinary dialogues for learni ng (Lyall et al., 2016). In schools, Schwarz and colleagues (2024) found that interdisciplinary dialogues are generally not productive and that guidance generally impairs the dialogic character of classroom talk. We hypoth esize that professional development (PD) programs in which teachers from different disciplines engage  in collaborative learning tasks (Horn & Kane, 2015) would help them to experience productive interdisciplinary dialogue and consequently improve deliberation in classroom talk. In this paper, we check the first hypothesis. This study focuses on the school disciplines of physics and biology. Phys ics learning is concerned with simplified systems that can be modeled on the basis of theoretical laws and therefore invite mostly deductive, mathematical reasoning. In contrast, the systems studied in school biology are complex  and therefore  invite primarily mechanistic and causal reasoning (Braaten & Windschitl, 2011). Discipline-based science teaching is still the norm in secondary schools, making it difficult to implement interdisciplinary programs (You, 2017). We propose that PD programs, often involving teachers from different disciplinary backgrounds, can provide a natural context (and starting point) for implementing and ex ploring interdisciplinary dialogic argumentation. In this study, we take advantage of such a PD program for middle school physics teachers that brings together in-field (IF) and out-of-field (OOF) teachers, which are assigned to teach physics despite having a background and teaching experience  in biology (Hobbs & Törner, 2019). The PD was designed to encourage both IF and OOF teachers to draw on their disciplinary epistemic practices a nd engage in interdisciplinary dialogic argumentation. W e adopt  a broad notion of interdisciplinarity (Markauskaite  et al., 2024): although situated in a disciplinary context (physics), learners bring diverse disciplinary practices into this shared space and create opportunities for knowledge integration across disciplinary boundaries. Research overview and questions In this study, we analyzed the discourse of 17 teacher triads who participated in dialogic argumentation activities designed to encourage them to engage in interdisciplinary deliberative argumentation, while foregrounding their distinct disciplinary epistemic practices. We had three types of groups: homogeneous groups of IF physics teachers, homogeneous groups of OOF teachers with  backgrounds in biology, and heterogeneous groups of IF and OOF teachers together. Our study included two phases. In the first phase, we examined the differences across the group types. Our research question was:  (1) What is the relationship between group composition and the characteristics of the dialogue that evolved among the teachers? We hypothesized that the epistemic diversity of lea rners in heterogeneous groups, together with an educational design that fosters deliberative argume ntation, would result in scientific argumentation t hat is deliberative and rich in epistemic practices. Our findings for question 1 indicate that the discourse in the heterogeneous groups was on average more deliberati ve than in the homogeneous groups, but the difference compared to groups of OOF teachers was small. However, when examining the heterogeneous g roups more closely, we discovered a bimodal distribution of their dialo gues, indicating two se parate clusters within the dataset: half of the groups were highly deliberative, far exceeding the discourse in the homogeneous groups, while the other half exhibited less deliberative discours e. These findings led to the second phase of the st udy, in which we asked:  (2) Among the heterogeneous groups, which interactional processes  were associated with more deliberative dialogues?

In other words, based on our finding that epistemic diversity is not sufficient to promote interdisciplinary dialogic argumentation, we sought to identify the factors that lead groups of teachers from different disciplines to successfully engage in deliberative, knowledge-constructing dialogues in science. Design guidelines for interdisciplinary dialogic argumentation This study was conducted within the Gateway to Physics program, which offers inquiry-oriented modules for 8th9th grade advanced classes. The modules are introduced in professional learning communities for middle-school physics teachers. Due to the shortage of physics teachers, many participants are OOF with biology backgrounds. Given this natural diversity, we designed several of the PD activities to promote interdisciplinary d ialogic argumentation among teachers. These activities required expanding the boundaries o f the traditional physics curriculum so that perspectives from other disciplines, particula rly biology, could benefi t learning. We drew on general principles for productive engagement in educational tasks (Engle & Conant, 2002) and guidelines for promoting interdisciplinary dialogic argumentation (Koichu et al., 2022) to create the following design guidelines:

• Problematizing content: presenting tasks that are context-rich and allow for more than one answer so that

participants can express different perspectives on the problem. Specifically, we searched for contexts that are more familiar to the life-sciences teachers-more real-life and "messy".

• Articulating norms: for scientific arguments (e.g., what justifications are considered adequate in science)

and for deliberative argumentation (e.g., conducting a respectful and issue-driven discourse).

• Group composition: triads were arranged to include teachers with dif ferent viewpoints and, when

possible, with different disciplinary backgrounds.  To balance expected power-relations, 2 OOF teachers were usually paired with 1 IF teacher.

• Scaffolding of argumentation: through a) Learning aids to help learners structure their arguments and

critiques. b) Testing experiments, usually presented after discussing initial hypothes es. Testing can lead to conflicts with the hypothesis and further discussion to resolve the conflicts.

• Low-content guidance: The instructor does not take control of the discu ssion, but rather prompts

participants to reason in order to reinforce their sense of authority and epistemic agency. This study focuses on an argumentative task centere d around the question “Do heavier objects fall faster?” Participants wrote their individual claims  and justifications in a preliminary questionnaire.  They were then grouped according to their responses and  disciplinary background and instructed to write dow n their arguments on a shared whiteboard and provide critiq ues for each argument. Later, participants conducte d experiments to test their hypotheses. To problematize the task, we did not limit the discussion to objects in vacuum (the traditional case in secondary physics), but extended the discussion to objects falling thro ugh air (where in some cases the falling time does depend  on mass). The context of air is more complex, yet natural to the OOF teachers, inviting mechanistic reasoning and justifications based on everyday expe riences. Furthermore, as the biology curriculum in our national context is much more inquiry-based, we relied on OOF teachers’ expertise with experimental practices and empirical reasoning to come into play in the activity. We conjectured that embodying the design guidelines described above would create opportunities for all teachers to employ their epistemic practices, leading to deliberative argumentation among the different teachers. Specifically, in the context of physics and biology, we looked for interdisciplinarity: we expected  teachers to integrate empirical justifications and mechanistic reasoning with theoretical and deductive explanations. Phase 1-comparing the discourse of homogeneous and heterogeneous groups We recorded and transcribed the discourse of 17 teacher triads participating in the ‘falling objects’ argumentative task in 7 different professional learning communities in 2021-2024. 8 groups were heterogeneous (i.e. IF and OOF teachers together), 5 homogeneous IF and 4 homogeneous OOF. Participants’ teaching experience ranged from 1 to 30 years (mean = 12.04 years, SD = 7.95 years). 13.2% of teachers were in their first 3 years of teaching. Coding of dialogic moves To characterize teachers’ discourse, we sought a method  that would allow us to quantify dialogic moves acro ss multiple groups  (Chi, 1997). There a re a growing number of coding instruments for measuring productive educational dialogues that vary in terms of the number of codes include d, the target speakers, and the type s of conversational moves coded. We drew on Felton and colleagues' (2022) coding scheme, whi ch aims to operationalize and capture deliberative argumentation among learners. The coding scheme comprises 47 dialogic moves grouped into 5 categories. The richness of codes allows for a detailed description of the dialogue, but requires excessive training of coders to achieve reliability. Following Bouton and Asterhan (2023), who identified 9 common elements across 7 well-known dialogic coding tools, we s ought to maintain Felton and colleagues' categories but reduce the number of codes to enhance reliability and coherence with other tools. Our simplification process resulted in the following 6 categories and 16 codes: a) Declarative initiations-claims; b) Questions-asking clarification?, justification?, or other? questions; c) Elaborative responses-in the

form of justification or clarification of one's own claim, challenging others’, or adding to their line of thought; d) Non-elaborative responses-agreement, disagreement, or reiteration; e) Meta-dialogic statements – either framing the boundaries of the discussion or addressing meta issues such as the aims of the dialogue; and f) Aside-activity management, professional identity, or completely off-topic talk. The ‘aside’  category was added to Felton and colleagues' original categories to reflect the characteristics of teachers' informal talk (Horn & Kane, 2015). The coding scheme enables the identification of different dialogue types. For example, disputative argumentation is characterized by short exchanges of declarative initiations and non-elaborative responses, while deliberative argumentation involves a higher proportion of elaborative moves, as lea rners engage in longer exchanges around each claim, mutually constructing knowledge. The analysis was conducted at the utterance level. The first author and an experienced co-coder independently coded 10% of the data and discussed  disagreements to refine the codebook and establish initial consistency. They then coded an additional 20% of t he data (sampled across group types and from differ ent professional learning communities), reaching an int er-rater reliability of 86%.  Discrepancies were discussed to reach consensus, and the first author continued coding the remaining data. Coding of epistemic practices In our analysis of interdisciplinary dialogues,  we are not only interested in the dialogical moves but also in the epistemic practices that are applied  in the discourse. That is, we wish to examine not only whether participants justify their claims, but also how they justify them  – what disciplinary epistemic practices do they rely on. We focus on the different sources that teachers used to just ify their claims (or to challenge the claims of oth ers). We drew on Sandoval and Milwood's (2008) distinction b etween authority, empirical, theoretical, and factu al justifications, as well as Ludwig and colleagues' (2021) coding of justifications in the physics lab. Our epistemic codes included: (1) Theoretical-reasoning based on theoretical concepts, principles, or laws. (2) Empirical-based on some kind of empirical evidence. This may include everyday observations, data, experiments, and more. (3) Authority-explicitly citing a source of authority or lack thereof for uncertain claims. Sources may include teachers, scientists, internet, etc. (4) Factual-repetition of the original claim  or assertion that their claim is a fact or common knowledge. (5) Intuition-reasoning attributed to emotive or intuitive views.

(6) Pedagogical-Justification based on pedagogical relevance or wid espread pedagogical practices. (7)

Unknowing – explicitly stating not knowing the justification (w hich is a different epistemic stance than not providing a justification). Following Ludwig et al. (2021), multiple sources could be assigned to one justification. Our study joins efforts in science education research to examine learners' epistemic practices in situ. That is, the ways in which learners justify and evaluate scientific claims in their actual learning interactions, rather than assessing their epistemic beliefs about formal science (Kelly, 2016). Several attempts have been made to assess epistemic practices in teachers' talk (e.g. Christodoulou & Osborne, 2014) and to assess both s tructuralargumentative and epistemic aspects of students' wr itten argumentations (e.g., Sandoval & Millwood, 2008), but analyses linking dialogic interactions to enacted epistemic practices in actual educational d ialogues are rare (Hähkiöniemi et al, 2022; Jiménez‐Aleixandre et al, 2000). The analysis of both dialogic moves and epistemic practices is necessary to capture the complexity of interdisciplinary interactions.

## Findings-phase 1

Figure 1 shows the distribution of dialogic moves across the different group types. We found a striking difference between the discourses  in the homogeneous groups of IF physics teachers and the other groups. 52.2% of the moves in the homogeneous IF groups were categorized as aside, while only 8.6% were elaborative, indicating that their discourse was not deliberative because they did not engage in the argumentative activity. We interpret this finding as a problematization  issue (Engle & Conant, 2002)  - the IF physics teachers assumed that the answer to the falling time problem was obvious (i.e., all objects fall at the same rate) and therefore did not see what there was to deliberate on. This echoes Dewey’s (1916) argument about the difficulty of raising doubts within the confines of a discipline. It seems that the driving question of the argumenta tion activity alone was not enough to motivate the IF teachers to engage, as it neither created conflict nor suggested they would learn anything new through the arguing process. We will show below that the presence of OOF teachers in the group, under certain conditions, succeeded in opening up a space for dialogue. In contrast, the heterogeneous groups and the homogeneous groups of OOF biology teachers were much more deliberative: 23.5% and 23.6% of moves were elaborative, respectively, and they had lower proportions of aside talk (31.2% and 41.2%, respectively).  The distribution of meta-statements is similar for all 3 group types (although they function very differently within the discourse, but this exceeds the scope of this paper). Contrary to our hypothesis, the heterogeneous groups did not  exhibit signifi cantly more deliberative dialogues than the groups of OOF teachers. The epistemic analysis also did not reveal the clear differences we expected between these group-types: The OOF groups had 31.31% empirical and 27.27% theoretical justifications and the heterogeneous groups had 32.75% empirical and 30.41% theoretical. The heterogeneous groups did rely less on authority (10.53% compared to 17.17%), but produced more factual assertions (7.6% compared to 3.03%). Our explanation for this unexpected similarity is that the homogeneous groups of OOF teachers nevertheless find themselves in an interdisciplinary situation, engaging in a topic outside their field of expertise. However, previous case analy ses indicating highly deliberative argumentation in the interdisciplinary groups (Perl-Nussbaum et al., 2023) prompted us to further examine the differences between the group types. When we looked at the distribution of moves in each of the homogen eous OOF groups, we found that they all shared the same pattern, namely more aside than elaborative moves. This indicates that the average distribution pattern of the OOF groups matches the distribution pattern of each of the individual groups. However, the examination of  the heterogeneous groups revealed that the average did not represent the pattern of the individual groups. Rather, we found a bimodal distribution of the dialogues, as can be seem in Figure 2. In 4 of the heterogeneous groups, there were more aside than elaborative moves, while the 4 other groups demonstrated a unique dialogic pattern that we did not observe in any of the homogeneous groups  – they had considerably more elaboration than aside talk. Teachers in these successful groups (which we refer to as HET+) were engaged in markedly more deliberative discussions, greatly exceeding the level of dialogue within the homogeneous groups. We consider this an important finding that supports  our conjecture that interdisciplinarity can contribute to dialogic argumentation in science education. However, in many cases (50% in our study), these groups are not successful. This raises the question: What is the difference between deliberative and non-deliberative heterogeneous groups? What makes a particular group successful in conducting deliberative and interdisciplinary dialogues and why do other heterogeneous groups fail to do so? * Phase 2-what makes a heterogeneous group successful?

To identify the interactional variable that distinguishes between  deliberative and non-deliberative heterogeneous groups (HET+ and HET -), we first looked at the distribution of codes within the dialogic ca tegories. We found no clear differences. For example, the groups had similar proportions of off-topic, management and professional identity talk within the aside category and similar proportions of clarify, add or challenge moves within the elaborative category. However, when reviewing the transcripts of unsucces sful groups, we found a tendency to avoid the task instruction of criticizing each other’s arguments. This led to a new research direction, in which we analyzed not only the percentage of challenges in the dialogue, but also the nature of these challenges. The challenges in the 8 heterogeneous triads (N=31 challenges) were analyzed bottom-up (Chi, 1997) to yield the following categori es: (1) Direct challenges-participants directly criticize each other’s argume nts. (2) Self-challenges-participants criticize their own arguments,  and (3) Third party challenges – participants formulate their critiques to  refer to non-present others such as Galileo (e.g. “ I think Galileo did not take into account…”), students (e.g “ I’m thinking of a student writing this-they did not see the experiment”), the task itself, and more. The categories of self-challenge and third-party challenge were collapsed into one category called indirect challenges to make the difference between more and less successful heterogeneous groups more salient. We also coded the challenges according to the disci plinary background of the challenger and the challenged (González‐Howard & McNeill, 2020): We distinguished between challenges from biologis ts to biologists, from biologists to physicists, and from physicists to biologists.

## Findings-phase 2

Figure 4(a) shows the distribution of challenge-types in deliberative and non-deliberative heterogeneous groups. The difference is striking: 84.2% of the challenges in the deliberative groups were direct  challenges and 15.8% were indirect challenges, while in the non-deliberative groups the challenges were evenly distributed between direct and indirect challenges (50%-50%).

Figure 4 Distribution of Challenge Types in More and Less Successful Heterogeneous Groups. If we look at the addressers and recipients of the direct challenges in each group (Figure 4(b)), we see that their disciplinary background plays an important role: In  the HET+ groups, 68.4% of the challenges were cros sdisciplinary, from the OOF biology teachers to the IF physics teachers. In the HET-groups, only 20% of the challenges were cross-disciplinary: 10% from biology to physics teachers and 10% vice versa. We conclude that the nature of the challenges, part icularly direct and cross-disciplinary challenges, correspond to more deliberative groups and are indicators of successful interdisciplinary dialogic argumentation. ## Discussion

The aim of this study was to examine the characteristics of  interdisciplinary dialogues among science teachers and to uncover the interactional factors that enable or hinder teachers from engaging  in deliberative argumentation. In the first phase of the study, we found that the dialogues in the heterogeneous group s varied widely and could be divided into two distinct clust ers: 50% of the groups were engaged in highly delib erative argumentation, while the other 50% showed low level s of engagement and deliberation. The successful gr oups also demonstrated scientifically aligned and interdisciplinary epistemic practices, as they combined empirical and theoretical reasoning, and almost did not rely on a uthority or on factual assertions. These results reinforce our conjecture that deliberative argumentation in science education can be enhanced through interdisciplinarity. In the second phase of the study, we asked what mak es an interdisciplinary group successful. We found that the challenges in the deliberative groups were mostly direct challenges, while in the non-deliberative groups only half of the challenges were direct. The other half were indirect challenges-teachers avoided criticizing each other and instead directed their challenges to themselves or to a third party. We do not claim that the nature of the challenges is the only factor contributing to deliberative argumentation. Rather, we claim that the various factors, including task design, individual goals, and power relations (e.g., based on gender, or disciplinary b ackground), come together to create a space in which direct challenges are or are not presented. Our qualitative analys is illustrates the rich, natural contexts that led teachers to avo id or enact direct challenges. In the less successful group, we showed how teachers pursued achievement goals and only partially understood the meaning of critique, resulting in a self-challenge that closed the opportunity for deliberation. This analysis echoes the findings of Asterhan (2018) - associating achievement goals with less productive peer argumentation (mainly avoidance) - in a natural and interdisciplinary context. The analysis of the successful interdisciplinary gr oup has shown how the OOF teachers claim ed power and directly challenged the IF teacher, opening disciplinary boundaries to complementary viewpoints. We have shown how this opening undermin ed the physics teacher's certainty, in line with Nikit ina’s (2005) model of interdisciplinary cognition, which begins with the unsettling process of overcoming monodisciplinarity  and recognizing one’s own limited point of view. This is consistent with our findings regarding the lack of engagement in the homogeneous groups of IF teachers. These teachers had no interest in arguing about what they perceived as an established fact. The interdisciplinary encounter with alternative perspectives, expressed by the OOF biology teachers, made them learn new things and uncover the complexity of this seemingly simple problem. Our study contributes to the growing body of research on the theory of interdisciplinary interactions and educational design. It challenges the simplistic assumption that interd isciplinary groups automatically lead to better argumentation, highlighting the role of direct cross-disciplinary critique s in this process (cf. González‐ Howard & McNeill, 2020). On a practical level, o ur findings have implications for the PD of OOF tea chers, pointing to the benefits of engaging OOF and IF teachers in heterogeneous groups, provided that the OOF teachers are empowered to voice their alternative viewpoints. More broadly, as teachers with different disciplinary backgrounds participate in many existing PD frameworks, our study suggests that this epistemic diversity can be leveraged to enhance disciplinary discourse.

The analysis in this study, while including a variety of teachers from different professional learning communities, is limited by the sample size of the teacher triads (N=17) which may not fully capture the diversity of interdisciplinary interactions. In addition, the specific context of the PD program may limit the generalizability of the results to other educational settings. Future research could build on our findings to explore interdisciplinary teacher interactions across diverse educational contexts.
