# The Role of Verbal Descriptions in Facilitating Computational Thinking Skills for BVI Learners

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster presents findings from a computational thinking study for blind and visually impaired (BVI) learners. Three instructors facilitated physical and tangible coding and electronics programming for three BVI youth. The poster highlights the essential role of verbal descriptions in facilitating computational thinking for BVI learners, including strategies and challenges of delivering effective verbal descriptions.

This study underscores the need for inclusive educational practices in informal learning settings to equip BVI youth with essential computational thinking skills and create inclusive maker space environments (Worsley & Bar-El, 2016). Prior work on the use of co-design with instructors showcases the benefit of tight integration of curriculum and technology when instructors are involved in the design process (Roschelle et al., 2006). However, little attention has been paid to instructors’ participation in adapting computational thinking activities for BVI learners. This paper is part of an ongoing three-year project on developing inclusive  learning environments for BVI learners through physical and playful maker learning (IMLS #LG-252360-OLS-22).

## Research design

The accessible computational thinking program was co-designed by three makerspace instructors (experienced coding instructors yet new to teaching BVI learners) and the research team, including the PI who is a blind researcher. A two-day summer camp took place at the Chicago Lighthouse in August 2023 with three participants (2 males and 1 female, aged between 15-20). The camp was comprised of three lessons —Code Jumper to teach coding concepts, Snap Circuits for electronics, and Snapino for hands-on engagement with hardware-software prototyping. Each instructor taught a specific lesson, while researchers aided when needed. Researchers analyzed instructors’ facilitation techniques using interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson 1995) of the video recordings and instructor focus group interviews.

One of the major adaptations for the instructors, when transitioning from teaching sighted learners to BVI learners, involved the extensive use of verbal descriptions. Below report the top three aspects that instructors identified as crucial for effective verbal description, along with the challenges they encountered in this process. BLV participants were satisfied with the interactions with instructors and enjoyed the learning experience as collected in debriefing session during summer camps.

Three aspects of effective verbal descriptions

1. Detailed and specific descriptions:  Verbal descriptions must be highly detailed, specifying shapes, positions,

and functions to help BVI learners, including identifying the correct object, navigating the object, and interacting with different components on the object. During the focus group, the instructors explained the complexity and lengthiness of describing Snapino to the BVI teens, which took approximately 10 minutes. “Instead of saying ‘Grab this from your kit’, you had to say ‘Reach into the power bag, you're going to feel around for a thicker  wire that's got a square end at one side and a rectangular end at one side. That rectangular end is going to go into your computer, make sure that this part goes down and the hollow end goes up, the square end is going to go into the large metal portion.’ So all of the super long wordy descriptions we don't really need to do, but in this case we did.” [Instructors’ Focus Group Interview] The importance of verbal descriptions to assist learners in identifying, navigating, and interacting with the object was crucial, when ambiguous references, such as “this” and “that”, were ineffective for BVI learners. In addition, the instructors reflecte d on the importance of using consistent terms for objects and actions to prevent any confusion.

3. Use of analogies and metaphors:  Analogies can be useful, especially when chosen with consideration of the

learner's experiences and sensory ability. An instructor employed a non-visual analogy to elucidate the concept of resistance and its effect on an electrical circuit. “They likened it to having a roommate with long hair that clogs the pipes, thereby slowing down the water flow.” [Interaction Analysis of the Video Recordings] This analogy helped to explain how resistance in a circuit impedes the flow of electricity without using visual cues. Three challenges in verbal descriptions

1. Descriptive skills, while avoiding verbal information overload: The instructors reported a learning curve

when developing the skill of describing concepts and objects clearly and accurately without visual reference and visual awareness. Also, there was a high cognitive demand on BVI learners to remember complex instructions and spatial information, which required instructors to be particularly mindful of their teaching pace and complexity and keep a balance between the number and detailedness of the descriptions provided. “I think trying to find ways to lighten the load of that memory requirement, while also still getting the same point across, was probably one of the biggest changes (from teaching sighted learners) … trying to lay things out in a way that you could touch and remember the touch without having too much information.” [Instructors’ Focus Group Interview]

2. Describing non-visual navigation of computing: Instructors needed to get familiar with non-visual navigation

code using the keyboard was a new skillset for the instructors: “So how do you navigate a computer just using keys? That's a totally whole new thing to me. I am like, I don't do that. And so even if it was more time with, I guess you would call it professional development, but getting used to as a teacher, because I'm like, how can I teach this if I don't even know it very well?” [Instructors’ Focus Group Interview]

3. Diversity in BVI needs: BVI learners have diverse needs and abilities, and what works for one student may not

work for another, requiring tailored approaches that can be time-consuming to develop. In our case, one participant had more functional vision than the other two. Utiliz ing non-verbal cues (e.g., finger point as reference) worked for the one participant with more vision but not for the others.: “How can I also differentiate between someone  who has some vision and someone who has absolutely no vision or someone who has absolutely no vision (and) maybe some motor issues or struggles.” [Instructors’ Focus Group Interview] This study underscores the importance of specialized training for educators, particularly in effectively facilitating tactile learning with BVI learners. This training may involve mastering the art of detailed and  specific verbal descriptions, which play a critical role in helping BVI teens understand and interact with various  tools and concepts in computational thinking.
