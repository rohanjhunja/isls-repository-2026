# Enlightenment and Counter-Enlightenment in the Learning Sciences

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Consequential research in the learning sciences aims to tackle real-world educational problems. Two pressing problems that currently face learning scientists are post-truthism and institutional racism. In this short paper, I argue that efforts to address each of these problems rely on conflicting philosophical underpinnings rooted in the enlightenment and counter-enlightenment movements, respectively. Although there is no immediate solution to this problem in sight, I believe that unpacking its roots and implications is worthwhile, nonetheless, because it can help explicate the intellectual foundations underlying a large swath of learning sciences literature.

Enlightenment and counter-enlightenment Isaiah Berlin (1997) distinguished between two movements in the history of ideas: enlightenment and counterenlightenment. The enlightenment movement, best articulated by philosophers like Immanuel Kant, was grounded in the view that “what is true, or right, or good, or beautiful, can be shown to be valid” for anyone who is able and willing to employ the ir innate, universal, and naturally endowed  reasoning skills  (Berlin, 1997, p. 262). Enlightenment scholars likewise argued that such universal knowledge could be translated into policy, technology, and art for the benefit of all humankind.

In contrast, the counter-enlightenment movement believed that “values are not found but made, not discovered but created” (Berlin, 1997 p. 262). Coun ter-enlightenment scholars – of whom Hegel was  arguably the most influential – maintained that different cultures, nations, religions, and tr aditions create different ideal forms of aesthetics, ethics, and even truth, and th us express a singular way of life that cannot be re duced to universal propositions located within individual agents. Furthermore, they maintained that the ongoing struggle between socially distinguishable groups  and their unique ways of knowing was what propels h uman history forward, and that the battle between oppressors and oppres sed was inseparable from humanity's journey towards an ultimate truth.

The contrast between enlightenment and counter-enlightenment becomes apparent when comparing Kant and Hegel's views on the notion of liberty and how it is achieved. According to Kant, liberation is a process of coming-of-age that is achieved by  individuals as they  learn to apply their innate and universal rationality to decipher the world around them without the help of others. In so doing they free themselves from the interpretive lenses imposed upon them, as well as from their own  immature tendencies, whi ch obfuscate reality rather than illuminate it. Thus, being free is mainly about individual knowledge. Knowing the truth (inasmuch as it can be known through a universal set of conceptual categor ies shared by all people) is liberating not because  it allows you to choose your own path, but rather because it clearly indicates the correct choice: "Sapere aude! Have the courage to use your own understanding! is thus the motto of the enlightenment" (Kant, 1784/1996, p. 58). Hegel held a view of truth that was social and historical, rather than individual and universal. According to this view, truth is distributed across individuals and the collective societies and historical contexts within which they are situated. Furthermore, Hegel viewed history as a dialectic series of clashes between such societies. He believed that a society's successful stru ggle for recognition and status also entailed elevating its unique way of knowing and its own truth over those of its rivals and predecessors. Thus, for Hegel too, as it was for Kant, liberty and truth were linked. But according to the counter-enlightenment view, f reedom is collective and can only be attained when an oppressed society overcomes a rival society that is oppressing it. As part of this process, a key element of any society's liberation is that its way of knowing is recognized and foregrounded (Hegel, 1807/1977). Expertise and funds-of-knowledge Expertise Experts are people whose knowledge and skills are more advanced and more sophisticated than those of novices. Since the 19 50s, expertise has been viewed as an important way to design and measure powerful learning environments. This was the position advanced by Bruner (1960/1999) following the Woods Hole conference, where “Physicists, biologists, mathematicians, hist orians, educators and psychologists came together” (p. 2) to design a new and updated curriculum. Decades later,  Bransford et al. (2000) drew on multiple cognitive  studies to highlight the ways in which experts acquire, org anize, and apply their knowledge differently to nov ices. Whether in the realm of chess, physics, or teaching, Bransford et al. argued that experts “chunk” their knowledge around big ideas, enabling them to acquire knowledge more easily and apply it more readily. Inspired by this notion of expertise, they concluded that curricula ought to cultivate “deep”  knowledge and emphasize metacognitive processes to develop learners’ ability to regulate their cognition in ways that emulate experts. Wineburg’s (2001) research on historical thinking i s a case in point. In a series of think-aloud experiments that compared how expert historians and  non-experts engage with historical controversies and documents, Wineburg identified several ways of thin king that were common among experts and set them ap art from novices. This includes sensitivity to a source’s context, uncertainty with regards to its reliability, careful and close evaluation of the available evidence and wher e it falls short, and an overall avoidance of the trap of “‘presentism’ – the act of viewing the past through the lens of the  present” (Wineburg, 2001, p. 37). Stated in enlightenment terms, thinking like a historian is liberating because it frees each individual learner from his or her tendency towards presentism, which can overshadow their ability to understand the world around them. Funds of knowledge Learning environments that foster expertise imply a  deficit approach because they view experts’ knowle dge and ways of knowing as better than those of non-experts and they aim to supplant the former for the  latter. One important critique of deficit theories of learning is that they perpetuate the racial and cultural domination of some groups over others. Expertise often reflects White, middle-class views of what and how people should know, and does not give voice to the knowledge and ways of knowing associated with other groups. The term Funds-of-Knowledge (FoK) was coined by Wolf (1966), who inve stigated the various funds and resources that come into play in poor household  economies. In the late 1980s and early 1990s, the term was introduced to educational scholarship by a Tucson-based group of researchers and educators who recogn ized the potential importance of learners’ FoK, especially for underprivileged students. Building on their approach, Hogg

(2011) emphasize d the importance of bridging the curriculum with various bodies of  knowledge and ways of

knowing that underprivileged learners develop in multiple contexts, including: households, family, community, peers, popular culture, life experience, racial/eth nic culture, and more (Hogg, 2011, Table 2).  Rodriguez (2013) highlighted the counter-enlightenment dimensions inherent to this line of sc holarship by pointing out  the connections between FoK and the struggle for social justice. In line with Hegel’s d ialectic theory of liberty  and truth, she argued that  FoK is entangled with a  struggle for recognition between social groups, eac h of which possesses unique ways of knowing, and one of which is currently subordinated to the other. Bringing an oppressed group’s ways of knowing to light is closely linked to how its members envision their liberation. To illustrate, consider Lee’s (2001, 2007) Cultural  Modeling Project, which was based on the premise that underprivileged African American learners “bring to the Language Arts classroom a rich array of knowledge that is useful for learning generative concepts and strategies for reading and writing” (Lee, 2001, p. 100). In Lee’s work, the prime example of such knowledge is Signif ying, a sophisticated type of language game that is  unique to African American English Vernacular. In a detail ed analysis of the di scourse in a predominantly black class, Lee (2001) showed how a learning environment that w as intentionally designed to capitalize on learners ’ knowledge of Signifying used this rich fund-of-knowledge as a scaffold to support learners in thei r intellectual growth. In the spirit of the counter-enlightenment, Lee’s discussion of the broader signi ficance of her work positioned it within a struggle for recognition by African Americans, a group whose truths are typically suppressed from a discourse dominated by the White middle class (Lee, 2007, p. 7). Contemporary challenges for science education There are many challenges facing humankind at the c urrent historical moment. Two of the most pressing are (1) post-truthism and (2) institutional racism. In this section I discuss them vis-à-vis science education. When viewed through this lens, the tension between enlightenment and counter-enlightenment in the learning sciences is key. Science education and the challenge of post-truthism The current era has been referred to as a “post-truth” world, one that is rampant with “fake news” and “alternative facts.” Although these phenomena are nothing new, their i nfluence has grown exponentially in tandem with the rise of social media. Post-truthism poses a real threat to democracy by sowing confusion and disagreement, which undermines the public’s ability to reason wisely about important policy-related questions (Breakstone et al., 2021). One example of the way in which post-truthism threatens the global community is how it e xacerbates problems related to scientific issues like climate change. Despite an overwhelming consensus within the scientific community, post-truthism has led to a decline in the public’s confidence in scientists’ ability to establish truth and weigh in on policy. How does post-truth undermine science? Kienhues et al. (2020) ide ntified three “gateways” that bad actors can abuse to infiltrate public disc ourse. The first is the normative nature of science, which means that although the scientific method is objective in  how it evaluates evidence and generates theories, more peripheral questions touching on what research to f und and which phenomena to investigate rely on mora l considerations. And once it is established that science is value-laden, it becomes easy to advance the misleading claim that scientists are motivated by a particular normative social agenda. The second gateway for post-truthism is the social nature of how scientific knowledge is produced, which is embodied in the process of peer-review and other agreed-upon mechanisms to establish consensus. These socia l processes contradict everyday ideas about how scientific res earch works, which erroneously assume that scientif ic truths emerge through immediate experience (e.g., observation). Post-truth can thus be advanced through misleading claim s that frame scientific consensus as nothing more than “a mysterious conspi racy” and peer-review as a system that is used to “suppress dissent” (Kienhues et al., 2020, p. 148-9). Finally, it is “especially difficult [for the p ublic] to understand how scientists can agree on a conclusion and simultaneo usly acknowledge the tentativeness a nd intrinsic uncertainty of some of the relevant evidence” (p. 150). This ma kes it easy to advance the post-truth agenda, by questioning the reliability of scientific claims and their predictive power, even when they are widely accepted by scientists. According to Kienhues and others (e.g., Osborne et al., 2022), these issues do not indicate that the scientific establishment is fundamentally flawed. T he main problem of science education is that learne rs do not possess the cognitive expertise necessary to engage fruitfully with science in the information age because science is so vast and complex, and it has become so hard t o differentiate scientific knowledge from fake news  and alternative facts. The implications for science education are profound. Feinstein (2011) maintained that traditional science curricula teach students to become “marginal insiders,” meaning that they learn to emulate scientists to a limited degree by engaging with some scientific ideas and methods. However, he argued that the goals of science education ought to be realigned to produce “compete nt outsiders” instead. Competent outsiders may not be able to produce or evaluate scientific evidence on their own, but they are capable of distinguishing between trustworthy and non-trustworthy sources. In a similar vein, Osborne et al. (2022) called for the development of a new generation of science curricula that teach students digital literacy; i.e., how to evaluate a source’s credibility, and how to determine whether it is grounded in scientif ic consensus, without hav ing to evaluate the validity of the scientific evidence itself (an unrealistic expectation). Science education and the challenge of institutional racism Another important trend in learning sciences resear ch over the past two decades has been a growing int erest in how DBR might be leveraged to promote social equity. Underlying this body of research are two fundamen tal assumptions. First, learning is a cultu ral process whereby individual growth is mediated b y cultural means including language, norms, and identity repertoires. Second, in many education systems today, some cultures are privileged over others, so that underprivileged lea rners are marginalized by  learning environments that do not offer scaffolding that allows them to capitalize on their own funds-of-knowledge. Indeed, the asymmetrical power relations between privileged and underprivileged groups exace rbate the difficulty facing minority students and marginalizes their participation in science education and by extension also in other scientific discourses. One compelling example for this point of view was a rticulated by Medin and Bang (2014), who challenged the claim that Western science is somehow superior to other ways of knowing. In their own wor ds, this is “so far removed from reality that it becomes a pernicious lie” (Medin & Bang, 2014, p. 161). Indeed, they argued that elevating Western science over Indigenous ways of knowing perpetuates the dominance of the White middle class over other social groups: “when scienc e is largely narrowed to a particular subgroup, it is almost inevitable that this subgroup will engage in ‘niche  construction,’ creating and reinforcing practices and values that favor that same subgroup and tend to exclude others.” Historically, this claim is presented within the broader context of oppression of Native Americans. For many decades European Americans used the education system as an apparatus of domination, which they deployed to disenfranchise Native Americans of their land and h eritage. Today, persecution of Native Americans is no longer official government policy. Yet Medin and Bang imply that this policy is perpetuated by other means, such as glorifying Western ways of knowing and elevating th em over Indigenous ways of knowing, which effectively marginalizes Indigenous people from the scientific establishment and the social capital that it embodies.

Medin and Bang (2014) also posited that elevating Western science over other ways of knowing is simply bad science, because there are many different accur ate ways to conceptualize and represent reality, and more diversity and pluralism candrive a more productive  scientific discourse. They support this claim with  findings that show that nature is psychologically closer for  Native Americans than for Westerners, which can ge nerate novel and fruitful ways of understanding what scien ce is and how to engage with  the natural world  we live in. Ultimately, these ideas led Medin and Bang to make “the case for broadening the ownership of science” by inviting Native Americans to expand scientific disc ourse with their unique ways of knowing, which are fundamentally different to Western ones.

## Discussion & Implications

The implications of this view for science education can be understood in terms of identity, methodology, and ethics. At the most fundamental level, this vie w requires that the people who embody and represent  science (e.g., scientists and science teachers) be more diverse, meaning that Native Americans shou ld constitute a larger share of this group. Methodologically, adopting this view might entail calling certain epistemological assumptions into question, such as the tendency in science to p refer psychological distance. Adopting the psycholo gical closeness that reflects the Native American view can promote a contextual and situational awareness that Western science often lacks and  might drive a major overhaul of science curricula and the scientific method. Finally, in terms of morality, the most important implication o f the counter-enlightenment view for science education is explicitly recognizing that science is a value-laden social project, and that the questions guiding funding agencies, scientists, and science educators, require careful consideration that takes these considerations into account.

## Concluding remarks

What role should science education play in the curr ent historical moment? To counter the threat posed by posttruthism, proponents of epistemic education have ad opted an enlightenment approach. Their assumption is that some epistemic processes are more reliable than oth ers, and that the scientific establishment embodies  the most advanced way of knowing currently in existence. Science is reliable, and it produces objective and verifiab le knowledge that must guide policy for the sake of hu mankind. Science must be safeguarded from bad  actors seeking to undermine it, and the knowledge it generates must be communicated more effectively to the public. In contrast, when faced with the challenge of insti tutional racism – which is deeply entrenched in the scientific establishment  – learning scientists advocating equity and social ju stice have adopted a counterenlightenment view. They have argued that all ways of knowing and singular truths can be associat ed with particular social identities and that this is just as true for Western (i.e., White) science as for an y other culture. Furthermore, they see scientific knowledge as a social phenomenon that is entangled within in a dialectic historic struggle, waged by disenfranchised groups striving for recognition. To triumph in this struggle would invariably mean to fundamentally alter science (e.g., by adopting psychological closeness instead of psychological distance).

In conclusion, t he nascent debate between enlightenment and counter-enlightenment voices within the learning sciences community is highly consequential, and potentially intractable. Rather than propose a solution to the challenges it may pose, my purpose here has been to articulate  the tenets of each perspective and  delve more deeply into their philosophical underpinnings. I believe this to be a necessary first step for any path forward, and hope that this paper generates additional interest and discourse surroun ding an issue that ought to feature more prominently and explicitly in contemporary research on learning.
