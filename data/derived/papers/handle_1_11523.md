# Growing Community: School Gardens in Secondary Schools

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The potential of school gardens to involve students in authentic learning is widely accepted in Australian primary schools and this is spreading to secondary schools. We report on a study on the use of school gardens in regional secondary schools: exploring, in terms of social capital, why they are developed and how they are used. We have found that, while the impetus varies, they offer unique opportunities to provide richer, more authentic experiences to their students and forge closer links to their communities. Unlike primary schools where parents are often invited to assist with a range of educational programs, the building of connections between secondary schools and their communities, needs to be active and targeted, through the building of a shared vision and communication pathways to facilitate breaking barriers and building social capital.

## Introduction

This study explored the establishment of secondary school garden projects in the Geelong area of Victo ria, Australia using an online survey and three interviews: School 1: Community Garden Representative (CGR). A volunte er provided insight as a community garden member involved with the shared community/sc hool garden from its inception ten years ago. The i nitial development of the garden was enabled by a Council grant. A school representative contacted a communit y gardening group who then developed the shared garde n. The school fence was repositioned to form a boun dary between the school and the garden. A community pathway ran through the garden. School 2: School Kitchen Gardener  (KG): A gardener with experience in the horticultur e industry and over ten years association with the Stephanie Alexa nder Kitchen Garden Project, in both primary and se condary schools. They were employed as a kitchen gardener t wo days a week at a secondary  school. The garden was in the school grounds with a community centre adjacent to the school. School 3: A Government School Principal (SP) of a Geelong school who had initiated the establishment and development of a food garden as a learning space for practical application and problem solving in mathematics, science, and design technology. The garden was in the school grounds, close to a public pathway.

## Findings

The motivations for developing the gardens differed between schools demonstrating school gardens as flexible, contextualised learning spaces that can be used in diverse ways to support students, staff, and curriculum. Some schools integrated their garden into school programs by a range of curriculum or learning outcomes. The attitude to the garden was summarised in one response as ‘a wonderful provocation for a range of learning exp eriences’. In some cases, the gardens were intentionally developed as outdoor learning space s to serve vocational Horticulture or Agriculture qualifications.  The garden projects offered students the opportunit y for experiential learning which provided authentic learning experiences in te aching vocational and industry skills. Garden learning environments engag e students with the outdoors, develop knowledge, skills, and dispositions for advancing sustainable practices (Flowers & Chodkiewicz, 2009) and a life-long interest in gardening. The survey responses from teaching staff across the  schools identified the positive impact on student engagement and the re-engagement of disengaged students.  This was in contrast with the CGR  (School 1), who expressed concern about being expected to engage students in the garden with little input from teacher s, commenting students ‘become disengaged and uninterested’. Lack of communication between the school and the community garden representatives was nominated as c ontributing to this problem. KG (School 2) shared the concern over a lack of a way to communicate  with teachers. The potential of the gardens for con necting to the community was acknowledged in terms of a desire to ‘give back’ to the community. In School 1 the establishment of community connections between the school and neighbourhood was a goal when a shared garden was initially proposed; however, over time active participation, responsibility and ownership appears to have shifte d to the community. This garden had the most potential to promote the school-community partnership through proximity. In practice it was least utilised by the school and highlights the nee d for formal avenues of communication and establishment of a shared vision for the garden. Successful garden projects do not happen organically. They need planning with the desired outcomes both identified and communicated, with buy-in from all the stakeholders. Secondary school motivations for inclusion of a garden learning space can be summarised into five main purposes:

1. Environment/biodiversity—pleasant space connected with nature.

2. Engagement—Alternative less stressful learning environment, promote communication and enhance

teamwork/social skills—everyone on an equal footing.

3. Contextualised/applied learning resource for various discipline and vocational areas (fieldwork).

4. Wellbeing—promote life-skills, health, food knowledge and lifelong learning.

5. Build community partnerships.

School gardens, by developing bridges and links, cr eating a more permeable boundary, resources, and opportunities previously unavailable, can be made a ccessible (Gonski et al., 2018). In primary schools, connections are developed through parents and volun teers helping with cooking, garden maintenance or knowledge sharing. In secondary schools, students (and community members) candevelop life-long learning; improve social and interpersonal skills, as well as  improve connectedness to community, through a prod uctive garden. Students strengthen mutual benefits by volu nteering to develop projects to improve their commu nities’ food security and at the same time develop ‘self-esteem, self-confidence and a sense of self-worth’ (Clerke, 2013, p. 8). As one school representative said ‘allow the students to give back to the community’. School garden projects can contribute to building i nformal relationships and connections. This can be particularly important to school communities that a re very culturally and/or socioeconomically diverse. Gonski et al. (2018) in the Through Growth to Achievement report emphasises that we cannot build social capital relying only on bonding and bridging we also must link through institutional and financial structures. The report suggests schools can improve student outcomes and meet indiv idual learning needs  by using rich learning and personal development opportunities within the community (Gon ski et al., 2018). The lack of established communication pathways was revealed as an issue through the interviews  and reduced the opportunities for schools to establish stronger relationships with their communities, through the common interest in the gardens. This was shown in each of the cases.

## Conclusion

This study revealed that in Geelong secondary schools, gardens are used as outdoor learning spaces in Science, Horticulture, Agriculture or Food Technology and mo re broadly to teach about the environment, healthy eating, and well-being. School gardens provide d the real-world context and opportunity to use applied learni ng pedagogies to connect to the curriculum. Participation in the gardening projects strengthened the local community and can transform community relationships. Our research demonstrates that in secondary schools there is potential to use the garden to establish closer links to the community, however deliberate planning is required to establish communication channels and strengthen school/community bonds. Although secondary schools expressed the desire to use the garden to connect to the community, apart from sharing produce, there were few mechanisms to achieve this.  In School 1 and School 2, lack of formal communication avenues between the school, garden organisers and the wider community in respect to the garden  projects was identified. In School 3, a lack of an initial shared vision led to  the eventual separation into distinct groups  and separate gardens. Links to the community were maintained through shared interests and calling on each other for advice and support. Given the expected benefits to both the community a nd the students it is timely to reimagine how secondary school gardens could be used to develop effective s trategies to sustainably improve community connection. We need to develop and disseminate models that create new social alliances (Black, 2008), pro mote lifelong learning, enrich the environment, improve student outcomes, c ombat disadvantage, and generate social capital. Gonski et al. (2018) recommended ‘a more consistent system-wide approach to developing strong schoolcommunity partnerships focused on enhancing student learning growth’ (p.46). School gardens are a vital element in this vision.
