# Teachers as Co-navigators: Scaffolding Student Uncertainty Navigation in Science Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Uncertainty is inherent in scientific inquiry and can function as either a barrier or a resource for learning. This study examines middle school teachers’ perceptions of students’ responses to uncertainty and the ways teachers scaffold uncertainty navigation in science classrooms. Grounded in frameworks of uncertainty navigation and learning regulation, we conceptualize uncertainty navigation as a multidimensional process across cognitive, affective, and behavioral domains, involving phases of recognition, acknowledgment, and management. Twelve teachers participating in a week-long professional development program engaged in a structured discussion on students’ uncertainty responses and teachers’ facilitation strategies. Qualitative interpretive discourse analysis showed that, without scaffolding, students’ responses to uncertainty were often negatively oriented. Teachers described two complementary co-navigation routes —direct and indirect scaffolding—enacted through strategies across dimensions and phases. The findings provide preliminary support for the conjecture that teachers’ co-navigation scaffolds students’ self-navigation of uncertainty, helping transform uncertainty into a resource for sustained scientific inquiry.

Uncertainty is ubiquitous in science inquiry and can be a resource for learning (Chen et al., 2024; Manz, 2025). It has the potential to spark positive cognitive, affective, and behavioral responses (e.g., curiosity, Litman & Jimerson, 2004; Shin & Kim, 2019; Jirout & Matthews, 2022; excitement, Jaber & Hammer, 2016). Yet, prolonged uncertainty often leads tonegative responses (e.g., overwhelm, frustration, fear) when its value is not perceived by teachers or learners (Barlow et al., 2018; Park & Ramirez, 2022). Teachers’ perceptions of uncertainty critically shape their instructional decisions and how they provide students with instructional support for navigating uncertainty (Beghetto, 2016). When teachers perceive uncertainty as a barrier to be avoided, they may over-structure lessons and inadvertently constrain opportunities for inquiry and curiosity. Conversely, when uncertainty is viewed as a desirable and productive element of learning, teachers are more likely to adopt creative and flexible instructional approaches, enhancing their capacity to address challenges and adapt to unexpected events in the science classroom. Previous research suggests that teachers may hold unsophisticated perceptions of uncertainty (e.g., be unaware of the productive potential of uncertainty; Watkins & Manz, 2022). Moreover, teachers often lack strategies to scaffold students to manage uncertainty during science learning (Chen & Techawitthayachinda, 2021; Chen & Jordan, 2024; Doyle, 1988). However, targeted professional development (PD) programs can enhance teachers’ capacity to value and strategically scaffold students’ uncertainty experiences (Starrett et al., 2025). For middle school students, teachers’ scaffolding appears to be an important condition for supporting productive uncertainty navigation. At this developmental stage, structured support may enable students to interpret and manage uncertainty as a resource for learning rather than solely as an obstacle. Without such support, students may be more vulnerable to disengagement and less likely to develop the skills required for sustained engagement in science inquiry.

The purpose of this study is to investigate teachers’ capacity to help students navigate the complex, multidimensional uncertainties inherent in science learning opportunities aimed at engaging students in scientific inquiry.

## Theoretical framework

Uncertainty navigation Uncertainty navigation in science learning is a complex process. We conceptualize uncertainty response as a multidimensional construct encompassing cognitive, affective, and behavioral dimensions. Cognitive responses involve how learners interpret, evaluate, and make sense of uncertainty (e.g.,  planning ahead, or mapping out; 1972 Greco & Roger, 2001). Affective responses capture the emotional experiences triggered by uncertainty, which can influence learners’ willingness to persist in inquiry (e.g., curiosity, upset, or worry; Lamnina & Chase, 2021; Greco & Roger, 2001). Behavioral responses  refer to the observable actions learners take to address uncertainty (e.g., excessive information seeking or distracting; Bottesi et al., 2019). Together, these dimensions provide an integrative lens for examining how students experience and navigate uncertainty as part of the learning process. Researchers interested in uncertainty navigation in science learning have argued that uncertainty can be both undesirable (when it leads to high anxiety and disengagement) and desirable (when it catalyzes sustained inquiry and progress (Chen et al., 2024;  Manz, 2018; Beghetto, 2016). Furthermore, researchers have theorized specific types of uncertainty management strategies that teachers can utilize to shape students’ learning, including raising, maintaining, reducing, or postponing  students’ uncertainty (Ha et al., 2024; Jordan, 2015; Ko & Luna, 2024; Chen & Jordan, 2024). However, students’ initial responses to uncertainty before working with it, and how teachers might support navigating their responses, are unclear. After reviewing the literature, it appears that little research has yet been conducted in this area. We propose that, before taking steps to leverage uncertainty through management strategies, uncertainty navigation also entails recognizing and acknowledging phrases (Jordan, 2015; Patel et al., 2024). Recognizing refers to being explicitly aware of or noticing one's uncertainty, which is the initial step to navigating uncertainty. Acknowledging means legitimizing, normalizing, or sharing uncertainty with others or articulating the uncertainty.

Regulated learning Previous researchers have proposed several theoretical models to explain how learners regulate their learning processes. Early conceptions of self-regulated learning (SRL; Schunk & Zimmerman, 1997; Zimmerman, 1990) describe how learners plan, monitor, and reflect on their performance. Students are viewed as active learners who contribute actively to their learning goals. SRL is an internal process, however, assisted and influenced by social interaction.  More recently, situated perspectives on learning have  extended self-regulation models to highly interactive, dynamic learning situations. Researchers (e.g., McCaslin, 2009; McCaslin & Hickey, 2001) explored a more social form of regulation —co-regulation, which conceptualizes regulation as a socially mediated  and dynamically supported process. Grounded in a social-constructivist view of learning, we assume that learning in classroom settings is both self-regulated by learners and co-regulated through interactions with others (e.g., teachers and peers).

Co-navigation of uncertainty: A conjectured model Recognizing that science learning inherently entails engaging with and leveraging uncertainty as a resource, we build on the theoretical foundations of self-regulated and co-regulated learning and posit that students navigate uncertainty in science learning through interrelated processes of self-navigation and co-navigation. We conjecture that uncertainty navigation is a collaborative effort between the student, peers, and the teacher in science learning contexts. The present study This exploratory study examines teachers’ perceptions of student responses to uncertainty and teachers’ conavigation strategies of uncertainty during middle school science instruction. Specifically, we address the following research questions: ● RQ1: What do teachers report about students’ cognitive, affective, and behavioral responses to uncertainty during science learning, and how do they perceive students’ self-navigation of uncertainty? ● RQ2: What do teachers report about how they scaffold students’ uncertainty navigation during science learning? ## Context and participants

This study was conducted during a second-year week-long summer PD program designed to develop teachers’ capacity to use student uncertainty as a pedagogical resource for science learning.  The twelve participating middle school science teachers had previou sly completed the first year of the PD and subsequently implemented the uncertainty-based pedagogical approach in their own classrooms during the academic year. In the second summer, these returning teachers engaged in a structured collaborative discussion focused on their classroom experiences. Specifically, they reflected on student responses to uncertainty they had observed and described the strategies they used to support students in navigating those moments. The conversation also explored how their co-navigation practices helped students recognize, acknowledge, and manage uncertainty in ways that fostered deeper engagement in science inquiry.

Data sources and analysis The teachers’ collaborative discussion was audio-video recorded (~70 minutes) and then transcribed (39 single-spaced pages). We used qualitative interpretative analysis of discourse, applying an iterative comparative approach that combined episode mapping,  open and axial coding, and structured memoing (Glaser, 2016; Sacks et al., 1974). Our goal was to characterize how teachers described students’ responses to uncertainty (RQ1) and the ways teachers scaffolded those responses across cognitive, affective, and behavioral dimensions and along the recognize-acknowledge-manage (R-A-M) phases (RQ2). We first constructed episodic maps of the discussion, marking topical shifts to delineate discrete “episodes” (Sacks et al., 1974) to guide interpretation. Within episodes, open coding was conducted at the incident level, with each incident defined as an utterance or short conversational turn expressing a single idea or action (Glaser, 2016). Coders assigned descriptive, data-near codes to characterize student uncertainty responses, teacher support moves, and other emerging ideas. Throughout this phase, we wrote theoretical memos to capture emerging constructs and tensions. Using constant comparison across incidents and episodes, we then conducted axial coding to relate these initial codes along three analytic axes: (1) dimension (cognitive, affective, behavioral), (2) phase  (recognizing, acknowledging, managing), and (3) support route (teacher-direct vs. teacher-indirect scaffolding). uncertainty navigation model. First, teachers observed that when students encountered uncertainty, most responses were short-lived or superficial. According to teachers’ reports, students often became cognitively stuck, experienced discomfort, “shut down,” or exhibited defensive behaviors. On their own, these cognitive, affective, and behavioral responses were insufficient to transform uncertainty into a resource for deep inquiry. In the absence of scaffolding, teachers rarely reported students’ positive responses to uncertainty or evidence of successful self1974 navigation. Instead, they reported frequently observed students experiencing undesirable forms of uncertainty, which led to frustration, withdrawal, or disengagement from sustained inquiry. Second, teachers highlighted two complementary scaffolding routes: direct scaffolding and indirect scaffolding. For direct scaffolding, teachers reported that they can invite students to clarify, prompt their self-reflection to help students recognize cognitive response; re-voice student thinking, normalize uncertainty as inherent to science practice to acknowledge cognitive response, probe defensive behaviors, or provide knowledge scaffolding to manage behavioral responses. For indirect scaffolding, teache rs also reported they can cultivate a classroom climate that legitimizes confusion to help acknowledge affective response, establish group norms for safely expressing uncertainty in order to help manage affective response. When a collaborative learning group normalizes “not knowing” as part of inquiry, they can acknowledge and manage uncertainty in a more productive way. Teachers emphasized that modeling uncertainty navigation was particularly important for helping students recognize and acknowledge uncertainty in learning, whereas group norm-setting played a strong role in sustaining students’ ongoing management of uncertainty.

This study provides preliminary support for the model’s conjecture that students, teachers, and peers may play complementary roles in the recognition, acknowledgment, and management of multidimensional uncertainty. In teachers’ perception, students’ navigation of uncertainty is co-constructed through social interaction, with self-navigation processes supported by the co-navigation of teachers and peers. Without intentional support from the teacher, uncertainty tended to remain a temporary disruption that elicited negative response. Co-navigation may function as a hinge between response to uncertainty and sustained and productive engagement.  Transforming uncertainty into a resource requires a socially supported navigation system embedded in classroom culture, where direct teacher moves spark uncertainty recognition and convert acknowledgment into management, leading to deep inquiry.

While direct scaffolding can effectively guide students through moments of uncertainty, it may also carry the risk of constraining their autonomy. Scaffolding uncertainty navigation aims to align teacher moves to drive and support students' agency as they make sense of their uncertainty (Chen et al., 2024), suggesting teachers employ adaptive, as-needed scaffolding that sensitively intervenes in students’ self-navigation. Indirect facilitation would be employed by deliberately creating opportunities for peer interaction and collaborative meaning-making (Manz et al. 2026; Watkins & Manz, 2022). This includes orchestrating peer inquiry, prompting students to seek clarification from one another, and establishing classroom norms that legitimize “productive notknowing” as a natural and valuable part of the learning process. We suggest teachers intentionally design and facilitate peer scaffolds rather than merely allowing them to emerge spontaneously, so that self-navigation behaviors—such as peer inquiry and hel p-seeking—become normative and routine features of classroom discourse. In doing so, teachers enact indirect scaffolding within the co-navigation model, fostering an environment where uncertainty is collaboratively regulated within the classroom community (Chen & Jordan, 2024; Manz et al. 2026).

Taken together, these findings contribute to conceptual and theoretical understandings of teachers’ perceptions of uncertainty navigation by framing it as a socially mediated process and proposing a coherent model for thinking about teacher co-navigation that can provide a foundation for subsequent empirical and design-based research. However, based on its exploratory scope, this study solely investigated  teachers’ perceptions, which may reflect their limited awareness of students’ capacity for self-navigation. Thus, future research should incorporate classroom observations and students’ perspectives to triangulate and extend these findings. Moreover, further investigation is needed to identify the optimal range of co-navigation scaffolding for uncertainty,  as excessive scaffolding may inadvertently constrain students’ agency to self-navigate.
