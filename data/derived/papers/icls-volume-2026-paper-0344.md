# Learning With and Against Place Towards Spatial Justice

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this symposium, we consider the diversity of ways that learners conceptualize and engage with places. Building on growing work in the Learning Sciences on learning in/with places, we bring together contributions that highlight the gap between learning with a place and learning about a place by examining cases in which learners complicate ideas of places as good or bad. The contributions show a wide variety of ways this can happen, from students’ changing concepts of a “developed” place, to how prior knowledge of a city can shape one’s perception of neighborhoods as safe or unsafe, to how children might reclaim or resist visions of their school as policed, to how community members resist simplified representation of their places. Together this work suggests increasingly nuanced ways of treating place that can in turn support designs of just learning in places.

Place-based education refers to a diverse collection of pedagogical practices and learning environments that center teaching and learning exchanges in/about specific real-world places and leverage these places to promote learning, action, and identity development (Gruenewald, 2003a; Sobel, 2005). Most enactments of place-based education understand that people have, can have, or should have strong positive affective and practical ties with place, and that these relationships are important for learning and developing happy, healthy, and empowered individuals and communities (Gruenewald, 2003b; Yemini et al., 2025). In short, learning happens in places, and places are important for learning.

In the learning sciences, place has figured centrally into studies of knowing and learning. Taking a wideangle lens, K. Taylor and Phillips (2017) explain that research on place and learning can be grouped into three bands: (a) research that considers all learning to be situated in spaces/places; (b) research that investigates or evaluates place-based pedagogies, or teaching and learning centered in places; and (c) research that examines place-making and the dynamic construction of physical and ideological places. Some of this research uses specific, real-world places as anchoring contexts to teach about (seemingly abstract) science and mathematics concepts. For example, critical spatial mathematics (Rubel & Nicol, 2020) is a pedagogical approach that uses the city as a context for teaching mathematical concepts like distance, area, and density, towards learning about local issues of spatial justice. Other studies have focused on the role of mobility and place-making in learning more generally. These scholars, working under the masthead learning on the move  (Marin et al., 2020), posit that movement is both learned and implicated in learning (Marin, 2020) and that competing notions of place or relationships with place shape how knowledge about the world is constructed (K. Taylor, 2020). Underlying much of the scholarship on place-based education, as well as scholarly and public discourse about place and people, is a moral conviction that fostering deep connections with place is inherently good, and conversely, that displacement (being unt ethered from place) is undesirable, or at worse, pathological (Malkki, 2009; Cresswell, 1992). These positive attitudes towards place are evidenced in everyday talk with  botanical and spatial metaphors, such as describing people as “rooted” or “uprooted” (Malkki, 2008) or being “in their rightful place” or “out of place” (Cresswell, 1992). Furthermore, a positive association with place is typically paired with a negative association towards all things mobile, including people, other organisms, ideas, and materials. This can be seen in the rising tide of anti-immigration rhetoric, evidenced in everyday talk that uses catastrophic metaphors, such as a “flood of migrants” (van Leeuwen, 1995).

In contrast we take on a conceptualization of place and mobility that see these as neither inherently good nor bad, but rather as political-material constructs (Cresswell, 2006; Deleuze & Guattari, 1986; Soja, 1989). That 2239 is, these constructs serve power-related purposes and can be used for emancipation or enclosement. For example, places could be structured in ways that are welcoming to some by being harmful to others. Hostile architecture and nationalistic movements work at differing spatial scales to secure space for some at the expense of others. Furthermore, this alternative vision understands that mobility does not threaten place, but rather, pla ces are constituted by the movement of people, other organisms, ideas, and  materials (Leander et al., 2010; Massey, 2005). From this perspective, a space/place can be viewed by what moves through it, rather than what stays put. We understand that both perspectives are necessary for understanding the learning that “takes place” but also “takes flight” (Deleuze & Guattari, 1986) in place-based education. It is crucial that educators and Learning Scientists interrogate the political dimensions of constructs such as place and mobility, particularly how these constructs are used to empower or disempower, help or harm, and support or constrain learning. In this symposium, we explore some of the affordances and the challenges that arise when researchers, practitioners, and participants put conceptions of place in the center of learning and activity. We aim to take forward the learning sciences’ understanding of learning on the move by bringing together work which examines the variations in how people learn and think about place, focusing especially on difficulties in/with place in/with place. In doing so, we bring the learning sciences into conversation with analyses from other fields, such as political geography, Software studies, Black studies, and urban studies. The guiding questions for the symposium are: ● What different conceptions of place  or relationships with place emerge during learning projects which center in and on locally meaningful spaces?

● When, where, and how might these differing conceptions/relationships produce both harmonies (moments where plural perspectives on space/place enrich peoples’ activities and promote some form of equity, justice, or shared understanding) and tensions (moments where dominant perspectives on space and place constrict learning, reproduce harmful social dynamics, or erase crucial spatial and social histories)?

● When do these harmonies and tensions surface, how do they shape learning, and how do people work through these conflicts around, about, and in place?

● With these harmonies/tensions in mind, how might we design for more just interactions in/with place? Our symposium contributions include people wrestling with place in both formal and informal settings and with varying participants on the political dimensions of space. The first contribution describes an ongoing partnership between urban planners, learning scientists, and community members as they grappled with competing visions of place while working together to construct a participatory map and short film (Hock). The second and third contributions examine how preservice teachers (Sherard & Lipscomb) and informal educators (Mahoney) attempt to center classrooms on issues of spatial justice, detailing how ideologies and classroom materials constrain critical reasoning. The fourth and fifth contributions examine how learners explore, reason about, and (re)st ory somewhat unfamiliar places (Uttamchandani & Curinga) and very familiar places (Milton & Walkington), with implications for researchers/practitioners hoping to improve learners’ critical engagement towards places. The final two symposium contributions e xamine place-making efforts, both physical (Germinaro) and virtual (de Freitas & Curinga), highlighting the need to center architectural design around youths’ experiences, needs, and desires. These contributions bring theories and practices from critical geography (Allen et al., 2019; Cresswell; 1992; Massey, 2005; McKittrick, 2011; Soja, 2010) into closer contact with theories of learning (Germinaro, 2025; Hall et al., 2002; Marin et al., 2020; K. Taylor, 2020). Doing so allows  researchers to explain the l inks between broader social and spatial phenomenon and day-to-day learning and living.  Looking across these symposium contributions, we see how harmonies and tensions together shape learning opportunities for people’s understandings both about and in place. Our symposium will close with remarks from Penington, who will bring her expertise in literacy and children’s conceptions of place to bear on synthesizing the work. Developing representational infrastructure to redevelop physical infrastructure Ari Hock In this symposium I report on the Corridors of Community Learning study, which seeks to better understand how residents are learning to address infrastructural injustices (Shea et al., 2023) related to a highway running through their neighborhoods. The study is an interdisciplinary effort of planners and learning scientists to develop local capacities for civic engagement in Buffalo, New York. Over the course of four workshops residents created an ArcGIS StoryMap, went on a walking tour, and produced a short film to tell stories of their community in relation to the highway. Through these activities, the participants and researchers grappled with tensions around how to 2240 represent the community. This paper describes one of these tensions, highlighting the role of representational infrastructure as a means to redevelop physical infrastructure in our neighborhoods. Representational infrastructures are the components within an activity system which support the way concepts form, evolve, and are distributed (Hall et al., 2002). These can include patterns of joint activity, as well as the way these patterns are reified within particular technologies and media. While representational infrastructures have been studied within the workplace (Hall & Horn, 2012), and in classrooms (Hall & Jurow, 2015), there has been limited scholarship examining them in the context of community settings. Within the present study, I examine how filmmaking can function as a kind of representational infrastructure which can make visible tensions in the way that communities are conceived.

The research team recruited residents of Buffalo’s East Side living near U.S. Route 33. We are interested in this area because local activists have been organizing efforts to remove the highway; we want to better understand how, within the context of proposed infrastructure reform, residents might develop new possibilities for their landscape. The East Side has been a predominantly Black, urban community since the mid-20th Century, when the U.S. government subsidized suburbandevelopment and homeownership for White Americans. Route 33 was built to bisect this community, promoting automobile access between suburbs and downtown Buffalo (H. Taylor, 2011).

Data sources for this study include audiovisual recordings from four community workshops, which took place over the course of one month. Initial analysis has involved editing videos of each workshop, an “intimate” process (Laurier et al., 2008) which includes rewatching the same segments several times, synthesizing several video and audio sources, and marking clips for notable interactions. I engaged participants in this process by screening multiple cuts of the walking tour film during the workshops and fa cilitating discussions about how to define and strengthen its narrative arc. For the present analysis, I looked for tensions in how the neighborhood was represented, which indicate possibilities for intersubjective sensemaking (K. Taylor, 2020). This involved triangulating workshop recordings with audio recordings and written notes from post hoc research team debriefs. One ongoing tension involved representing a community as culturally and geographically diverse as the East Side. Given we were only able to go on one walking tour, and the short film would be based on that tour, participants had to collectively determine where it would take place. During the first workshop, after discussing different viable routes, the group ultimately agreed to tour Jefferson Avenue, one of the East Side’s main commercial corridors. But upon watching a rough cut of the film during the third workshop, one participant noted that “[the East Side is] more than just Jefferson. For me, that’s what’s missing [in the film].” Others agreed that since the film would represent the East Side, it ought to avoid collapsing their community’s diversity to a particular thoroughfare – exacerbating the very problem it was seeking to address in relation to Route 33. The research team responded to this in two ways: recording an introduction to contextualize the film as one story among many; and encouraging participants to include stories from other neighborhoods on a digital map. Participants favored these solutions, approving of a new version of the film during the final workshop. Future work will explore how to support these storying efforts as ongoing activities, so that they may become more embedded into residents’ lives. In this way, these creative media might work in tandem, serving as representational infrastructures to convey a diversity of cultures and possibilities for redeveloping our communities. Ideologies about urbandevelopment and reasoning about the city in STEM preservice teacher education Max K. Sherard, Kemper Lipscomb One way of enacting critical place-based pedagogies (Guenewald, 2003a) in STEM classrooms is to center instruction on local issues of spatial justice—such as the distribution of urban heat islands within a city (Wilson, 2020). To successfully understand a spatial justice issue, teachers and students must make connections between present day spatial patterns (e.g., the distribution of trees, population density) and historic struggles for power in society (e.g., racial segregation, exclusive home lending prac tices) (Soja, 20 10). Geographers refer to this entanglement—the connections between society, history, and spatiality —as trialectics (Soja, 1989). Developing a trialectical orientation to phenomena, however, is a difficult endeavor. One barrier to trialectical thinking is developmentalist spatial ideology, which understands: (a) spaces to be discrete, bounded, and closed systems (e.g., the nation state); and (b) differences between spaces to reflect individual trajectories along a predetermined path (Prado, 2022). This ideology is most salient in the international context, through categories such as “underdeveloped”, “developing”, and “developed”. This ideology backgrounds the political dimensions of spatial difference. That is, for one space to become “developed,” another space must be plundered.

In this paper, we investigate the spatial ideologies that arose as a group of preservice STEM teachers learned about the causes of urban heat islands in Austin, Texas. The teachers participated in three thirty-minute 2241 virtual neighborhood explorations (Sherard et al., 2025) using Google Street View, Google Maps, and other resources (e.g., historic texts). Guided by theories about the relationship between ideologies and reasoning (Daniel, et al., 2023) and methods from critical interaction and microgenetic analysis (Philip & Gupta, 2020), we searched the data for key moments when teachers were explaining why spaces look the way they do. In this different uses of the word “developed” that preservice teachers employed to make sense of the city: the stage definition of development and the politicized definition of development. The stage definition of development refers to any instance where preservice teachers were evaluating spaces/places along a continuum, from underdeveloped to overdeveloped. We delineate three aspects of this definition: First, the stage definition focused primarily on visual aspects of the built environment, such as roads, sidewalks, single family homes, apartment buildings, and offices. For example, a group of teachers exploring a neighborhood in East Austin described the area as “developing but kind of bar e,” referring to “empty” plots of grass in between newly built mid-rise apartments. Second, the degree of development (i.e., more or less developed) could be aesthetically evaluated as beautiful or ugly and morally evaluated as good or bad. In some instances, “more developed” spaces were evaluated positively, such as when one group described a West Austin neighborhood as “more developed” and “very nice part of town”. In other instances, “more developed” spaces were evaluated negatively, such as when a different group described East Austin as being “over developed” because of the perceived abundance of concrete. Finally, the stage definition was often used as an adjective, describing entire spaces as being either more or less developed. This was evidenced in conversation with phrases like “this neighborhood is more developed,” where the verb “is” acts to make development a quality of space. The social actors responsible for developing space were frequently excluded from discussion. The politicized definition of development refers to any instance where preservice teachers evaluated particular spaces/places as either developed or undeveloped, and explained that these differences were produced by human actors, for particular purposes. We delineate three aspects of this definition: First, the politicized use of “development” focused on the presence of socially valued goods or resources, such as grocery stores, parks, schools, or transportation infrastructure. The presence of these resources/goods identified a neighborhood as “developed” and the absence identified a neighborhood as “under-developed.” Second, evaluation of spaces/places as “good” was linked to the presence of socially valued goods and resources, rather than to the perceived aesthetic or socioeconomic qualities of spaces. Finally, this use of the term “development” tended to foreground the social actors who are responsible for “developing” spaces. For example, after examining a historic redlining map of Austin, one student explained that “all the ‘nice things’ [gestured air quotes] that are in a city, were definitely not going to be developed…in [redlined areas].” His use of the phrase “not going to be developed” indicates that a social actor or group is responsible for not developing particular neighborhoods within the city, even if those social actors are not explicitly named.

We see the stage definition as more aligned with developmentalist spatial ideologies, whereas the politicized definition is more aligned with a trialectical perspective on space. Being able to identify these various ideological orientations to space is nec essary to support teachers in developing critical accounts of space. We think this is a worthwhile endeavor, because research has shown that ideologies shape the way people reason about phenomena (Daniels, et al., 2023; Philip et al., 2017), and apolitical  ideologies must be identified and challenged to support sociopolitical clarity, especially when teaching about spatial justice. Connecting reflections of pedagogical moments to socio-spatial structures Charlie Mahoney Scholarship on place-conscious learning is often attentive to concepts like spatial justice (Soja, 2010), the relational nature of place (Massey, 2005), and the central role of capitalism in urban change and dispossession (Harvey, 2012). However, given how intertwined place-conscious learning is with myriad disciplines and histories of injustice, it can be hard for facilitators of this learning to predict how these tensions will arise. In response to this difficulty, I explore the ways that advocates within built-environment fields confront place-centered injustice as they engage others in learning about the built environment. I analyze interviews with ten people who I call “built environment engagement facilitators.” This purposefully broad term includes pe ople in more explicit learning settings, such as youth spaces dedicated to learning about urban planning, as well as people whose work is more directly rooted in advocacy but equally involves the facilitation of learning with diverse participants. Given the various ways these facilitators encounter the impacts of injustice in their work, I use a grounded theory approach to intensive interviewing (Charmaz, 2025) to identify the interactions of place, power, and learning. In this paper, I describe a specific case (Yin, 2018) of Denise to illuminate how these facilitators view injustices as 2242 they come up in their work, how these interact with their goals and values in advocating for more just built environments, and how various dominant economic and political systems shape these processes. In her interview, Denise described a situation in which the justice-centered goals of a program for middle schoolers learning about the built environment was in conflict with the materials being used. The classroom in which the activity took place was designed to be an affirming space within the school day for students from historically marginalized communities. After a lesson on local history, including various injustices during colonization and industrial development, students were given the following prompt, in English and Spanish: “Create a collage, mind map, or draw a part of [your city] that’s important/familiar to you. Include elements from the past and the present.” One of the main materials students used for this activity were architecture and landscape architecture magazines. Denise, a graduate student in landscape architecture, noted in their interview the disconnect between these magazine images, the history presente d earlier in the activity, and these students’ communities (both lived and imagined). Denise said: I just noticed when the kids were collaging that one of the girls was like, “These houses are so nice,” and they found all these dogs just sitting around, like, “I guess I'll just put dogs in my collage.” And in my head I was like, oh yeah, all of our maga zines are donated from people in the built environment [professions].

Denise’s realization stemmed from a conflict between built environment professionalization (the source of the magazines) and histories of unjust housing that the students have experienced (the ability to buy a home and tailor it is bound up in race-and class-based inequities). While the facilitator and the program were explicit in their pursuit of justice-centered learning about the built environment, the activity was nonetheless tied up with systems of place-centric oppression.

Pedagogically, one impact of these materials was that the student in Denise’s quotation decided her collage would simply mirror what she saw in the magazine pictures. However, the goal here was for students to be intentional about how they designed their c ollage; the activity’s directions suggested they create something “important/familiar to you.” Cutting out images from a source whose audience is largely wealthy and white is at odds with this goal. To be fair, a creative activity like this should be open to unexpected ideas, and students are not required to have a strong justification for every choice. However, the way in which the student seemed to acquiesce to the materials was not aligned to the purpose of exploring meaningful connections to local places and histories.

While Denise’s story is rooted in tangible learning materials, it is inseparable from structural factors that Denise realized after reflection. In similar ways, other facilitators in these interviews shared experiences in which seemingly small moments reve aled place-centered injustices with more structural origins. This includes connections between personal identities, funding sources, and government agencies. As a whole, these interviews shed light on how the concepts from critical geographies discussed above are revealed in learning environments aimed at addressing place-centered injustices. By putting these ten facilitators in conversation with critical geographies, this paper maps salient tensions that practitioners and advocates are encountering as they facilitate place-conscious learning across very different contexts. Linking places: How prior knowledges of place shape mobile epistemologies in a spatial justice course Suraj Uttamchandani, Matthew X. Curinga We examine a field trip during an undergraduate first year seminar taught by the authors dedicated to the study of spatial justice (Soja, 2010) in order to illuminate how seeing new places as linked to old ones shaped students’ understandings in both positive and negative ways. During class, students examined and would eventually design data-driven maps of New York City and Long Island. K. Taylor (2020) positions spatial epistemologies as including both grid epistemologies (official, map-based representatio ns) and mobile epistemologies (“ground truths” that follow from embodied experience in places). The field trip was meant as an opportunity for students to compare and contrast their grid epistemologies with an on-the-ground experience. However, the field t rip revealed the ways that the experience of “the city” during the field trip was shaped by individual histories in ways that created unexpectedly varied outcomes. On the field trip, students attended a museum exhibit, debriefed in Central Park, and walked as a group 20 blocks down Lexington Avenue from East Harlem to the Upper East Side. We positioned the walk as a transition between neighborhoods and encouraged students to look for signs of such a transition. We build on Massey’s (1991) conceptualization of place as “articulated moments in networks of social relations and understandings…which includes a consciousness of its links with the wider world” (p. 28) to 2243 show how two students understood their experience on the field trip by linking to prior understandings of place and prior experiences of other places. Analytically, we engage in case studies (Yin, 2009) of two students. More specifically, we perform a discourse analysis (Wood & Kroger, 2000) of their reflective artifacts. Adelaide grew up in Queens. She described where she lived as “a safe and friendly neighborhood” that included “a ton of great restaurants owned by a variety of cultures” and a nearby park as having a “beautiful view” and being “a great place to run on the tracks.” After the trip, Adelaide contrasted Harlem and the Upper East side along many dimensions: in terms of class/poverty, history, housing costs, and types of businesses. But when asked why this learning mattered, she wrote that “it is important to be  aware of your surroundings” and that “from my experience I would be safer in the [Upper East Side] if I was alone at night than Harlem.” Despite beginning with a positive conception of the city, Adelaide’s conclusions about the field trip became grounded in her prior experience and framework of safety, appealing to the hypothetical of walking alone at night to understand her trip experience even though that was during the day and with a group. Desiree offers a contrasting case. She grew up in Brooklyn but had then moved around Long Island. In her first reflection, her only discussion of the city was that she had always wanted to play a particular sport (anonymized for confidentiality) and that was “something my mom found hard for me to do in the city.” Desiree wrote of Harlem, “I guess I expected it to be less calm. The walk…was very calm, no beeping horns, or mentally untreated people yelling at you.” She noticed a difference in the diversity and type of food options available in the twoneighborhoods, with Harlem having “more authentically small businesses of different ethnicities (like food)” whereas in the Upper East Side “turned into these italian restaurants or aesthetically tourist pleasin g restaurants.” She made connections to her own experiences in the suburbs where, despite living near a highlytrafficked and well-off main street, she had to travel far for “ethnic food.” She wanted to know more about why proprietors would choose to set up in poorer neighborhoods. We interpret this as demonstrating that Desiree had not yet made the connection to differing rents in different neighborhoods, but that she had become curious about further exploration of this issue. Desiree’s initial view of the  city (as lacking space and as noisy) supported a positive conception of Harlem and those working in it while also prompting questions about her own hometown. Our field trip was meant to support students’ understanding of spatial justice through walking. Both Adelaide and Desiree connected their experience on the field trip to their prior understandings of the neighborhood, the city, and their home neighborhoods. In this way, both students developed nascent conceptions of places as linked (Massey, 1991). However, those links also made them see the same walk extremely differently: Desiree’s negative initial conceptions were a basis for more positive and curious views, whereas Adelaide’s positive initial conceptions lead her to relatively narrow positioning about safety. This contribution illustrates how mobile epistemologies become highly individualized, as compared to grid epistemologies, and suggests design opportunities on how limited or even negative prior experiences with place (whether the walked neighborhood or one’s hometown) can actually open up opportunities for critical perceptions of place (Gruenwald, 2003a; K. Taylor, 2020).

“Place as...”: Tensions in conceptualizing place in math walks informal learning research Saki Milton, Candace Walkington “I think my attitude for math has gotten, like a little bit more interested...I’m really more excited going here. Because I’ve come to learn and come to socialize with my friends. And I’m learning more things about the community and its culture.” [emphasis added] When Sofia, a 7th-grade participant, describes her experience with math walks at “the Center,” her words raise critical questions in place-based mathematics education set in informal settings: What mathematical engagement, interest, and attitude development do we see when place is viewed as a “novel container” in which mathematical activity occurs? What mathematical engagement, interest, and attitude development do we see when place is viewed as a “socially constructed space” where learners leverage their community and cultural capital to engage in mathematics? This eight-month qualitative case study (Creswell & Poth, 2018) draws on interview, video, focus group, and learner artifact data to compare two distinct conceptualizations of place: interest theory versus critical theories of place-based cultural identity.

In the MathExplorer Project theory of change, place is framed through interest theory (Hidi & Renninger, 2006), where informal sites serve as neutral backdrops where novelty, salience, and personal relevance trigger initial engagement. Student-authored walk stops, peer collaboration, and augmented reality tools in a mobile gaming app maintain this situational interest leading to positive shifts in learners’ mathematical attitudes (Ainley et al., 2002; Harackiewicz et al., 2008). Framed through design-based implementation research, this theory of 2244 change continuously evolves, co-developed by researchers, learners, and practitioners as project activities unfold. This conceptualization offers several advantages for researchers and educators: measurable attitudinal outcomes (e.g., increased individual interest), accessible entry points through technology and personalized learning, and opportunities for mathematical problem-posing (Silver, 1994). However, this framing treats the Center’s cultural significance—as a social services hub in a historically Black neighborhood (80% Black, 20% Latine, $39,486 median income) featuring a civil rights museum and gathering space—as secondary to its utility for  mathematical exploration. Place, thus, becomes interchangeable; any informal site with similar pedagogical features could theoretically produce comparable interest development. While the framework acknowledges student background characteristics, it does not theorize how learners’ existing relationships with specific places might mediate mathematical engagement. Place is viewed as static container rather than socially constructed space, obscuring cultural meanings and histories embedded in local environments. Several critical theories, including Community Cultural Wealth (Yosso, 2005), counterspaces (Solórzano et al., 2000), and rightful presence (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2019), help reconceptualize place as a repository that recognizes cultural meaning and learner belonging. Findings show that participants used the Center not just to “do math,” but to reimagine what math could be and who it could be for. Learners selected walk stop locations, including a statue of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., a community mural, and the Center’s outdoor stage, and posed math questions (Silver, 1994) in the app: “What is the ratio of the bricks to the holes in the mural wall?” or “How does the statue’s height compare to Dr. King’s actual height?” This reframing reveals the Center as a “pedagogy of place,” where sociological, political, and ecological dimensions connect learners’ experiences to their local environments (Gruenewald, 2003a; Sobel, 2005), situating culture, community, and local histories as valuable entry points into math learning (Lee, 2017). Math walks became counterspaces, where dominant norms were challenged. At the same time, inadequate Wi-Fi and unsafe campus areas mirrored broader structural barriers, surfacing new questions about how place is resourced, accessed, and made visible in under-resourced communities. These competing conceptualizations of place created productive tensions. When learners expressed excitement about math, was that interest triggered by novelty or rooted in cultural affirmation? When they mathematized landmarks, were they pursuing individua l curiosity or reflecting on a historical cultural icon? Interest theory helped explain the design mechanics; critical theories helped explain why those mechanics resonated differently across learners and walk stop locations. Math walks offer a powerful case for layered conceptualizations of place in informal math learning. The enhanced theory of change integrates both framings, using critical theories to inform not only what learners do in place, but how place itself is constructed, experienced, and made meaningful.

Youth engaging the intersectional Black spatial imaginary through design Kaleb Germinaro This paper examines how five youth in Seattle’s Central District and Southend participated in an architectural design-build project that centered Black spatial histories as they were designing a space for a deaf-serving organization. Allen et al. (2019) “position black geographies not as a catch-all for all geographies of race but instead as a term encompassing works that emphasize black experiences, as well as alternative visions and articulations of space drawn from these experiences” (p. 43). Working from this definition, I examine how youth learn through the layered and intersectional spatial histories of the Black community, both as a pedagogical and methodological point of departure for the learning environment that invokes the Black spatial imaginary. Where Soja (2010; 2013) discusses injustice in abstract or structural terms, Black geographies and the spatial imaginary focus explicitly on addressing how anti-Blackness structures space – from plantation geographies to carceral landscapes. Spatial imagin aries, both physically and figuratively, have shifted ideologies that disrupt how one engages and conceptualizes relations to various places. Watkins (2015) explained that spatial imaginaries have discursive agency and power, “(re)producing, and changing, social perceptions about places even among those whom have never been there” (p. 509). Dominant spatial imaginaries serve as discursive frames that guide social and political trends affecting action (Watkins, 2015). As Nickson (2021) notes, “although ideologically and materially powerful, dominant spatial imaginaries are not the only way to view space and resources” (p. 305). Using critical ethnography, this study documents key activities and moments where students leveraged their spatial knowledge and relationships to design for their own communities while invoking an intersectional Black spatial imaginary. Ethnographic observations and analysis focus on how students engaged with the histories and present-day realities of their relations to these neighborhoods during the design process; identifying what’s missing and what’s possible. An analysis of how students engage a Black spatial imaginary over the program helps us understand how they disrupt anti-Blackness and displacement 2245 of communities and their stories through their experiences; ensuring future possibilities. Using the constant comparative method (Glaser & Strauss, 1967), I engaged ongoing categories as themes crystallized and their validity verified, or not, through referencing videos, interviews, natural conversations, field notes, and artifacts. Rethinking safe learning environments in terms of the “right to opacity” Elizabeth de Freitas, Matthew X. Curinga This paper reports on an interdisciplinary project, collaborating with practitioners in architecture, which explored the affective atmosphere in NYC school buildings. Space is at an absolute premium in NYC; the small high school movement, begun in the early 2000s, collocated  multiple schools in old and often dilapidated buildings, where they compete for resources with charter schools (Kurgan, 2005; Meyer, 2015). These evolving intricate spatial arrangements involve contested territory but also allow for interesting hybrid uses of space (Kurgan, 2013, 2017). Following K. Taylor and Phillips (2017), we seek to show how a sense of place and belonging is produced in unexpected ways in these school buildings. We stay focused on one school building in NYC, originally built in 1975 using an unusual triangle geometry (Figure 1). The building had been part of massive changes occurring in school policy in NYC in the 1970s, in part due to racial and economic disparities, as well as increasing concern about school violence (Ravitch, 2019). The school building is located adjacent to NYC Police Headquarters. This intensely policed location means that the building is surrounded by police vehicles and police security watch posts. Surveillance cameras and police officers control the movement of students around the school. 2246 In addition to walking tours, sensory ethnographic methods, and interviews with staff, we draw on research methods from architects and designers, and in particular the methods of “investigative aesthetics” articulated by Fuller and Weizman (2021). This approach reclaims computational techniques and repurposes software to serve social justice causes (de Freitas et al, 2019). Fuller and Weizman (2021) describe various software methods that expose contested territories which are not properly reported or represented by regular data their method of using software to extract and visualize digital data pertaining to social justice, with an emphasis on the role of speculation when synthesizing data (see also de Freitas & Trafi-Prats, 2024). This approach pursues “hyper-aesthetics” to expose the hidden spatial experience, explaining that “to hyper-aestheticise is to heighten, elicit or exacerbate the capacity of bodies, technologies or states of matter to sense and increase perceptual experience; it entails an increase in sensitivity and can perhaps augment a capacity to care” (Fuller & Weizman, 2021, p.58). While this approach opens up new kinds of visibility, we are also focused on how a “right to opacity” is crucial in negotiating that experience (Glissant, 1997). Figure 1 A Satellite Image of the School (Left) and a Sketch of the School from the Authors’ Field Notes (Right) In our case study, we solicited submerged student feelings about the lived architecture using software experiments, in which models of the school building were cast in a digital game aesthetic, tapping fantasy and speculative fiction imaginaries. The game model simulated the familiar school, but defamiliarized the building through the game aesthetic, evoking an eerie and bizarre version of the school, and inviting students to modify it, using options that were surreal and abstract (Figure 2). We deliberatel y chose not to offer realistic features, in order to invite the students into a more speculative mode of engagement. The game aesthetic allowed students to dramatize their movement using an avatar, and express their emotion and embodied experience of navigating the space.

Figure 2 Game Model Built in Unity 3D Software By tracing the flow of student paths, and lines of sight, across this complex geometric space, patterns of movement and visibility emerged, revealing the importance of a triangular labyrinth that linked different spaces within the school. The data suggests students experience the school as a warren-like structure, where crisscrossing paths form sites of intensive affect (Figure 3).

2247 Students used the software to re-imagine the fortress-like school building (with its turrets and narrow slit windows) not as a prison – surprisingly – but as a warren-like safe-space. Students reclaimed the interior space as a secret den of hidden paths, a complex labyrinth where they could claim their “right to opacity” in the midst of the police-security watch towers and the intense surveillance saturating the school location. We posit that the dynamic software models generated collaboratively by the res earchers, the students, and the architects, make visible some of the complex ways that relationality, mobility, and visibility are linked to place consciousness. In addition, our findings complicate some of the more conventional approaches to designing and maintaining safe learning environments, by exposing the ways in which tensions between urban youth and police are manifest in the built environment, where young people forge distinctive ways for resisting and modulating containment, control, and surveillance.
