# Students’ Traversing between the Real and Mathematical World through Diagrams

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Diagrams’ role in mathematical reasoning is well-studied. However, their role as cognitive artifacts in the process of triggering the modeling is yet to be fully characterised. The modeling process involves multiple nonlinear steps that connect the real a nd mathematical worlds, ranging from understanding, simplifying, mathematising, working mathematically, interpreting, validating and exposing. We investigate the role of cognitive artifacts in enabling this process in learners. We observe groups of school students in grades-4th to 7th while they make a scaled map of their surroundings, a task that requires them to model actively. We analyse the role of diagrams they make, as cognitive artifacts in shaping their modeling process, by specifically highlighting how diagrams trigger or enable specific modeling steps. The findings can help in the purposive usage of diagrams as pedagogical tools in bringing real-world contexts into mathematics classrooms in a systematic way.

STEM practice involves engaging with theories, laws about phenomena, which are usually cognitively inaccessible (Pande & Chandrasekharan 2017). These are considered model-building activities, forming the core of the scientific practice (Suppe 1989). The phenomena in the real world are made accessible by representations of multiple kinds (Knuuttila 2011). Mathematics provides the necessary language to represent these models. Hestenes (2010) builds on this, primarily in the context of STEM learning. Mathematical modeling is the process of describing a real system by making assumptions, establishing appropriate mathematical relationships, interpreting the results and checking the validity of the same against the real situation at hand, to create sharable and manipulatable mathematical models as cognitive tools (Blum, 2011). Mathematical modeling could facilitate learners to playfully experiment with their own assumptions and the mathematical constructs to make sense of real-world phenomena in an integrated fashion (Brady & Jung, 2021), hence also expanding their view towards mathematics beyond linear computations (Lehrer & Pritchard, 2002). Transitioning between the real and mathematical world is an important step in the mathematical modeling process. Two differe nt conceptions exist in the literature on students’ connections made between the two worlds during their mathematical modeling process. In the older conception this connection was conceptualized as a direct translation as (S; M; R) where S is the situation to be modeled, M, is the external representation in mathematical terms (e.g., graphical, symbolic, or verbal representations), and R is a relation that maps the objects and relationships found in S to the mathematical objects and relationships (Blum, 2011). Whereas Schwarzkopf (2007) talks about interplay between mathematics and the real world from a symbolic interactionism lens as situated within the classroom interaction process, and through a common understanding constructed through different framings from empirical enrichment and the mathematical structure of the word problem.

Only recent studies have explored the influence of the learning context such as types of modeling tasks (Jung & Brady, 2023), settings as classroom vs summer camp (Brady & Jung, 2021) and modes as pictures, 3D models, and real settings (Jablonski, 2023) on  students’ modeling processes. We take a 4E cognitive perspective to undertake the students’ cognitive processes emerging in the interactions with artifacts (a part of the learning context) during their transitioning between the real and mathematical world while doing mathematical modeling tasks.

Cognitive perspective on mathematical modeling We are taking cognitive perspective on mathematical modeling (Blum & Leiss, 2007; Ferri, 2006) as we are interested in understanding students’ cognitive processes happening due to their interaction with the artifacts while they are doing a mathematical mod eling task. Cognitive perspective on mathematical modeling (Blum & Leiss, 2007; Ferri, 2006) explains the modeling process through a modeling cycle comprising two worlds-real and mathematical worlds and seven sub-stages. Going from one stage to another is  known as a modeling action happening during the mathematical modeling process. There are seven such modeling actions-Understanding, Simplifying, Mathematising, Working Mathematically, Interpreting, Validating and Exposing. For our analysis, 2066 we have focused upon these modeling actions, specifically those connecting the real and mathematical world (Blum & Leiss, 2007).

Cognitive artifacts and diagrams and their affordances for modeling 4E cognitive framework provides an overarching paradigm emphasizing the role of artifacts and the environment in shaping human cognition (e.g. Menary 2010). In the case of STEM, these artifacts can be called cognitive artifacts as they fundamentally affect  the way human beings process the entire information. A cognitive artifact is an artificial devic e designed to maintain, display, or operate upon information in order to serve a representational function, by modifying the original cognitive task or creating a new one (Fasoli, 2017). We are taking the definition of ‘Diagrams’ as defined by Pierce & Doerfler, according to which diagrams are any type of inscription constituting a system of operations on it. Eg: Matrix (Dorfler, 2005). Under this notion mathematical activity is not just a  mental activity, but is empirical and perceptual. And diagrams are not just representations of students’ mental mathematical processes but objects of mathematical reasoning themselves, where the operations on them are perceptual and facilitate reasoning. We have taken this notion of diagrams to see how it is acting as a cognitive artifact in influencing students' modeling actions during their mathematical modeling process.

Research objective Our study specifically investigates the role of such cognitive artifacts in shaping the learners’ modeling process. Diagrams are recognised to be an important form of representation in modeling. So our research question is- “How do diagrams serve as a cogn itive artifact in influencing students’ transition between the real and mathematical world while they are doing a mathematical modeling task?” In this paper, we use a series of observations of grades-4th to 7th students engaged in tasks requiring modeling, and specifically analyse the role of diagrams as cognitive artifacts in one or many modeling steps. This could help in better and deliberate design of lessons and experiences for triggering mathematical modeling in learners.

## Research design and methods

We addressed the above research question in the context of a larger PhD study. This work is a part of a series of pilot studies conducted with students in the grade range-4th to 7th, who worked together in groups for the grade appropriate “Map Modeling Task” from Lehrer & Pritchard (2002). ● The first pilot study was conducted in a lab with one group of 3 students. They were asked to redesign a portion of the provided existing IIT Bombay campus map into a scaled map, which could be used by their new classmates for navigation, with the help of dynamic Google Maps as a supporting technological tool.

● Addressing the limitations from the first pilot study, the second study was conducted by changing the task to redesigning their room by creating a floor plan, so as to integrate the actual measurement process as well. This was conducted with two groups, girls and boys groups with 4 students in each group. They were provided with the required artifacts such as Length measurement tools-Ruler, Meter tape, Inch tape; Grid paper; Exemplar Map & Floor Plan.

The study data consisted of videos, screen & audio recordings through tablets, students’ map drawings and writings, researchers’ reflection notes and students’ post study interviews. The video data was analyzed with a qualitative analysis approach and other sources of data were used for triangulation. Principles of the interaction artifacts. The four Interaction Analysis Focis-Structure of Events, Artifacts & Documents, Trouble & Repair and Temporal Organization of Activity were used throughout in the analysis. Each episode represents a transition between the real and mathematical world, go ing back or forth or both between the two worlds, while interacting with the cognitive artifacts. Each turn, including speech, gestures and actions on objects, were taken as the unit of analysis and for coding the data. For coding modeling a ctions, their definitions (Blum and Lei ss, 2007; Ferri,

2006) and indicators (Brady & Jung, 2021) were used from Cognitive Perspective on Mathematical Modeling.

For coding cognitive artifacts definition by Fasoli (2017), and for diagrams the definition and explanations by Dorfler (2005) were used.

Diagrams helping in simplification and mathematisation steps Using diagrams the learners are focusing on the relevant aspects of the real world, idealizing and removing the non relevant things to accomplish the task. When the students were asked to make a floor plan of their room, the girls’ group started by first creating a relative map drawing of the room (Fig-1). And afterwards they measured the different objects in the room which were there in their drawing one by one, and wrote down their dimensions alongside the respective drawings of each object.: Figure 1 Initial Map Drawing Created by Girls’ Group From this drawing, we could infer that the girls’ group simplified the real-world object into a simple set of geometric shapes. The above drawing depicts both their selection of objects to be included for their map like the door, desks, table, white board etc. while ignoring others like the objects on the table, such as duster, their own bags, books etc., depicting their simplification modeling action. And also the various objects are represented in the form of 2D mathematical shapes, mostly rectangles, dep icting their mathematising modeling action as the real 3D objects were perceived and abstracted as 2D mathematical shapes. Diagrams are helping in anchoring their measurements Learners are measuring various quantities based on the diagrams that they have made. Whatever they have measured is coming directly from what they have drawn. They did not measure anything which they hadn’t drawn. For instance, when the girls’ group was measuring the different parts of the chair, like the back, seat, etc., one of the girls N, started measuring its legs as well, upon which the other girl S, stopped her saying that they didn’t draw that in their map drawing, and all of them started laughing at N. Also, they measured not all the dimensions of these objects but only the ones they drew in their map drawing. The above is an example when the model changes the interpretation of the real world, that is, the girls were perceiving and measuring the obj ects in the room using their drawing model, which guided their appropriate actions while working with real objects. Diagrams, in turn, trigger other relevant epistemological resources While measuring the dimensions of the whiteboard on the wall, one of the girls, P, raised a concern about measuring its upper border line (breadth) since they were unable to reach there, upon which S suggested that it would be the same as that of the lower  one. Hence, the trouble in the real world, that is, the inability to reach an object that needs to be measured, was resolved using an abstraction from the mathematical world, that is, perceiving the board as a mathematical shape of a rectangle as depicted  through their drawing, and using its property of equal opposite sides for their effective measurement. So students’ other epistemological resources, such as their understanding of the properties of a rectangle, were deployed for their efficient measurement. Two different distance measuring features in the Google Maps-1) Directions, allowing measuring distance by putting initial location and destination, & 2) Adding Pins, enabling dropping pins and measuring straight line distance (Fig-2), were used by the students for calculating distance between places. While looking and matching the distance figures in each other’s route models, P started asking questions to S on how he got the distance figures for various parts of the route - “How much are you getting till KV school? 380 metres?”, “But how? Did you measure through the road?”, “Tell me first from where to where you have taken?” the origin point, distance as length between two points and that it should be calculated via a laid path in the context of the map emerged while interpreting and validating different representations as created by the two students for the same route and including different distance figures. We have found intermediate diagrams created by the students in mediating students’ transition between the real and the mathematical world during their mathematical modeling process. As elaborated with the two instances, one in which the mismatch in the mathematical world, when the students got two different distance figures for the same path, was resolved through the real aspect of the distance in the context of a map to be measured via road and not directly. And the other one in which the trouble in the real world, when the students were unable to reach at the upper breadth of the board on the wall, was resolved using a mathematical abstraction of equal opposite sides of the rectangle. In both these instances the modeling actions associated with the transitioning between the two worlds were influenced by the various aspects of the intermediate diagrams, their role in communicating each other’s simplified models of the situation, multiple diagrams together with their different affordances. Hence during space modeling, diagrams are found to be integrating both the real and mathematical aspects of measuring. So measurement which could otherwise belong only to the mathematical world is consistently being linked with the real world through the intermediate diagrams created by the students. In the first pilot study when the two students found different distance figures for the same route in their inscriptions, they started questioning their measurement process, and hence interpreted the numerical figures with respect to the real aspects of the distance like reference to the origin point, distance as length between two points and that it should be calculated via laid path in the context of map. In the second pilot study we found that their measurement of various objects in the room was highly influenced by the simplified map drawing created by them. It leads to their effective measurement by directing them in what objects to measure in the room. Confirming the notion of models as not only a product of the modeling process, but intermediate diagrams as models which also influence their perception towards the real world during modeling. Within mathematical modeling literature Brady (2018) also claimed about the role of cartesian representations as a modeling resource and hence supporting the bidirectionality between the real and mathematical worlds. With our work beyond standard represent ations, we are also trying to exhibit the significance of intermediate students’ created diagrams in influencing their cognitive processes during mathematical modeling.  The role of diagrams as objects of mathematical reasoning (Dorfler, 2001 & 2005) is well known, however its role in mathematical modeling is not very well characterised. Furthermore, these can be found to be supported by works emerging from the 4E paradigm as well such as the one which looks at affordances of representational media for geometric manipulations (Karnam et al., 2020 & 2021).
