# Integrating the Learning Sciences into Technical Infrastructure for the Future of AI in Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly influential in education, attention must extend beyond AI applications to the technical infrastructure on which those applications depend. Datasets, models, benchmarks, and governance mechanisms are already shaping the future of educational AI, yet these foundational resources are often developed without sufficient engagement with the learning sciences. This workshop explores how learning scientists can contribute to the design of such infrastructural public goods in ways that better reflect what is known about learning, while also addressing concerns related to safety, privacy, ethics, and equity. By bringing together researchers working at the intersection of AI and learning, the workshop seeks to identify promising lines of work, articulate key tensions, and stimulate future collaboration on learning-science-informed AI infrastructure. Overview The learning sciences offer strong conceptual foundations for the design of educational AI, drawing on longstanding research on scaffolding, feedback, self-regulation, and collaboration (Quintana et al., 2004; Hattie & Timperley, 2007; Järvelä et al., 2016; Sawyer, 2022). Yet these principles still only lightly shape the datasets, benchmarks, and model documentation on which AI systems depend. Training data rarely reflect what is known about productive learning interactions; benchmarks tend to measure task completion or factual accuracy rather than the quality of scaffolding or the depth of learner understanding; and model documentation seldom describes how a system's design relates to evidence on how people learn. Recent work by major AI developers signals growing interest in grounding educational AI in learning sciences principles (Jurenka et al., 2024; LearnLM Team, Google, 2025), but without more intentional integration into underlying infrastructure, educational AI risks prioritizing efficiency or engagement over meaningful support for learning. As past waves of "personalized learning" have shown, technology that optimizes for engagement without attending to how learning works can reproduce rather than address longstanding inequities in educational opportunity. This challenge is particularly pressing as new efforts seek to build openly shared educational AI resources as public goods. The K–12 AI Infrastructure Initiative, a five-year, $26 million effort led by Digital Promise and a coalition of partners, is funding the development of tutoring interaction corpora, learning-aligned benchmarks, and model documentation frameworks that embed pedagogical principles by design (Digital Promise, 2025; Roschelle et al., 2025). For such resources to genuinely advance learning, learning scientists must be actively involved in their co-design alongside AI engineers, education practitioners, and policymakers — contributing not only domain knowledge but also the critical perspectives needed to navigate questions of safety, privacy, ethics, and equity. This workshop therefore explores how the learning sciences can be systematically integrated into these infrastructural public goods. It brings together learning scientists, AI developers, education practitioners, and funders to examine where and how learning sciences expertise is most needed in the development of technical infrastructure for educational AI. Its goals are to inform participants about emerging infrastructure efforts and the role learning sciences can play in shaping them, create space for participants to share current work and articulate concerns, identify promising directions for future research and investment, and stimulate the formation of cross-sector collaborations. Building on recent ISLS programming on AI and education (International Society of the Learning Sciences, 2022, 2024), the workshop positions the learning sciences not only as a field that evaluates AI applications after they are built, but as one that can help shape the foundations on which educational AI is constructed. Description of the event The GoGo Board was invented in 2001 as a low-cost and learner-friendly robotics and scientific sensing platform. In the years since, the project has undergone several design iterations, each affording new possibilities for students across diverse ages and backgrounds to explore powerful ideas in computing and STEM. In this workshop, participants will also engage with GoGoData (2025), a new open-source plugin that connects the GoGo Board to the Common Online Data Analysis Platform (CODAP) for real-time data communication and visualization. CODAP, developed by the Concord Consortium, has a long-standing history as an open-source environment for data analysis and modeling that supports inquiry-based learning. Together, GoGoData, the GoGo Board Kit version 7, and CODAP create a participatory sensing ecosystem that enables students to collect, share, and interpret livedata while examining the learning sciences principles underlying the design of these tools [1-3]. Workshop Format The workshop will be organized into five main activities. I-Historical framing. Participants will begin with a brief discussion on the evolution of tangible interfaces within the Learning Sciences and basic education contexts. This discussion will be situated alongside the rise of big data practices and participatory or crowd sensing, showing how learner-generated, real-time data from distributed sensors can reshape inquiry, evidence making, and classroom discourse. The framing will surface design lessons on openness, transparency, and agency, linking data practices to issues of provenance, privacy, and ethics, and highlighting how collaborative data networks can support critical engagement and community action. II-Exploring the GoGo Board 7 / GoGoData / CODAP. Participants will explore the core features of the GoGo Board version 7 and the GoGoData plugin, examining how each component emerged from research data and i terative design decisions. Using onboard controls, they will visualize sensor values, directly before connecting to a computer to interact with GoGoData and its integration with the Common Online Data Analysis Platform (CODAP). Through this connection, participants will experience how real-time sensor data can be collected, transmitted, analyzed, and explored within CODAP’s interactive environment. Facilitators will highlight how each feature embodies specific design principles that link tangible experimentation with data-driven inquiry. III-Participatory sensing exploration. Participants will collaboratively select a variable to measure, such as temperature, luminosity, pollution, or sound level, using a GoGo Board 7 kit sensor. Working in small groups, they will connect the GoGo Board to CODAP through the GoGoData plugin and deploy sensors in different internet-connected areas across the UCI campus. Each group will collect and share real-time data through the open-source plugin integrated with the data analysis platform, contributing to a collaborative network of distributed measurements. As data accumulate, participants will analyze emerging patterns across locations, drawing inferences about environmental or contextual factors. The activity will conclude with a collective discussion where groups interpret their findings, reflect on methodological decisions, and connect their insights to broader perspectives on participatory sensing, open data collaboration, and critical data literacy in education. IV-Conclusion and gallery walk. Participants will share and discuss the outcomes of their activities, reflecting on the lessons learned and insights gained throughout the workshop. The session will conclude with a collective show-and-tell, allowing groups to present their findings, highlight key observations from the participatory sensing exercise, and connect these experiences to broader questions about learning, design, and data literacy. V-Post-workshop interaction. Optional follow-up opportunities will be provided for participants who wish to continue collaborating after the session. These interactions may include online discussions, exchange of design ideas, and updates on future developments of the GoGoData platform. Participants will be invited to share reflections, resources, and potential research connections that extend the learning and collaboration initiated during the workshop. Throughout the workshop, facilitators will guide discussions connecting the design of GoGoData, the GoGo Board, and CODAP to learning sciences and constructionist frameworks, emphasizing how participatory sensing and data exploration are enhanced by open-source ecosystems. The session will highlight how openness, transparency, and accessibility in these tools empower learners and researchers to collaboratively collect, analyze, and interpret real-time data. Rather than serving as a technical training, the workshop offers a hands-on exploration of how open, theory-informed technologies developed through over twenty years of design-based research can foster agency, collaboration, and critical engagement with data. In this half-day workshop, you will engage with GoGoData (2025), a new open-source plugin that links the GoGo Board microcontroller, sensors, and Internet of Things (IoT) communication to the Common Online Data Analysis Platform (CODAP) for real-time data exploration. Together, these tools create a participatory sensing ecosystem where learners and researchers can collaboratively collect, share, and interpret livedata (Figer 1). Beyond experimenting with the platform’s features, you will analyze GoGoData as a design case informed by constructionist and learning sciences perspectives, building on over two decades of iterative design research with the GoGo Board. This workshop offers a unique opportunity to experience how open-source technologies can empower communities to transform local data into meaningful insights and to apply these theoretical and design perspectives to your own educational research and development projects. Workshop format The workshop is planned for in-person delivery, with the option of hybrid participation if necessary. The program will include interactive, hands-on activities supported by remote contributions from members of the international workshop team, ensuring collaboration and engagement across locations.
