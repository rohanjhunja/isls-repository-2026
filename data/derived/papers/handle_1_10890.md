# Exploring Student Perceptions of Group Quizzes: Interconnections with Learning Theories and Implications for Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores learner experiences of group quizzes in a higher education mathematics class, grounded in learning theories including cognitive constructivism, social constructivism, situated learning theory, and self-determination theory. Through network analysis we identified four distinct clusters of student experiences and theory. Our findings underline the value of group quizzes, suggesting that a collaborative approach benefits many students. However, some clusters expressed neutrality or preference towards individual quizzes, highlighting the need for a hybrid approach. This research has broad implications for designing inclusive learning strategies, advocating for a blend of individual and collaborative activities to address different student needs and dispositions.

## Background and theoretical framework

Collaborative learning has been widely recognized a s a valuable approach in educational settings, prom oting student engagement, knowledge construction, and soc ial interaction (Miyake & Kirschner, 2014; Scager et al., 2016). Through collaborative activities, students e ngage in dialogue, negotiate meaning, and build the ir understanding of concepts and problem-solving strategies  (Vygotsky, 1978). Group quizzes can provide opportunities for students to actively engage with their peers, share perspectives, and c ollectively construct knowledge. Group quizzes can support cognitive cons tructivist principles by exposing students to diver se approaches and solutions, encouraging reflection on  collaboration within group settings, and fostering engagement with different perspectives (Brown & Cam pione, 1994; Piaget, 1977). Group quizzes can provide students with opportunities to engage in authentic problem-solving tasks, apply their knowledge in practical situations, and learn from the collective expertise of their peers (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Intrinsic motivation may also play a role (Deci & Ryan, 2008).

## Methods

This mixed-methods study analyzed learner experiences in an in troductory mathematics course at a Research 1 university consisting of 100 students where individual quizzes were used for formative assessment, as well as group quizzes in which students were put in small groups of 3-4 people in order to take the quizzes collaboratively. At the end of the semester, students were asked to reflect on their learning experiences in a form of a survey with Likert questions and open-ended questions asking them to refl ect on their learning experience. The qualitative data was coded for principles from the learning theories in which we grounded our research. The Likert questions were auto-coded. To explore the relationships and patterns wi thin the coded data, we calculat ed Pearson's correlations for all pairs of codes. These correlation matrices were then exported for further analysis. By applying Girvan-Newman cluster analysis, we identified cohesive clu sters of codes, and we also calculated betweenness centrality measures to determine the significance of individual codes within the network.

## Findings

Our research examined the quantitative Likert quest ions and alignment of learner experiences with prin ciples from learning theories within a significance level of P < 0.05, and clustered with a Q = 0.580. Students with experiences aligned with the blue cluster  (90%) expressed neutrality towards the effectiveness of i ndividual quizzes compared to group quizzes, while also agreeing that group quizzes and collaboration aid in learning. Their approach aligned with the cognitive constructivist theory principle of diverse approaches and solutions, reflection on collaboration within group settings, diverse per spectives, and reflection on time management, as we ll as the social constructivist theory principle of engaging with other perspective s. They enjoyed collaborating and engaging with different viewpoints. The black cluster (70%) strongly agreed that group quizzes and collaboration are effective in aiding learning, while disagreeing with the effectiveness of individual quizzes. Their experiences aligned with all four of the learning theories outl ined. These students engaged in the community of pr actice (situated learning theory), peers simplifying elements of the task (social constructivist learning theory), confidence (self-determination theory), and focusing on process inst ead of results (self-determination theory). Learning and practice is important to these students who strongl y agree with collaboration.  The gray cluster (55%) expressed neutrality towards the effectiveness of individual quizzes while also agreeing that group quizzes and collaboration aid in learning. Their approach aligned with the co gnitive constructivist theory principles of individual construction of knowledge, diverse  pathways, and reflection on communication and inter action. It also aligned with the social constructivist theory principles of co-construction of knowledge and peer scaffolding. This group of students sought group quizzes as knowledge consolidation as they were able to utilize the group setting to their advantage. The red cluster (45%) strongly agreed that individual quizzes are effecti ve in aiding learning, while disagreeing with the effectiveness of group quizzes  and collaboration. Their approach aligned with all  four learning theories, including the situated learning theory principle of developing interpersonal relationships, the self-determination theory principle of becoming more com fortable with their own abilities, the cognitive constructivist principle of productive failure, and the social constructivist principle of understanding peers.

## Implications and conclusion

This study used network analysis to examine student  reflections on group quizzes in a higher education mathematics class. The findings revealed that different students' experiences align with various learning theories, including cognitive constructivism, social constructivism, situated learning theory, and self-determination theory. This confirms that there is no single "right" learn ing theory and that experiences aligning with diffe rent theories can co-exist within a diverse student body (Bransford et a l., 2005). The clusters illustrate the heterogeneit y of student experiences and preferences, implying that a one-size-fits-all approach might not be effective. Flexible, diversified, and adaptive learning experiences are needed to meet all students' needs. A significant p ortion of experiences related to collaborative learning, vali dating the emphasis on collaboration in several the ories. However, the red cluster highlights that many stude nts also find individual quizzes effective, alignin g more with cognitive constructivism and self-determination theory. This suggests that for some s tudents, individual work is crucial to their learning. The study underscores the importance of understanding and accommodating the diverse learning needs and preferences of students.

This study has multi-layered practical implications. Firstly, the strong  preference for group quizzes in the black and blue clusters highlights the importance of incorporating peer-collaboration activities in the learning process. Teachers could include mo re group quizzes in their curriculum to cater to students who thrive in collaborative environments. Secondly, the gray clus ter's impartiality towards individual quizzes, yet favorability towards group quizzes, suggests that a blended peda gogical approach integrating cognitive and social constructivism could cater to a wider range of student dispositions and yield a richer learning experience. Lastly, this research opens avenues for future inquiries into the long-term effects of group quizzes on student engagement and academic achievement, as well as their determin ants of success, across various educational formats. The findings underscore the vital role of peer interact ion, hands-on practice, and diverse learning scenarios enabled by group quizzes. They serve as a roadmap for educa tors to develop dynamic, inclusive learning strateg ies that embrace collaboration and cater to the heterogeneity of student needs and dispositions.
