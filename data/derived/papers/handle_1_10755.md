# Reimagining Justice-Oriented Science Education through Disaster Memories: Evidence from the Buffalo Blizzard of 2022

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Abstract:In line with the increasing emphasis on the role of science education in challenging injustices and promoting social justice, this paper presents an ongoing project that seeks to address disaster justice issues in the context of the Buffalo Blizzard of 2022. The project uses the theoretical lens of disaster justice to document the experiences of families, science teachers and community leaders in Buffalo to identify the injustices during the blizzard that left 31 people dead. The social justice issues revealed from the interviews—those of distribution, representation and recognition—lead to co-constructing principles and priorities for science and climate education in Buffalo, NY. In this paper, we present the study background, theoretical framework, and early findings that emerged from interviews with community members. The findings suggest ways in which science education can help redress the needs and goals of marginalized and vulnerable communities in the context of climate disasters.

Study background The Buffalo Blizzard of 2022 was an extreme weather  event that struck Buffalo, New York during the hol iday season. It left an indelible mark on the city, both in terms of its severity and its social justice implications. In just four days, Buffalo was buried under an astonishing 50 inches of snow. The storm unleashed its fury wit h hurricane-force winds, creating whiteout conditions that made  visibility nearly impossible. A disproportionate number of the deaths occurred in Buffalo’s black and brown community. This underscores existing inequa lities such as deteriorating housing stock, lack of transp ortation and intense segregation in the city of Buf falo. This extreme weather event exposed the economic and soci al inequities, as well as the racial and class divi des that have been exacerbated over a long period of time, t hat place marginalized populations at greater risk of extreme weather events. At the same time, it also exposed t he glaring gaps in how we address disasters in educ ation, particularly disaster risk reduction and resilience in science education in the U.S., while significant work in this area has largely been conducted outside the nation (Schenk et al., 2019). An enduring history of inten se segregation and redlining, lack of access to public  transportation and food resources, economic disparities and a lack of climate justice infrastructure exacerbated the blizzard’s deadly impact in Black and Brown urb an communities. As Kneeland (2022) reported, long-term policy failures in Buffalo had created vulnerable conditions for extreme weather events. In the context of education, these complexities o f disasters point to the need for treating the natural/scientific and human/social aspects of disasters in a holistic manner (Park, 2020). Against this background, this study is documenting the experiences of urban science teachers, Black and Brown community leaders, and families who were dire ctly and disproportionately impacted by the Buffalo Blizzard. Specifically, this study explores: (a) fa milies' exp eriences and reflections of the Buffalo Blizzard, preparedness, access to support and resources, school recognition and resources, family needs, cultural and racial impacts (in terms of loss of life and geographical/community impacts), inequalities and injustices, and emotions;

(b) science teachers' curricular and instructional approaches to weather and climate, instructional and curricular

needs and gaps, approaches to cultural and racial impacts of climate-related disasters (in terms of loss of life and geographic impact), and their emotions related to th e Buffalo Blizzard of 2022; and (c) community leade rs' experiences and perspectives of the Buffalo Blizzar d, their approach to preparedness, community needs,  cultural and racial impacts (in terms of loss o f life and geographical/community impacts), and emo tions. The research questions that guide the study are: What are the experiences, reflections, and related emotions of science teachers, community leaders, and families about the Buffalo Blizzard? What disaster justice issues are involved in such experiences? Disaster justice and science education Disaster resilience is defined as “the ability of a system, community or society exposed to hazards to resist, absorb, accommodate, adapt to, transform and recover from t he effects of a hazard in a timely and efficient ma nner.” (UNDRR, n.d.) This definition suggests that understanding the process of recovery after a disaster is critical to building resilience to future disasters. The role of education in building disaster resilience is crucial, as emphasized by the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015-2030 (United Nations, 2015). A key step for disaster justice is to understand people’s first-hand experiences and emotions related to the disast er. Qualitative research suggests that emotions such as hope, worry and fear  interact to shape how individuals and communities respond to disasters (Kleres & Wettergren, 2017). Our focus on the disaster-related emotions of the Buffalo community is closely aligned with culturally responsive and community-driven science teaching. According to National Academies of Science, Engineering, and Medicine (20 22), one of the key approaches to equity in K-12 science education should be to see “science and engineering as part of justice movements.” It also emphasizes the need to prioriti ze “social movements that address the communities’ needs and goals, and then finds ways for science and engineering to support the progress toward those projects.” (p. 25) Such an approach to equity provides a strong ration ale for considering disaster justice as a broader a im of K-12 science education. Disaster justice can be achieved  by supporting marginalized and vulnerable people t o understand disaster risk and participate in making decisions about resources and actions across all ph ases of disaster management (mitigation, prepar ation, response, and recovery). Disasters often rev eal and magnify existing injustices in society: Those who are impoverished and vulnerable are less prepared for disasters and less able to respond to and recover from them. Apart fro m this issue of redistr ibution (i.e., allocation of information, resources and services), there are issues relating to equitable representation (i.e., inclusive decision-making, youth involvement, community participation) and recogniti on (i.e., respect for identities, vulnerabil ities, and histories) in the context of disaster. In this way, the disast er justice framework allows for a critical investig ation into the relationship between power, human communities and m ore-than-human communities (National Academies of Science, Enginee ring, and Medicine, 2022). In short, the study aims  to understand the residents’ experiences through the lens of disaster justice to inform how we teach about climate justice in K-12 science education.

## Research design

A qualitative, social design-based experiment methodological approach is guiding this study. Social design-based experiments (SDBE) were first developed to systemat ically address “urgent problems of practice relevan t to redressing educational inequities for vulnerable student populations and communities” (Gutiérrez et al., p. 332). This approach is appropriate for this study because  it is iterative and first seeks to understand urge nt issues, and is consistent with our goal of using this understanding to co-develop a science education curriculum framework for disaster justice and resilience. This approach can generate “new visions, theories and tools (technological) for teaching and learning that can advance change in [a nd out] of educational settings” (p. 3 34). The core principles of SDBE relevant to this study’s focus on history a nd historicity; prolepsis, bringing the past to the  present; employing a dynamic model of culture; and emphasis on resilience and change; and an end goal of transformation and sustainability.

Our research design itself was motivated and inform ed by the idea of representational disaster justice. As Rumbach and Németh (2018) note, “decisions about  who gets what, as well as the mechanisms of re-distribution, are very often left to historically powerful actors rather than the likely beneficiaries of such actions” (p. 343). To challenge these power dynamics in disaster (education) policymaking, our study participants include science educators (e.g., biology, earth science, en vironmental science, middle school science, and ele mentary science, informal science programs) who work in Buf falo Public Schools that serve racially, ethnically, and linguistically diverse students. Community leaders from local organizations working in youth services,  public health, climate justice, food (in)security and equitabl e mobility will also be invited. Finally, Black and  Brown families who have school-age children in the Buffalo Public Schools and/or a re actively engaged in the schools and their community were invited to participate. Interviews analyzed qualitatively usin g an iterative process of coding and generating categories and themes (Braun & Clarke, 2019), focusing on categories of experien ces, constraints and complexities of the blizzard as it relates to instructional components, needs, access and emotions. To ensure the sensitivity and respect required for disaster research, the project team included resear chers with extensive experiences of working with vulnerable communities and an understanding of ethical and psychological issues associated with disaster research. An ethics  application for the study was reviewed and approve d by the institutional review board at the University at Buffalo.

## Emergent findings

In this section, we describe early, emergent findings from the interviews that have been conducted during the first two months of the project (September to November 20 23), mainly around the first research question abou t the residents’ experiences of the blizzard. The findings revealed consistent patterns that focu s on (a) reflections on how winter weather has shifted over the course of t heir lifetimes; (b) visceral racialized emotions th at centered fear of the blizzard; (c) communities banding toget her to support neighbors and community members; (d) community members as researchers documenting their entire experience throughout the blizzard; (e) seei ng technologies as a core solution for community alert s and information relate d to extreme weather events, and (f) Snowy Man as the equivalent for Smoky the Bear. We briefly discuss the main themes below. Shifting patterns in winter weather.  All of our participants who were born and raised in  the community where the blizzard occurred, reflected on the changes in winter weather patterns. Primarily, they noted that winter temperatures were warmer and that the total amount of snow had decreased s ignificantly. Still yet, all of the participants shared concerns about “bigger” snow events. Raquel expressed, “the winters are getting milder but if you grew up in [northeast city], once the lake freezes over, we get less snow, right, so the systems we see are lake effect… because the winters are trending milder, th e lake is open longer, so we see these extreme even ts… will become more of a winter thing…”. Raquel’s reflection offered insight about the understandings of winter weather and visible changes with milder conditions. What is represented here are pieces of knowledge and residents tried to make sense of the storm. How community residents  make sense of phenomena and construct their knowle dge is significant for building more coherent understandings. To add, the impact of the blizzard put in mo tion these attempts to understand and make sense of weather and climate phenomena. Racialized emotions. For all of the participants visceral and transparent racialized emotions were evoked during the interview. The emotions were synonymous with the retelling of the experiences and involved crying, utterances noting fear and being scared, and importantly statements that they had never experienced anything like it. The reflection that summoned ideas that they ma y not live to tell what they experienced and how it  felt was profound. These emotions were racialized and reflec ted the role of our participants as comm unity leaders, community members or families. For example, one of the community leaders noted that because she was trapped in her home because of the delay in snow removal in  her neighborhood, she was unable to provide the se rvices that her organization usually offers. This experien ce prompted feelings of both helplessness and anger  (that her community expected help from her organization when she tooneeded help). Community members banding together.  For all of the participants, they checked on neighb ors, helped digging them out, offered food and phone charging opportunities. Simultaneous ly, participants also noted that they equally received offers of help from other nei ghbors and community members. So, there was this bi-directional opportunity for assistance. For example, Sharon explained that she had neighbors on her street who let her stay at their homes after she lost power. For S yreeta, who is a community leader,  her community block neighbors started to congregate at a local universi ty in their neighborhood. It is here that community  members charged their phones, shared food and stories. It became a center for support and resources. Community members as researchers.  The interviews revealed that some of the participan ts began to document the details of their own experiences, feelings, and hopes during the blizzard. For Sharon, the interview started with her noting that she had photographs and notes based on her experiences. For example, Shar on took photographs of all the different layers of clothing that she wore after losing electric power in her home. She noted that as the temperature fell, she had to increase the number of layers in order to stay warm. Interestingly, she noted that using the toilet became the most uncomfortable part of the experience. In addition, Sharon also took notes to document her experience and recollection of her life during the blizzard. Technology as a solution to winter weather. The participants noted the value of an early warning system warns people of upcoming extreme events and helps s ave lives. They were clear about the inadequacies o f how information was shared before, during, and after th e storm. In this regard they noted the lack of suff icient and timely information to warn residents of the severit y of the storm. As a solution, they all suggested a  text alert system which is more likely to reach the majority of the population. The Snowy Man as the messenger. All of the participants reacted to the notion of a snow mascot. Similar to the messaging with Smoky the Bear, the participants noted that a mascot or messenger for winter weather would help to remind students and community members about the conditions of extreme weather. Harold stated: A snowman to be able to start to get our kids to un derstand about winter weather and the ways in which it can be Beautiful, wonderful … but then there's this extreme part of it, which can  be deadly, right?

These findings illustrate a range of issues related to different dimensions of disaster justice reconstructed from the vivid memories and recollections of the re sidents of Buffalo and offer insights into how the idea of “science and engineering as part of justice movemen ts” can be implemented in the context of a disaster  that disproportionately affected historically marginalized people in a community. In sum, the emergent themes suggest that climate education in Buffalo should be centere d on (a) prioritizing the community’s voices, emoti ons and experiences related to disaster, (b) redressing historical inequities in the city th at exacerbate the impacts of disasters, (c) exposing and challenging the injusti ces that evoke racialized emotions among residents,  and (d) emphasizing agency and solidarity of individuals and communities for climate justice movements. Understanding the experiences of communities is a first step in efforts to promote learning and subsequent action.

## Future steps and implications

This paper has outlined our project that aimed to promote disaster justice through science education, in the context of the recent, deadly blizzard that affected margin alized communities in Buffalo, NY. Once the remaini ng interviews with community members are completed, th e project will progress to the next phase of the pr oject where two coalition meetings will be organized to d evelop a set of values, priorities and principles f or science education that can redress the disaster injustices in Buffalo that were identified from the interviews. In the coalition meetings, the themes generated from the initial interviews wi ll be used as guides and prompts to elicit the community’s visions for climate justice education based on their memories of the 2022 blizzard. The early findings suggest that the lived experience of climate disaster, loss and trauma has the potential to transform science education in a city with a dee p-rooted history of state violence, racial segregatio n and redlining, and socioeconomic disparity. Importantly, these findings reinforce the temporality of docum enting reflections of communities after disasters and extr eme weather events. Closely tied to these reflectio ns are the racialized emotions which also fuel a desire for ch ange and action. Finally, when considering how comm unities learn about weather phenomena, the representation o f a mascot was applauded as a way to make informati on about winter accessible. In this way, the project a ims to serve as an example of a science education p roject that attends to the social justice needs of a community and amplifies the voices of people who are rarely h eard or represented in education policymaking.
