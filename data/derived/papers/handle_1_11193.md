# Data Fusion in Classroom-Based Multimodal Learning Analytics: A Systematic Literature Review

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In classroom environments, this paper presents a systematic review of Multimodal Learning Analytics (MMLA) following PRISMA guidelines. It explores the relationship between learning indicators, multimodal data types, and data fusion strategies to improve MMLA's decision-making efficacy. The main findings include: 1) Complex mappings exist between learning indicators and multimodal data, including within learning indicator layers; 2) Engagement stands out as the foremost learning indicator; 3) Video data predominates in MMLA applications; 4) Feature-level fusion is the leading strategy for data integration. The study also highlights both theoretical and technological challenges, emphasizing the vital influence of learning theories.

## Introduction

In traditional classrooms, individualized attention is  challenging due to the limited capacity of one tea cher overseeing many students. To address this limitatio n, researchers are exploring the potential of techn ological interventions, particularly leveraging  Artificial Intelligence, 5G, and Big Data technolog ies for automated recognition and analysis of student learning states  like engagement, emotional state, and attention  (Buono et al., 2023). This falls within Learning Analytics (LA), now enriched by advancements in data collection, le ading to the evolution of Multimodal Learning Analytics (MMLA). According to communication theories, multimodality involves information exchange using various communicative modes such as text, auditory, language, spatial, and visual elements  (Faigley et al., 2002), encompassing sources like posture, f acial expressions, speech, and brainwaves. MMLA integrates interrelated multimodal information sources during classroom sessions for robust decision-making and interventions in the complex classroom e nvironment (Ochoa, 2022). The inherent heterogeneity of multimodal data necessitates sophisticated data fusion strategies for effective pattern recognition and application. Yet, comprehensive reviews on thes e strategies within MMLA remain scarce  (Mu et al., 2020). This study conducts a systematic literature review on learning indicators, multimodal data, and data f usion methods in classroom environments to address this gap.

## Method

Search strategy Under the guidance of PRISMA (Moher et al., 2010), we conducted a systematic review of MMLA research in the classroom setting.  We searched the related literature  across three databases: Scopus, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect. Drawing upon Mu et al.’s study (2020), the search string we used was (multimodal AND learning AND analytics OR mmla OR (Learning Analytics AND mu ltimodal)) AND (classroom OR lecture) AND (data fusion OR integrate OR integration). Publication dates were limited to January 1, 2018, to October 18, 2023, and a total of 243 articles were retrieved.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria To ensure the quality of the systematic literature review, the included studies had to meet the follow ing criteria:

(1) Utilization of case study or experimental metho ds; (2) Collection of multimodal data reflecting di verse

information sources; (3) Implementation of the stud y's experiment or case in a classroom setting; (4) Explicit description of the methods or processes for multimodal data fusion. The exclusion criteria were defined as follows:

(1) Lack of peer review; (2) Duplicate articles; (3) Non-English papers; (4) Unavailability of full-text access. Two

coders executed the inclusion and exclusion processes based on these criteria. The review process is illustrated in

Figure 1.

Figure 1 Flowchart of Inclusion and Exclusion Based on PRISMA Coding Following inclusion and exclusion criteria, 14 articles were selected for analysis, from which data were extracted including author(s), publication year, classroom en vironment type (face-to-face, e-learning, blended learning), learning indicators measured, multimodal data types, data fusion strategies (data-level, feature-level, decisionlevel), and algorithms for data fusion. Analysis revealed a predominance of face-to-face settings (78.6%) over elearning (14.3%) and blended learning (7.1%). Regar ding data fusion, feature-level fusion was most common (57.1%), followed by decision-level (21.4%), and data-level (7.1%) fusion, with some studies (14.3%) combining feature and decision-level strategies.

## Results and conclusion

Learning Indicators in classroom environments Based on the coding results, common learning indicators identified in MMLA research in classroom environments include engagement, attention, emotional states, and learning behaviors. Some studies have also explor ed indicators such as student collaboration, interest levels, and stress levels. In addition, some resear ch focuses on how to analyze the process of learning or teaching activities, rather than the measurement of indicato rs, often using specific visualization charts, such as dandelion diagrams (Fernandez-Nieto et al., 2022) and orchestration graphs (Prieto et al., 2018). Specialized charts indicate a trend in analytical studies shifting from individual entities to shared characteristics and interactions among pa rticipants (Goldberg et al., 2021). Among these indicators, engagement takes center stage. Its significance lies in its multidimensional meta-structure (Fredricks et al., 2004), encompassing student behavior, cognition, and emotion. Multidimensional indicators such as engagement can fit the rich information provided by multi-modal data and form the top layer of the system arc hitecture. The lower layer includes simpler data-mapping metrics such as attention, emotion, and learning behavior, as shown in Figure 2.

Despite variations in classroom contexts affecting data collection, advancements in sensor technologies have eased environmental constraints. Facial, body postu re, and eye data are commonly used in this research, mainly sourced from videos captured by cameras, a cost-effective and non-intrusive method. Video footage can be processed to extract audio, image, and text, aiding the recognition of features like head posture and skeletal points. Additionally, contextual information, a less-discussed but crucial category in multimodal data p rocessing, is essential in MMLA to address data diversity challenges in complex contexts (Shankar et al., 2019). Understanding contextual details of students, teachers, and classroom activities is critical, with knowledge of students' identities improving system decision support during behavior e valuation (Trabelsi et al., 2023).Grasping the intricate relationship between multimodal data and learning indicators is pivotal for the advancement of MMLA (Di Mitri et al., 2018). A single indicator, theoretically, can correspond t o a myriad of data types. For instance, emotions can be recognized both by facial expressions and by  extracting sound emotional features from auditory streams. One-to-one, one-to-many, or many-to-many relationships are established between multimodal data and learning indicators in research (Mu et al., 2020), with the selection of specific data types posing a complex challenge. This selection is influenced by specialized theories fro m disciplines such as learning sciences, neuroscien ce, and psychology, while considerations of pedagogical req uirements and ethical concerns further guide decisi ons on inputs for subsequent analysis (Jia et al., 2020).

## Methods for Multimodal Data Fusion in Classroom Environments

Data fusion, defined as the effective amalgamation of informatio n from diverse sources  (Chango et al., 2022), has demonstrated its ability to enhance decision-making outcomes, particularly when applied to multimodal data compared to single-mode data (Luo et al., 2022). Data fusion methods  include data-level fusion, decision-level fusion, and feature-level fusion. Data-level fusion combines features directly, decision-level fusion aggregates decisions from classifiers, and feature-level fusion combines features for analysis and dec ision-making, striking a balance between complexity and capturing essential information. In classroom-based MMLA research, featurelevel fusion has become the most commonly used data  fusion strategy due to the advances made in featur e extraction by deep learning and machine learning.  Techniques like recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) address dimens ionality challenges and capture interactions betwee n patterns through specialized fusion layers.  The application of specific algorithms varies acros s data fusion strategies. Data-level fusion commonly uses Weighted Average Fusion,  Kalman Filtering, and Average Fusion. Feature-level fusion utilizes algorithms like PCA, SVM, Random Forests, and deep learning models such as CNNs, RNNs (e.g., ResNets and LSTMs)  (Buono et al., 2023). Decision-level fusion relies on Voting Mechanisms and Rule-based Methods. Algorithm selection depends on data characteristics; for example, temporally dependent data benefits from spatio-temporal algorithms like LSTMs (Buono et al., 2023).  The choice significantly influences decision-making outcomes, prompting some studies to use multiple methods and conduct comparative analyses for optimal results. No inherent hierarchy of quality exists among data fusion methods; their suitability depends on the context.  Weighted-based fusion may work well for structured data like  accuracy rates or grades, while unstructured data like images or v ideos may benefit from machine learning algorithms or deep neural networks for improved results.

In the evolution of MMLA, challenges have emerged b oth technologically and theoretically. Technologically, developing effective fusion algori thms remains a persistent hurdle.  More crucially, the integration of advanced technology in MMLA necessit ates robust guidance from learning theories, which,  as Ochoa (2022) points out, is currently underrepresented in research. This lack of theoretical guidance is evident in the inconsistent definitions and applications of le arning indicators across studies. For instance, "learning behaviors" may be categorized differently, including observable actions (Yue et al., 2019), state descriptions like "engagement" and "boredom" (Trabelsi et al., 2023), or comprehensive indicators describing learning e fficiency and quality  (Qu et al., 2021). Additionally, while MMLA research typically evaluates students'  knowledge, physiology, and intentions (Blikstein & Worsley, 2016), the exploration of student knowledge is underemphasized in current classroom-based MMLA research, indicating a potential research direction linking knowledge mastery with explicit behavioral data. Despite advancements in overcoming technical obstac les, effectively leveraging technology in educational contexts still requires t he scaffolding provided by learning theories.  In MMLA, the symbiosis between theory and technology forms a critical foundation for devising comprehensive solutions.
