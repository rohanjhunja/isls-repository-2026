# The Connected Spaces Dashboard: Capturing Productive Resistance Through Design Based Research

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The Connected Spaces (CxS) project aims to foster communities of practice between disparate makers through a suite of co-constructed technologies. This study focuses on one of those technologies, the CxS Dashboard, as a useful case study in Design Based Research (DBR). Through a systematic process of mapping students’ resistances and researchers’ accommodations, our DBR approach allowed us to make rapid, theoretically-backed adjustments to the technology in order to refine and evaluate it in context.

## Theoretical background

Informal makerspaces are spaces in which people wit h shared interests and of varying skill levels can gather to share ideas, knowledge and technology. These spaces have the potential to become communities of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991) by allowing students to collaborate  with peers and mentors while also expressing their  unique identities through personally relevant making (Tissenbaum et al., 2019). One approach that is gaining prominence in promoting students’ sense of ownership, belonging, and competency in maker environments is Maker Portfolios (Keune & Peppler, 2017), as documentations of students’ work and as adaptive representations of their identities. The Connected Spaces (CxS) Dashboard is a tool that  combines aspects of Maker Portfolios with structures that support students’ peripheral awareness and participation, as well as the promotion of constructivist learning and the development and reexamination of s tudents’ identities (Godwin, 2016). Aiming to addre ss the need for peer-to-peer collaboration, the Dashboard uses a system of “affinities” (Kumar & Tissenbaum, 2022) as an alternative to badging, enabling students to ass ociate themselves with maker concepts, regardless if they have gained some arbitrary level of competency. Through ambient awareness of their peers’ affinities (Ogata & Yano, 2000), the Dashboard facilitates students’ help see king behavior with their peers. These patterns of b ehavior support constructivist ideas of knowledge generation  through student tutoring (Roscoe & Chi, 2008), as well as acting as a component of a larger system of technology serving to create an extended community of practice across spaces and promoting boundary sp anning connections with makers in disparate locatio ns (Bawankule, et al., 2023).

Methodological approach This work follows a design-based research approach (Barab & Squire, 2016) in w hich the Dashboard is under continuous, intentional, and iterative revision gro unded in well-founded educational theory. Pickering (2010) introduces a framework to understand how  a scientist engages in a dialectic of resistance and accommodation which can include revisions to goals and intentions  as well as to the material form of lab equipment. By recognizing that our tool is undergoing consistent iteration, while acknowledging that our students ha ve agency in relationship to our attempts, their resistance b ecomes productive. In response, we revise our goals  and modify the technology in response to students’ observed us e of it, including the ways they may resist it, for  example by ignoring or attempting to modify it. To capture our evolving goals throughout each iteration of our CxS programs, we utilize conjecture mapping (Sandoval, 2013) to r epresent our research conjectures, designed embodim ents, and theoretically grounded mediating processes. Our  research question emerged from this approach: How does the use of Design Based Research combined with Pick ering’s approach support researchers in making iter ative design revisions?

Examining three iterations of the dashboard Over a period of three years, we conducted after sc hool programs and summer camps where we deployed Cx S technologies to support a project-based STEM curriculum. While each iteration was slightly different in terms of the number of participants, they were consistently drawn from the same two populations of youth from l ocal community groups that predominantly served Black/Af rican American boys or girls respectively. This stu dy examined key changes that resulted from a conjecture map-based approach, mediated by Pickering’s framework, to the design and iteration of the Connected Spaces Dashboard. ## Key findings

In our first iteration, we noted that students had a complex relationship with their Dashboard profile s, and didn’t notice the ambient display functionality. While ins tructors encouraged students to add images that dem onstrated previous student work, not many of them uploaded those images, with one student indicating that he didn’t actually want others to see the work he had done in the past. Additionally, rather than changing their profiles spontaneously, most changes to their pictures and affinities were at the beginning of the session. We attempted to accommodate these nuances by shifting our patterns of engagement towards the Dashboard’s use as a refl ective tool, and promoting the use of comments on others’ profiles. In our second iteration, students exp ressed interest and appreciation towards the use of affinities, und erstanding the relational aspect of seeing a studen t with a matching affinity. We also took note of students’ e volving representations of self through their profi les, progressing from a simple cursory representation of their traits towards a representation of their interests and skills related to their final projects. In our third itera tion, it became clear that our focus on the Dashboa rd within the larger ecosystem (See Figure 1, A1) was an avenue worth pursuing, as we were able to sm oothly include scaffolded activities (B5) that referenced it as a rich identity resource. We observed students exploring their peers’ profiles to spark connections (C2), which were then further facilitated by instructors across spaces via technology in the CxS ecosystem (C1, C5).

## Conclusion

One concern that frequently emerged in our discussi ons was the degree to which instructors were interv ening in interactions that our technologies were meant to facilitate autonomously. One of our main goals for the Dashboard was to promote peer-to-peer collaboration and constructivist learning, so when ins tructors short-circuit student questions in order to avoid momentary chaos, were w e depriving students of the opportunity to grow? We  saw several instances of the Dashboard being used to promote collaboration, but students still demonstrated resistance to our suite of tools, perhaps because they were us ed to a traditional method of engagement with learn ing – they would come to the instructor before their peers. We  were able to explore and iterate rapidly as  this question emerged, allowing us to meet student needs as well as reorienting the design of not just our technolog y, but our research as well. Our work with the Dashboard provides a strong case for the use of Design Based Research, and particularly, the use of a framework that foregrounds student resista nces, and by accommodating them, involves students directly in the design process.
