# “Love Struggle, Love Self, Regardless*:” Arts Organization Leader’s Use of Socio-Historical Analytic Artifacts in Their Determinations of Successful Teaching Artists

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Black artists fill the (art)world with images, objects, sounds, and experiences that disrupt sociopsychological functions shaped by anti-Black racism (Fanon, 1952; Wynter, 1999, 2005; Gikandi, 2011). Learning in and through the arts has been a major vehicle for animating Black communities’ resistance to dehumanizing experiences across space and time. Out-ofschool time arts organizations serve as fugitive learning ecologies where creative risks can be taken up and celebrated as material and embodied forms of resistance. This paper details the ways three Black women-identifying out-of-school time arts organization (BW-OSTA) leaders use social analytic artifacts (Vossoughi, 2014) derived from an historical art exemplar to determine what makes a successful teaching artist. Using a socio-historical approach to remediation (Cole & Griffin, 1983) and risk (Lee, 2009), this paper tethers the politics and ethics of an exemplary Black artist to the determinations of successful teaching made by three contemporary BW-OSTA leaders.

## Introduction

As Sylvia Wynter (2005) stated, the invention of “Man” in modern humanism was made possible by ontological and epistemic violence that rendered Black people antihuman (p.128-129). Black arts and Black aesthetic movements have long served as interruptions to this violence, as the material, ideological and sonic creations provided opportunities to create habits of mind within but not of the structuring violence of enslavement and its afterlife. Acts of risk-taking and resistance to anti-Black violence that occur through learning in and through the arts can be seen in the New Negro Movement This generation of formal artists includes the life and works of Miss Augusta Savage, a sculptress, teaching artist, and community-based organizational leader. Her story is of vital importance for demonstrating the way Black artists have used the means of cultural production to engage the symbolic material of daily life to recreate self-representations without rearticulating dominant relations (Gaztambide-Fernández, 2013, p. 227) and the risks involved in those pursuits. Miss Savage’s everyday applications of symbolic creativity to symbolic materials and resources throughout her life shaped her political and ethical commitments in important ways. As James Baldwin and Frantz Fanon have stated, “the purpose of an education is to learn how to live” (Baldwin, 1963) and “in the world through which I travel, I am endlessly creating myself” (Fanon, 1952/2008, p. 179). As Augusta moved through the (art)world, she recreated herself to meet the needs of her community.

Arts Organization Leader, Miss Augusta Savage Miss Augusta Savage began sculpting animal figurine  “friends” using red clay in her backyard at an ear ly age. Born to a family of 14 children and two working parents, her father discouraged her artistic skill, believing it was sinful and refusing to acknowledge the practice as a form of psychic refuge for the introverted young girl.  As a result of her mother’s encouragement, Miss Savage m oved from Florida tonew York where she trained professionally and met, during the New Negro Movement, many of the greatest race scholars, artists, and activist in history. As an employee of the Federal Arts Program (FAP), an effort under the Works Progress Administration (WPA) to boost morale after the Great Depression (L ance, 1982), she was a fierce and demanding advocat e for young people’s artistic development and gainful employment for professional artists.  After teaching some formal and community-based arts courses, Miss Savage opened the Savage Studio for Arts & Crafts in 1932, became the first director of the Harlem Community Arts Center in 1937, and opened the Salon of Contemporary Art, the first gallery in America devoted to the exhibition and sale of the works of black artists, in 1939 (Hayes, Buick, Cook, & Savage, 2018). Her accomplishments allowed for the Harlem community to have free and work-as-you-go art classes, student teaching experiences, gainful employment in the arts, and opportunities to exhibit and sell artwork in an all-Black gallery (U.S. National Park Service).

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1581 © ISLS At the height of her success, Miss Savage fell on h ard times. The Salon for Contemporary Negro Art closed within three months of its opening due to a lack of artists, collectors, and a dedicated audience who could contribute to the consistent creation, sale, and gr owing discourse of art by Negro artists. The parano ia of the emerging era of McCarthyism positioned Savage under a great deal of scrutiny for her activities with young people. As Sharif Bey highlights in his 2017 article, Augusta was seen as a nuisance to a great deal of WPA administrators for advocating for Black artists to have more gainful employment opportunities under the FAP. She also helped establish the Vanguard Club, a youth arts and activism guild that was supported by well-known communists (Bey, 2017). With hesitant support from the government, the Harlem Community Arts Center and with federal funding for the arts slowly being reallocated to war effort s, Augusta's professional and personal investments seemed to disappear from the public view.

Her latest piece was created in 1959, three years before she passed, while living in Saugerties, NY. While in Saugerties, Augusta purchased and renovated a home with a farm and art studio that met her basic needs. The contours of her life, seemingly coming full circle are too uncanny to be by accident. From a small girl imagining a life amongst the animals to a now 53-year-old woman with her own farm, income, and space to work on whatever art she pleases, Miss Savage continued to blossom in her own self-determination to create the life she imagined as a child.  She continued to teach and create art for 15 years. She was diagnosed with cancer at age 69 and passed away in the following year on March 30th (Hayes, et al., 2018).

## Theoretical framework

Learning risk & resilience in and through the arts In my use of a socio-historical approach to re-mediation, I position the thinking of each BW-OSTA leader deep “into the past to trace the basics of [racial] literacy all the way back to the beginning” (Cole & Griffin, 1983,p. 69), mapping the moments of consequential learning within and across temporal, social, and spatial scales of action (Gutiérrez, Engeström, Sannino, 2016, p.278) during slavery and its afterlife. The life of Miss Augusta Savage is full of acts of refusal, fugitivity, and escape-important habits of mind, or social analytical artifacts (Vossoughi, 2014) that necessitate a cultural-ecological framework of risk that locates consequential learning in and through the arts in the everyday practices of Black survival. Learning to live while constantly (re)creating yourself is an expansive notion of learning through everyday activity. In the life and works of Miss Savage, we see the ways that learning in and through the arts is a consequence of dynamic, adaptive processes linked to issues of identity (Lee, 2009, p.78). In both the telling of Miss Savage’s story and the survival of her artwork, the many modes of risk and resilience present in her psychosocial resources for arts-based praxis can be discursively situated as a precursor to those of contemporary teaching artists. Using the cultural-ecological framework for learning and development, I view Miss Savage and her creative praxes as ideological resources or habits of mind. In arts-based organizations, whose daily work continues to be fraught with refusals to anti-Black racism and celebrations of creative risk-taking and resilience, young people, teaching artists and organizational leaders continue to make sense of and respond to the on-going and emergent ways they experience historical inequality (Lee, 2009, p. 78). These responses are access points to knowledge pathways that reduce cognitive labor and provide support for learners to persist (Lee, 2009, p. 86). These arts-based knowledge pathways are funds of knowledge (Gonzalez, Moll, Tenery, Rivera, Rendon, Gonzales, & Amanti, 1995) that have developed over and across time and are historical examples of expansive learning and boundary crossing (Engeström & Sannino, 2010, p. 13).  Black people have learned and continue to learn about the antagonistic relationship between Blackness and humanity. I use a socio-historical approach to re-mediation and risk to demonstrate the ways BW-OSTA leaders in this study are accessing psychosocial resources from the past and locating their determinations of successful teaching artists in opportunities for creative risk-taking, escape, and refusal of anti-Black inquiries and methods. In the next section, we review our methods for analyzing these resource and knowledge pathways.

## Methods

The conditions of the historical moment call attention to the ways those methods have been culturally produced in crisis. Data for this study was collected throug h Zoom interview due to the COVID-19 social distanc ing and travel restrictions. We use critical visual discourse analysis to analyze each BW-OSTA leaders’ sensemaking in their mission statements and interview data, alongs ide analysis of their activities during the intervi ew context. Analyzing the visual alongside the textual re-media tes the discursive practices of Miss Savage into th e methodology for data analysis.

Through web content and audio-visual recordings of interviews, we used normative-evaluative reconstructions (Carspecken, 1996) to conduct a critical discourse analysis using web content and Zoom interview data. Through several rounds of low and high level concept and value coding followed by meaning field mapping, ICLS2022 Proceedings 1582 © ISLS we determined each leader’s conception of “success,” “successful teaching,” and/or “successful teaching artists” to understand what politics and ethics shape their thinking. Depending on one’s proximity to power and  their positionality within arts organizations, organizational values held by leaders are shaped by the politics and ethics they have about “success.”

## Findings

In her focus group interview “R” names that she is very intentional about preserving the rich artistic history of Detroit, particularly their Motown legacy and names that successful teaching artists know of this history and reclaim it through their arts practices. She connects these ideas to the young people of the city by stating that a part of the vision of success is to retain them through their “neighborhood growth strategy,” which includes promotion and celebration of “what we have in Detroit.”  These practices bespeak Miss Savage’s efforts to maintain art, crafting, and gallery space for Black artists and enthusiasts in Harlem. Similar to Miss Savage, “R” created pathways to art space and community membership as a means for young people to risk reinvesting in an art identity as citizens of Detroit.

“J” states that her organization’s mission is to “eradicate racism and its damaging effects.” In her organization Teaching Artists (TAs) need to be cultural matches and understand the endemic nature of racism to work towards that mission.  The idea of teachers needing to be cultural matches is a long-held belief (Clark & Brown, 1986; Carter, Karani, Jackson, Jackson, Lucas, Neckerman, Podolny, Pollock, Tyson, 2003) of Black educational spaces. It helps teaching artists navigate anti-Blackness and racism in ways that reach the young people they serve and reduce the risk of additional institutional and interpersonal harm. Considering the many intersectional identities young people have, TAs need the cultural capital to creatively negotiate ideological and institutional constructs of power. J’s ideas about successful teaching artists operate alongside a history of cultural repertoires of practice (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003) that protect youth who are multiply minoritized. This is a belief held by Miss Savage who supported the efforts of black youth group The Vanguard, who was supported and advised by known communists, despite the threat of McCarthyism. Miss Savage was very much aware of the risk of being willing to assist but knew of the importance of young people being able to speak their mind and receive the political education they desired to protect their mind, bodies, and spirits. “S’s” organization unabashedly mirrors the spirit of the owner, herself. The organization’s mission is interesting in that it intends to “interpret and mirror culture and community, investigate race, gender, and personal identity in relations to post-Blackness” or this idea that Blackness itself is an identity that can be transgressed. The erasure of Blackness would mean that all systems dependent on race (i.e., humanism, capitalism) would cease to exist as we know them. By shaping an arts organization around trans-ontological ideas like a post-black world, TA success is identified in the embodiment of these ideas.  TAs uplift the times where S’s essence shines through in both their teaching and the youth’s learning and engagement. In a similar, albeit humble manner, this form of embodiment relates to one of Miss Savage’s most infamous quotes: “I have created nothing really beautiful, nothing really lasting, but if I can inspire one of these youngsters to develop the talent I know they possess, then my monument will be in their work” (Hayes et al., 2018). These curiosities towards post-Blackness in “S” & Miss Savage demonstrates a desire to transcend the oppressive reach of racial discrimination and embrace the cultural-historical methods that work towards achieving that.

## Conclusion

This paper positions the historical activities of A ugusta Savage in relationship with the political and ethical commitment BW-OSTA leaders make in their determinations of who is considered a successful teaching art ist. Following Carol Lee’s (2009) ecological model of multiple pathways for access to psychosocial resources, these political and ethical commitments serve as pathways that inform the ways leaders view successful teaching. As is a part of the long history of Black arts and Black aesthetic movements, the preservation of city history, cultural representation at multiple levels of engagement, and understanding the individual body and collective creative space as locations for embodied risk and resistance  are important mediating artifacts for successful t eaching in contemporary art organizations.
