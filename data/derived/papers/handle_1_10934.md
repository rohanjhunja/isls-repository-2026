# Storied Community-Based Learning Environments: Designing with Storywork, Storylistening, and Narrative Inquiry

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We explore how the methodologies of Storywork, Storylistening, and Narrative Inquiry can support community-engaged scholarship. We discuss the varying approaches to storying contained within these three traditions and suggest that community-engaged scholars can benefit from drawing on elements of each to (1) consider the value of place-stories circulating within research spaces, and (2) to design educational spaces that convene a diversity of place-stories, further enriching and complicating researcher engagement with stories.

## Introduction and background

Scholars in the learning sciences community have sh own increasing interest in how stories might be use d in studying and designing learning environments. In th e proceedings of the International Society of the L earning Sciences, researchers have drawn from various disciplines to conceptualize stories as essential to theorizing or to methodology. Vogelstein et al. (2023) attend to stories of teachers supporting multilingu al students’ learning in computing, building stories “with theory” as they contribute to “ stories of theories of syncretism and translanguaging” (p. 806). Shaw et al. (2023)  draw on Black feminist-womanist storytelling methodologies to create space for reimagining, healing, and transformation. Vandenberg et al. (2022) emphasize stories as learning outcomes, conduct ing focus groups with elementary school teachers to des cribe how their students develop interactive stories as they engage in computational thinking. It is encouraging to see scholars drawing on a variety of conceptual foundations to advance our understanding of stories in educational contexts. At the same time, there is limited research exploring how these conceptuali zations, and the ir associated practices, fit within methodological traditions that inform our work as c ommunity-based scholars. Here we look across the contexts of our research, including climate change resilience in rural communities and the housing crisis in urban communities, to understand how three well-conceptualized methodologies involving story  can guide our work designing community-based sociotechnical systems that aim to  support socio-ecological transformation across scales of practice.

We find that our storying practices emerge at the intersection of multiple design goals, including: (1) convening a diversity of place-stories, (2) creating spaces that support individuals to hear their neigh bors’ placestories, and (3) connect ing individual place-stories to larger cultural place-stories. Here we use the first of our goals as a context to explore three well-defined sensemaking traditions: Storywork, which describes storytelling and storylistening practices within various Indigenous ways of knowing and being (Archibald, 2008; Blood et al., 2012), Storylistening, developed within the public humanities and focuse d on supporting civic decision-making (Dillon & Craig, 2021) and Narrative Inquiry, which draws on Deweyan pragmatism to study lived experience as story (Connelly & Clandinin, 2006). By highlighting perspectives and practices across Storywork, Storylistening, and Narrative Inquiry that have supported our understanding of community-based research, we hope to support other researchers in enriching and complicating their engagement with th eir own stories, as well as those of the communities with whom they work.

Convening a diversity of place-stories Considering our localized community-based scholarship, it is foundational to conceptualize the importance of connecting place to stories. Within the Storywork tradition, Blood et al. (2012) use the term “place-story” to “emphasize the personal and intimate connectivity that people feel to the land and stories linked to specific places” (p. 48). The term place-story therefore signifies the relationship between the places that lifeforms inhabit and the stories which they use to make sense of their exper iences inhabiting that place. We find a similar association between story and place in Narrative Inquiry, with an emphasis on “the specific concrete, physical, and topological boundaries of where the inquiry takes place ” (Connelly & Clandinin, 2006, p. 480). Both of these traditions emphasize that w hen convening a diversity of stories, researchers should attend not just to the content of the stories, but to their geographic proximities and resonances, as well. Beyond this attention to “the where” of our storied community-based scholarship, we also consider “the who,” as we strive to gather, create, or contest community. Storywork most directly supports these design goals, offering guidance for convening groups of learners by emphasizing the imp ortance of sharing place-stories to foster relationships between storytellers and story listeners, including across generations. Narrative Inquiry, in contrast, is typically focused on a researcher-participant dyad, and is particularly useful for diving into individuals’ stories (Connelly & Clandinin, 2006). Storywork literature also highlights “the when,” encouraging learning scientists to consider designing for temporal flexibility (Archibald, 2008). Allowing place-stories to be told at the best time and in the best place, we are able to cultivate meaning and nourish relationships within communities. Designing to support a diversity of viewpoints, including more-than-human stories, is vital to convening diverse place-stories. Storywork emphasizes this diversity by revealing ecological  linkages within a “web of relationality” (Blood et al., 2012, p. 64). The tradition of Storylistening also emphasizes a diversity of stories, but with the primary goal of supporting civic decision-makers. This tradition supports researchers in attending to the points of view  that are represented in focal stor ies or shared across multiple stories. Both S torywork and Storylistening demonstrate how designing for diversity can forward a group’s thinking. For example, in Marin & Bang’s (2015) design meetings with science teachers, Storywork helped “teachers [to] understood story meaning [which] became a productive site of tension” (p. 46). T eachers buil t off others’ experiences to design and implement robust curricula. Within Storylistening, researchers design for iterative cycles of recruitment to gather the unique perspectives required to meet a civic decision-making goal. Tension within diverse place-stories is both expected and welcomed in all these methodologies. Within the context of Narrative Inquiry, Clandinin and colleagues (2010) recognize how tensions produced by convening diverse place-stories can have value, in that they point to important aspects of relationships; attending to tensions can be “a key methodological strategy” (Clandinin et al., 2010, p. 83). Storywork and Narrative Inquiry both ask designers to recognize and surface tensions in thei r work to ensure that conflicting viewpoints are not brushed under the rug, while Storylistening asks researchers to actively seek multiple viewpoints. Within our communitybased work we find this understanding has inspired designs that incorporate a variety of expressive modalities, as participants may not feel comfortable directly contradicting others’ perspectives.

## Conclusion

We find that Storywork, Storylistening, and Narrative Inquiry all offer guidance for supporting community-engaged scholars to consider the kinds of place-stories that are circulating within our research spaces. We encourage learning scientists to consider the metho dological paradigm s that best correspond to their research contexts, while considering how stories may be used to redress historical, racialized injustices, as well as to repair human relationships with the rest of the natural world.
