# Teaching in the Wild: Dilemmas Experienced by K-12 Teachers Learning to Facilitate Outdoor Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Benefits of learning in nature are well documented. However, there are many challenges and open questions in preparing educators, particularly K-12 teachers, to facilitate engaging and impactful outdoor learning experiences. Here, we take a cultural historical activity-theoretical approach to analyzing the dilemmas faced by 14 fifth-and sixth-grade teachers and one principal as they prepared for and facilitated a week-long outdoor learning experience for students. Drawing on surveys and video data, we identified four dilemmas negotiated by teachers and illustrate how they represent systemic contradictions between the activity systems of classroom learning and learning in the wild. We discuss implications for understanding and designing for learning in nature and teacher professional development.

## Introduction

Positioning learning as a cornerstone of healing, resilience, and community means designing alternative learning experiences that support the agency, interests, and inquiry of young people, while also connecting them to broader human and more-than-human (MTH; Marin & Bang, 2018) communities. Creating such alternative learning experiences requires preparing teachers to facilitate them. This study focuses on such an alternative experience, School of the Wild (SOTW), and examines teachers’ experiences preparing for and facilitating students’ learning in this environment. SOTW is a week-long, place-based, outdoor learning experience, designed to connect fifthand sixth-grade students with their local, MTH community and engage them in authentic, place-based, studentdriven inquiry, exploration, and problem-solving. However, it is facilitated by their regular classro om teachers. This presents questions  and challenges  regarding: (1) how teachers view the outdoors; (2) how they adapt to teaching in nature; and (3) how we might best prepare them to do so. We draw on survey and video-ethnographic data to specifically understand the dilemmas faced by teachers in preparing for and facilitating SOTW. The benefits and challenges of outdoor learning There are many documented benefits to outdoor learn ing in natural spaces. Young people who spend more time learning outdoors have reduced stress levels compared to their peers who spend more time indoors (Dettweiler et al., 2017). Learning in, and about, their local environments  helps young people gain knowledge about habitats, ecosystems, wildlife, and conservation practices ne eded to become informed citizens and stewards of th e land (Ardoin, 2020). Learning science outdoors also encourages students to make concrete connections to communityrelevant science concepts, making STEM learning more engaging and relevant to their lives (Miller er al., 2015). However, facilitating outdoor learning can be chall enging, particularly for educators accustomed to classroom teaching. Glackin (2016) found that teachers with more traditional, teacher-and content-centered views of science teaching regarded outdoor science learning as just a “fun” experience  with little learning benefit. Professional development (PD) programs specifically for outdoor learning often ta ke a more formal approach to teaching, associated with a lack of teacher prepar ation to utilize the outdoo r environment effectively to engage students (Ayotte-Beaudet et al., 2017).  This contrasts with the type of engagement with nat ure illustrated and advocated for by informal learning researchers. For example, Boxerman (2013) found that when outdoor learning experiences create opportunities for interest-driven inquiry, they generate memorable moments of discovery that become resources for future science learning. Marin and Bang (2018) also illustrated how through mutual inquiry outdoors, adults and young people can make use of s emiotic resources in the MTH world to support learn ing through indigenous forms of knowledge building, such as “walking, reading, and storying land” (p. 89).

## Theoretical framework

We apply a cultural-historical activity theoretical  (CHAT; Engestr öm, 1987; 2001) lens to the experiences of teachers preparing for and facilitating SOTW. This framework focuses on SOTW as an activity system that contrasts with typical K-12 classrooms. It attends to differences in the object of instruction/learning and the role of interactions between subjects, tools and signs, community context, rules, and the division of labor in the activity system. From CHAT, we draw specifically on  how systemic contradictions  (Engeström, 2001) —“historically accumulating structural tensions within and between  activity systems” (p. 137)  — are catalysts for change. For example, the tension between student-led inquiry and teacher-led instruction is a systemic contradiction. CaspariGnann and Sevian (2022) connected the idea of system ic contradictions to  the dilemmas (Windschitl, 2 002) teachers face when  choosing between alternatives in implementing innovative pedagogy. They argue  that negotiating these dilemmas is what makes innovations in teaching practice  possible. They also identified how a CHAT perspective can identify sources and resolutions of dilemmas in pra ctice, by identifying elements of the activity system(s) in tension or contradiction in a  given dilemma. Here, we build on the prior work of Ramey & Stevens (2023) applying this framework to understand dilemm as faced by new STEAM facilitators and use this framework to examine the dilemmas faced by teachers in planning for and facilitating SOTW.

## Method

Participants included 14 fifth and sixth grade teac hers and one principal who participated in professional development for SOTW during summer 2023 and facilitated the program for their s tudents in fall 2023. All educators were white  (11 female, 4 male). We collected pre-and post-surveys and video during PD and while teachers facilitated SOTW for students. Both PD and SOTW took place at county and state parks near participants’ schools in the midwestern United States. Building on methods to capture learning on the move (Taylor & Hall,

2018) in natural spaces  (Marin & Bang, 2018), as well as our own work using  video-ethnographic methods to

study STEAM learning (Ramey & Stevens, 2019), we asked teachers and up to five students per group to wear visor-mounted cameras to capture conversations and interactions. A member of our research team accompanied teachers during PD and SOTW to observe and record field notes. When available, researchers also wore cameras. Video recordings were analyzed via interaction analysis (IA; Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Hall & Stevens, 2015). T his method focuses on how knowledge and attitudes a re used in and changed through verbal and nonverbal embodied interaction. IA allowed us to see how teachers’ interests-and knowledge-in-use were enacted in practice in micro moments of interaction with other  teachers, students, and the material, MTH context.  This included syncing and merging multiple visor camera video and audio recordings to analyze moments from multiple perspectives. We attended particularly to how dilemmas manifested and were negotiated during the PD and SOTW. Surveys and field notes were analyzed using an iterative process of reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Using this approach, we identified themes grounded in the data but then also put them in conversation with theory and video data to iteratively refine categories.

## Findings

Our analysis of survey and video data yielded four dilemmas experienced by teachers. We describe each below. Teacher-led activities versus student-led inquiry An explicit goal of SOTW, articulated throughout the PD, is to let student interests and inquiry gui de learning. Teachers’ pre-survey responses showed that this frame was inconsistent with some of their initial thinking about teaching in nature. For example, in response to the  question “Is there anything about SOTW that you're nervous about?”, one teacher wrote “ Having enough things to do with the kids.” A nother wrote “Planning for outside/uncontrollable situations will maybe be mor e difficult.” Still others expressed nervousness ab out “Teaching something I am not an expert on” and “Not knowing things.” In answer to the question “Wha t do you expect the SOTW program to be like?”, one teacher wrote “I am wondering how to fill the days, ” and another wrote “learning broad stroke information about nature that I pass along to others”. These comments indicate that teachers envisioned enacting teacher-led instruction rather than student-led inquiry. Some teachers expressed greater initial alignment with s tudent-led inquiry. For example, in answer to the question “What do you expect the SOTW program to be like?”, one wrote “spontaneous learnin g that builds off what we find in nature”, and another wrote “Students get to learn and try things in a more open-ended format.” Others expected and were  enthusiastic about learning and experiencing nature “along with” or “a longside” their students. However, in practice, even after the PD, nearly all  activities we observed were structured by a teacher with a pre-determined goal and positioned the teacher as content expert. Several teachers enacted games in which students were assigned roles (e.g., prairie plants or water, hawks or coyotes). Some activities left more room for creativity and inquiry than others (e.g., making te mporal art with natural objects, searching the woods for birds). However, even in these activities, the main goal, rules, and constraints were pre-determined by teachers. These findings suggest a dilemma facing teachers: whether to  facilitate student-led inquiry or  deliver teacher-led instruction. We suggest that this dilemma arose from a  systemic contradiction between the activity system of formal schooling and that of SOTW, specifically in terms of the object of activity (inquiry versus instruction) and the division of labor (teacher-versus student-led learning). Safety versus exploration When we asked teachers on the pre-survey, “Is there anything about SOTW that you're nervous about?”, the theme that appeared most (N=6) was concerns about safety.  Specific safety concerns varied, and included pois on ivy, other poisonous or harmful substances, touching liv ing things, water, inclement weather, allergies, and someone getting lost. However, in answer to the question “What do you exp ect the SOTW program to be like? ” several teachers (N=4) identified “exploration” or “exploratory learning” as expected experiences. This included three of the same teachers who had identified safety concern s. Another teacher articulated an expectation “To overcome fear; ‘get comfy with being uncomfy.’” These comments highlight another dilemma: how to balance student safety with free exploration in nature. This dilemma emerges from a systemic contradiction within the SOTW activity system, in which safety and exploration are simultaneous, yet seemingly contradictory objects of activity. Analysis of video provides further evidence for thi s dilemma and shows how teachers navigated it. For example, Mr. Jackpine started day one of teaching in the woods by having students lead a “Rock, Paper, Scissors Hike” where at each fork in the path, two students played the game Rock, Paper, Scissors to decide which way to go. This was followed by bird watching, where groups of three or four students explored the woods on their own with binoculars, bird guides, and journals. These a ctivities made relatively more space for students’ free exploration. However, while students were exploring, this same teacher shared that he had had nightmar es the previous night about losing a student in the woods, and he appeared visibly nervous when two of the bird watching groups didn’t return promptly when he blew the whistle for groups to return to the designated meeting spot. Learning about versus learning from nature Another explicit goal of SOTW, communicated during PD, is that learning should be place-based. In other words, students should have opportunities to learn not just about nature (as in a classroom) but from nature. This created a dilemma for teachers accustomed to the classroom activity system and its largely human-made tools and signs. Marin and Bang (2018) demonstrated that the land and the MTH world can serve as semiot ic resources for learning. However, during SOTW, we found many teachers struggl ing to make use of the se semiotic resources. While, overall, they used a variety of MTH resources (plants, animals, water) and external tools (photos, journals, binoculars, smartphone apps) to mediate learning, some activities made better use of MTH resources than others. For example, Ms. Juneberry facilitated two games with her students. One engaged them in learning from nature. Some students played Harrier hawks hiding a nest of eggs (golf balls) in a 20 by 20 foot section of prairie. The other students played coyotes searching for the  eggs. The students discovered that it was extremel y difficult to find the eggs amongst the dense prairie plants. Ms. Juneberry then shrunk t he search area to 10 by 10 feet to illustrate what happens when prairies are destroyed. Students discovered that this made the hunt easie r. In the other game, students learned about nature. This game was about the role of prairie plants in preventing soil erosion and runoff but was played in an open area of mown grass. Here, Ms. Juneberry scattered poker chips, representing soil. Some students played the role of water, runni ng across the grass and picking up poker chips as t hey ran, while others played either farm crops planted in ne at rows or prairie plants planted in random formati ons. The goal of the plant s was to tag the water without moving their legs. If water students were tagged by farm crops, they had to sit down for three seconds, but if they were tagged by prairie plants, they were out of the game. While both games seemed engaging for students and led to productive discussion afterward, the first made direct use of natural semiotic resources, while the second could just as easily have been played back at school. Learning on the move versus learning in place A final dilemma faced by teachers builds on Marin and Bang’s (2018) research on learning on the move in natural spaces. During SOTW, we observed teachers facilitating learning both on the move and in place, and we observed a dilemma they encountered in achieving the right speed of movement to afford dynamic inter action with the MTH world. For example, during the  “Rock, Paper, Scissors  Hike,” students explored the forest a t a  pace somewhere between a brisk walk and a run. While this pace, set by students, engaged them and allowed them to exert energy, it afforded little thoughtful engagem ent with the MTH world. In contrast, the prairie group spent much of the day in a mown field adjacent to the prairie, sitting, discussing, writing, and waiting. This also afforded little interaction with the MTH world and left students restless. A middle path between these two extremes seemed to allow for the greatest engagement with the MTH worl d. However, it also represented the greatest departure from the typical organization of activity in school s, where students are either sitting still (in the classroom) or running around (in gym or at recess). In the wild, we observed two forms of movement that afforded dynamic engagement with nature. The first occurred in transit as groups of teachers and students walked from the start-ofday meeting spot to sites in the prairie, woodlands, or wetlands where planned activities would take place. While some teachers led this walk at a rapid pace, others intentionally slowed students down, drawing their attention to features of the MTH world, such as plants, animals, or water. The second occurred during activities specifically designed to engage students dynamically with nature, such as a game of Prairie Bingo, a search for natural items to make temporal art, a bird watching hike, or a search for subjects to photograph and describe through poetry.

## Discussion and conclusions

These findings improve our understanding of teachers’ perspectives on outdoor learning and how they shape the teachers’ and students’ experiences in nature. They build on the work of Boxerman (2013) and Marin  and Bang

(2018) to improve our understanding of learning in nature and some of the barriers to implementation. Our work

also expands on Caspari-Gnann and Sevian’s (2022) work by using a  CHAT lens to identify and understand dilemmas faced by teachers in adapting to teaching in nature and their corresponding pedagogical practices. Our identification of four specific dilemmas faced by teachers highlights how they arose from systemic contradictions and were negotiated in practice. This has implications for the design of PD, preparing educators to better support outdoor learning. Specifically, we argue that PD sh ould directly address these four dilemmas, illustrate the pros and cons of different ways of navigating each, and provide concrete examples of solutions to each dilemma that are aligned with desired practices for facilitating learning in nature, including student-led inquiry, safe exploration, learning from rather than about nature, and engaging in dynamic interactions with nature while on the move.
