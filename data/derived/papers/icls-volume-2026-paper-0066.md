# Making Sense of Systemic Racism in a High School Biology Classroom: The “Just Genetics” Unit

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Drawing on frameworks for Justice-Centered Pedagogy and Rightful Presence, we designed a genetics unit that engages students with reasoning about racial health disparities in the context of diabetes. The unit focuses on the relationship between past and ongoing structural racial inequities, the resulting non-neutrality o f physical and social environments, and their outcomes for the health of marginalized communities. We provide a design story tracing our design conjecture, how we embodied it in learning activities and artifacts, and the ways in which these were taken up b y high school students in terms of their understanding of the structural aspect of racism. Our findings suggest that while students recognized that racialized groups endure harmful environments, they did not fully grasp how historical and ongoing racism created and reproduces these environments.

Racial health disparities are prevalent in the United States and manifest as higher rates of morbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, asthma) among Black, Latinx, and Indigenous people. These disparities are not due to any innate biological differences between socially constructed racial groups but rather due to racism shaping the environments in which people live (Scheuermann et al., 2024). Marginalized communities are more likely to reside in areas with less access to nutritious food, safe housing, qual ity healthcare, and higher pollution; all these factors directly impact one’s health (Graves & Goodman, 2022). These harmful environments are not happenstance but were structured through past and present policies with lasting effects in the form of raciall y segregated neighborhoods, environmental hazards, underfunded public services, and ongoing barriers to wealth accumulation (Linde et. al, 2022). These sociopolitical dimensions of health disparities are rarely addressed in science instruction. While students do learn about interactions between the environment and the body the political and historical reasons that make some environments riskier than others remain invisible (Visintainer, 2023). There is a long history of critically oriented educators who recognized the need for politicized education that not only teaches students the disciplinary knowledge needed to succeed but also orientates learning towards change making (e.g. Freire, 1970; Gay, 2000; Ladson-Billings, 1995; Calabrese Barton, 2003). Yet, grasping the structural dimensions of racism is not trivial and there is a tendency to perceive racism as primarily interpersonal prejudice (Kendi, 2019). Moreover, Philip et al. (2016) highlight that without a deep interrogation of racism and power, educational efforts can inadvertently reinscribe dominant ideologies. Here we heed the call for science education (Morales-Doyle et al., 2020), and more specifically genetics education (Duncan et al., 2024), to take a political turn and make visible the sociopolitical. Towards this end we designed, in collaboration with high school teachers and youth, a justice-oriented genetics unit focused on health disparities associated with diabetes to help students make sense of how structural racism results in harmful environments that lead to poor health outcomes for marginalized comm unities. In this paper we describe a core design conjecture and embodiments (Sandoval, 2014) to support understanding of how students made sense of racism as structural and “baked into” environments as a social determinant of health. Specifically, we asked:

1. How did students reason about the relationship between racism, environment, and health?

2. In what ways did these activities help students make sense of racism as structural versus interpersonal?

Theoretical frameworks This study is informed by the Justice-Centered Science Pedagogy proposed by Morales-Doyle (2017), and that builds on critical pedagogy (Freire & Macedo, 1987) as well as culturally relevant pedagogy (Ladson-Billings,

1995) to create anti-oppressive education as a means for social transformation (Morales-Doyle, 2024). According

to this framework, students should master and critique scientific knowledge so they can use it to both analyze and address social justice science issues. Students should develop a critical consciousness to understand and challenge 555 the social order and structural inequities. We also draw on the Rightful Presence framework proposed by Calabrese Barton & Tan (2020). While Justice-Centered Science Pedagogy orients instruction towards systemic inequities involved in socio-scientific issues, Rightful Presence centers students’ lived experience of, and expertise about, such inequities. In this way youths’ concerns, desires, and epistemologies are explicitly invited as rightfully present in the classroom. Supporting students’ rightful presence also involves desettling and expanding what counts as science (what questions and data matter), who can engage in science (positioning youth as local experts), and towards what ends (science for social transformation). Educators  in learning environments that engender students’ rightful presence act as political allies working alongside and in support of students in their struggle to shape just futures.

Both frameworks emphasize the need to: a) ground instruction in relevant social-justice phenomena, b) invite students’ lived experiences and understanding of their communities, and c) develop students’ critical consciousness. Guided by these common emphases, we designed the “ Just Genetics” diabetes unit. We chose to anchor the unit in diabetes because of its relevance as a social-justice scientific issue to the community which the schools served. While students did not know the biological mechanisms of diabetes, they were familiar with how it affected the lived experiences of community members and how environmental factors-such as diet, stress, and activity levels play out in their lives. Unit activities (described below) engaged students with critical analyses of how racial and economic disparities contribute to diabetes risk  (Banerjee et al., 2024; Doucet-Battle, 2021). Our aim was to help students develop critical consciousness regarding the social determents of health (i.e., income, access to nutritious food and healthcare) that result in diabetes health disparities. Importantly, the unit addressed core ideas in genetics through the anchoring phenomenon of diabetes (NGSS Lead States, 2013). Our core contentions is that to develop critical consciousness about social determinants of health, students need to understand that the environments people inhabit are not neutral, not benign, and not happenstance. By this we mean that environments are not neutral in that they are experienced differently by different racial groups, they are not benign in that some environments are harmful, and this harm is experienced predominantly by minoritized groups, and environments are not happenstance in that they are designed by socio-political forces such as racism. Drawing on Sandoval’s conjecture mapping (Sandoval, 2014), we structure this paper as a design story in which we explicate one of our design conjectures, the associated embodiments, and then describe how these embodiments played out during the implementation of the unit in two high school classrooms. Design conjectures are essentially broad-level hypotheses about how to support learning towards a particular outcome. The frameworks informed multiple conjectures, and we’re focusing on one of them-the conjecture that: to support students’ understanding of health disparities as stemming from structural inequities we would need to help them conceptualize the environment (in gene-environment interactions) as not neutral but rather as harmful and as intentionally structured to be so. We developed multiple embodiments (design features) that we hoped would support students’ understanding of the environment as such. We next describe the unit and focus our attention on the specific embodiments (design features) that were designed to realize this conjecture. The “Just Genetics” diabetes unit In collaboration with two high school teachers, we developed a unit with 8 lesson sets varying from 1-5 days of instruction. In Lesson Set 1, we used stations to expose various aspects of diabetes as a phenomenon, and the students developed driving questions that were subsequently addressed in the unit. In Lesson Set 2, Student s explored their question: what happens inside the bodies of people with diabetes and created evidence-based models of the biology of diabetes, including at the cellular and molecular level. In Lesson Sets 3-5, the students explored their questions about how genetic mutations can cause diabetes as well as how diabetes can be inherited. These lessons served to address core ideas in genetics (the central dogma, inheritance patterns) with a focus on multifactorial inheritance and polygenic risk scores. During the implementation, the students had a unique opportunity to attend and host a booth at their local cultural festival. We leveraged this opportunity and supported students in creating a survey to distribute during the cultural festival, asking community members questions related to their risk factors associated with diabetes. The data from the surveys was then used in Lesson Set 6, during which students explored several environmental and behavioral risk and protective factors for diabetes (social determinants of health). Students analyzed their survey data alongside various information resources regarding the risk and protective factors such as income differences between racially segregated neighborhoods, the cost of insulin under different insurance plans, and the role of ultra processed foods as a risk factor in diet. This lesson emphasized how environmental and structural factors (e.g. healthcare access and insulin costs) shape Type 2 diabetes risk more than genes. In Lesson Set 7, students engaged with concepts in population genetics to understand how humans share overwhelming genetic similarity with more variation within so-called “racial groups” than between them. They also played a simulation game that modeled the contribution of genes and the environment to T2D ris k illustrating how harmful environments are the key factor causing racial disparities in 556 diabetes. To explain how environments became harmful, we discussed housing segregation and disinvestment using a video about redlining policies in the U.S. Finally, in Lesson Set 8, students created a concept map in which they organized and connected the major ideas covered in the unit  including both biological and social/environmental. They also developed local plans for action to address some of their findings from their community ethnography regarding mitigating diabetes risk in their community. However, due to time constraints, the students could not implement their ideas regarding advocacy and education. The unit included multiple embodiments that we hoped would support students’ understanding of the environment as non-neutral and intentionally structured to be so (our conjecture). We focus on three  of them: (1) looking at state level data about social determinants of health  (in preparation for the community ethnography survey construction in Lesson set 3), (2) playing a simulated health game and watching a video about redlining policies (Lesson set 7), and (3) creating a concept map  (Lesson set 8). These activities specifically engaged students in thinking about the structural aspects of racism as it relates to opportunities and barriers that different communities have and the resulting impact in terms of health. The unit was implemented in two high schools in the same district in a sanctuary city in New Jersey, USA. The schools serve a student population of which over 92% identify as Hispanic, and 80% of students are listed as economically disadvantaged (NJ School Performance Report, 2024). One of the collaborating teachers taught in a smaller STEM magnet school and implemented the unit with 21 students (mixed 9th and 10th grade) in an elective course (denoted as Class 1). The second collaborating teacher taught at a larger zone high school and implemented the unit with 30 students (11th grade) in an honors biology course (denoted as Class 2). Both teachers helped co-design the curriculum with university researchers and implemented the units in Spring 2024. This study represents the initial phase of design-based research (Design-based Research Collective, 2003; Hoadly & Campos, 2022) in which we  begin testing our design conjecture by examining the extent to which our embodiments resulted in the learning processes and outcomes we hoped to bring about (Sandoval, 2014). We used multiple sources of data to elucidate how students conceptualized racism (interpersonal versus structural) and how they reasoned about the relationship between racism, the environment, and health. Specifically, data for this study included: artifacts that students produced, video-recorded classroom interactions, and fieldnotes recorded by Authors 1 and 2. We analyzed the physical artifact materials alongside the video-recorded classroom interactions of the focal embodiments treating them as products of ongoing meaning-making that were shaped by and, in turn, shaped the discourse and activity structures of the classroom. We analyzed video data through content analysis (Krippendorff, 2018) to provide a sense of students' reasoning about racial inequities and systemic racism related to diabetes. We also conducted eight post-unit interviews (30-45min long); student responses to one of the interview questions were analyzed for this paper. For this question students were shown their class’s concept map and asked, “What does racism have to do with Type 2 diabetes?”. The interviews were transcribed and then analyzed by a ll authors. Through an interactive, inductive process, we developed a coding scheme for this question to capture the key ideas students expressed about the relationships between racism, the environment, and diabetes. Afterwards, the entire team reviewed the coding scheme and shared the themes they noticed in the responses (Braun & Clarke, 2006). The authors then coded the entire data set and attained 85% intercoder reliability, coding disagreements were resolved. The analysis provided additional evidence about students’ understanding of the concept map activity (embodiment) and their understanding of structural racism as shaping environments by the end of the unit. We report on our analyses of students’ understandings as manifested during the four activities that embodied our design conjecture and the interviews which reflect their conceptions at the end of the unit. Embodiment 2: Simulating the contribution of genes and environment to T2D The next embodiment we discuss is a participatory simulation activity during the seventh lesson set. The aim of the game was to help the students understand that in the multifactorial gene-environment interactions of diabetes, the environmental factors matter more than genetic factors in determining risk of diabetes. In particular, we hoped students would notice that social determinants of health, which reflect the non-neutrality of environments, result in a person’s risk being correlated with how they were racialized and classed. Modeled (with modifications) after the privilege line simulation, students received character cards (with information about genetic risk, race, income, stress level, activity level, diet etc.), they lined up at a central starting line and then moved forward or backwards based on genetic or environmental risk and protective factors. The game resulted in a spread from high to low likelihood of diabetes reflecting racial and class disparities in Type 2 Diabetes prevalence. Figure 2 a,b 2a. Visual of the Stepping Game Card Setup. 2b. Visual of the Stepping Game Card Results. After completing the activity, students filled out handouts asking: “How far are you from diabetes? What was the biggest risk factor?” The majority of students in both classes stated that the biggest risk factors for their characters were their level of UP F (ultra-processed foods) consumption and stress.  When asked to reflect on the placement of the characters in relation to each other, students concluded that the character’s furthest away from the diabetes line were all white, while the ones closest to the  diabetes line were either Black or Hispanic. During the class discussion of their observations from the game, the general sense was that the environment, rather than genes, best predicted diabetes risk. Students  experienced through the experience of characters and movement in the space, the reproduction of the data graph they saw earlier in the unit in Figure 1a. Our analysis reveals an emerging understanding of environments as non-neutral and as harmful. However, it was not clear to us, at this point, whether students understood why (and how come) the environment itself is shaped as it is. During the post-unit interviews, we showed the class concept map to the group and probed their understanding of the connection between racism and diabetes. Some groups stated that racism might ‘make a person more stressed’, which would increase their risk of diabetes-a racism-as-interpersonal explanation. Other 560 groups stated a lack of  opportunity, such as racism causing minoritized groups to have trouble finding jobs or living in disinvested  neighborhoods-a more structural explanation. 561 Insights from the post-unit revision meetings informed the revisions we need to make to the unit. It was apparent that students were not fully grasping the structural aspect of racism in the original lessons, and that there is a need to strengthen the exposure to and scaffolding around sociopolitical concepts in the unit activities. To create a more equitable society we need to support the next generation, our students, in developing critical consciousness (Freire, 1970). By taking a political turn in science education (Morales-Doyle et al., 2020), we worked with dedicated teachers to  develop a genetics unit that weaves in the social, historical, and political dimensions of a phenomenon impacted by both genetics and the environment. The “ Just Genetics ” unit was designed to address the complexity of genetic phenomena as multifactorial and to address the societal structures and forces that shape our environments, and consequently, how our genes function in these environments leading to stark health disparities. Our findings illustrate the successes and challenges of implementing this approach. We initially conjectured that to support students’ understanding of health disparities as stemming from structural inequities we would need to help them conceptualize the environment (in gene-environment interactions) as not neutral but rather as harmful a nd as intentionally structured to be so. Our findings suggest movement towards this goal. As shown in our results, students demonstrated an understanding of multifactorial genetics with both genes and the environment playing a role. They recognized that the environments in which people live are not neutral but shaped by systemic inequities such as income inequality, availability of nutritious food, and access to healthcare that influence healt h outcomes. Through our first embodiment-looking at state level data about social determinants of health-the students were able to situate environmental risk factors within their own contexts and suggest causes for the observed differences in environments across the state. There was a tension, however, between attributing racial disparities to choices groups make versus structural barriers over which people have no control. Our attempts to provide the historical context for geographical differences (i.e., redlining policies) in the second  embodiment had mixed success. We do see some evidence in the concept map (third embodiment) that students perceive racism as having both an interpersonal and a structural component and that both contribute to the making of harmful (non-neutral) environments that impact health. Yet, understanding how racism structures environments to be harmful, was more tentative and nascent. While some students identified redlining as an example of structural racism influencing health, their explanations primarily focused on the outcomes of these policies, rather than elaborating on the historical or intentional mechanisms that produced them. Moreover, some students still explained segregation as due to people wanting to live near others of the same ethnicity (this was true even in the post interviews). Our sense that students did not fully grasp the intentional and structural nature of racism was further confirmed in our design revision meetings  with the youth. These results echo findings from other justice-oriented science education research. Previous studies have shown that students engage more deeply with science when learning is connected to their local communities and framed around issues of social and environmental justice (Bang et al., 2012; Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2019), and when students investigate such issues, they begin to connect scientific ideas to systems of power and inequity (Morales-Doyle, 2017; Rubel et al., 2016). These studies highlight challe nges: students may initially struggle to connect scientific concepts to social issues (Morales-Doyle, 2017), teachers may find it difficult to facilitate classroom practices constrain students’ critical perspectives (Bang et al., 2012). In particular, scholars note that while students can often recognize inequities, moving toward explanations that link inequities to systemic and historical causes is  difficult (Morales-Doyle, 2024). Our results illustrate that although students made progress, the work of deepening critical consciousness is neither straightforward nor guaranteed. Our implementation faced limitations due to its length and the challenges of effectively addressing the standards while incorporating the sociopolitical dimension. In the next design iteration, we intend to reorganize the storyline so that discussions of power, race, and environment are interwoven (early and often) with key genetic concepts, rather thandiscussed largely during the second half of the unit. We also plan to refine the design conjectures guiding the unit to better capture the entanglement of science and society. In our initial design, the relationship between disciplinary concepts and sociopolitical analysis was under-specified, resulting in uneven opportunities for students to see how explanatory accounts of health and disease are shaped by st ructural conditions. We also did not provide enough examples of what structural racism looks like and how policies that may seem benign can have racist outcomes. In our next iteration, we aim to include more explicit interrogation of housing segregation and the policies and processes that led to disinvested neighborhoods and the consequent harm to the communities who live in them. Through these revisions, we aim to advance both the design’s coherence (in aligning disciplinary and justice-oriented goals) and the theoretical understanding of what it means to teach genetics as a site of sociopolitical reasoning.
