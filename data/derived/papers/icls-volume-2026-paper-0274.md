# Characterizing Instructional Scaffolding in Interlocking Models During a Phenomenon- driven Science Unit

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Students coordinate multiple representational modalities into coherent explanatory systems when they engage in complex scientific phenomena. This coordination is theorized as interlocking models, where representations mutually constrain and support one another. While many curricula embed modeling supports, less is known about how interlocking can be scaffolded by teachers’ real-time classroom instructional moves. In this study, we analyzed three hours of classroom video to examine a high school physics teacher’s scaffolding moves during the implementation of a phenomenon-driven magnetism unit. Our findings reveal three pedagogical moves that served to support students’ interlocking of empirical, diagrammatic, and verbal models and modalities during whole-class activity. Further, these moves unfolded in an iterative scaffolding sequence. We highlight how the moves generate d contradictions, constraints, and gaps for productive interlocking and how they form a preliminary scaffolding framework that can support complex modeling practices in science classrooms.

Contemporary reforms in science education position modeling as a c ore practice that helps learners build an understanding of  scientific phenomena and engage in the epistemic work of science (Schwarz et al., 2009; National Research Council, 2012). Situating modeling among the eight science and engineering practices, the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) encourages students to use, generate, test, and revise representations through cycles of inquiry (NGSS Lead States, 2013). [In this study, we use representations  as externalization forms used in the conceptual work of modeling, and models  as representations taken up in service of explaining how and why phenomena work (Schwarz et al., 2009; Nersessian, 1999)]. In science classrooms aligned with this vision, students encounter phenomena that are only partially visible and conceptually layered and are prompted to develop models that surface and reorganize their ideas, connect everyday intuitions with formal scientific constructs, and foster generative explanations (Lehrer & Schauble, 2000; Schwarz et al., 2017). However, students seldom use a single representation. Rather, they coordinate multiple models  across modalities such as internal mental imagery, diagrammatic sketches, physical artifacts, data displays, and linguistic explanations (Nersessian, 1999). Working with these multipl e representational affordances  requires students to knit them into coherent explanatory systems.

The challenge of coordinating multiple models is particularly salient in phenomenon-driven instructional units, where students’ questions drive sequences of investigation and representational work (Reiser et al., 2021). Manz and Georgen (2023) theorize  that in such settings, students’ conceptual progress depends on interlocking models – in which students work to align and integrat e heterogenous representations such that claims, mechanisms, and predictions mutually constrain and support one another.  Their analysis identifies four forms of epistemic work of models when they interlock: a) contradictions – tensions between resources as they are brought together from different models, b) constraints  – where aspects of a model system articulate a must-have for another, c) gaps – visible insufficiency in understanding that focus further activity, d) representational surplus  – meanings and implications that modelers might not have fully interrogated. These  forms, therefore, differentiate interlocking from individual cognitive competencies implicated in representational competence (Kozma & Russel, 1997; Ainsworth, 2006) and meta-representational competence frameworks (diSessa, 2004). While these frameworks’ focus is on individuals’ abilities to reason and translate between represen tations, interlocking foregrounds the distributed and emergent nature of learning generated when representational systems are combined for sensemaking in classroom activities.

While prior work has designed checklists, rubrics, and curriculum-embedded scaffolds to guide representational work (e.g., Zangori et al., 2015; Siller et al., 2023; Carroll & Park, 2024; Schwarz et al., 2022), there’s a need for more studies that  examine teachers’ real-time instructional moves to support students’ representational work  during interlocking. Framed within  a social-constructivist setting that fosters the co-construction of knowledge  (Vygotsky, 1978), teachers’ in-the-moment moves can be viewed as mediating tools that make interlocking possible. Characterizing these moves in practice and how they generate forms of epistemic 1857 work during interlocking, therefore, is our study’s focus. Hence, our study is guided by the question: In the context of a representationally rich  instructional unit, h ow did a teacher scaffold students’ in-the-moment model interlocking during phenomenon-driven science instruction? This study is situated within a three-week high school physics unit on magnetism and electromagnetism, using  a magnetic levitation (maglev) train as the anchoring phenomenon and co-designed with teachers using the storyline approach (Reiser et al., 2021). In the unit, students explored the phenomenon by developing initial models of how invisible mechanisms cause levitat ion and propulsion; investigating field patterns with iron filings; and constructing diagrammatic and explanatory models of magnetic fields. Specifically, our analysis focuses on activities in the first three lessons, in which students explore magnetic poles, attraction/repulsion, and the mapping of magnetic fields. The multimodal nature of these lessons had students shift between physical investigation of iron filings around magnets, diagrammatic representation using field-line sketches, verbal explanation through articulating pole location and field behavior, and tactile experience through the feeling of attraction and repulsion. Our focus is on Reid, a high school physics teacher at a rural school in the western U.S. Reid and others participated in a year-long cycle of professional learning (PL) on storyline-based instruction (Biddy et al., 2021). Reid implemented the maglev unit in the same school year in his classroom, comprising 28 students.  We focus on Reid because of  our intrinsic interest i n his interactions with students as a critical instance of scaffolding responsively in a phenomenon-driven instruction. Our data sources are video recordings (three hours across three lessons) of Reid’s implementation, student-generated artifacts, and researcher s’ memo. We transcribed  video recordings using Otter.ai. We used reflexive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2019) to identify and characterize Reid’s micro-level instructional moves and prompts  related to model construction and interlocking. Our unit of analysis was Reid’s prompts and interactions with students when multiple representations were surfaced. Thereafter, we organized these pedagogical moves into a preliminary scaffolding sequence. We present a vignette that illustrates how Reid scaffolded students’ interlocking of empirical, diagrammatic, and verbal models and modalities through his pedagogical moves during a whole-class activity. Earlier, students had investigated iron filings, creating visual patterns around magnets of varying configurations and shapes in their small groups. During these investigations, Reid went around worktables, seeding interlocking by nudging students to compare their observed pattern with a field-line diagram drawn by a student (Oliver) on the whiteboard (Figure 1a), which served as an initial consensus diagrammatic model. (Reid drew the two-view circular magnet on the whiteboard, Figure 1b) We label this as “orienting composite representation (move 1).” Reid created a representational structure that would require  interlocking by asking students to integrate their physical iron filings observations, verbal descriptions, and initial diagrams into a unified model with orthogonal views. This task required that students reconcile what they had seen (empirical model), said (verbal modality), and sketched (diagrammatic model) into a coherent representation. While students individually worked on this task, Reid  gave feedback. Then, without first building any class consensus around the students’ lateral-view drawing, he projected a side view of a circular magnet showing the poles at the edges  (F igure 1c). Reid then continued with the whole-class discussion: Lesson 3 [45:50-46:45] Reid:  But if the poles are here, then where do the lines converge? On the sides or at the poles? Noah:  At the poles Reid:  They converge at the poles, right? For us, our magnets never look like this. For us, the poles were on the side (lateral view in Figure 1b), so that's where the magnets went. But if the magnet looks like this (Figure 1c), then that's where the lines converge. (Reid built a consensus of the image in Figure 1c by drawing the field lines around it, as in Figure 1d.) Reid:  And then from this angle (pointing to the first image in Figure 1b), if you had, like, just a flat side, which is a pole facing up… what did the iron do? Liam:  It kind of came up.

Reid:  It just came up. Just like that did. So, I'll just have stuff like this (he drew a field-line emergence, Figure 1e). Kind of comes up and out, right? This segment has two moves. Reid enacted the move, “ checking representational alignment (move 2)” when he asked the convergence question, prompting students to verify the consistency between their empirical observation and diagrammatic composite model. Articulating where the iron filings converge from the empirical observation is a must-have for the diagrammatic model, which makes the convergence question function as a constraint (Manz & Georgen, 2023). A definite consensus was arrived at when Reid confirmed by stating, “ They converge at the poles, right?” Reid followed move two with “linking perspective to observation (move 3)” by prompting students to map their tactile experience during the physical observation (“what did the iron do?”) to  the specific diagrammatic perspective of the composite model. When Liam responded that “it kind of came up,” he directly drew on his tactile experience during the investigation. Reid therefore bridges the gap between the physical observation and its diagrammatic representation by drawing the emergent field lines. Similarly, in our characterization of scaffolding interlocking, we suggest that Reid’s decision not to build a consensus model around the lateral view and instead transition to and project the side-view was purposeful. It created a dissonance between students’ drawing and the new perspective, which requires them to productively interlock both views within a composite model.

Immediately after the exchange above, Reid decided to extend the composite model to a two-magnet system, which is central to the maglev train phenomenon. He stacked two circular magnets having like poles face each other (this initially proved difficult to achieve) under a can and prompted students to predict what the pattern would be when iron filings are sprinkled over a paper barrier: Lesson 3 [48:40 – 50:10] Reid:   What do you think the iron is going to do in the middle? Drake:  Nothing… it’s not going to be there… they’re pushing each other Reid:   They’re pushing each other, but they’re both going to attract the iron. Let’s check it out. (He sprinkled the iron filings) Reid:   What do you see the lines doing? …They connect pole to pole. Do they do the same in the middle? (Students: No) No, right? … they diverge. These magnetic field lines… will always connect north to south, but they never connect north to north… In the interaction above, the iron filings became a co-participant in students’ sensemaking (Manz & Georgen, 2023). When the physical model pushed back on Drake’s prediction (and others, in agreement), it produced a contradiction, which was immediately visible. Reid successfully leveraged this contradiction by converting it into 1859 another constraint (“always connect north to south but never connect north to north”) that became a rule for all subsequent models. Reid thereafter flipped one of the magnets in the configuration he created for another round of hypothesis and testing with the students. He then asked students to add both configurations to their composite model, which was another enactment of move 1 at a complex level. One cogent finding in our analysis and characterization is that the three identified moves form a scaffolding sequence that is iterative  rather than linear. Each iterative cycle function s to deepen students’ ability to interlock models. Table 1 Pedagogical Moves Forming the Scaffolding Sequence Move Description Epistemic function Orienting composite representation Articulating the goal of synthesizing multiple models into a unified composite model and defining its structure Establishes shared purpose and creates a representational structure that warrants interlocking multiple modalities Checking representational alignment Prompting students to verify the consistency of critical features of composite model with empirical observations Focuses modeling activity by enforcing constraints; one system (empirical observation) is a must-have for another (diagrammatic model) Linking perspective to observation Promoting students to map tactile experience during experimental observation to specific composite model perspective Promotes fluency across modalities, bridging the gap between abstract representation and physical experience In this study, we have offered a preliminary analysis of how interlocking models can be scaffolded through a teacher’s in-the-moment pedagogical moves. Firstly, we identified a sequence of moves (Table 1), which supports students in integrating empirical, diagrammatic, and verbal models and modalities of magnetic fields. The moves extend the interlocking models’ framework into a teaching-focused account that offers the specifics of the mechanisms through which interlocking can be achieved in classroom interactions. In addition to the four forms of epistemic work that interlocking models do, our analysis offers a preliminary characterization of what teachers do to generate these forms of work. This provides  an in-situ, responsive dimension of modeling instruction by complementing curriculum-embedded scaffolds (Schwarz et al., 2022). Secondly, the teacher’s scaffolding sequence is iterative and not linear. Reid started from a single-magnet composite model to two-magnet configurations, which are fundamental  to the maglev  train phenomenon. He cycled through the moves as he transitioned from lower-level to higher-level configurations. The iterative  nature of this sequence suggests that scaffolding interlocking effectively mirrors the epistemic nature of models and the iterative nature of modeling practices for learning progression during scientific modeling (Schwarz et al., 2009).  Thirdly, our findings highlight the role of external representations as tools in mediating the co-construction of scientific understanding. These tools position models as co-participants in sensemaking (Manz & Georgen, 2023). The iron filings, verbal descriptions and diagrams in unison participated in the interlocking process. These findings add to the understanding of responsive teaching (Robertson et al., 2015) in a modeling-rich environment, showing that Reid’s responsiveness extended beyond student ideas to leveraging representational opportunities created when models interact.

Our analysis also has implications for practice. The scaffolding sequence of these  pedagogical moves provides a practical framework that teachers can adapt when they navigate instructions that have complex multi-representations, like the maglev train unit. Professional learning programs can therefore utilize this framework to help teachers recognize moments where interlocking can be leveraged and how they can enact these moves for productive engagement with students. Furthermore, as the maglev storyline unit deliberately afforded model interlocking, efforts at curriculum development can also focus on embedding key moments in complex phenomenon-driven units where multiple modalities a re present and are in tension, and where these scaffolding moves are necessary for leveraging different modalities and affordances. The present analysis provides an empirical hook for an extended theoretical synthesis and mapping of these moves to the four forms of work that interlocking models do (Solola et al., in preparation). However,  we have laid that groundwork by identifying and characterizing the existence of teacher moves at the grain size of real-time classroom interactions and demonstrated how these moves create the conditions needed for interlocking to occur.
