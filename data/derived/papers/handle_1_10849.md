# Racial Justice Learning: Pedagogies of Witnessing

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this symposium, we explore learning racial justice across different research contexts, from social movements and formal educational settings using the framework of Pedagogies of Witnessing. We take up the possibilities and limitations of witnessing how people learn about racial justice within varied contexts. We use data from across formal and informal learning environments to trace 1. liberatory witnessing, 2. harmful witnessing, and 3. multimodal aspects of witnessing as a methodological approach. By bringing our studies into conversation, we identify lessons about when and how witnessing can serve as a productive or damaging pedagogical practice, and explore the broader lessons for theorizing learning racial justice.

## Introduction

Social movements for racial justice have been prese nt in North America for as long as processes of rac ialization and ideologies of race and racism have existed. Ind igenous nations, slave revolts, abolitionists, migr ant worker resistance, civil rights activists, and countless o thers have long engaged in acts of resistance and o rganized toward more just futures. Current iterations of soc ial movements attending to racial justice hold hist orical antecedents, yet these movements are also operating  within present-day political dynamics where emergent strategies are necessary and unfolding. Situated wi thin and in relationship to the many unfolding raci al justice movements, youth, educators, and grassroots activists are also engaging in their own iterations of ena cting racial justice within the education system as curriculum, professional development, and collective action. Th ese localized and context-specific movements for racial justice in education are connected to broader and historical movements for racial justice occurring across multi ple scales. We consider how mutual and joint witnes sing of learning about racial justice can occur within spac es of social movements and within formal institutio nal settings. We also contend with the constraints and harm that occur when witnessing is not mutual and s tructures of racial inequality are left intact. By doing so, we reveal the specific strategies, practices, and e pistemologies that foster or inhibit joint and mutual witnessing of racial justice. Racial justice work has appeared differently based on time, place, and condition, and yet at its core has always relied on collective action to problematize and resist policies, practices, and ideologies of r acial supremacy. Despite this ever presence, in the learn ing sciences, very little of our work has explicitl y attended to racial justice learning and collective action. Furt hermore, even less learning science research attend s to the ways learning for and about racial justice is a joint ac tivity. This is a rich area for learning scientists  to explore, as many of our theories of learning and empirical stud ies focus on disciplinary learning. While this work  is incredibly valuable, the contours of learning racia l justice are fundamentally different, as this lear ning challenges hegemonic ideologies made invisible to d ominant groups through their ubiquity and rendered ungrievable in dominant discourse. We build from th e emerging research on social movements in the lear ning science literature  (Curnow & Jurow, 2021) to consider how and what peop le learn as they witness while organizing for racial justice. As racial justice activists, educators, and scholar s, we feel the urgent need for the learning sciences to contribute more actively in he lping educators and organizers understand when and how people become politicized around racial justice, ga in language for naming social conditions that are h egemonic, and to imagine and fight for worlds beyond.

In this session, we come together from our differen t positionalities with commitments to racial justic e to discuss the convergences across our research con texts and analyses to identify the contributions th at sociocultural theories of learning bring to bear, a s well as future directions for learning sciences w ork to contribute to the struggle for racial justice. We w ork from the idea of pedagogies of witnessing to ex plore the ways that witnessing has often been built into raci al justice education, as racialized communities col lectivize and bear witness to each other's experiences, and a lso as white people are invited or expect/ed to wit ness the testimonies of harm of racially minoritized people. Using pedagogies of witness as a framework for ana lysis, we explore the beauty, the harms, and the limitations of witnessing as evidenced in our discrete research settings. Operationalizing key concepts Racialization in north america Across contexts, many different definitions of race, racialization, and racial justice are operational ized. Even between the studies in conversation in this symposi a, we orient to different bodies of theory to under pin our analysis. Our work is anchored in diverse North Ame rican contexts, and so these definitions attend fir st to racial logics that operate in North America, though we rec ognize that the essence of these is place-specific and grounded in the histories and relations of power in  those places. And yet, for the purposes of this di alogue, we offer a few foundational definitions to ground our discussion. We use the term racialization to analyt ically consider how people or groups come to be classified  beyond identity and how these categories are conne cted to political, social, and economic dynamics that have consequences for people’s life opportunities (Scott  & Bajaj, 2023). Within our definition of racialization, we c onsider it to be an ongoing social process rather t han a natural or biological phenomenon. Extending from that defin ition, we attend to ideas of racism as “the state-sanctioned or extralegal production and exploitation of group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death” (G ilmore, 2007, p. 28). This definition draws our attention t o structural racism rather than interpersonal expre ssions of prejudice and makes space to examine the violences played out across people’s lives. Like the other terms, the concept of ‘racial justic e’ has varying and contested meanings resulting in a series of definitional dilemmas. Here, we invoke ra cial justice to signal a meaningful struggle toward  what Patel & Price (2016) describe as “a reimagination of soci ety without a color line as the arbiter of differen tiated wellbeing” (p. 63). We do so not to recast superficial articulations of the concept, but to instead, serio usly contend with how we might “not just [undo] injustice but to  [consider] what freedom might entail” or demand of ourselves and each other (p. 79). For us, racial ju stice is a process of repairing historical and ongo ing harms, enabling full participation, and building spaces fo r thriving. This requires attending to intersecting  systems of oppression simultaneously and working toward collective liberation (Combahee River Collective, 1977). Learning racialization and antiracism We notice a shift in the learning sciences, where attending to racial justice is moving into focus (Le ibovitz, 2023; Philip 2011; Philip et al. 2016; Vakil & McKi nney De Royston, 2019; Philip & Pham, 2021; Lee et al., 2021; Shah & Yadav, 2023). This is a reflection of the shift toward research on the political and ethi cal dimensions of learning (Politics of Learning Collec tive, 2017), which draws on much longer trajectorie s of cultural dimensions of learning (Nasir et al., 2020; Nasir & Hand, 2006). Here we differentiate between research about racially minoritized groups and racial justice work, which explicitly attend to learning about and intervening in racial hierarchies, building from the recognition t hat “The racialized nature of our society gives rise to the assumption that any study primarily inv olving non-White students is effectively a study about race whether or not the idea of race or the effects of b eing raced are understood or explored” (Vakil, et a l. 2016, p. 198), rendering whiteness neutral and unmarked. Rac ial justice work attends to power relations and his tories built into laws, policies, and practices, interrogates these social relations, and re-mediates them. We are invested in a conceptualization of racializa tion as a learning process, where explicit and implicit references to racial categories and racism  are intentionally or unintentionally tied to the d isruption or stabilization of material resources and relationshi ps of power. Referencing race is never a neutral pr oject and this is particularly true in the context of racial justice organizing given the inherent political nat ure of this work. By attending to spaces of racial justice organizing, we capture how meanings of racialization, racism,  power, equity, and justice get taken up in joint activity and how people contest, reify, and transform existi ng relations of power within their racial justice organizing wor k. With our focus on how and what people learn in t he context of organizing for racial justice, we also a ttend to how the act of witnessing, witnessing acro ss shared experience and across difference, holds possibiliti es and pitfalls. To do so, we apply a conceptual fr amework of pedagogies of witness to capture the type of design that affords for racial meaning-making that promotes racial justice. We are invested in a type of racial meanin g (Patel & Price, 2016) that is divorced from the i dea of the color-line or hierarchical understanding of race. We introduce pedagogies of witnessing as a promising aspect of learning about racial justice, in part, because of its affordances of generating processes of racializ ation in terms of what race could and should mean.

Pedagogies of witnessing This symposium is organized around our conceptual f ramework – pedagogies of witnessing for mutual and collective racial justice learning. Prior research on witnessing has illustrated how witnessing encomp asses double meanings of both eyewitness testimony and bearing witness (Oliver, 2001; Villenas, 2019). Eyewitness testimony is based on the ways firsthand knowledge calls up h istoricity and our subject positioning, whereas bearing witness occurs to that which cannot be seen or recognized s ince it demands the imaginary. Yet, both aspects are integral to the act of witnessing. In t he context of organizing for justice, Villenas (201 9) illustrates how the act of witnessing testimonios from three women of Color community leaders contrib uted to a more expansive race-conscious counterpublic where people imagined how t o practice solidarity, coalition building, and organizing, and to be in community. We build on  research that illustrates the power and capacity f or learning that occurs as people bear witness to racial justice organizing. In the context of how researchers bear witness in t heir work, scholars have drawn attention to how researchers can shift their role from authority to witness (Bang, et.al., 2016; Vakil et al., 2016; Vi llenas, 2019). This shift requires an acknowledgment of how resear chers are implicated in pre-existing and ongoing tensions within settings that often intersect with social po sitioning and power (Bang et al., 2016). Other appr oaches call for a paradigmatic shift of racial justice activism  that focuses on how embodied ways of being in rela tionship within organizing spaces is an act of activism in i tself, and in turn, can facilitate healing while en riching our analyses of organizing for social change (Pour-Khorshid & Pham, Forthcoming). We draw from this growing body of research on witne ssing to consider how pedagogies of witnessing (Villenas, 2019) and with-nessing (Bang, et.al., 2016) can open up opportunit ies for mutual learning between research partners, communities/organizing spaces, a nd ourselves. We also consider how pedagogies of witnessing can open up new analytical possibilities  and relationalities that cannot occur without join t commitment to actualizing shared understanding and embodiment of collective liberation. We also analyz e the limits and constraints of opportunities for mutual learning when witnessing is not a joint or mutual endeavor.

## Analytic themes: Witnessing toward liberation, harm, and/or method

Using findings from our different research contexts, we explore three central themes: 1) liberatory wi tnessing,

2) harmful experiences of witnessing/being witnesse d, and 3) multimodal witnessing as method. We prese nt

findings from our respective research projects to h ighlight three examples of the promise and peril of pedagogies of witnessing for building more just learning experiences. Theme one: Liberatory witnessing A central theme we see emerge across our studies is  that of the liberatory power of witnessing toward racial justice. We see this happening in two ways: first, witnessing shared/similar experiences between and a mong Black people, Indigenous people, and people of colo ur, where identifying shared experiences within whi te supremacy is a life-affirming practice. The second is across racial dif ference, often with white people learning through bearing witness to the testimonies of other racialized people. Joe’s research traces how witnessing for racial jus tice fostered collective experiences that politiciz ed members of the fossil fuel divestment activist grou p. Through a multi-year participatory action research project with, the RadLab she analyzed what and how activist s learned about racialization, gender, and colonial ism, and how their learning shaped their interactions and st rategies. Her research provides practices and tools  to account for when people are becoming politicized and points  to the ways that witnessing for racial justice is one such access point where we can study how people become politicized for racial justice in a liberatory way. Witnessing shared experience. Across the RadLab’s writing, we often orient to i deas of alternative spaces, where we analyze the importance of spaces b eyond the larger Fossil Free UofT meeting spaces, w here the dynamics were oriented toward justice politics,  and where the participation of Black, Asian, and I ndigenous people was central (rather than marginal, as it was  in the large group). In these spaces, people share d their experiences of racialization at UofT and in the cam paign. The experiences of articulating their experi ences, having them validated, and noting the shared experi ence across people was a significant and powerful experience. As dynamics in the large group polarize d as racial justice became contentious, these affir ming alternative spaces were essential in keeping the Bl ack and Asian members in the group and grounded, pr oviding spaces that were affirming, and where they could de brief and recenter without having to defend their experiences, ways of knowing, or right to space.

Witnessing across difference. In the RadLab’s analysis of climate activism, we sa w examples of racial sensemaking crystalize across difference, as truste d white women were invited into spaces where their Black and Indigenous colleagues discussed their experienc es in the group and in the world. Sometimes this ha ppened as casual shittalk, but other times it was as a mor e direct callout when the group had let them down. An example of this occurred in the first summer of the campaig n, when some members of the group were developing a  new brochure to use in recruiting at Frosh orientation.  When Keara and Amil saw the draft at the bar, they  were extremely unhappy with the representation on it and  the way climate activism was framed through normat ive rather than climate justice lenses. Keara and Amil had some words with Joanna and eventually took the lead on redoing the brochure, explaining why they thought t he brochure centered white framings of climate and erased racialized impacted communities. Throughout the nex t school year, their interventions were traceable i n Joanna’s talk in the main group, as she advocated for the stances that had been stressed to her. Collective witnessing. Talia’s research, a qualitative four-year ethnography, is focused on white and/or privileged parents organizing for racial equity in k-12 schools in Oakland, California. She illustrates the process of a group of white and/or socio-economically privileged parents, Parents for Equity  (PFE), collectively becoming racially literate, and how this process of  collectively becoming racially literate informed t heir organizing strategies for school integration. She a pplies the theory of racial literacy to the context  of how white and/or privileged parents collectively make sense o f, contest, and rearticulate racialized ideologies that are attached to education policies. This case study ill ustrates how PFE collectively developed epistemolog ies, tools, and practices  to identify, address, and organize against racism t hrough contentious micro-interactions of collective learning for racial literacy. By attendi ng to contentious micro-interactions of collectively becoming racially literate, Talia illustrates how PFE members engaged, contested, rearticulated, and transformed racialized ideologies of educational equity through their coll ective learning and organizing. This theoretical and methodological expansion of racial literacy to the collective context of organizing spaces provides in sight into how organizing tools and practices for educational equity are shaped by the process of PFE members collectively becoming racially literate as they make sense of education policies. Theme two: Harmful experiences of witnessing/being witnessed As we have argued, the act of witnessing can be a v ery powerful mediator for learning. In its purest a nd most generative form, the act of bearing witness allows for a sense of togetherness and relationality (Vill enas, 2017). This is in part due to the vulnerability that is pr oduced when people allow themselves to be witnessed. Yet, we rarely contend with what being witnessed demands of  people and how identity politics gets taken up in the process of witnessing for racial justice in ways th at cause harm and limit transformative possibilitie s. For our second theme, we consider how people of color are b urdened and harmed through the process of being witnessed in racial justice spaces. We also examine  how epistemologies of deference inaccurately claim  to give racially minoritized people power but in the contex t of elite spaces, people of color become stand-ins for the entire racial community they represent and how this  process placed elite people of color as representa tives for all people of color.

The burdens of being witnessed: Narratives from Black women faculty in a university intergroup dialogue program. Aireale presents data from a qualitative study of f aculty’s experiences in a year-long intergroup dialogue (IGD) program called Crit PD (anonymized). Organized by the campus’ Women’s Cent er, Crit PD was designed to support learning and instit utional change by bringing together an interracial group of faculty at a midsize, R-1 midwestern Historically White Institution of high er education for sustained dialogues. Participation in the program included attending a t wo-day critical reflection session and monthly meeting s where participants engage in dialogues about the in tersection of racism and sexism within and across r acial groups. Each cohort had a balanced representation o f white faculty and Faculty of Color — a necessary feature of interracial dialogue. In fact, Crit PD’s theory of change was grounded in assertions about the prom ise of “interactional diversity,” or the idea that bringin g together diverse groups of people with varying so cial identities can support learning and change (Gurin e t al., 2002). Thus, the interracial dialogues in Cr it PD were positioned as a racial justice initiative on campus  that can lead to meaningful institutional change. What I found through in-depth semi structured  interviews with Crit PD participants, however, was much more nuanced. Narratives from Black women faculty revealed how th e same power dynamics that necessitate racial justi ce initiatives, like intergroup dialogues on racism and sexism on campus, are reproduced in and constitut ive of the learning that happened in Crit PD.

When asked about what they learned from participati ng in Crit PD, both Tanya and Sarah spoke at length about emotion management. Hochschild (1990) describes emotion management as the calibration of an experience or expression of emotion to existing dis play rules, or norms of the environment. Emotion management can be personal (where you’re managing y our own emotions), interpersonal (where you’re managing other people’s emotions), or both (Francis, 1997). Tanya, a STEM professor, spoke to this in her reflection on the role she played in Crit PD, “it’s  always this thing that you have to make them [e.g., white folks] feel better.” When asked explicitly what it was that she learned in Crit PD, she pointedly resp onded, “I need to meditate more.” Tanya was very concerned ab out not having her frustration readily apparent to other Crit PD participants. She elaborated, It doesn’t do me any good to show that frustration because in showing that frustration, what people see that as is an angry Black woman, right? And so what I learned is that I’ve got to do my meditation on the outside, as an example, theore tically, and actually, literally — literally and figuratively, so that I don’t show that. I kind of need to show that I can move on. Her rationalization was that this is needed to adva nce learning. “Once people see [my frustration], th ey freeze, and they don’t learn.” So rather than being authent ic to her feelings, she chose “to bury all of that within me and then kind of be there for [her white colleagues].” Sarah, a multiracial Black woman in the Social Scie nces, highlighted the realizations that emerged from Faculty of Color being together in Crit PD. “W hen you put all the [Faculty of Color] in one room,  it felt like we were actually able to breathe.” She then interpreted the implications of her previous statement by saying, “It means that every day when we go to work, we put  on a coat, and we walk around with this whole pers onality that’s created so we can be acceptable. And we can’ t be ourselves. And the only time we can be ourselv es is when we get together.” The experience of Faculty of  Color co-witnessing each other in meaningful and authentic, rather than superficial and performative, was impactful to Sarah because being witnessed by  white faculty in that same setting brought up a lot of ne gative emotions that she previously compartmentaliz ed. Emotional labor and demands for emotion management made Crit PD especially taxing for Sarah. Yet, the act of surfacing emotions that were once buried through  acts of mutual witnessing was a lesson for Sarah. The cost of keeping feelings bottled up was too high. When a sked how to support Faculty of Color in Crit PD, Sa rah quipped, “They need to have someone meet with a psy chiatrist separately afterward, each one of us, bec ause I think what is buried, we want to keep it buried.” T ogether, these narratives point to how equity-minded PD programs like IGD operate as racialized spaces that  too often perpetuate an inequitable division of la bor for learning, and thus, undermine the racial justice aims to which they aspire. Deference and identity politics. Literature attending to the complexity of racialize d and classed dynamics of organizing and academic spaces has foun d that elites working towards justice engage in “ep istemic deference” in their attempt to meet the call to “li sten to the most affected” or “center the most marg inalized” (Táíwò, 2022). While these practices stem from moti vations of increasing the social power of marginali zed people, research has found that epistemic deference  can inadvertently work to counter  marginalized groups’ interests, especially when occurring in elite space s. For instance, in the context of Talia’s research, members of Parents For Equity (PFE) have outside power and inf luence because of their relationship to privilege a nd power as white and/or socio-economically privileged people. Thus, when the part icipants engaged in forms of deference such as “passing the mic” to spokespeople  from marginalized groups within their elite space,  they utilized a strategy reliant on “racial reaganomics”  in  the sense that the power held by elite people from marginalized groups would eventually trickle down t o people from marginalized groups who were not able  to access the elite space (Táíwò, 2022). Instances whe re PFE members engaged in acts of deference directe d attention towards the attention economy, that is, who speaks and how often, instead of int errogating the material economy, which produces the social system that marginalizes the spokesperson in the first place. Talia illustrates how these interactions of deference in elite organi zing spaces provide cover for the abdication of res ponsibility and ultimately insulate activists from connection a nd moments of transformation. Furthermore, this nor m of “passing the mic” tokenized and essentialized  marginalized people within the elite organizing spa ce, disproportionately empowered elite members of oppre ssed groups, and weakened relationship bonds of comradeship in the struggle for radical change.

In a contentious meeting where PFE members were deb ating how they wanted to publicly position their organizing group as either for or against sla ted school closures that disproportionately impacte d majority low-income Black and Latinx OUSD public schools, the ma jority of PFE members initially called the slated school closures “racist”. However, once the only Bl ack PFE member, Tanya, expressed discomfort in publ icly calling the slated school closures racist, pointing  to PFE’s inability to know the District’s reasonin g for closing certain public schools, other PFE members began to switch their stance. In explaining her discomfort w ith PFE calling the slated school closures as racist, Tanya  said, “Pardon the naive question, but is OUSD real ly targeting majority Black schools…maybe it’s just that I’m not  radical enough.” In response to Tanya’s concern ab out calling the district racist, twonewer white PFE me mbers, Jenny and Claire, revoked their previous “kn ee-jerk support” for PFE to call the slated school closures  as racist in their public statement. For instance,  Jenny responded in PFE’s private slack channel, “I want t o walk back my knee-jerk support for calling the closures racist, I think I jumped the gun a bit before think ing more deeply about it.” Jenny’s revoking of her initial support to call the closures “racist” was indicativ e of a theme found in Talia’s data where newer and white PFE members often deferred to Tanya’s assessment when i t came to racism. Tanya’s opinion about racism, as the only Black member of PFE, seemed to hold significan t weight among the newer PFE members who engaged in an epistemology of deference, often deferring to he r when it came to calling certain policies or disco urse racist. Jenny’s choice to defer to Tanya exemplifies her de sire to center Tanya’s voice as the only Black memb er of PFE while failing to recognize her deference to Tan ya empowered Tanya as an elite member of an oppress ed group while ignoring the demands from the low-income Black and Latinx OUSD communities.

## Theme three: Multimodal witnessing as method

Through her two-year pilot study with K-12 teachers of color, Josephine took an exploratory  approach to examine how teachers of color lived, felt, and embo died ways of understanding race, racism, and power as untapped forms of knowledges that can become (or ha ve already become) pedagogical resources for critic al civic and Ethnic Studies education. She developed multimodal witnessing as a methodological and analytic tool for juxtaposing dimensions of social activities and perspectives that are concurrently happening at the same time and across time.

Drawing inspiration from a rich scholarly lineage o f researchers who engage critical multimodality to capture multiple and overlapping forms of individua l and collective meaning-making practices (e.g., Campano et.al., 2020; Marin et.al., 2020), Josephine used a udio/video recording, artifacts, photographs, writt en field notes, and interview transcripts to multimodally ca pture how teachers draw from various meaning-making resources (e.g., language, image, sound, gesture, m ovement) to attend to students’ individual and coll ective antiracist learning. Given the complex social inter play of embodied pedagogies as historicized, princi pled, and improvisational practice (Philip, 2019), witnessing  as a joint endeavor between researchers and resear ch partners grounded in Women of Color feminisms (e.g., Pour-Khorshid & Pham; forthcoming; Villenas, 2019; Wilcox, 2021; Winn & Ubiles, 2011) expands opportun ities for understanding the joint nature of cogniti ve, emotional, spiritual, and contextual dimensions of learning and teaching. Together, critical multimoda lity and witnessing as a joint endeavor offers expansive ana lytical possibilities for examining and representin g the simultaneity of multiple and, at times, conflicting  perspectives of antiracism that emerge in shared s ocial space pointing to how teachers and students work together  through collective sensemaking to proactively and improvisationally (re)center the dignified humanity and livelihoods of communities of Color.

Using multimodal data as meaning-making resources about their embodied and affective understandings of racial (in)justice, teachers of c olor can move from “disarticulated [racial] grief” (Cheng,

2001) to collective acknowledgement of similar raci al wounds as a principled foundation for developing

pedagogies of collective resistance (Grinage, 2019)  and “thick solidarity” (Liu & Shange, 2018). Moreo ver, developing a language of self empowers teachers of color to see themselves and communities in their humanities and full personhoods, rather than solely  from a lens of marginality, oppression, resistance  and as “other” (Nyachae & Pham, 2024). Witness as method r epositions researchers as co-learners, research partners, and essential knowledge holders.

## Significance and contributions

The 2024 conference theme around healing is urgentl y necessary as fascism is on the rise globally. Bef ore we can heal, we need truth and clarity about the condi tions, histories, and legacies of racial supremacy (and in other contexts, caste, etc.). This work responds to calls for Learning Science  researchers to be engaged and contribute to the project of justice in ways that are historic ized and bend toward justice. Joe’s research traces  how witnessing for racial justice fostered collective e xperiences that politicized members of the RabLab g roup. Her research provides practices and tools to account fo r when people are becoming politicized and points t o the ways that witnessing for racial justice is one such  access point where we can study how people become politicized for racial justice. While we acknowledg e how this new theoretical framework of pedagogies of witnessing provides insight into how and what peopl e learn for racial justice, we also found that when witnessing for racial justice was not mutual, the l earning was deeply constrained and racialized harm was produced. Aireale’s research provides testimony int o how faculty of color in a higher education contex t experienced undue burden of emotional labor and emo tional management in professional learning contexts focused on diversity, equity, and inclusion. Her re search contends with the witnessing spaces when lea rning for racial justice occurs in multi-racial settings and invites future studies for desi gn-based research that considers the emotional labor that faculty of color endure in  the very spaces that are aiming to ameliorate the harm they are themselves producing. Through the lens of racia l literacy, Talia’s research captured how white and /or privileged people navigate the complexity of racial ized and class dynamics of organizing spaces. Her r esearch also provides practical insight for multi-racial coalitions organizing for racial justice in supporting individuals, particularly those that are white and/or privileged, to develop more robust analyses of power, race, a nd inequality. Josephine examined how teachers of colo r witnessed ways of understanding race, racism, and  power as untapped forms of knowledge that can become peda gogical resources for critical education. Her resea rch provides methodological insight and innovation to h ow witness as a method positions researchers and re search partners as co-learners and agentic knowledge holders and creators. Our focus on the liberatory power of witnessing tow ard racial justice is a critical intervention in th e learning sciences. Each of our studies illustrate t he possibilities of anti-racist organizing when witnessing is a joint or mutual endeavor. Yet, our studies serve as  a cautionary tale of the limitations of anti-racist organizing when witnessing is not mutual, and how the harms and labor of witnessing reify and reproduce the very racialized structures that organizers aim to amelio rate. Given that racialization is an ongoing, situa ted, and historically significant process, we also believe t hat as people witness learning for racial justice t hey can interrupt existing racialized hierarchies that prod uce social inequities. In this sense, we argue that  pedagogies of witnessing is a new and important theory that offer s insight into how people learn from witnessing act ions towards racial justice. This is a modest step towar d learning sciences research contributing to commun ity and school-based racial justice learning and reflects the dema nds that our field take responsibility for addressi ng pressing social issues. Our hope is that in witness ing and theorizing collectively, our field will bol ster our commitment to racial justice and engage in the project of racial justice with increased urgency and vigor.
