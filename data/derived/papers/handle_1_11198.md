# Learning From Errors and Worked Examples to Address Common Misunderstandings in Linear Functions Knowledge

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
A series of studies were conducted to explore designs for instructional interventions, including worked examples and learning from errors, that can address misunderstandings in linear functions (LF) knowledge of college students. Study 1 focused on identifying and modeling errors that students most commonly make during the essential conceptual and procedural components of the problem-solving process for different types of LF items. Study 2 then addressed common errors by testing the efficacy of different forms of worked example (WE) presentation. Findings from Study 2 led to changes in the design of the WE which were then tested in Study 3. Overall, the findings support the importance of tailoring learning interventions to the learner. Instructional interventions using WE and learning from errors can be tailored to address common misunderstandings for important mathematical topics such as LF.

Major issues One topic that college students struggle with and c ould benefit from instructional intervention is lin ear functions (LF). Reasoning with and about LF is considered ess ential knowledge for college and career readiness (e.g., Conley et al., 2011). Understanding essentials of l inear algebra requires cognitive flexibility, such as moving between tabular, algebraic, graphic, and semantic representations, as well as moving between abstract, algebraic, and geometric languages, sometimes all in one task (Mainali, 2 021). Evidence suggests that college students experience considerable difficulty solving even simple LF problems (Mielicki & Wiley, 2016). The focus of Study 1 was to explore and model common types of errors that students make in these types of problems that lead to pervasive difficulty. Observations about the nature  of the errors led to the design and development of  an instructional intervention comprising worked examples and error-processing tasks. Worked example (WE) presentation as an instructiona l intervention has proven to be an effective learning tool. To increase this benefit, WE can be paired with error-processing tasks (e.g., Booth et al., 2013). The focus of Study 2 explored what specific design of W E and error-processing tasks is most beneficial for which learners. There were two major observations from St udy 2. While presenting WE for LF algebra problems with and without error-related processing tasks did result in significantl y higher performance in comparison to only solving problems, exposure to this intervention was not equally beneficial for all learners. The disparity in benefit from these interventions r equired further exploration to 1) explain the disparity in outcomes, and 2) augment the current interventio n design to address this issue. This was the focus of Study 3. Literature on learning from errors indicates that f avorable prior knowledge of the domain is essential  to meaningfully engaging with error processing tasks (e.g., Kapur, 2016). As such, students may require s upport to guide them through meaningfully processing erroneou s examples. Thus, there may be additional condition s beyond those established in the current literature that inform the effectiveness of WE with errors as an instructional intervention. Study 3 tested the efficacy of an aug mented design of the WE with error-processing tasks tha t promote the retrieval of relevant prior knowledge t o support error processing for those who otherwise have difficulty doing so. The following sections expand on the theory, methods, results, and implications of Studies 1, 2, and 3.

Theoretical and methodological approach(es) pursued: Study 1 Study 1 was conducted to understand the nature of c ollege students’ misunderstandings in the solution of LF problems. The study explored solution accuracy on L F problems presented in different contexts, common error types, and consideration of major conceptual and procedural errors exhibited by students. This included examining the cognitive underpinnings of the solution process  with a focus on the students’ schem atic knowledge for representation of the given problem types. This was  done by qualitative coding of students’ solutions to description, graph, and table LF problems with a pa rticular focus on errors to provide insight into th e cognitive underpinnings of misunderstandings that students typically have in this knowledge domain. In Study 1, data were collected from 151 college students enrolled in an introductory psychology class at a U.S. research intensive public university with  a diverse student population. Students were given 21 total LF problems, 7 graph, 7 table, and 7 description items, in an open-response format which required subjects to demonstrate the actions they take in generating a solution. Responses were scored based on accuracy, and erroneous responses were further analyzed based on  the nature of the error as mapped to a specific discrepancy in LF knowledge.

## Major findings, conclusions, implications: Study 1

Accuracy analyses showed that overall performance w as highest on description problems (M = 5.46, SD = 1.34) and lowest on graph (M = 3.08, SD = 2.40) and table (M = 3.85, SD = 2.66) problems. Considering these performance levels, comprehensive error analyses focused on responses to Table and Graph problems. Study 1 data suggest that there are common conceptual and procedural errors that students consistently make while solving these problems. Students demonstrated a tendency to make rational, rule-based errors that are conditional on the format in which the problem info rmation is presented. The errors observed in both p roblem types demonstrate that students commonly conceptualize tables and graphs as a compilation of individual points, instead of understanding that those points make up a conceptual entity (Kaput, 2018). Thus, it seems t hat the surface-structural characteristics of these problems contri bute to the erroneous solutions of these types of L F problems.

Theoretical and methodological approach(es) pursued: Study 2 In mathematics learning, a common root of most erro rs is an overgeneralization of rules, meaning that the conditions of the rules were encoded in an overly general way and as such apply to more situations than is correct or appropriate. Therefore, they are  activated and applied in situations incorrectly. Th us, correcting the error generally relies on the specialization of the target knowledge (Ohlsson, 1996). The specialization hypothesis states that to correct errors caused by overly generalized rules, more information about the respective task environments needs to be integrated into the rules. One common approach to achieve this using error lea rning is by integrating error-processing tasks in WE.  Tasks that involve detecting and correcting errors have been shown to benefit learning because they promote adaptive conceptual thinking (Beege et al., 2021; Kapur, 2016). Study 2 focused on testing the learning benefits of WE paired with different error-processing tasks. 278 total subjects were presented with a pretest of bas ic LF knowledge, then the instructional materials w hich comprised the intervention, followed by a LF post-test serving as a learning outcome measure. The intervention presented 2 of either table or graph LF problems th at were correctly worked out, t hen 5 of one of the three experimental variations of the same problem type: LF problems that are: (1) worked-out solutions requiring error detection, (2) worked-out solutions that indicated an error, or (3) worke d-out solutions to the problems with no error. The errors exemplified in the erroneous WE were the most common of the error types observed in Study 1 for each problem type.

## Major findings, conclusions, implications: Study 2

Visualization of posttest data indicated non-normal distributions for all groups, and a Shapiro-Wilk test of normality indicated that the data was non-normal (W = 0.79, p <.001). For all groups most sc ores fall near the extremes at 0 and 7 as opposed to centering around the respective scale mean, as observed in Study 1. This suggests that intervention exposure shifted the pat tern of student performance for these problems such  that they are answering most or all the questions either correctly or incorrectly regardless of the condition mean. Posttest performance was compared to a baseline per formance estimate established by the normative results obtained in Study 1 for those same problems. For both graph and table problem groups all three intervention conditions led to improved performance compared to the Study 1 baseline estimate, but the Error Indica ted (Graph: U = 5081, p <.001; Table: U = 3867, p = 0. 05) and No Error (Graph: U = 4276, p <.001; Table:  U = 4525.5, p = 0.03) groups showed significant differences in performance. The Error Detection groups did not show performance differences relative to the Study 1 baseline estimate (Graph: U = 3981.5, p = 0.30; Table: U = 4004, p = 0.27).

However, for those whose misunderstanding was not directly addressed in the intervention, relevant prior knowledge was not successfully activated, so, they did not map the focal conceptual features of the solution in the example to their knowledge of LF. As a result, thos e students answered most or all the posttest questi ons incorrectly. This suggests that interventions with error-processing tasks may only be beneficial if the pres ented errors reflect the specific misunderstandings that students have. Theoretical and methodological approach(es) pursued: Study 3 Study 2 findings emphasize the role that prior know ledge plays in the learning process. The learners w ho were able to benefit from the intervention demonstrated evidence of learning benefits as observed in the im provement of performance on the posttest. The intervention wa s able to facilitate meaningful learning processes when the error in their knowledge was made salient to them i n the WE and revision of the faulty knowledge was e nabled through error-processing tasks.

As useful and beneficial erroneous WE may be, certa in learners are not able to benefit from this intervention design because the current intervention designs do not support learning in those who have  low prior knowledge of the topic. Research shows that favorable prior knowledge of the domain is essential to meaningfully engaging with error processing tasks (e.g., Zhang & Fiorella, 2023). Error detection and correc tion requires domain-specific declarative and practical knowledge (Kapur, 2016), and a common misunderstanding students seem to have is overly generalized domain knowledge structures. As such, students may require support to guide them through meaningfully processing erroneous exam ples, with the goal of specialization of the target knowledge.

One strategy in improving the current design of thi s intervention could be through the integration of conceptually-relevant prerequisite knowledge analogs. Research b y Sidney and Alibali (2015) and Leech et al.,

(2008) has shown the benefit of exposure to such an alogs in learning new concepts because it activates  relevant

conceptual prior knowledge before instruction to facilitate learning. This is because presenting structurally similar analogs can facilitate the retrieval of prior conceptual knowledge needed to process subsequent information. The nature of the errors observed in Study 1 and 2 indicated that subsequent instruction should focus on specialization of LF knowledge. Thus, structural analogs of prerequisite LF knowledge could be designed in a way that presents what students do know about tables and graphs and guide them to cons truct accurate representations of them in the context of LF. To th is end, the analogs presented  in the intervention in Study 3 guided subjects in reconceptualizing tables and gra phs through mapping the individual points either from a table to a graph or from a graph to a table. Guiding them through forming the graph or table themselves makes it salient that these points can be assembled to form an entit y which can be extracted to form an algebraic expression to represents each entity.

Study 3 included 234 subjects who were first presen ted with the same pretest as Study 2, then the structural analogs were presented before one of three intervention conditions, 1) error detection, 2) error indicated, or 3) no worked example. The condition with no work ed example, which differs from Study 2, test ed whether exposure to the analog itself could lead to learnin g benefits. Lastly, all groups were presented with the same posttest as Study 2.

## Major findings, conclusions, implications: Study 3

Visualization of posttest data again revealed non-normal distributions for all groups. A Shapiro-Wilk test of normality indicated that the data were non-normal (W = 0.61, p <.001). For all groups the dat a reflected a negatively skewed distribution as opposed to an eve n distribution between the extremes 0 and 7 as obse rved in Study 2. This suggests that the change in the intervention has caused a shift in the pattern of student performance for these problems, such that most subjects are now solving posttest problems correctly. To test the efficacy of the addition of structural analogs to the intervention, posttest performance a fter intervention exposure was compared to a baseline pe rformance estimate established by the normative res ults obtained in Study 2 for those same problems. For bo th graph and table problem groups all three interve ntion conditions led to improved performance compared to performance in Study 2, but the Error Indicated gro ups (Graph: U = 968.5, p =.01; Table: U = 1316, p = 0.00) and Error Detection in Table group (U = 837.5, p = 0.00) showed statistically significant differences in per formance. The Error Detection Graph group (U = 1215.5, p = 0.21), as well as both groups in the No Example con dition, which was compared to the Study 1 baseline performance to e stablish the role of only analog presentation, did not show significant performance differences relative to Study 2 performance (Graph: U = 3109.5, p =.10; Table: U = 3321.5, p = 0.130). Findings from Study 3 showed a significant improvem ent in performance for those who were exposed to erroneous WE that were paired with structural analogs that specifically addressed common misunderstandings that students had. This indicates that the addition  of the analogs facilitated the processes that were  needed for certain students to benefit from the WE and error processing tasks. Moreover, we found that presenting the analogs on their own to students d id not lead to learning benefits, further indicati ng that the fundamental aspect of this intervention is pairing the analog with the erroneous WE.

## Potential significance of the work

This series of studies brought to light several findings that ultimately led to the design of an effective intervention. Study 1 established the glaring need for an intervention for LF topic knowledge. Findings suggested that learning from WE with and without different error-processing tasks could be a potentially useful inte rvention given the nature of the errors observed, which was the focus of Study 2. Findings from Study 2 indicated differential levels of performance improvement relative to the different intervention designs. This was addressed in Study 3 in which erroneous WE were paired with analogs that focused on facilitating the construction of accurate repres entations of graphs and tables. Ultimately, this led to an overall increase in learning benefits indicating that the discrepancy between the mental representations that students co nstruct and the problem situation, which has caused  low performance in these problems, can be addressed by pairing WE error-processing tasks with analogs. Findings from these studies point to significant we aknesses in how students were previously instructed, what they ultimately learned about LF, and ultimate ly, their relative inability to engage in solving L F problems of the type often found in multiple areas of higher  education study. Furthermore, results from the int ervention studies take into account that typical student misu nderstandings have implications for how students co uld be instructed when they learn algebra earlier in their  education, as well as the importance of diagnostic assessment procedures to help detect and correct common misund erstandings that could be used to select effective instructional adaptations for individual students.
