# Reinventing Measurement Models for the Learning Sciences: Reconceptualizing Psychometrics to Accommodate Context as Intrinsic to Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper introduces an approach to measurement that positions context and situativity as an intrinsic part of the measurement model in a manner compatible with the learning sciences’ view of learning as a transactive process within a broader sociocultura l ecosystem. This approach relies on a mixed methods approach to measurement in which qualitative data are integrated into a moderated nonlinear factor analytic (MNLFA) framework. Using data from a longitudinal student of Ph.D. students in the biological sciences, we demonstrate that sensemaking context elicited through interview data could be incorporated directly into the model to enhance the comparability of scale scores collected across multiple timepoints. On multiple items that demonstrated item parameter drift (IPD) without moderation, the inclusion of a relevant idiographic contextual variable permitted the fitting of a moderated model that eliminated IPD.

From the worldview of the learning sciences, psychometric measurement has a stark and fatal flaw: it frames the influence of context and situativity as “error,” “bias,” or “confounding variables” which detracts from the ability to measure the “true score” of learning or some other outcome (Feldon, 2024a). Accordingly, such measures are inherently “too narrow or too partial to give a true sense of what thinking and learning are all about” (Schoenfeld, 1992, p.180). Because the holistic learning ecosystem  is itself the fundamental mechanism that gives rise to learning in a meaningful sense, context must be considered signal rather than noise (Barab, 2022). For this reason, it is unsurprising that a 2014 survey found that  only 20 of 253 learning scientists (~8%) used quantitative methods exclusively (Sawyer, 2022). Following an additional decade of evolution in the learning sciences, we speculate that today the proportion of primarily quantitative learning scientists is likely to be substantially lower.  However, just as the learning sciences continue to refine its focus and methodologies, psychometrics and measurement theory have continued to develop. While perhaps three decades late to the party, research in measurement theory began to acknowledge not only the importance of considering context, but the intrinsic role it plays in psychological and learning phenomena (Pellegrino, 2022). Most of these emerging approaches develop validity arguments based on  use/situation-specific interpretations of scores (e.g., evidence centered design; Mislevy, 201 8) or interpretations of conventional measurement approaches anchored within specific curricul a (e.g., learning progression-based interpretation; Wilson, 2018). While valuable, these strategies each fundamentally treat context as something separate from the learning that they measure—scores on learning assessments are interpreted against the context  in which they were collected or used.  Thus, they are not fully aligned with the understanding of the learning sciences that learning entails situated cognition where sociocultural mechanisms cannot be meaningfully partitioned off as distinct from the core phenomenon of learning (Pea, 2016; Schoenfeld, 1992). This paper introduces a new strategy to the learning sciences for the development of measurement models that directly reflect the inherent role of situativity and context inextricable from  learning as a phenomenon.  The approach engages new mixed methods strategies as well as recent statistical advances to integrate qualitative perspectival and experiential sensemaking into quantitative symbol. The result is a strategy for quantified measurement that centers constructivist mechanisms at the core of the relationship between an aggregate score (e.g., from a scale or multi-item assessment), the learner, and the context in which they are situated. To convey the underlying concepts and application of this approach, this paper discusses central conceptual and methodology issues, then presents an exemplar use case. ## Conceptual framework

234 Departure from quantitivity in the learning sciences The 1960s and early 1970s saw optimism among education researchers for the value of controlled experiments as the primary (if not only) way to meaningfully contribute to an understanding of learning and verify improvements in educational practice (Kennedy, 1997). However, this optimism quickly gave way to increasing skepticism regarding the validity and utility of quantification when applied to learning in context. Debates regarding methods and measurements focused on, among other things, the role of covariates, reliability, and interaction effects. Many concluded that the issue was unsolvable: Even Lee Cronbach threw in the towel in 1975, when he conceded not only that it was impossible to design studies that could take into account all the relevant variables, but that even if we succeeded at that, the findings from social research would not accumulate over time but instead would “decay” because the social contexts were constantly changing…We will never, he concluded, be able to generate stable findings" that can provide the basis for theories about social phenomena, including the phenomenon of teaching and learning (Kennedy, p. 5). Implicit in this account is that learning cannot be studied independent of the contexts in which it occurs  and that the learner cannot be the sole subject of study. T herefore, learner, learning, and context must be studied as a complete system (Pea, 2016).

From the complete system perspective, Greeno  (1997) argued that non-situated approaches relied on a problematic factoring assumption. The factoring assumption treats the cognitive processes of learning as entities which can be pulled out of the contexts in which learning occurs and analyzed meaningfully as a separate construct. The assumption thus positions identity, environment, and societal context as variables which surround the “real” object of study—individual cognitive processes  that permit learners to acquire knowledge as meaningful when disconnected from context. This separation of elements into factors that are theoretically stable across settings makes it possible to measure, compare, and generalize performance without needing to fully account for situational differences within traditional psychometric frameworks. However, the core premises of the learning sciences are incompatible with the factoring assumption and require a measurement strategy that fundamentally engages learning phenomena as situated and fundamentally enmeshed in context. Measurement models without the “Factoring Assumption” Quantitative perspectives  which embrace the factoring assumption  tend to treat relationships, practices, and structures as severable aspects of context, which can be parsed out and included or excluded from analyses without undermining the ability to derive meaningful insights about learning.  However, we adopt a  situated view, in which learning cannot be parceled into “knowledge” and “context” factors, because it occurs within and emerges from the interactions of multiple complex s ystems which transcend the individual. Greeno (1997) emphasized that this makes the study of learning more complex, but also more faithful to the  reality of the phenomenon. In our approach to measurement, we conceptualize context as intrinsic to learning or other psychological phenomena in a novel way.  Rather than positioning contextual or personal sensemaking aspects of learning as error variance (traditional psychometric approach) or covariates (factoring assumption approach), we conceptualize these aspects as fully moderating the relationships between individual measurement items and the latent (indirectly measurable) construct, which an aggregate scale or sum score represents. In a conventional unidimensional measurement model (see Figure 1A), the target of measurement is represented as a single latent factor that predicts variance in each of the items comprising a scale.  Additionally, the attained value of the latent variable and each manifest (i.e., directly measured) item are influenced by residual variance (i.e., error) (Lord & Novick, 1968). However, Stewart (200 8, p. 119) argues that in conceptualizing quantitative analyses which engage situativity and other social constructs (e.g., identity, context), models must preserve the relations amongst the broader social context, local causality/history, and structurally reflect “current social interactions … related to [individual] characteristics and prior conditions and reactions.”  To do so, a measurement model must reflect the influence of such elements in every parameter of the model and allow for individual differences in the experience of these elements to operate in the model to yield a measure value that is appropriately interpretable. In statistical terms, such individual sensemaking and context must moderate the estimation of the model’s factor loadings, item intercepts, latent factor score, and residual variance (see Figure 1B). Such a measurement model presents the opportunity to engage in measurement of learning in a way that reflects the theoretical foundation of the learning sciences.  While still conceptualizing contextual and in-the-head aspects of learning as differentiable to some extent, this approach encapsulates their collective interaction within the model in deriving a value represented by the latent factor score. 235 This perspective maps onto a statistical technique known as moderated nonlinear factor analysis (MNLFA; Bauer, 2017) Although MNLFA was not specifically developed as either a mixed methodology tool or a model amenable to the premises of the learning sciences, its purpose is to accommodate “background characteristics [that] could alter the process by which differences on  the latent factor produce differences in item responses, as represented by the item parameters (e.g., intercepts or factor loadings in [factor analysis], or difficulty and discrimination parameters in an IRT model)” (Curran et al., 2018, p. 828). Preserving qualitative meaning in a quantitative framework A major criticism of quantitative research is that it often reflects a reductionist approach to nuanced phenomena that rely on human sensemaking (Eisner, 2017). In contrast, most modes of qualitative research attempt to conceptualize the meaning-making of participants in a highly nuanced fashion, while recognizing that there are limits to the extent we can attain intersubjectivity (i.e., fully shared understanding of perspective or experience between a participant and a researcher; Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Zah avi, 2007).  In both the quantitative and the qualitative approaches, there is meaning held by participants which cannot be fully represented in the research product. Thus, the difference lies in the extent to which the researcher endeavors to understand the nuances of sensemaking and represent them in the construction of categories used for analysis (e.g., codes, themes, variables). The fact that both quantitative and qualitative educational research methods rely on such classification strategies to facilitate analysis highlights an opportunity: While categorical variables used in quantitative analysis may often be generated without much—or any —nuance (e.g., survey checkbox categories of race, sex, or gender), this is a choice of the instrument creator. Indeed, when Stevens (1946) introduced his theory of scales of measurement and defined categorical variables, he was explicit that nominal values are determined by researchers’ ability to separate data into qualitatively distinct groups ba sed on criteria they define.  These criteria can be as nuanced and detailed as the researchers themselves decide. Thus, approaches to qualitative research which  rely on the definition of codes and themes for identifying categories of meaning or experience (e.g., grounded theory [Glaser & Strauss, 1967], thematic analysis [Clarke & Braun, 2021]) also meet Stevens’s criteria for categorical variables.  While the practice of rigorous grounded theory or thematic analysis entail far greater detail and nuance in identifying its categories than is typical of quantitative research, such granularity does not prevent researchers from examining data and determining whether a given instance does or does not meet the criteria to be assigned a particular code.  This categorization can accordingly be represented in a binary fashion —either a code does or does not apply to a certain utterance or observation, in which case a researcher can choose to represent the code status as a 1  (yes) or 0 (no) without any loss of the meaning of the code itself. On this basis, thorough analysis resulting in the thick description of personal meanings or interactions can be integrated into quantitative analyses with far greater preservation of complexity and situativity than is typical of less effortful quantitative practice. 236 This binary representation can then be incorporated into an MNLFA measurement model as a context or sensemaking moderator, as illustrated in Figure 1B (Note: The presentation of this argument is obviously very condensed; for a more detailed explanation, see Feldon [2024b]). Item parameter drift as a consequence of ignoring situativity In psychometrics, item parameter drift (IPD) is a form of differential item function (DIF) in which the properties of the measurement model for a particular instrument change over time. IPD is a threat to the validity of a measure, because if a measurement model is inconsistent over time, it is impossible to determine whether observed changes are due to true change in the target of measurement or differences in the way that items relate t o the common factor for some other reason. This problem is commonly referred to in the statistical literature as a lack of measurement invariance (Millsap, 2011).

It is our position that one important reason for IPD is that conventional psychometric approaches do not take into account changes in social context or individual sensemaking.  In the use case introduced in this paper, for example, how graduate students formulate their beliefs about confidence in their research abilities (i.e., self-efficacy) and make sense of items attempting  to measure those beliefs is highly likely to be influenced by the nature of their relationship with their faculty advisor.  Such relationships are complex, multifaceted, and evolve over time (Chesnut et al., 2015), so it would be logical that the ways in which students understand and respond to a measurement instrument would change as a function of the context in which their experiences are embedded. If such context is not built into the measurement model as an intrinsic aspect of the phenomenon, then it is likely that the model itself would perform inconsistently over time as the context shifts, making it impossible to meaningfully compare self-efficacy scores over time—even within person. If such contextual and sensemaking elements of the situated phenomenon were incorporated into measurement models and  enhanced measurement invariance, the obtained data could be of higher quality and better reflect the totality of the phenomenon under study, because it would provide both improved technical characteristics and reflect a more holistic theoretical perspective of the phenomenon.  In other words, the validity of the instrument would be enhanced, because it would capture a greater proportion of relevant variance in a way that preserves or improves the ability to interpret scores consistently across groups or over time  without reliance on the factoring assumption, consistent with the central premise of situativity in the learning sciences. To demonstrate the prospective value of the MNLFA approach to defining measurement models, the following question must be answered: Does the inclusion of a qualitatively derived contextual/sensemaking variable as a moderator in an MNLFA model reduce the magnitude of IPD, compared to a traditional factor analytic measurement model that excludes characterizations of contextual or sensemaking variables ? Magnitude of IPD can be assessed in two ways: 1) overall difference in the consistency of values over time for parameters in the measurement model (e.g., path coefficient, y-intercept, residual variance), and 2) the estimated standard error associated with  parameter values over time.  Both  can be represented graphically, where model parameter values can be represented as a line across time points.  The flatter the slope, the less IPD is evident (i.e., an instrument showing no IPD over time would have no change in parameter values, so the slope of that line would be zero).  Similarly, when the values are graphed as a line of best fit, the less variation in values results in a smaller estimated standard error.

The use case illustrated below was drawn from a national U.S. longitudinal study of Ph.D. students in the biological sciences (n=336) across 53 universities. Participants completed multiple annual surveys over 8 years. Semi-structured interviews were conducted annually with a subset of n=94 participants, yielding a total of 548 transcripts (for full data see https://osf.io/hymus/).  Only data from the first 5 years of the study were included in this analysis, because students began to complete their degrees after that time, resulting in both study attrition and a broader divergence in contexts as participants obtained jobs across academia, industry, and non-profits. Measurement instrument For the current analysis, the relevant measure within the annual survey battery entailed 10 items to assess students’ research self-efficacy using the Research Experience Self-Rating Scale (RESRS; Kardash, 2000).  A confirmatory factor analysis was conducted for each year of  data to verify the unidimensional nature of the measure and to 237 obtain parameter estimates characterizing how each item related to the latent factor. Model fit was assessed using root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), comparative fit index (CFI), and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI). Threshold values of acceptable fit using RMSEA/SRMR were met if less than or equal to.08 and values for CFI/TLI were greater than or equal to.90 (Hu & Bentler, 1998). Thematic analysis to identify faculty advisor relationship as an aspect of context The qualitative data set included only participants for whom there were at least two interviews more than one year apart (e.g., data from a participant interviewed in years 2 and 4 was retained; data from a participant interviewed only in years 2 and 3 were excluded). This decision was based on the observation that substantive changes tended to be visible within analysis of transcripts not from one year to the next but across a minimum of two years. The final coding framework included a number of themes, with the specific one utilized in the current analysis labeled Relationship with PI/Major Advisor.  Occurrence of a code within an interview with a given participant at a given time point was recorded as 1, absence of that code was recorded as 0.  Missing data (i.e., no interview for that person at that time point) was noted with no value recorded. Qualitative analysis of interview transcripts proceeded in three phases conducted collaboratively by four researchers. In Phase 1, initial exploratory phase, our analysis was guided by the principles of thematic analysis (Clarke & Braun, 2021). We took a semantic, inductive approach to letting themes emerge from the interview data, with the goal of identifying patterns to derive themes that would show change over time and ultimately to understand the changes in views and experiences of partici pants. At this stage, an initial set of topics and codes emerged. The same two researchers then compared, reviewed, refined, and collapsed/expanded the topics and codes. They discussed the codes to establish boundaries for definitions, specifically inclusion and exclusi on criteria and reached agreement on those boundaries.

In the second phase, we took an inductive approach to identify major themes of change from a small sample of four participants. To let the data guide this analysis, two researchers started by looking for the participants’ self-reflection on change  in their later interviews, namely from year 4 and year 5, which generally were the participants’ final years of their doctoral programs. Next, we read across the participants’ interviews from years 1-3, specifically looking for experiences and thoughts re lated to each of the hindsight reflections on change we had previously identified. For example, in year 5 we summarized an excerpt describing a participant’s experiences with their mentor as “changed a lot, now like peers.” We looked in earlier years for descriptio ns of that mentoring relationship prior to the change and identified in year 2 an excerpt summarized as “feels intimidated by PI” and in year 3 another summarized as “built trust with PI after completing qualifying exams.” From looking across and then within, this process identified longitudinal change from the participants’ own perspectives. The last phase of analysis was dedicated to applying the final codebook at scale to all interviewed participants. Through iterative rounds of coding and discussion on a subset of transcripts wherein all disagreements were resolved through discussion, the rating team reached a standard of at least 80% interrater agreement on all codes.  A second subset of  transcripts was rated independently by all raters to confirm that interrater agreement was at least 80% on all codes.

Professional relationship with primary faculty mentor One code that emerged as salient to understanding doctoral students’ experiences during their interactions with their major advisor (also referred to as principal investigator or PI) was characterizing a positive professional relationship.  Professional relationship was defined as a mentoring relationship that was satisfactory to the student and described as appropriate for meeting their professional needs. Professional mentor relationships were generally supportive, encouraging, and perceived as fitting wi thin the academic culture. Further, there was a perception of holistic care from the mentor that persisted even in the face of disagreements (e.g., “when we disagree it’s still civil”).  Participants described their PI relationships as professional nearly twice as often as all other relationship code categories.

Students felt secure in these relationships, having the assurance that their mentors had their best interests in mind. One student described their mentor relationship this way: Honestly, I have a great relationship with my PI. We’re friendly. We talk about things other than work. I feel comfortable, obviously, talking with him if there’s issues or if problems arise, be they directly related to our research or if they’re problems related to things in the department or the program. Students describing positive professional relationships with their mentors felt their mentors were genuinely invested in them and cared about them as individuals, and not just as students. Interactions were often described as being “supportive,” “encouraging,” and “accessible.” The majority of conversations revolved around 238 professionally-related topics, with one student explaining, “When there’s data hot off the bench, we can talk about that. They, also, were very excited about long-term findings in terms of projects so we talk about that all the time, or how to design the projects and the best way to do them. They’re always available to bounce ideas off of.” Sometimes, students additionally commented that their PIs were also interested in their personal lives, developing social relationships to complement their academic interactions. Students reporting positive professional further described satisfaction with how their professional needs were met through scaffolding of their efforts to establish themselves as competent, skilled professionals. Hence, mentors in this category were invested in guiding their students’ professional paths, even when these paths were less than typical. For example: [My PI] is supportive, and he’s also giving me the space—I was gone for weeks at a time doing interviews on the other coast, and whatnot, and he was supportive of that —understanding of that. He’s given me a lot of feedback, and he’s given me the space to do my own thing, especially with these postdoctoral interviews, for sure.

Students whose experiences were not coded as positive professional relationships “felt that no one really cared” and were “forced [to] work independently,” even when support and guidance were needed. These students indicated that they “would have benefited from better mentorship.” Some even experienced their PIs as being intimidating, aggressive, or passive-aggressive. In these instances, students made efforts to teach themselves and figure things out on their own rather than engage with their PIs. Evaluation of moderators accounting for item parameter drift For calculating IPD across years without moderation, we ran a series of confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) on student survey responses for the RESRS items (see Panel A of Figure 1). I ntercepts, path coefficient values, and item residual variance values were recorded for the CFA in each year. To model the potential progression of IPD in the CFA models and integrated model was estimated by fixing all but one item parameter across years (e.g., variable path coefficients  with fixed item intercepts and residuals) a set of non-moderated parameters were calculated for each survey item. To calculate moderated parameter estimates (see Panel B of Figure 1), we used moderated nonlinear factor analysis (MNLFA). Similar to the CFA, parameter estimates were calculated for each year in the MNLFA approach. Unlike CFA, MNLFA also allowed parameter estimates to vary based on qualitative codes indicating the presenc e or absence of a positive professional relationship between the graduate student and their advisor for that year.  IPD from the two approaches was compared based on slope of the line of best fit for the modeled parameter changes across 5 years and the standard errors around those lines. For space efficiency, we rely primarily on the visualizations of these trends in the following section. We used R versions 4.5.0 and 4.5.1 (R core team, 2025) with lavaan (Rosseel, 2012) and a custom R package for MNLFA (Shehzad & Feldon, 2025). This use case included only one such moderating variable; it is possible that the inclusion of multiple qualitative moderators encompass ing additional aspects of relevant context in future research would lead to even better performance.  To date, multiple moderators have been assessed in individual models, with only significant indicators included in one final model (Kush et al., 2023).  However, potential interactions between moderators or nested moderators have not been examined due to the low likelihood of substantive impacts for traditional variables.  However, given the important qualitative relations between societal systems, culture, and individual sensemaking, future research should investigate the possible role relationships among moderators. One of the historic concerns about quantitative measurement is that it treats data drawn from individuals as interchangeable (Novick, 1966), which inherently divorces individuals’ identities and contextualized meanings from the inferences which can be drawn from their data.  Using qualitative data within the categorical moderator of an MNFLA measurement model reinfuses the relationships between individual items and the latent variable representing the target phenomenon with the constructed meanings held by participants. Moderating factor loadings, intercepts, and residuals provides an avenue for the qualitative factor to account for how individuals make sense of questions and select a response value.  To the extent that accommodating these influences in the model decreases IPD, it enhances the comparability of scores across time through the inclusion and valuing— rather than the erasure and presumption of irrelevance —of personal meanings embedded within context.  Future research in the learning sciences may exp lore additional applications of mixed methods MNLFA both for developing measurement models and for analyzing quantitative structural relationships between phenomena in a manner that incorporates qualitative aspects of individuals, their dynamic interactions, and broader contextual influences.
