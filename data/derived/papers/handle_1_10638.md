# Characterization of Students’ Learning and Reasoning about Mental Folding in a Spatial Educational Game

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the characteristics of elementary school students’ learning and reasoning patterns of mental folding training in a spatial educational game, where students imagine folding a cube net and finding its opposing sides. A total of 402 fourth graders used the educational game for three weeks, with one lesson per week. Behavioral log data and interview data were collected. The results show that students developed the exclusion strategy, identifying easier pairs of opposing sides first and leaving the harder ones for last. They also employed a sequential strategy when the three pairs of sides had similar difficulty levels.

## Introduction

Spatial ability is considered a critical human ability that plays an important ro le in everyday tasks and success in Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) (Kornkasem & Black, 2015).  Mental folding is a complex spatial ability that involves folding two-dimensional patterns or materials into three-dimensional objects and representations (Glass et al., 2013). The process of mental folding involves complex information processing, including identifying, storing, and extracting info rmation, establishing mental representation, and me ntal operations. Compared to other spatial abilities, mental folding has been relatively understudied. Shepard and Feng (1972) initiated research on mental folding tasks. They proposed a mental folding task in which participants saw six connected squares, ne eded to form a visual representation of the shape, and psychologically manipulated the representation to fold it into a cube along the edge. Based on this task, previous studies mainly explored factors influencing the difficulty of ment al folding activities. For instance, Roberts and Bell (2003) revealed that the number of squares involved in the mental folding operation influenced the difficulty of different tasks. Moreover, Sun and Feng (2016) introduced distance as a new in dex of the difficulty of operation, and orientation might be another fact or considering the studies about three body axes in Tv ersky’s model (Franklin & Tversky, 1990). However, there is a lack of research on the mechanisms of men tal folding. Additionally, the participants in previous studies are mostly adults, not elementary students, who are in a crucial period for spatial ability development (Piaget, 1968). They often have difficulties in transitioning from 2D objects to 3D objects or vice versa (Ma'Rifatin, 2019), which predominantly refers to mental folding ability. Researchers have explored digital game-based learning environments as a persuasive pedagogical tool for conducting spatial training, as they are visuospatial in nature (Feng et al., 2010). Moreover, elements, such as challenges, objectives, and feedback, can motivate students to keep exercising their spatial abilities. Furthermore, educational games appear to be a promising environment for studying the reasoning process of spatial abilities. Although the underlying cognitive processes of ment al folding are not directly observable, students’ behaviors can be recorded in game log data. The method of sequential analysis can enable researchers to use a visualized behavior transition diagram to explore students' be havioral and cognitive  patterns (Pan et al., 202 3). Lag Sequential Analysis (LSA) is an analytical method f or sequential analysis that computes the significan ce of a behavioral transition based on a statistical procedure. Significant transitions are identified by comparing the actual transition frequency with the expected frequency without identifying the hidden states in advance. Therefore, LSA is helpful in understanding the learning and cognit ive reasoning process in mental folding  by identifying hidden behavior patterns.

Given the above, the purpose of this study is to explore the characteristics of students’ learning and reasoning patterns of mental folding tasks in a spatial educational game of cube nets. Specifically, this study aims to answer the question: How do elementary students reason about folding a net and finding its opposite sides?

## Research design

This study recruited 462 fourth graders and one teacher with two years of math teaching experience from a public elementary school in Beijing, China. After cleaning  the log data, a total of 402 students (202 males and 20 0 females, Mage = 9.45, SDage = 0.62) with complete data were included in the analysis. Based on an analysis of the National Mathematics Curriculum Standard and studen ts’ textbooks, the students already possessed some basic knowledge of the learning contents in the educational game, such as the properties of a cube, but they had not learned the content presented in the game.

Cube Elimination was adopted as the educational game environment. Students were instructed to imagine folding a two-dimensional net into a three-dimensional cube and unfolding the cube into the ne t, including the task of folding a net and finding its opposite sides. The sides were eliminated from the net when students selected the correct answer. Two kinds of cognitive scaffolds were provided to help students overcome the difficulties in the transformation between the net and the cube (i.e., a three-dimensional cube model and a dynamic animation). Students’ in-game behaviors were collected through log data. In this study, a  convergent mixed-methods research design was adopted to collect the data. The study was conducted over three weeks. The first week included a ten-minute introduction activity, followed by a fifteenminute gameplay session where students played the game Cube Elimination on a tablet individually at their own pace. The second week included  a forty-minute gameplay session. The last week included a twenty-minute gameplay session, after which semi-structured interviews were conducted. Twenty students were included in the semi-structured interview s, and audio recording s w ere collected. Among them, thirteen students (2 girls) participated in individual interviews lasting eight to twelve minutes, and seven students (4 girls) participated in a focus group interview lasting thirty minutes. These  students were selected based on observation s (e.g., students passed the level quickly, and students had difficul ties in reasoning). The questions in the semi-structured interviews included: What did you like to do in Cube Elimination? Do you  know how to pass the levels quickly? Do you have any special skills to pass the levels? Did you find any rules for passing the levels?

## Data analysis

Lag Sequential Analysis (LSA) was adopted to explore students’ learning and cognitive  reasoning patterns. Students’ response data were coded. They  selected and eliminated two sides on the net at a t ime and needed to find all three pairs of opposing sides from a net. As a result, this study coded each pair of opposing sides in a net to explore students’ reasoning sequences. Based on the shape of the net, the eleven types of cube nets were divided into four types (Nur’aeni et al., 2019), as shown in Table 1, and the pair of opposing sides were coded accordingly. A letter B was added after a response’s code if it was the first eliminated pair of the net in order to identify the elimination sequence of each net. A total of 283,356 pieces of data were included in the analysis. GSEQ 5.1 was used to conduct LSA, examining the statistical sign ificance of behavioral patterns from students loggi ng in to finally exiting the educational game. The frequency and the adjusted residual results of transitions were calculated. Moreover, a behavioral transition pattern was ident ified as significant (p < 0.05) if the z-score of the behavioral transition was greater than 1.96 in the adjusted re sidual results. At the same time, the frequency of the transition should be greater than 30 transitions in the freque ncy results. Gephi 0.9.2 was used to visualize  the results of the LSA. The qualitative interview data were analyzed simultaneously with the purpose of validating the LSA results and further understanding students’ reasoning.

## Results

The visualized LSA results of students’ response data in the Flame map are presented in Figure 1. In terms of the “One-Four-One” net, the patterns it0B→it1 and it1B→it0 are more frequent than patterns starting from ensB, indicating that students pervasively first identif y the opposing sides of the “Four” shape part  instead of the two “One” parts. Moreover, the pattern ensB →F is significant, indicating that identifying the two “One” parts first leads to a next wrong response more frequently than identifying the sides of the “Four” part. In terms of the “OneThree-Two” net, the pattern it0B →it2 is the most frequent one of the beginning elimination, followed by the pattern it2B→it0, whereas the it3 pair of sides is always the last one to be eliminated. Regarding the “Three-Three” net, students often begin with the pattern s it7B→it8 and it8B→it7, indicating that students usually first identify the opposing sides on the same “Three” shape part instead of the sides located on the separate “Three” shapes. Figure 1 Net Folding Reasoning Patterns for the Four Types of Net Integrated with students’ interview discourse, the above cognitive patterns suggest that the opposing sides located on the same row and  separated by one side seem easier to imagin e and identif y for students. Moreover, it is easy for students to find the rule for such pairs and internalize it as the reasoning strategy. For example, Student 2 mentioned, “In the levels of the Flame map, I found a rule that if there are three sides located side by side, I can use my  two hands to click the sides separated by the middle si de respectively (and eliminate them).” Students 4 and 6 reported, “Clicking the sides separated by a side will eliminate them. ” Student 8 said, “In the Flame map, which required me to find the opp osing sides of the net, many pairs of opposing side s were separated by a side between them.” Additionally, the patterns also indicate an exclusion strategy in which students tend to identify the easier pairs first and recognize the hardiest pair by eliminating the easier ones. In terms of the “Two-Two-Two” net, the patterns it4B→it6 and it5B→it6 are significant, indicating two frequent reasoning sequences shown in Figure 2. The number marked on each pair of sides in Figure 2 represents students’ reasoning sequence identifying the opposi ng sides, suggest ing a sequential strategy  that the order of students’ reasoning about the pair of opposing sides is based on the order of sides on the net. ## Discussion and conclusion

This study examined the characteristics of students ’ mental folding reasoning patterns in an education al game environment. The results of the LSA and the interview analysis suggest students’ use of an exclusion strategy and a sequential strategy when imagi ning folding a net and identifying its opposing sid es. The exclusion strategy appeared when students reasoned about opposing side s of the “One-Four-One,” “One-Three-Two,” and “ThreeThree” nets, where three or more sides are situated next to each other on the same row. In these situations, students found the pair of opposing sides separated by a side in the middle to be easier to recognize. Previous studies have shown that the number of squares involved in the me ntal folding operation influences the difficu lty of different tasks, which can help explain this finding (Shepard  & Feng, 1972; Roberts & Bell, 2003). For instance,  the it3 pair of sides, which is connected to the largest nu mber of squares compared to the other two pairs, is  usually the last to be eliminated in the “One-Three-Two” net. Accordingly, students tend to eliminate t he easier pairs first and identify the harder ones by excluding the other  two. The sequential strategy appears when students  reason about opposing sides of the “Two-Two-Two” net, which is a centrally symmetrical shape. The difficulty levels of identifying the three pairs of opposing sides are similar for students, as the number of connecting squares of each pair is the same. When the three pairs show similar difficulty, students tend to identify the pairs based on the left and right order of the sides.

## Limitations and future research

One limitation is that this study explores students’ mental folding reasoning patterns based on the net folding and opposite sides task. Future research could examine tasks involving other manipulations and shapes. Methods such as think-aloud could be used for a nuanced understanding of how students reason about mental folding.
