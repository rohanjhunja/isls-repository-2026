# Insights from Expert Maker Teachers' Embodied Learning Designs

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper addresses the need for knowledge on designing maker education to support its successful integration into formal schooling. We present a case study of a learning design iteratively refined by an expert teacher to support the learning of 6th-grade students in a maker course. In this course, students design and manufacture laser-cut escape room riddles. This case study is part of a larger research project examining the learning designs of four expert maker teachers, using the grammar of conjecture mapping. The aim is to advance principled practical knowledge on how to design maker education that effectively supports students' learning in school contexts. We focus on unveiling and articulating the learning trajectories that expert teachers foster and the embodied learning designs they use.

## Background

Maker education is a growing approach in which stud ents engage in hands-on learning by inventing and constructing technology-based projects, utilizing tools ranging from basic hand instruments to sophisticated digital fabrication technologies (Martin, 2015). Since its emergence two decades ago, maker educa tion has been celebrated as a potential revolution in schooling (Blikstein, 2018), with the promise of preparing stu dents for a technology-driven 21 st century society (Weiner et al., 2018). Despite its potential, the integration of maker education into formal K-12 schooling has been limited and remains largely peripheral (Godhe et al., 2019). In maker learning, students engage as designers, in ventors, and fabricators of artifacts, simulating t he work of professionals in real-world contexts—often referred to as authentic learning (Hod & Sagy, 2019). While this approach offers opportunities to narrow the ga p between school and society, it places high demand s on students, requiring substantial support to facilita te their participation and growth in these complex environments (Tabak & Reiser, 20 14). The intricate system of support that enables s tudent learning in such contexts is called learning design  (Koper, 2005). Learning design encompasses elements  such as task structuring, discourse, participation norms, and the ways these elements interact throughout the learning process (Cobb et al., 2003). The bottom-up development of maker education (Blikstein, 2018), alongside a tendency among maker educators to adopt a hands-off approach to pedagogical structuring (Vossoughi et al., 2021), has led to a gap in both practical and theoretical knowledge about  designing maker experiences that provide the necess ary supports to foster student learning (Godhe et al., 2019). Th is gap is particularly pronounced in school context s, where factors such as large class sizes, limited timefram es, and diverse students’ nee ds place additional demands on learning designs (Sormunen et al., 2022).

Experienced teachers and their learning designs off er valuable insights into structuring maker learnin g to support students in school contexts (Rouse & Rouse, 2022). These expert educators, having refined their designs through years of iterative practice, possess unique and innovative knowledge (Beavan, 2017). In this research, we aim to unveil and articulate this knowledge through  both practical and conceptual lenses. Our goal is to produce what Bereiter (2014) calls principled practical kno wledge (PPK)—a bridge between knowing how to implement effective practices and understanding the reasons b ehind their effectiveness. Principled practical kno wledge fosters context-sensitive application, innovation, and well-grounded theoretical insights, crucial for adva ncing maker education and fulfilling its educational promises (Vossoughi et al., 2021). Existing research has begun to explored how maker e ducators support students’ learning by identifying strategies for balancing frustration (San Juan & Murai, 2022), mapping key scaffolding practices (Turakhia et al., 2024), and articulating modes of teache r-student interaction that foster learner agency (Vos soughi et al., 2021). We aim to broaden understanding of how maker learning can be designed to support students’ learning by asking: How do expert maker teachers design their courses to support maker learning in school contexts?

## Methods

We conducted four semester-long case studies (Yin, 2009) of expert maker educators from diverse contexts within Israel’s formal education system. Each case study provided a detailed exploration of an educator's learning design through interviews, observations, and artifacts. Fo r this paper, we focus on one educator, Tamara, pro viding an in-depth account of her refined learning design and its impact. In line with our ethnographic approach, we employed  multiple instruments to explore and articulate the learning design and its impact. Conjecture mapping (Sandoval, 2014) served as our primary analytical framework, providing a structured grammar for documenting and understanding the relationships between design elem ents, intended learning outcomes, and the mediating processes observed in practice. We focus specifically on articulating the embodied design elements that were evident in Tamara's classroom practice. To further deepen our understanding of the design elements and their practical imp act, we employed the constant comparative method (Strauss  & Corbin, 2008) as an iterative process of analyzin g data across various sources. Through this approach, comb ined with our broader understanding of the case stu dy, we identify the key design elements that effectively supported student learning in Tamara’s course. While this paper focuses on a single case and emphasizes embodied design elements, the broader aim is to provide a comprehensive conjecture map detailing  connections between these elements, mediating proc esses, and resulting learning outcomes for all four cases.

## Settings and context

Tamara is a renowned maker education expert and the pedagogical manager of a regional educational makerspace in northern Israel, serving hundreds of students du ring their formal school day. As one of the country ’s first educational makerspace, this center has become a central hub for aspiring maker educato rs and a key destination for any new maker education initiative in the count ry. Under Tamara's leadership, the center collabora tes with neighboring schools, offering semester-long courses often co-designed with local teachers. In this study, we focus on a semester-long elective course led by Tamara for 6th graders,  titled “Laser Cut Escape Room Riddle – A Gift to School,” comprising twelve weekly 1.5-hour sessions. In this course, eight students, all girls, were guided through the design and fabrication of an escape room riddle, intended for teachers to use in a school escape room activity. This cours e was selected as an exemplar of Tamara's pedagogy,  as she recommended it to best illustrate her approach to maker education.

## Data collection and analysis

Data collection included a 1.5-hour semi-structured pre-course interview mapping Tamara’s high level conjectures, intended learning design and desired l earning outcomes. Eight classroom observations were conducted to capture the unfolding of the embodied design, including students' reactions and participa tion in mediating processes. Observations were recorded through field notes, photographs, and digital artifacts. The first author occasionally engaged students in brief conve rsations about their work and held four informal in terviews with Tamara after class to clarify her intentions b ehind specific observed actions or embodied design elements. This approach offered insights into the actual learning design, which often extends beyond what educators initially plan or explicitly express (Resnick & Kazemi, 2019). Finally, a 45-minute semi-structured group interview was conducted with four randomly selected students from  the course to gather their views on the learning g oals, the purposes of design elements, and their impact on learning.

In this paper, we spotlight a specific aspect of the analysis of Tamara’s case study, presenting eight core embodied design elements that encapsulate her approach to supporting student learning. In the discussion section, we delve deeper into three of these elements, highlighting why they are particularly significant and exploring their unique contributions to the learning experience. Future research will weave these elements into Tamara's complete conjecture map, connecting them to mediating processes and learning outcomes.

## Findings

As unveiled through interviews and observations, Tamara’s learning design was structured into three progressive learning units, each building on the previous one t o guide students from highly scaffolded tasks towar d autonomous work. Unit 1, which Tamara called the “P ampering Project,” had students working individuall y to design and laser-cut decorative items to be taken home. The purpose of this unit was to introduce foundational technical skills, such as using design software and  preparing files for laser cutting, while also providing students the opportunity to enculturate the expected forms o f participation. Tamara used highly structured clas sroom routines and instructional guidance to ensure students managed to learn and perform the basic tasks successfully, thus building both confidence and competence.

Unit 2, which Tamara called the “Half-Baked Project” further scaffolded student autonomy and was conducted in pairs. Students were tasked with compl eting a teacher-selected secret alphabet escape room riddle, using partially pre-designed files. They indepe ndently selected their secret alphabet and applied their technical skills to adapt the design. Tamara continued to pro vide scaffolding for new technical skills, while al so relying more heavily on students using offline supportive m aterials and encouraging peer teaching. Procedural aspects, such as work division and progress monitoring —new to this unit —were scaffolded through classroom discussions, procedural instructions, and often thr ough Tamara’s direct management of team dynamics. T his approach allowed students to take on more responsibility while still benefiting from structured guidance, helping them navigate collaboration and project management.

Finally, Unit 3, the “Autonomous Design and Creation of an Escape Room Riddle” challenged students to work in pairs to independently design and manufa cture an escape room riddle from concept to final p roduct. This unit pushed students to apply their skills  in a fully autonomous project, requiring independen t problem-solving, project management, and collaboration as they took full ownership of their work. Categories of learner support embodied in Tamara’s learning design We identified eight embodiments uniquely supporting  student’s learning in the authentic context of Tam ara’s maker course. We provide a concise explanation of e ach, grounding them in relevant literature and spec ific examples from the embodied learning design as it unfolded in the classroom. Extensive structuring of the learning process  refers to the multifaceted scaffolding that support ed students' participation and learning in complex tas ks (Reiser & Tabak, 2014). This included establishi ng clear classroom routines, sequencing learning units with increasing complexity, breaking tasks into manageab le steps, and providing direct skill instruction. The word extensive underscores the wide-ranging presence of these supports throughout her design.

Scaffolding student independence refers to strategies of supported transfer of respo nsibility to students, gradually increasing their ability to perform tasks independently (Reiser, 2018). She encouraged students to tackle challenging tasks with confidence, highlighted complex aspects of the tasks to guide students through challenges, and fostered peer teaching to promote collaborative learning. Maker products as a motivational hook  refers to how Tamara uses the appeal of aesthetical ly pleasing, socially valuable laser-cut products to sustain student motivation through complex tasks (Blikstein, 2013). By having students create take-home artifacts early on, she motivates their learni ng of technical skills. Tamara also personally ensures final products meet high standar ds, enabling students to present them proudly at sc hool. Success as a pedagogical tool for building competen ce is evident in Tamara’s emphasis on success as essential for fostering students' self-efficacy (San Juan & Muari, 2022). This rationale i nformed pedagogical decisions, such as steering students away from idea s that could derail them from completing their p rojects, ensuring they experienced success.

High teacher involvement in leading student project s was a defining aspect of Tamara’s approach and aligns with literature that describes maker activit ies as joint endeavors between teachers and student s in makerspaces (Vossoughi et al., 2021). Tamara worked closely with students, openly offering advice and criticism. She often took on the role of a project manager, as signing tasks, setting time frames, and closely mon itoring students' progress.

Authentic work attitude  reflects Tamara’s approach to treating the classroo m as a professional workspace, emphasizing real-world responsibilities and consequences (Authors, 2 019). She fostered a focus on creating artifacts of personal importance to students, promoting accountability and high performance. Cultivating a supportive atmosphere  refers to the creation of a positive and emotionall y supportive environment (Turakhia et al., 2023). Through rituals like clapping and singing to celebrate a classmate’s success, addressing all students by name from day one, and fostering a straightforward, affectionate, family-like discourse, she reinforced students’ confidence and made the classroom a safe space for risk-taking and learning. Allowing time for creative exploration  involved giving students a moratorium period where they could experiment and explore ideas without the pressure o f immediate outcomes (Papert, 1993). Tamara structu red the course so that only the last 20% of class time was dedicated to the final project. This allowed students to develop conceptual skills gradually through smaller, scaffo lded projects, such as the Half-Baked Project, before tackling the main escape room riddle project. This gradual b uildup provided students with the opportunity to de velop a deeper understanding of the project while building competence and creative confidence.

## Discussion

Three of the embodiments are especially noteworthy.  While they build on established scholarly concepts, they extend these ideas in innovative ways within the school-based context of her learning design. The extensive structuring of the learning process embodiment exemplified in Tamar’s learning design contrasts with the open, hands-off pedagogy often associated with maker education. It provides grounded evidence supporting the argument that maker education can be, and perhaps should be, a guided and stru ctured process, especially in school contexts (Campos et al., 2019). Tamara takes an active role, often acting as a project manager, expertly balancing her guidance with supporting student agency and independence, which aligns with Vossoughi et al.’s (2021) concept of joint activity, where te achers and students work collaboratively in a way t hat scaffolds students participation in complex maker practices i n a way that supports their growth in agency and do es not hinder it. Tamara extends this concept by embedding  joint activity within a formal school context. Her  design exceeds the level of structuring and teacher’s inte rference with student’s work described by Vossoughi  et als (2021). in a way that complies with the unique scho ol context including limited time and high student-to-teacher ratios. Despite her extensive structuring approach, Tamara successfully fosters student independence and agency. This is supported by the implementation of other em bodied design elements articulated in this paper su ch as the supportive classroom atmosphere that encourages risk-taking and Tamara’s emphasis on authentic work attitudes that promote accountability and autonomy. Tamara's approach shows how extensive teacher involvement and clear structuring can foster student independence a nd agency, building on the concept of joint activit y and extending it to the specific context of schooling.

Using Maker Products as a motivational hook  was an interesting aspect of Tamara’s learning desi gn, demonstrating another way she used teacher interven tion to ultimately increase student agency. Blikste in (2013) highlighted the motivational potential of digital f abrication, allowing students to create socially va luable, sophisticated artifacts that showcase their technological knowledge and skills. Tamara leveraged this by using an advanced laser-cutting machine and high-quality materials, enabling students to create impr essive artifacts they took pride in. However, Blikstein also warns of the  risks —students and educators may focus on producing polished products at the expense of student agency and learning complexity. He explains that increasing student's agency and complexity of projects often leads  to products that are less satisfying, creating a ne gative incentive. Some of Tamara's actions, such as refining students ' projects and personally troubleshooting student's  errors to make sure their products meet high standards, indeed limit student agency over their work and reduce its intrinsic complexity. However, Tamara justifies this approach by emphasizing the value of a positive success experience— students taking home impressive products is crucial  for building their competence and creative confide nce. Observations of student participation indicate that these interventions do not diminish student agency; rather, they foster independence. By enabling students to exceed their current capabilities and create artifacts they and others value, Tamara's appr oach supports their growth in competence and agency, achieving a deliberate balance between teacher involvement and authentic, student-driven work. Allowing time for creative exploration was a distinctive element of Tamara's learning design, particularly her deliberate use of a prolonged moratorium period. During this time, students actively participated in learning activities related to creating laser-cut escape room riddles without yet having to desig n and implement their own projects. Lack of time to execute a complex project  is often a significant challenge in authentic lear ning settings (Authors, 2019) and is particularly pronounced in m aker education (San Juan & Murai, 2022). Given t his, it was surprising to see that Tamara dedicated only the last 20% of the course to the challenging project of independently designing and creating the riddle. Tamara explained  that this postponement gave students time to “thin k and dream” about the project, which she saw as essentia l for fostering deeper engagement and avoiding supe rficial outcomes.

Tamara’s design structured the course as a gradual pathway that led to the final project, supporting both student exploration and conceptual development alon g the way. Throughout the course, students were awa re of the final project goal, providing context for their  learning and encouraging reflection on escape room  riddles, which enriched their engagement. A key component wa s the Half-Baked Project, the second unit of the course, where students practiced aspects of designing an es cape room riddle without making overarching decisio ns like the type of riddle or overall theme. Instead, they focused on executing specific aspects of a pre-designed project, allowing them to make smaller but meaningful decisi ons. This sequencing helped students engage in high-level thinking about what makes a riddle engaging, fun, and appropriately challenging. Interestingly, maker projects that simulate authent ic practices often begin with the most cognitively complex elements —planning and decision-making—from the start of the project. This approach works for authentic practitioners who have the expertise to make informed design decisions early on. However, for students who are complete novices, this authentic approach can be challenging and unsuitable (Blikstein & Worsley, 2016). Tamara’s approach flipped this process by allowing students to first build their knowledge and compete nce gradually, leading up to the point where they could  plan and make design decisions. This sequence prov ided a more accessible entry point into complex learning and supported meaningful participation. Together, these elements of Tamara's learning desig n highlight how expert maker educators can adapt and refine practices to make maker education feasible and effective in formal school settings. By examining how Tamara's embodied design choices align with and exp and upon existing principles, this study provides i nsights into how expert knowledge can guide the implementation of structured, supportive, and authentic maker learning environments in schools. These findings can contribute to a principled practical knowledge base for the design of maker education in formal school settings.
