# Switch mode: How Prior Experiences Shapes How Learners Program in a Hybrid Programming Environments

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents a case study on the differing experiences of two students learning to program with a hybrid programming environment designed to support learners transitioning from block-based programming to text-based programming. Despite different levels of prior experience and confidence, both students found their level of comfort with programming by developing their own methods for authoring commands based on the supports available in the environment. This work explores how the same environment supported learners in developing distinct programming approaches suited to their confidence, preference, and previous experiences.

## Introduction

Block-based programming environments  are an effective method to introduce novice learning to the practice of programming (Weintrop, 2019). There is a wide range of paradigms and genres of blo ck-based programming environments, and the capabilities of block-based tools and the types of programs that can be w ritten with them are rapidly expanding (Lin & Weintrop, 2021). A growing number of block-based programming environments support the transition to text-based programming, such as MakeCode Micro:bit, Pencil Code, and VEXcode (Lin & Weintrop, 2021). An emerging approach is to embed mini text editors inside blocks as a way to blend the tw o programming approaches in a single interface or program. One such tool employing this approach is Switch mode (Figure 1).

This paper presents a case study drawn from a larger research project f ocusing on how Switch mode is useful for a range of learners, including those who are eager to move to Python as well as those less confident or experienced. This work advances our understanding of how learner s' self-perception of programming ability shapes the way they use programming environments designed to scaffold the transition from block-based to textbased programming. Furthermore, this work highlights how such programming environments can be designed to support learners at various points along the spectrum of interest, confidence, and prior knowledge.

## Background

Block-based programming uses a programming command as puzzle piece visual metaphor to reduce syntax errors and eliminate the error message (Maloney et al., 2010). Users use a drag-and-drop interaction to author programs, using visual cues in the form of the shape and colo r of the programming command to guide how they can be assembled. If two programming commands cannot be joined to form a valid sequence, the envi ronment prevents them from snapping together, thus preventing syntax errors while authoring programs. Block-based programming has been proven to successfully motivate children and make programming easy (Moors et al., 2018).

As students’ progress towards high school, undergraduate, or eve n computer science careers, they will have to switch their programming ability from Block-based programming to traditional text-based programming language such as Python or Java.  Lin and Weintrop (2021) identified three different design strategies used to support the block-to text-based transition: (1) One-way transition, (2) Dual-modality, and (3) Hybrid. One-way transition environments allow the user to see or edit text-based programming inside the block-based programming, but they cannot go back from text-based programming to block-based programming. Dual-modality environments support both block-and text-based authoring and allow the learner to move back and forth between the two modalities. Finally, hybrid environments blend features of th e block-based programming  and text-based programming into a single interface that contains features of both modalities simultaneously. One key aspect of hybrid programming environments is the support for keyboard-based editing of block-based programming (Kölling et al., 2017).

These modalities are important  and have an influence on students’ knowledge and in terest. When comparing students who use block-based programming and those who use text-based programming, the research found that students who use block-based programming showed greater learning gains and a higher level of interest in future computing courses (Weintrop & Wilensky, 2017). Furthermore, a comparative study involving tangible versus graphical interfaces in programming robots within a museum context revealed a notable preference among users for the tangible interface (Horn et al., 2009). Furthermore, students performed better on typical course exams when they were taught using dual-modality representations in lecture and were provid ed dual-modality tools compared to traditional text-based programming in an early college computer science setting  (Blanchard et al., 2020). All three  studies show that the modalities matter and that they  can impact  user interest and learning. Research has also shown how learners will develop p rogramming practices unique to different modalities  that leverage specific features of the interface, like using a blocks-palette to spur ideas or dragging-and-dropping block commands into text-based canvases for help with syntax (Weintrop & Wilensky, 2018).

## Methods

This case study is part of a larger research projec t focused on transitioning students from block-based to text-based programming. In the study, a group of high sc hool students (N=8) from a mid-western city in the U.S. received a semester long course using a hybrid  block-based programming environment called VEXcode (Figure 2). VEXcode serves as a one-way transition environment that allows users to control a virtual robot in a simulated world. VEXcode also has the ability to author Python commands within a block, a feature called Switch mode (Lin et al., 2023).  The goal of Switch mode is to scaffold students in transitioning from block-based programming to text-based programming.

Figure 2 VEXcode Environment In Switch mode, users have two primary methods of authoring code: (1) directly typing in a newly dragged Switch mode block and (2) utilizing the convert feature available in the right-click menu. The Switch mode block functions similarly to the traditional blocks found in the block palette (Figure 1a), with the added capability for users to type Python commands directly into it. This block is also equipped with an autocomplete system, identical to those found in many integrated development envir onments (IDE) (Figure 3c). As users begin to type inside the Switch mode  block, the autocomplete system suggests the corresponding text-based commands, which can be selected by pressing the Enter or tab key. This system also prompts the user to type the correct order of the parameters in the functions. In addition, users have the option to view the full underlying Python command by clicking on the code viewer icon located on the top right (Figure 3d). Each block in VEXcode is supported by a detailed help document, accessibl e through an icon on the top right or right click on the block and select “block help”, which explains each block's functionality (Figure 3e) and includes a section on Python commands that includes detailed information on the parameters (Figure 3f). The conversion feature is specifically designed to aid novice programmers in connecting the underlying Python code to its block form. Although VEXcode includes a comprehensive code viewer (Figure 3d) that displays the entire program code, this can be overwhelming for beginners trying to correlate Python commands with their corresponding blocks. To address this challenge, every block in Switch mode is convertible. Users can right-click a block and select “Convert Block to Switch Block” to see the corresponding Python command within a Switch mode block. This feature not only reveals the underlying  code, but also allows users to further modify and experiment with the code, simulating a text-based programming environment. Each stack block, such as the Switch mode block in Figure 2b, is also a multiline block, meaning that the user can hit enter or return key on their keyboard to go to the next line. The multilin e Switch mode block itself is expandable and can be used as in Figure 4c.

The students were enrolled in a semester-long high school computer science programming course  with project-based learning. The goal of the course is to teach the foundational concepts of  computer science. The study includes six white males, one multi-racial male, and one white non-binary individual. Students work individually through these assignments and projects, but they can seek help from each other and the in structor. While using Switch mode, students worked through fo ur different activities. “Disk Mover” had students control the virtual robot to pick up and place colorful dis ks at pre-specified locations on the map (Figure 4a). “Crash the Castle” had students program their robot to knock o ver a virtual castle (Figure 4b). “Navigating the Maze” had students navigate a predefined maze (Figure 4c). The final activity is “Coral Reef Clean up” which is a one minute task challenging learners to move their robot around a virtual seafloor to collect as much trash as possible (Figure 4d). After the initial five weeks of instruction using the blocks-only programming environment, the students were invited to participate in the first  semi-structured interview asking regarding their experience of using VEXcode. After the second five weeks of instruction using th e Switch mode  programming environment  (Figure 1), the students were again invited to participate in  the second semi-structured interview about their authorship in block and Switch mode. One of the interview questions was,  “What's the strategy you use to move from block and Switch mode?” Through this question, the goal is to understand how they describe their authorship strategies in a hybrid programming environment.

Before the study, each student completed a survey which included five questions each on confidence, motivation, engagement, and authenticity, and a knowledge-based assessment, including 20 multiple choice questions about programming. These metrics are designed to understand the prior knowledge of the students and their attitude towards programming.

This case study focuses on two participants, Madison and Richard, since they represent two different backgrounds and previous experience. Madison is a 9 th grade non-binary white student who had completed an HTML, JavaScript, and CSS course. Richard is a ninth grade male Asian American / Pacific Islander student who did robotics club in middle school and has extensiv e programming experience including  numerous block-based and text-based languages. Richard also had extensive Arduino  programming experience  with integrating thirdparty sensors using C++.

## Discussion

Compare and contrast From the attitude survey, Madison’s confidence and motivation are lower than Richard. Their knowledge-based assessment is also lower than Richard. But both of them were able to find a way to program in Switch mode despite their different prior experience.

Madison, with their nascent exposure to programming, embarked on a learning journey with a focus on using the most comfortable method for each command. As they advanced, Madison developed confidence through the process of block-based programming, gradually bridging into Switch mode by leveraging the convert feature to map commands, which allowed them to cement their  understanding and gradually transition into typing the Python code inside Switch mode block. For any commands that they are not familiar with or  never used before, they would stay in the comfort zone by using the bl ock form only. The mixed of modality in Madison’s case is the key aspect of a hybrid programming environment, particularly the one Switch mode was built on. On the contrary,  Richard entered high school with significant programming knowledge.  Richard’s strategy relied on his experience with Arduino, using help documents to decode the underlying code of each block. His goal was clear, “go to Python”, an ambition that informed his approach to the programming environment from day one. Thus, he used the help document to understand the underlying Python command, used the convert feature to see, and tinker with the Python code inside the Switch m ode. But once he is familiar with the command, he would just type it out and compare it against the help document to double check on any errors. The comparison of Madison and Richard’s experiences underscores the need for a nuanced appr oach to computer science education, as proposed by (Weintrop et al., 2020). The two students' disparate backgrounds and aims in the subject results in different ways to author programs in the same programming environment. The ability to tailor learning experiences to meet learners at their current level and support them in advancin g is especially critical in computer science education given the wi de variance in prior experience and interest of students. Madison’s gradual confidence build-up using blocks and Richard’s consultative approach  to text-based programming highlight two distinct approaches of how previous experiences shape how the learner program in a hybrid programming environment.

## Implications for designers

The research reported here contributes to a novel approach to designing hybrid programming environments that support the authorship between fully block-based and fully text-based authoring tools. The insights gained from the experiences of Madison and Richard speak to the potential of hybrid programming environments to  address the needs of individual learners. Traditional programming environments, which typically present a single modality choice between block-based and text-based modalities, often fall short in providing a scaffolded transition across the entire spectrum of prior experience. A hybrid environment, such as Switch mode, introduces the flexibility to accommodate multiple programming strategies the learners were able to pick the approach that th ey are most comfortable with. The goal of hybrid environments is to support learners transition from block-based programming to textbased programming. This research, while focused on two individual narratives, seeks to show how hybrid  tools support a diversity of programming approaches and harmonize a classroom's diverse educational needs a nd preferences, allowing each student to progress in a  manner aligned with their own abilities and objectives. The key lies in allowing each student to progress at th eir own pathway, in a way that al igns with their own prior experiences. Such an approach underscores the importance for educational tool designers to consider the wide spectrum of learning experiences when creating programming environments.  By doing so, we can ensure that these environments are not only more inclusive, but also more effective in meeting the varied learn ing goals of students.

## Conclusions

The case studies of Madison and Richard show how students' perceptions and goals interact with how l eaners utilize programming tools. Madison's journey from not comfortable in programming to confidence in typing some of the text-based command and Richard's strategic approach to mastering a new programming language from the help document demonstrate that the ways in which learners engage with a tool are deeply intertwined with their individual aspirations and prior experiences.

As we consider how perceptions and goals shape tool  use, it becomes evident that the design of educational technologies must be sensitive to this dynamic interplay. The evolution of Madison's confi dence through the support of a block-based programming environment, and Richard’s methodical use of documentation to understand and navigate Switch mode, reflect two distinct paths informed by their prior experiences and goals. This study underscores the importance of designing educational tools that recognize and respond to variance in student perceptions and goals. The design of these tools should not only facilitate a smooth transition across different stages of learning, but also provide a platform that learners can adapt to fit their unique pathways, improving their learning experience in the field of computer science.
