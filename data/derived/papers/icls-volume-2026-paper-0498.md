# Choosing “Togetherness” in a Teacher-Researcher Partnership

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Partnerships can support teachers as they work toward rehumanizing classrooms. Togetherness among educators, which Baldinger et al. (2025) propose as involving solidarity, mutually, and human connection, can support humanizing, justice-focused learning for educators. This paper investigates ways in which two teachers and a research partner resisted limiting institutional pressures to choose togetherness in their ongoing work to design humanizing and liberatory learning experiences for young people.

Baldinger, San Francisco State University, evrabaldinger@sfsu.edu Abstract: Partnerships can support teachers as they work toward rehumanizing classrooms. Togetherness among educators, which Baldinger et al. (2025) propose as involving solidarity, mutually, and human connection, can support  humanizing, justice-focused learning  for educators. This paper investigates ways in which two teachers and a research partner resisted limiting institutional pressures to choose togetherness in their ongoing work to design humanizing and liberatory learning experiences for young people. In recent decades, scholars have increasingly called for approaches to teaching and learning that resist the dehumanization of students and teachers that is commonplace in modern schooling (del Carmen Salazar, 2013). Approaches which center “cultural responsiveness” call on educators to make space for students as whole people, with unique sensibilities, cultural assets, preferences, and ways of thinking (Paris & Alim, 2017). Systems of schooling, however, are designed in ways that are fundamentally dehumani zing, particularly for historically marginalized students and those teachers committed to them. Some research has helped us to understand how teachers might find or create opportunities to resist oppressive systems to work toward humanizing their classrooms (Gutiérrez, 2016; Picower, 2011; Reyes et al., 2018). A growing body of research attends to ways in which spaces for teachers’ learning together can be organized to support and sustain teachers in this work (Garcia, 2019; Scott & Philip, 2022; Philip et al., 2022). This paper seeks to explore ways in which partnerships can humanize and sustain teachers and their justice-focused work. Scott and Louie (2024) “ focus on teacher learning that is geared against social stratification and hierarchy, toward cultivating ways of being together —in the classroom and beyond—that are rich with possibilities for people to be accepted, valued, and nurtured in their full humanity  (p. 546).” They coordinate microanalysis of interactions in two teachers’ work groups with analysis of local arrangements to investigate how teachers negotiate conceptions of “us” and “them,” how these negotiations  are mediated by local arrangements, and how they connect with teachers’ opportunities to learn. They demonstrate that teachers’ and par tners’ construction of  solidarity in interactions involved intentional work against constraining aspects of local arrangements, such as scarcity of time, hierarchical positions and roles, and forces that frame teaching as technical and depersonalized. They found that constructions of solidarity, or an inclusive  sense of “us,” invited rich opportunities for negotiating contradictions and tensions inherent in the work of teaching, and thus opened important opportunities for learning. Baldinger  and Louie (2025) investigate ways in which a coach and teacher negotiate the “messy,” human, interactional work of constructing togetherness in service of their mutual learning. They found that when the pair made choices toward togetherness ––solidarity, mutuality, and a sense of shared humanity––new opportunities for learning together became available.  In this paper, I explore the question, how did participants in a teacher-researcher partnership choose togetherness in their ongoing work?

## Context, participants, data collection, and preliminary analytic methods

Jill and Zeze teach at Liberty High School, an urban, alternative school in the United States. During and following the COVID-19 school shut-down, they shared deep dissatisfaction with the increasingly dehumanizing learning experiences being offered to students. In response, they set out to design and implement a new, in-person “Cohort” program, in which students could earn credits for all required areas through collective work on 12-week interdisciplinary projects.  When I learned of Jill and Zeze’s plans, I asked to partner with them  to learn, as a researcher, about their work together designing Cohort and to support their collaboration in any way that surfaced as useful. They  agreed, and our partnership began, with my role understood to be that of a participant-observer (Kawulich, 2005). We planned for me to attend twice monthly 2-hour planning sessions and to  collaboratively design how we used our time. I attended and video-and audio-recorded 12 planning sessions during the first year of Cohort and 8 during the second year, took field notes, and participated to varying degrees. To begin analysis, I examined field notes for segments  of interaction  in which choices related to solidarity, mutuality, and human connection were implied. I then turned to audio or video records of those instances, to determine  whether these choices were, in fact, visible, and eliminated  from consideration those segments in which these choices were less clear. I examined the remaining data, looking for interactional choices that functioned to support solidarity, mutuality, and human connection in the partnership, and identified themes for ongoing coding.

## Emerging findings – choosing togetherness

Preliminary analysis revealed the following ways in which Jill, Zeze, and I chose togetherness in our ongoing interactions. (1) We regularly invited each participant to share feelings. (2) We regularly reflected together about how our ongoing work related to “big picture” ideas that we were collectively committed to. (3) We arranged our participation to  capitalize on  our different roles while resisting hierarchies  often associated with those roles.

(4) We made choices for our collective enjoyment. The following episode exemplifies these patterns.

Our third meeting, which, like each of our meetings, took place over shared food and on couches, began with our developing routine of checking in about how each of us were faring and any developments since our previous meeting. After our initial check-in, Jill and Zeze accepted my offer to share reflections from our previous sessions. The following conversation followed.

Evie: I think maybe… going to the big picture connects us a bit. We get to remember why we love the work, and the love is the joy and the humor in it and all of that… [My notes say] “this is amazing in these ways,” with a bunch of bullet points … There’s stuff about joy and the way you name really sad, hard things, but with lightness… the way you talk about kids, naming intense shit with kids, but without heaviness … That feels like a real spiritual, emotional skill you all have fostered. It seems important… How intertwined your work is with your love and knowledge of and your attention to your students…, and watching you build that knowledge  [of students] together. How intertwined the design and planning is with that feels super inspiring and amazing… Jill:   (to me) You asked me before- (to Zeze) C uz I was saying the honeymoon was definitely over.

Zeze: (laughs) Oh, yeah!

Jill:  Evie had said “what gets you through that” and I was like, “I don’t know” … (to me) It was huge… after you were here last time. So absolutely like t o have someone here who’s not actually in-doesn’t actually know these students and is not in the work say “here’s what I’m seeing” is like…after that last Friday, I was like, “ Oh, okay, I cando this really hard thing again… I was like, “oh my god, yay, Evie’s coming.” Like just hearing back … “ here’s what I’m hearing you doing, ” it’s like “Whew!” Like, It’s worth it. It’s not-like I was saying, getting stuck in the trees…. Zeze: Y es, yep!

Jill:  N ot seeing the forest for the trees. That meeting helped me to get back to the forest... In this episode, our collective capacity to reflect on the “big picture” is named as a way to connect to our shared love and commitment to “the work.” Jill points to this experience of connection and shared commitment as important to help “get [her] through” the challenges of her work. She also names as important for this my role as a partner who is “not actually in” the day-to-day work of teaching in Cohort. The time we collectively chose to give to this reflection allowed me to leverage my role as an observer to share my own experiences of inspiration and learning related to watching Jill and Zeze work,  thereby resisting dominant hierarchies that position educational researchers as more expert than teachers. And, as in the previous episode, we shared food and laughter. Liberty HS is subject to institutional pressures  and dominant narratives that are dehumanizing and demoralizing for teachers and contribute to dehumanizing and narrow instructional design. In the Cohort partnership, we made choices to resist many of these pressures. We resisted the pressure to prioritize quickness and efficiency, instead choosing to allocate time to feelings, shared reflections, and our own enjoyment of our time together. We resisted dominant narratives that position teachers as less knowledgeable than researchers, instead choosing interactional routines that allowed us each to offer our expertise and perspectives from our own unique positions.  We resisted dominant conceptions of teaching and learning as technical and separate from the “softer” aspects of being human, instead intertwining our talk about instructional design with considerations of the feelings and experiences of students and teachers. Similarly, we resisted pressures to seek demonstrable, oversimplified, measurable “outcomes,” instead focusing our attention of the processes of learning and being that we were working to support for students and for ourselves.
