# A Transpositional Grammar Cyber-Social Study of Student–AI Relationships

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As AI-mediated feedback becomes embedded in higher education, understanding how learners construe relationships with AI and human reviewers becomes increasingly more important. This pilot applies Transpositional Grammar (TG) to student prompts, AI-generated images, and reflections in a cyber-social learning environment. Graduate students (n = 7; P300– P306) at a Midwestern U.S. research university contributed coursework artifacts coded collaboratively by a human researcher and GPT‑5, yielding Cohen’s κ = 0.761. TG analysis identifies five recurrent transpositions —expansion, visualization, reinterpretation, materialization, and affective deepening —and surfaces relational accountability as a new dimension of learner expectations. The full study (n = 35; P300–P334) will add a second human coder to triangulate interrater reliability and inform the design of affect-sensitive human–AI feedback ecosystems in graduate instruction.

AI-supported feedback now shapes core practices in higher education, yet we know comparatively little about how learners represent their relationships with AI tools and human reviewers across modalities. In cyber-social learning environments, feedback is simultaneously interpersonal and computational, blending peer interaction, AI assistance, and platform analytics into a coupled socio-technical process (e.g., CGScholar; Cope & Kalantzis, 2023). This hybrid ecology warrants multimodal analysis attentive to how meanings travel between prompts, image, and reflection (Zapata et al., 2025).

Transpositional Grammar (TG; Cope & Kalantzis, 2020; Kalantzis & Cope, 2020) offers a principled lens for tracing such cross-modal shifts. By modeling meaning functions, Reference, Agency, Structure, Context, and Interest, and their transpositions, TG equips us to examine how students’ depictions of AI –human relationships migrate from prompts to generated visuals to reflective commentary. Guided by TG, our analysis attends to five transpositions, expansion, visualization, reinterpretation, materialization, and affective deepening, and advances the construct of relational accountability  to characterize learners’ expectations of reciprocity, transparency, and responsibility in human–AI feedback.

Methodologically, the study mirrors its subject matter: researchers adopt a cyber-social approach by engaging GPT-5 as a co-researcher alongside a human coder, operationalizing distributed cognition within the analytic workflow. The pilot draws on graduate coursework artifacts from Spring 2025 at a Midwestern U.S. research university (n = 7; P300– P306), with plans to e xtend to the full corpus (n = 35; P300 –P334) and incorporate an additional human coder to triangulate interrater reliability. This design ena bles us to link representational patterns to the affordances and frictions of AI-mediated feedback while maintaining methodological transparency. We address the following research questions: RQ1: What do student prompts reveal about learners’ representations of AI –human feedback relationships? RQ2: What do AI-generated images reveal about AI’s interpretation of its relationship with students? RQ3: What do student reflections reveal about learners’ interpretations of AI–human relationships? By situating multimodal learner representations within a TG framework and a cyber-social method, the study aims to clarify how meanings about human –AI feedback is constructed and transformed across modalities, informing the design of more authentic and affect-sensitive feedback ecosystems in graduate education.

## Literature review

Learner perceptions in cyber-social contexts Across cyber-social learning contexts, learners hold nuanced and often contradictory attitudes toward AI and human feedback. Hughes et al. (2024) found that graduate students using CGScholar blended skepticism with openness toward AI-generated reviews, suggesting that acceptance of AI feedback is neither straightforward nor uniform. Zapata et al. (2024a) extended this finding, showing that while human reviews are perceived as more authentic, students nonetheless prefer a future hybrid model — revealing a tension between relational trust and practical utility. Zapata et al. (2024b) demonstrated through activity theory that calibrated GenAI feedback can be 454 perceived as a community member within feedback activity systems, and Tzirides et al. (2024) showed that combining human and AI tools enhances AI literacy and critical assessment capacity. Taken together, these studies show that learners evaluate AI feedback through relational expectations, not accuracy alone. However, prior work has not examined how those expectations are constructed and transformed across modalities, or what learners mean when they hold AI accountable for affective and relational norms. Our study addresses this gap. AI as qualitative co-researcher Emerging work validates AI as a coding partner. Cho (2025) co-coded classroom dialogue with ChatGPT, reporting κ = 0.56. Ni et al. (2024) introduced QualiGPT for inductive/deductive coding of online community posts, achieving κ = 0.57 (inductive) and Fleis s' κ = 0.46 (deductive). Xie et al. (2024) applied chain-of-thought prompting with GPT-4, achieving substantial agreement (κ ≥ 0.60) across eight codes and excellent agreement (κ ≥ 0.79) for three codes. These benchmarks guided our human– AI collaborative coding design and establish that κ = 0.76 represents strong inter-rater reliability for complex multimodal qualitative analysis. Transpositional grammar in multimodal research TG articulates five interrelated functions of meaning across modes (Cope & Kalantzis, 2020; Kalantzis & Cope, 2020). Transpositional Grammar offers three analytical advantages over related frameworks that make it particularly well-suited to this study's aims. First, unlike Kress and van Leeuwen's (2001) social semiotic multimodal analysis, which focuses on the relatively stable grammatical properties of specific modes, TG conceptualizes meaning forms as inherently migratory — constantly transposing across text, image, space, body, sound, and speech rather than remaining fixed within any single mode (Hughes, 2025). Second, where O'Halloran's (2008) systematic functional-multimodal discourse analysis foregrounds the co-occurrence of modes within a single text, TG traces the transformative processes that occur when meanings shift across modalities — precisely what this study requires in moving from prompts (P) to images (I) to reflections (R). Third, while discourse analysis (e.g., Halliday & Hasan, 1980) remains primarily oriented toward language, TG's five meaning functions — Reference, Agency, Structure, Context, and Interest — operate simultaneously across all modalities, enabling a unified analytical language for multimodal, cyber-social data (Cope & Kalantzis, 2020; Kalantzis & Cope, 2020). While prior applications analyze text and image in educational settings, none examine student –AI feedback relationships. TG's focus on how meaning shifts through transpositions (P→I→R) makes it ideal for capturing dynamic relational constructions in cyber-social feedback contexts.

## Context, sample, and ethics

This pilot analyzes a 20% sample (n = 7, P300–P306) from a graduate cyber-social learning course at a large U.S. public research university (Spring 2025); the full study expands to the complete corpus (n = 35, P300– P334). Participants were graduate student s and working professionals in education technology and learning design– related fields. Generative AI was integrated throughout the course: students drafted ≥3,000-word essays, received rubric-aligned RAG-enhanced AI feedback, and completed blind peer review in CGScholar. Afterward, students generated AI images depicting experiences of AI versus human feedback and wrote accompanying explanations; these images and reflections are analyzed here. The study received IRB approval (IRB24-1772). Participants consented via a voluntary pre-course Qualtrics survey, could opt out without penalty, and all data were de-identified prior to analysis; the research team held no instructional or grading responsibilities. Data layers

1. Prompts (Layer 1): Students wrote textual requests for AI to visually depict their perceived AI –human

reviewer relationships.

2. Images (Layer 2): Students were free to use any AI image-generation tool (e.g., ChatGPT, Leonardo,

Gemini). This reflects authentic cyber-social practice in which learners mix tools rather than operate within a single platform. This cross-platform variability is treated as a boundary condition but does not compromise the TG framework, which focuses on meaning functions rathe r than stylistic fidelity.

3. Reflections (Layer 3): Student-written evaluations of the AI-generated images.

Transpositional grammar framework TG analyzes five functions: Reference (entities/properties), Agency (actions/status), Structure (relations/grouping), Context (setting/modality), and Interest (affect/stance). Each data layer was coded 455 for these functions. Transposition tags logged meaning shifts: P→I (prompt to image), I→R (image to reflection), and P→R (prompt to reflection).

Collaborative coding and Inter-Rater Reliability R1 (human researcher) and R2 (GPT-5) independently applied an iteratively refined TG codebook across all pilot layers, followed by adjudication. Refinements clarified evidence rules (e.g., assigning status only when explicit control/deference cues are pres ent), distinguished Context versus Structure indicators, and standardized use of multimodal mix, symbolism/metaphor, and transposition tags. To reduce disagreements driven by paraphrase rather than concept, inter-rater reliability was calculated after semantic normalization: synonymous labels were collapsed, “absence” labels were standardized, prompt/reflective tokens were treated as matches when overlap met a Jaccard threshold of ≥ 0.40, and image entity sets were treated as equivalent when one set was a s ubset of the other. This approach aligns with multimodal qualitative coding where constructs can be expressed in multiple valid phrasings and yielded Cohen’s κ = 0.76. Future work adds a second human coder (R3) for triangulated IRR on the full corpus (P300–P334).

Inter-Rater Reliability Human–AI collaborative coding achieved substantial agreement (Cohen’s κ = 0.76), supporting the robustness of the co-researcher approach for complex multimodal data. Prompts Student prompts consistently frame AI and human reviewers as comparable yet distinct agents. Three themes emerge from the prompt data:  (1) Role Delineation: students explicitly partition AI and human reviewers into distinct but comparable roles;  (2) Legitimacy Negotiation: prompts reveal students working through the credibility of AI as a feedback provider; and (3) Structural Framing: students consistently invoke contrast language ("vs.," "compare," "split-screen") to organize their representations. Prompts such as “Show a split-screen with an AI reviewer on the left and a human reviewer on the right, each highlighting strengths and weaknesses of my writing” invoke TG’s Reference, Agency, and Structure functions by naming entities (AI, human reviewer), actions (review, highlight), and explicit contrasts (“vs,” “compare”). These framing strategies reveal students’ need to delineate roles while negotiating the legitimacy of AI feedback. Images AI-generated images literalize the prompts’ contrasts but often transcend them by depicting collaborative configurations. Two themes emerge from the image data:  (1) Literalization: AI outputs faithfully render the contrasts requested in prompts; and (2) Collaborative Transcendence: images frequently exceed the prompt's contrast logic by depicting AI and human reviewers in configurations of co-presence and equal partnership. One representative AI-generated image from P305 positions a humanoid robot and two human peers equally around a book, each pointing or reading, a triadic scene of shared inquiry. Reflections In reflections, students validate the AI’s faithful mapping of prompt semantics but introduce critical relational critiques. Two themes emerge from the reflections:  (1) Fidelity Validation: students affirm the AI's accurate mapping of their prompt semantics; and (2) Relational Critique: students introduce critical evaluations of emotional expressiveness, identity representation, and reciprocal agency that were absent from the original prompts. One student wrote, “I appreciate the depiction of all reviewers collaborating, but the AI looks too robotic; it should show more warmth.” These reflections engage TG’s Interest and Context functions through evaluative stance and experiential references, highlighting expectations for emotional expressiveness, accurate i dentity representation, and reciprocal agency.

This pilot study offers two contributions, aligned with the three research questions. Prompts reveal learners’ role delineation, legitimacy negotiation, and structural framing, positioning AI as a comparable yet distinct feedback agent. Images literalize p rompt contrasts but often transcend them through collaborative configurations, suggesting partnership-oriented relational defaults. Reflections introduce relational critique, holding AI accountable for affective resonance, identity alignment, and reciprocal agency—what we term relational accountability in this context. These findings underscore relational accountability as a central design concern for computer-supported collaborative learning systems that integrate AI as a feedback partner. Limitations and future work: First, the pilot sample (n = 7, P300–P306) comes from one graduate course at a Midwestern U.S. research university, limiting generalizability across contexts. Second, the small participant group means the five transpositional shifts and the relational accountability construct should be treated as emergent. Third, while the AI co-researcher model yielded κ = 0.76, it currently relies on a single human coder; the full study (n = 35, P300–P334) adds a second human coder (R3) to enable triangulated IRR. Fourth, multiple image-generation tools introduce cross-platform variability in how Agency is rendered; the full study will examine function-specific transpositions in greater depth and test TG-informed feedback tool designs in experi mental settings.

By applying transpositional grammar  to a cyber-social phenomenon using AI co-researcher methods, this pilot uncovers predictable relational transpositions across modalities and introduces relational accountability  as a critical dimension of student –AI feedback interactions.
