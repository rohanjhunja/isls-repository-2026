# Teacher Perceptions of AI-Generated Feedback on Student Scientific Models

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Effective feedback is essential for supporting student learning in complex tasks such as scientific modeling, yet teachers often struggle to provide high-quality feedback. This study explored the utility of MLLM-generated feedback from teachers’ perspectives through a focus group with four middle school science teachers. Overall, participants regarded the feedback as promising. They valued the feedback’s degree of detail, supportive tone, and potential to deepen teachers’ knowledge and enrich classroom discussions. Participants also raised important concerns about the feedback’s alignment with instructional contexts, length, and readability.

Scientific modeling is recognized as a fundamental practice in science education that enables learners to construct and refine explanations of phenomena through iterative model development and revision (Chiu & Lin, 2019; Schwarz et al., 2009). Teachers are key  mediators in modeling tasks, as they guide students with formative feedback that encourages iterative model creation, evaluation, and refinement (Halloun, 2007). Feedback guide s learners along their learning path, helping to reduce the gap between their current understanding and desired learning goals (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). In practice, however, due to limited time and heavy workloads, teachers often struggle to provide consistent, high-quality feedback. Most feedback tends to be superficial and lacks the detailed guidance needed for  fostering meaningful learning; this limitation persists during modeling tasks, where shallow evaluative comments do little to advance students’ conceptual understanding or epistemic growth (Ruiz-Primo & Kroog, 2018).

To address these challenges, researchers have begun exploring the capabilities of generative AI (GenAI), specifically large language models (LLMs) and multimodal LLMs (MLLMs) that can interpret text and visual input, to generate feedback (Lee et al., 2024; Seßler et al., 2025). While these efforts demonstrate the technical feasibility of LLMs and MLLMs,  it is equally important to investigate teachers’ perceptions of AI-generated feedback, as adoption depends on their beliefs and perceived value (Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010). We expand earlier explorations of Gen AI’s educational potential  and leverage MLLMs to generate feedback on students’  scientific models. The study is part of a larger investigation, where we establish that MLLMs can effectively analyze various model types in different learning contexts (Lee at al., in preparation).  In this study, we focus on teachers’ perceptions of the generated feedback and  examine: How do middle school science teachers perceive the MLLM-generated feedback?  Specifically, what values do teachers identify in the generated feedback for scientific modeling? What concerns do teachers raise regarding the feedback? What recommendations do teachers propose for improving the feedback to support classroom implementation? Design principles of AI-generated feedback When considering what makes feedback effective, it is important to address both how teachers structure their feedback and how learners interpret and act on that feedback (Carless & Boud, 2018). W e identified four design principles to guide our prompt development and ensure that MLLM-generated feedback aligns with established best practices from prior research  (Cowie & Bell, 1999; Winstone et al., 2022). Feedback should 1) align with epistemic and instructional objectives, 2) reflect learners’ performance outcomes, 3) offer guidance for subsequent learning steps, and 4) have a supportive tone.

We did not limit the length of the feedback because it was not intended to be delivered directly to students, but as detailed outputs to evaluate LLMs' underlying rationale for assessing students ’ models. However, we designed the prompts to carefully consider grade-level readability.

## Method

The study involved four female in-service middle school science teachers, each with over 11 years of public school teaching experience in the southeastern United States.  All had prior experience using AI tools in their teaching, with two reporting high level of AI self-efficacy and two moderate levels. We interviewed the teachers during a one-hour focus group session to explore their views on feedback created by an MLLM (Claude Sonnet 2707 3). During the interviews, teachers first provided feedback on two selected students’ models, then reviewed MLLM-generated feedback on the same models and answered  to semi-structured questions. We conducted a n hour-long follow-up interview with one teacher (T 3), who expressed strong interest in utilizing AI in her classrooms. The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, and supplemented with questionnaire responses adapted from Chounta et al. (2022).  All teacher participants were deidentified using an I D system (T1 –T4) to ensure confidentiality.

We used thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2012) to analyze the focus group and follow-up interviews. The coding process was conducted inductively at the sentence level through an iterative procedure. Two authors collaboratively coded the entire dataset, first developing an initial set of codes and subcodes on teachers’ perceptions of AI-generated feedback, organizing these codes around feedback usefulness and limitations in instructional practice, and then refining and consolidating them into themes through two rounds of discussion. All four teachers positively evaluated the MLLM-generated feedback, regardless of their prior experiences with AI tools. Their perceived value of the feedback was categorized into three main sub-themes. First, the content-rich nature of the MLLM-generated feedback could enhance teacher learning. Teachers described the feedback as detailed and informative, noting that it offered pedagogical advantages beyond what they typically provided through their own feedback practices. They also recognized that the MLLM feedback served as a professional learning tool that supported their ongoing growth in understanding scientific content and modeling practices. Teachers appreciated opportunities to develop their own content knowledge through reviewing the feedback, especially on unfamiliar or novel scientific topics or different modeling practices. Second, teachers viewed the MLLM-generated feedback as a catalyst for classroom discussion and reflective thinking. They shared that the AI feedback could stimulate meaningful conversations and provide opportunities for students to think more deeply about their models. Third, teachers identified the supportive tone and the structured use of reflection questions as critical elements for engaging students, fostering accountability, promoting revision, and encouraging deeper thinking. All four teachers explicitly valued the inclusion of reflection questions within the feedback. Reflection questions thus served as structured, accountable guidance, aligning with established characteristics of effective feedback (Shute, 2008). Teachers positively received the supportive tone as a key motivational component which reinforces previous findings in the literature that highlight the significance of tone in feedback alongside its content (Carless & Boud, 2018; Lipnevich et al., 2016).

Teachers expressed several concerns related to implementing the MLLM-generated feedback, particularly regarding its length, readability for younger or struggling readers, and grade-appropriateness. Although our prompts explicitly directed the MLLM to produce feedback appropriate for  the targeted language levels, teachers remained concerned about the reading level and complexity of the generated feedback. They noted that many students in their classes require additional reading support. These readability concerns predominantly centered around feedback length.

Insights from the focus group illuminate areas for improving the AI-generated feedback. The first consideration is adapting the feedback to students’ language abilities. In science classrooms, providing instructions tailored to students’ language proficiency and developmental stage ensures clear communication and understanding (Brookes & Etkina, 2007). Another language-related theme identified in the teacher interviews was adding translation options for English learners. Scientific practices involve multiple forms of language use, including speaking, listening, writing, and reading. Thus, offering translation options could help students and teachers overcome language barriers, focus on scientific content and more freely explore their ideas. A second consideration is aligning the feedback content with instructional contexts, such as grade-level standards and instructional goals. Future prompts could include explicit guidelines on vocabulary complexity and curricular standards. Furthermore, teachers play a crucial role in matching the feedback with their instructional goals. All participants preferred shortened versions of the feedback, focusing on “Improvement Guidance” and “Reflection Questions” to elicit student thinking.  These considerations underscore the importance of a hybrid human-AI feedback approach (Bredeweg & Kragten, 2022; Holstein et al., 2020). Teachers and students should exert oversight to provide information about the learning standards,  goals, and contexts and customize the feedback for different pedagogical purposes.

Our findings show that teachers perceived the MLLM-generated feedback as highly valuable and well-aligned with established effective-feedback principles. However, with only four participants, the sample size was small, limiting the generalizability of these findings. Nonetheless, this study marks an important first step in co-designing AI-driven feedback with educators, and we hope to advance this work through future research that investigates its impact on student learning in classroom contexts. 2708 Braun, V., & Clarke, V.
