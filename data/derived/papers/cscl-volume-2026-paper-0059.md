# Regulating Epistemic Emotions in CSCL: Comparing the Higher- and Lower-Performing Groups

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Epistemic emotions (e.g., curiosity, surprise, confusion) are important indicators of learners’ affective engagement in computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL). While prior work studied learners’ static epistemic emotions, this study examined their dynamic, sequential patterns by comparing higher-and lower-performing groups. 44 university students participated in collaborative learning ideation tasks in dyads, where their verbal interactions were collected and coded for epistemic emotion s through content analysis and sequential mining. The findings show that high-performing groups manage to channel confusion and curiosity into constructive exploration and affective closure, whereas low-performing groups often fail to resolve such tension, leading to anxiety-driven loops. It highlights the importance of evolving epistemic emotional patterns rather than static affective states to understand and optimize collaborative learning processes and outcomes.

Keywords: Collaborative Learning; Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning; Epistemic Emotions The effectiveness of collaborative learning depends largely on the quality of group members’ interdependent interactions (Barron, 2003; Chen, Zheng, et al., 2025). I n computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL), epistemic emotions are affective states (e.g., curiosity, surprise, confusion) that arise from the appraisal of the alignment or misalignment between new information and prior knowledge or beliefs during interactions, which serve as indicators of learning experiences and outcomes, particularly when facing challenging learning situations (Muis et al., 2018). A growing body of research examines how epistemic emotions facilitate or constrain the learning processes and outcomes (Graesser, 2020; Järvelä et al., 2020). For instance, positive emotional states such as curiosity and enjoyment enhance learners’ engagement, exploration, and achievement, while negative emotions like boredom and frustration predict learners’ disengagement and lower learning performance. However, some studies also indicated that negative emotions (e.g., confusion) may also lead to positive learning outcomes if they are appropriately resolved (Muis et al., 2015). These mixed findings highlight the complexity of the relationship between epistemic emotions and learning performance. Prior research has primarily examined either the prevalence of epistemic emotional states or the links between individual emotions and le arning outcomes. However, rather than treating epistemic emotions as static affective states, it is important to understand how they unfold over time during collaborative learning. Accordingly, this study investigates the temporal dynamics of learners’ epistemic emotions and how these dynamics shape the persistence, adaptation, or disengagement of higher-and lower-performing groups in CSCL. Specifically, this study addresses the following research questions:

(1) What differences in epistemic emotions are identified between higher-and lower-performing students in CSCL?

(2) What differences in the transitional and sequential patterns of epistemic emotions are exhibited by higher-and

lower-performing groups in CSCL?

## Literature review

Epistemic emotions and verbal interaction in CSCL In CSCL environments, emotions are deeply situated within the socio-cognitive dynamics of collaborative inquiry (Pekrun & Perry, 2014). Unlike general academic emotions  (Obergriesser & Stoeger, 2020), epistemic emotions are defined as responses directed toward knowledge construction processes, arising when learners appraise the (mis)alignment between new information, prior knowledge, and existing beliefs (Muis et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2024). Common manifestations  include curiosity, surprise, enjoyment, confusion, frustration, anxiety, and boredom (Muis et al., 2015), as well as neutral states (Arguel et al., 2019).  These internal affective states are externalized through verbal interaction, allowing them to be systematically captured and analyzed. 394 Epistemic emotions and learning performance Epistemic emotions function as critical predictors of collaborative learning performance (Graesser, 2020). Positive epistemic emotions, such as curiosity and enjoyment, are consistently linked to deeper cognitive engagement and superior achievement. Conversely, negative epistemic states, including boredom, frustration, and persistent anxiety, tend to impede cognitive processing and undermine learning outcomes (Muis et al., 2018). In the context of CSCL, Yang et al. (2025) observed distinct emotional profiles based on performance levels, where high-performing groups exhibited significantly more adaptive epistemic emotions (e.g., curiosity and interest), whereas low-performing groups were characterized by maladaptive states (e.g., anxiety, frustration, and boredom). Notably, adaptive emotions, particularly curiosity and well-managed confusion, were found to drive richer knowledge co-construction (Yang et al., 2025). Its impact on learning is determined largely by its resolution. When successfully resolved, confusion acts as a catalyst, sparking renewed curiosity and fostering productive collaborative inquiry. However, when confusion remains unresolved, it risks devolving into frustration and ultimately boredom (Baker et al., 2025), thereby reducing engagement and inhibiting performance. Sequential patterns of epistemic emotions Recent research highlights the importance of analyzing epistemic emotions as dynamic, sequential processes that unfold throughout collaborative learning (Hou et al., 2025). Sequential pattern mining (SPM) and lag sequential confusion → joy, which are associated with productive inquiry (Hou et al., 2025). Conversely, trajectories where unresolved confusion escalates into frustration or boredom signal a breakdown in emotional regulation. These sequential patterns diverge significantly based on group performance, which highlights the regulatory function of emotion transitions (Yang et al., 2025). High-performing groups are characterized by their capacity to regulate negative states, frequently converting them into productive ones (e.g., curiosity, enjoyment), whereas low-performing groups tend to remain in or transition between unproductive emotions (Yang et al., 2025). Even though the presence of epistemic emotions shaping engagement and learning outcomes is well documented, the underlying patterns governing their temporal evolution remain underexplored (Yang et al., 2024). This study traces the sequences and transitions of learners’ epistemic emotions while comparing group performance to shed light on the patterns of epistemic emotion that facilitate or hinder productive collaborative learning.

## Method

Experimental design This study employed an experimental design involving 44 participants (22 dyads) recruited from a university in Singapore. The experiment was conducted within a CSCL environment facilitated by a shared online workspace. A crossover design was implemented wherein each dyad completed three comparable product ideation tasks in a randomized order during a single session. Each task lasted for seven minutes. To support knowledge construction, participants were instructed to engage in verbal interaction via Zoom while simultaneously working on a shared Google Slides interface (see Figure 1).

Verbal transcripts were generated using the Whisper automatic speech recognition (ASR) system, which produced time-stamped transcripts for each participant. To ensure accuracy, two researchers independentl y reviewed the machine-generated transcripts against the audio recordings, corrected transcription errors, and reconciled 395 discrepancies before analysis. The unit of analysis was the turn of talk, defined as a segment beginning when one participant started speaking and ending when another participant took the floor or when a substantial pause occurred. Verbal data were coded using a scheme adapted from established CSCL frameworks (Han et al., 2021; see Table 1). Each turn was categorized into one of eight discrete epistemic states (see Table 1). To assess collaborative learning outcomes, the design artifacts generated across the three tasks were evaluated using an adapted multidimensional scoring rubric (Chen, Peng, et al., 2025). Performance was quantified on a 5point scale across four distinct dimensions: Completeness, Elaboration, Functionality, and Innovation.  Groups were then divided based on median scores into higher-performing (n=11) and lower-performing (n=11) groups. Descriptive analysis revealed the presence of eight epistemic emotions across both Higher-Performing Groups and Lower-Performing Groups (see Table 2). In terms of frequency, Neutral states (E4) emerged as the predominant state, accounting for approximately half of all coded turns for both groups. Following this, both groups exhibited substantial levels of Confusion (E1) and Enjoyment (E3). Other emotions, such as Curiosity (E6) and Surprise (E7), appeared less frequently but played roles in triggering exploration. Negative emotions like frustration (E2), anxiety (E5), and boredom (E8) occurred only occasionally. Overall, the frequency distributions indicate that both performance groups experienced emotions, with only slight quantitative differences. What are the differences in transitional and sequential patterns of epistemic emotions exhibited by higher-and lower-performing groups in CSCL? To investigate epistemic emotion dynamics during collaborative learning, we applied Sequential Pattern Mining (SPM) using the SPADE algorithm in R (Zhang & Paquette, 2023). Following Yang et al. (2022), a minimum support threshold of 0.60 was set to retain only the most common patterns. The analysis revealed distinct sequential patterns between the groups (Table 3). Higher-performing groups exhibited adaptive trajectories, successfully resolving epistemic tension (curiosity, confusion) into productive or neutral states (e.g., Curiosity →  Enjoyment [0.94]). Longer sequences, such as Curiosity → Enjoyment → Neutral, further demonstrated their ability to achieve positive resolution, and anxiety-related sequences were notably absent. Conversely, lower-performing groups displayed maladaptive tr ajectories where tension frequently escalated into unresolved emotional loops. This was evidenced by patterns like Surprise → Anxiety [0.75] and longer chains such as Curiosity → Anxiety → Surprise. Although lower-performing teams occasionally exhibited positive transitions (e.g., Curiosity → Enjoyment [0.81]), these were less frequent and stable. Ultimately, higher-performing groups effectively regulated epistemic challenges, whereas lower-performing teams frequently became entrapped in anxiety-driven cycles that hindered collaboration. While both groups experienced a similarly broad range of emotions, they differed significantly in emotional regulation over time.

High performers demonstrated resolution-oriented trajectories, transforming epistemic tension (e.g., curiosity, confusion) into positive states like enjoyment while completely avoiding anxiety. This aligns with research characterizing resolved confusion as  a productive struggle (D’Mello et al., 2014). Conversely, low performers displayed maladaptive trajectories where tension frequently escalated into unresolved anxiety. The different epistemic emotions trajectories show that learning performance may be achieved by effectively regulating the negative emotions during the collaborative learning process, underscoring the role of epistemic emotion regulation in knowledge construction and integration.
