# Advancing Ecological Conceptualizations of Learning through Studying World-Making Practices in Interdisciplinary Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning for complex world-making practices cannot be understood without embracing theoretical perspectives that acknowledge the complexity and dynamics of the interconnected and distributed among people and their environments nature of such practices. This symposium aims to advance the conceptual foundations for studying learning as an ecological phenomenon. It builds upon the recent special issue of Journal of the Learning Sciences, “Beyond disciplinary engagement: Researching the ecologies of interdisciplinary learning” (Markauskaite et al., 2024). The papers in this special issue adopted diverse ecological perspectives to study interdisciplinary learning, offering unique insights into how personal resourcefulness, teamwork dynamics, institutional processes, and disciplinary cultures intertwine in this context. However, the underpinning conceptual issues of theorizing and empirically studying learning as an ecological phenomenon were not specifically examined. In this symposium, we extend this work to unpack different ecological conceptualizations of learning and discuss critical directions for further development.

## Introduction

The focus of the symposium and the major issues addressed Today’s complex challenges cannot be solved by draw ing on knowledge from a single discipline; therefor e, preparing learners for transformative action that addr esses those challenges requires engaging them in cr eative knowledge practices and learning that span across a nd extend beyond disciplinary boundaries —inter-, trans -, cross-, multi-disciplinary, and other variants of hybrid knowledg e work. Such learning is necessarily distributed to include interaction among multiple people, ideas, artifacts, tools, and other objects within the heterogeneous social, cultural, and material environments. It is also dynamic to span multiple processes —thinking, emotion, discourse, and action —all constitutively intertwined with one another. Su ch learning is also multilayered  and includes simultaneous learning of individuals, groups, organ izations, and communities. Understanding such heterogeneous, relational, and multilayered phenomena requires turning to the ecological conceptualizations of knowledge practices and learning that enable explor ing how people learn to co-construct knowledge for transformative action in its complexity (Markauskaite et al., 2023). Over the years, a range of ecological perspectives have been developed to study diverse learning phenomena across scales, from individual cognition (Hutchins, 2010) to the performance of educational ecosystems (Hecht & Crowley, 2020). While these perspectives have been broadly used in the empirical studies of learning, they have often been used superficially. As Hecht a nd Crowley (2020) observe, the ecological framework s in educational research and practice often have been a pplied at a descriptive level to “frame the ecosyst em as a complicated set of interconnected elements —but not a true complex problem” (p. 264). They do not explain the behavior and unique emergent qualities of the ecosy stem. Recognizing key elements and connections in t he ecosystem is important, but this does not go far enoug h to reveal the ways in which these elements intera ct. Understanding these interactions is critical for enacting systemic change that repositions teachers, students, leaders, and diverse community members as transformative agents engaged in joint world-making practices of the future. Most importantly, engagement with deeper ontological, ep istemological, and axiological questions about what is actually meant by ecology is central to advancing the ecological research agenda. In our recently co-authored Special Issue of Journal of the Learning S ciences, “Beyond disciplinary engagement: Researching the ecologies of interdisciplinary learning” (Markauskaite et al., 2024), we aimed to present empirical research that explores interdisciplinary learning oriented toward today’s complex issues in its complexity. One of its aims was to “1) advance ecologi cal perspectives that encompass concepts and method ologies for studying complex heterogeneous learning practices and 2) apply these perspectives to the research of interdisciplinary learning—of how people learn across and beyond disciplines.” (p. 213). The papers in this special issue adopted diverse ec ological perspectives to study interdisciplinary le arning, offering unique insights into how personal resourcefulness, teamwork dynamics, institutional processes, and disciplinary cultures intertwine in this context. However, the underlying conceptual questions and issues of theorizing and empirically studying learning as an ecol ogical phenomenon were not examined systematically.  Such examination is critical for advancing an interconnected ecological research agenda. In this symposium, we adopt a dialogic and self-critical stance, aiming to extend our work by juxtaposing and unpacking the distinct features and limitations  of various ecological conceptualizations of learni ng and discussing directions for future developments. Organization of the symposium We bring into the dialogue six projects that use ec ological perspectives to investigate interdisciplin ary learning for transformational world-making practices. All project teams will explore th eir adopted ecological perspective by reflecting on five guiding questions: 1) What ma kes the adopted approach ecological? 2) What are th e ontological, epistemological, and axiological premises of this approach? 3) What does the adopted approach  reveal about learning, and what it does not reveal? 4) How can it be used to inform educational practices? and 5) What are the next steps in advancing this approach? Following the design of the special issue, the symposium will have two discussants, allowing the in-depth exploration of the theoretical (Kali) and methodological (Reimann) aspects.

## Overview of the contributions and their collective significance

Novis-Deutsch and Cohen consider how interdisciplinary learning in the humanities integrates knowledge-building and identity work within an ecological framewor k, highlighting the humanities’ dual capacity to fo ster both personal growth and social cohesion.  This underscores the importance of “Both/And” reaso ning for embracing the complexity inherent in interdisciplinary learning ecologies. Papendieck and Clarke show how two different-but-related sociomaterial frames can be used to develop complementary, multi-level narratives of relational learning processes i n an interdisciplinary course-based research experience. By moving between these frames in analysis, they are better able to produce accounts of learning that respect ecological complexity and also remain tractable for design. Arthars and Markauskaite combin e an epistemic games theoretical framework with an ec ological analytical lens to explore how graduate student teams co-construct shared understanding of wicked interdisciplinary problems. Their work advances understanding of the ecology of collective meaning-making and learning, particularly in contexts where teams lack shared linguistic resources to convey disciplinary ideas. Muukkonen and Kajamaa examine a simulation as devel oping ecology for integrating epistemic and capacity-building goals to prepare students for complex, rea l-world challenges in knowledge work.  Their work contributes to the conceptualization of educational ecologies by addressing the interplay of epistemic and practical orchestration of interdisciplinary learning.

Damşa and colleagues also explore simulation-based learning, focusing on how elements of a simulation scenario, designed as a micro-ecology representing a complex problem environment,  are activated to facilitate student interaction and interdisciplinary problem-solving. Schwarz and colleagues conceptualize and exemplify the implementation of an interdisciplinary program in schools as a  multifaceted ecological enterprise. Their work contributes to th e understanding of how multiple stakeholders are involved in handling design and or ganizational tasks. It also shows the subtlety and fragility of interdisciplinary learning processes.

Collectively, the contributions instantiate an ecological epistemological stance and exemplify its usefulness in studying learning for complex world-making practices. Together, they invite us to engage in critical, selfreflective dialogue about implicit assumptions, trade-offs, and limitations that must be considered in future work aimed at advancing this ecological agenda. They will be the focus of this symposium. An ecological view on interdisciplinary learning in the humanities Nurit Novis-Deutsch, Etan Cohen Over the past decades, the learning sciences as a s cholarly community has shown learning to be a multi faceted process that involves various types of interactions, tensions, and humandevelopments. One way to conv ey this complexity is by construing learning  in ecological terms, as “the sum of contexts where the learner self-directs her activity, cultivating relationships and using, producing and sharing resources” (Sangrá et al., 20 19). In other words, an “ecological view of learning” helps us se e that, within any learning environment, various processes unfold simultaneously, on different planes of activity and within various contexts, even as they shape and constitute one another (Markauskaite et al., 2023). In ou r research on interdisciplinary learning in the hum anities (Humanities-IL), we viewed the concept of learning ecologies as  a generative metaphor, a cognitive device that psy-chologists have long argued can play a crucial role in the advancement of scientific discourse and discovery: Many scientific and mathematical hypotheses start their lives as little stories or metaphors, but they reach their scientific maturity by a process of conversion into verifiability, formal or empirical, and their power at maturity does not rest upon their dramatic origins (Bruner, 1986, p. 12). In this spirit, taking up an ecological view of lea rning opened us to the possibility that Humanities-IL might simultaneously mediate multiple learning processes — an insight that we then grounded in empirical data. Indeed, as we conducted our empirical investigation over the course of four years on Humanities-IL among teacher teams and in classroom settings, we realized that we were observing two distinct forms of learning. The first was crossdisciplinary knowledge building: the process of gaining, organizing, and construct ing knowledge by drawing on different subjects (Boix Mansilla et al., 2000), and the second was transdisciplinary identity work, a process of self-authoring (Rahm et al., 2022) based on the construc tion of learners’ identities, values, and worldview s, fostering their personal development and flourishing. By drawing on the ecological metaphor, we were able  to understand both processes to be part of a single, multifaceted, learning ecology. We found the ecological lens — one that differentiates between knowledge building and identity work while allowing for the possibility that they are in tertwined — to be insightful when interpreting our classroom observations. Of the four teachers we observed, two  highlighted knowledge building, while two put a gr eater emphasis on identity work. Nevertheless, both proce sses were evident in each of the classrooms, and wh en one process was foregrounded, the other was never entirely absent. We also realized that understanding the relationship between cross-disciplinary knowledge building and transdisciplinary identity work is key to designing learning environments that capitalize on the unique affordances of Humanities-IL in schools. We, therefore, drew on the ecologica l metaphor to further our exploration of their potential interrelations. When viewed through an ecological lens, any concept ualization of Humanities-IL in either/or terms, either as cross-disciplinary knowledge building or as transdisciplinary identity work, falls short. A more credible view, in line with ecological learnin g perspectives, would consider both processes to pl ay out in tandem. Based on the existing literature, we suggested two tracks for this:

1. The two processes can be consecutive, with knowledg e building evolving (although not necessarily) into

identity work, and shape-shifting back, with each coming to the fore at time s and receding into the background at others.

2. Any cross-disciplinary knowledge building can also be interpr eted as transdisciplinary identity work, and

vice versa, coalescing to enact dialogical education, by linking knowledge and identity (Freire, 1970/1996). Generating new and relevant insights about the world helps learners engage in self-authorship as they reposition themselves vis-à-vis their social surroundings, while meaningful ide ntity work contributes to efforts to learn about the world, by crossing disciplinary boundaries and integrating them. With this we realized something worth pondering about learning ecologies: They are not only distributed in space and time, among individuals and across different contexts. Rather, they can inform our category-building as well. When learning ecologies inform the design of learning environments, a “pedagogy of abundance” (Kop, Fournier, & Mak, 2011) often surfaces. However, abundance can be a frame-of-mind rather than a reflection of the richness of choices and materials available to learners. Abu ndance, in t his case, is the ability to engage in “both/and” reasoning (Novis-Deutsch, 2018). This is a complex way of viewing social reality, while appreciating the multiple perspectives, values, and goals that make up the social fabric. The humanities have a major role to serve in any so ciety trying to make space for both diversity and a common ground (Schwartz, 2021); space for individuals' needs and those of society. One of the postulates of the relations-based ecological perspective is that "in learning, not only knowledge but also the whole person is transformed. Learning is seen as a process of constant becoming, in which knowledge is continuously (re)constructed based on information available in the environment" (Markauskaite et al., 2023, p. 35). Our study sugge sts that interdisciplinary learning can transform not only the whole learner but their communities as well. Tracing relational learning processes in an interdisciplinary course-based research experience Adam Papendieck, Julia A. Clarke Ecological perspectives on learning are often deployed descriptively rather than transformationally, emphasizing the complicated nature of learning without necessar ily informing better design (Hecht & Crowley, 2020). Ecological frames could be more effecti vely leveraged for change by using them to analyze learning as a “relational process” playing out in complex systems of interact ing human and nonhuman elements, a process that can  be theorized from various complexity perspectives, and intervened upon for better outcomes. In this study, we demonstrate how two different-but-related ecological frames—Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) and Actor-Network Theory (ANT) —can be deployed as dual lenses —or “terministic screens” (Burke, 1966)—to understand and design for learning. We used them to analyze the Curiosity to Question course, a complex course-based research experience intentionally designed to foster learning through disciplinary diversity. Both CHAT and ANT are “ecological” frame s in that they set the focus of analysis (in differ ent ways) on activities and processes that play out among int errelated human and nonhuman actors, and link (in d ifferent ways) local/individual processes of learning with b roader systems and network-level dynamics. As terministic screens, their function is to provide specific technical terminologies and constructive interpretive possibilities. As complementary terministic screens, we show how CHAT and ANT can g enerate different-but-related technical accounts of key “episodes of interaction” (Hecht & Crowley, 2020) that are not only ecologically descriptive, but also actionable for the purposes of design and improvement. Both CHAT and ANT are grounded in metaphysics that reject or fuse problematic dichotomies such as material/ideal, subject/object, and represented/rea l. CHAT, however, is a fundamentally human-centered metatheoretical perspective, rooted in a Vygotskiandialectical and relational ontology, where humans and the material world are interactively produced and transformed in  activity. In contrast, ANT is more of a methodolog ical sensibility that does not privilege the ontological status of humans and builds upon a flattened actant-rhizome ontology (Latour, 1999). It provides a technical vocabulary not for theorizing human learning per se, but for describing how human and nonhuman actants become enrolled and mobilized in broader actor-networks of stabilized relational processes. In short, CHAT provides a human-centered developmental lens on activity in the course, emphasizing cyclical processes of learning and transformation undertaken in and among object-oriented activity systems (Engeström, 2000). ANT, meanwhile, offers an instrumental perspective on the political-rhetorical-material processes of translation by which scientists become situated and stabilized as actors in broader research networks, producing scientific competence in assemblage.

Recognizing that both CHAT and ANT attend to the role of “objects” in orienting and mediating activity, we adopted an “object tracing” approach to analyzin g activity in the Curiosity to Question course, foc using on relational processes as they played out around a common boundary object over the course of the semester. This boundary object—the scientific paper that all students were required to produce over the course of the semester— was broadly valued, meaningful, and shared across t he diverse scientific and social worlds in which co urse participants operated and to which they were accountab le. Data sources included participant observation f ieldnotes, student surveys, interviews, and student-produced artifacts. We iteratively coded our data to construct longitudinal narratives focused on how learners approached and grappled with contradictions and noncoherence in the course. By moving back and forth between CHAT and ANT to trace the “dynamic relational processes” (Hecht & Crowley, 2020) at play around the bo undary object, we constructed semester-length narratives of both developmental processes of human-centered learning and instrumental processes of networked scientific productivity. Our analysis showed how individuals interacted with and through the boundary object (scientific paper) over the semester, grappling with contradiction and noncoherence both inside and outside the course in ways that were productive for both (1) individual learning and (2) the assembly of broader scientific  research networks. Drawing on the different technical languages and co nceptual frames brought by CHAT and ANT helped us p rovide more than a description of learning as complic ated, and articulate actions that designers of inte rdisciplinary learning experiences could take to promote learning through productive engagement (Engel & Conant, 2002) that is at times disciplined and at other times syncretic. While tensions between CHAT and ANT have been well-articulated (Spinuzzi, 2008), we suggest that contr adictions between them might be well-worth tolerating in service of theoretically articulate and tractable acco unts of learning in complex ecologies. Together, th ese dual perspectives can help us design pr oductive, equitable, and sustainable learning and r esearch processes that respect the rich, wild, dynamic complexity of the ecological contexts in which those processes unfold. Examining how students construct shared understandi ng of complex interdisciplinary problems: An ecological epistemic games approach Natasha Arthars, Lina Markauskaite Contemporary university education plays a crucial r ole in preparing students for world-making practices. This includes preparing students to address global wicke d problems that present as environmental, economic,  and social crises. Wicked problems are difficult to define as they are inherently complex, evolving, and interconnected. They can be framed from different perspectives and cannot be addressed by a single discipline (Rittel & Weber, 1973). Preparing students to tackle wicked problems requir es equipping them with capabilities to work across disciplines and construct shared understanding of such problems. Shared understanding has been conceptualized in different ways: some emphasize overlapping mental models or cognitive alignment, some focus on exchanging and integrating multiple perspectives through interaction, while others focus on distributed, multimodal activities that involve the broader environment (Arthars et al., 2024). While it is acknowledged that joint meaning-making draws heavily on discourse and team members’  personal resourcefulness, including their disciplinary knowledge and personal experience, the process of constructing shared understanding in interdisciplinary contexts is under-conceptualized and under-researched, posing challenges for supporting development of students’ capabilities. In our study, we combined the epistemic games theoretical framework with an ecological analytical perspective to examine how interdisciplinary student teams construct shared understanding of complex, wicked problems. Our main research question was: What ki nd of epistemic game do students play, and how, whe n they construct a shared understanding of wicked problems? Epistemic games refer to forms of inquiry involving  goals, entry, and exit conditions, and moves that guide knowledge construction (Collins & Ferguson, 1993). In collaborative knowledge work, they support knowledgeable action by serving as generative patte rns of inquiry (Markauskaite & Goodyear, 2017). The  epistemic games framework offers a lens for examining the pro cess of constructing shared understanding to identify recurring, game-like patterns of interaction. Complementing the epistemic games framework, an ecological perspective views meaning-making as inseparable from embodied activity within  the broader, distributed environment. By employing an ecological analytical perspective, we aim to articulate how construction of shared understanding in interdisciplinary teams occurs through distributed, multimodal interaction that involves humans and nonhumans. This perspective focuses on how gestures, language,  and other modalities interact dynamically to shape  joint meaning-making (Goodwin, 2018; Hutchins, 2010; Markauskaite et al., 2023). We conducted ethnographic case studies of four teams (3-5 students in each) that worked on interdisciplinary projects in two graduate courses (5-13 weeks in duration). Data included over 90 hours of video observations, field notes, interviews, and digital artifac ts. Video-recorded episodes where teams constructed shared understanding of their complex problem were analyzed,  and multimodal interactions (e.g., gestures, langu age, and artifacts) were coded across episodes to identify entry and exit conditions, epistemic goals, moves, and constraints. This allowed us to identify and iteratively refine game-like patterns that recurred across episodes and teams. Our results showed that students’ joint meaning-making processes were dominated by several epistemic moves that exhibited game-like qualities. These moves focused on explicating and integrating different perspectives, such as knowledge sharing, knowledge validation, and negotiation, and on grounding abstract ideas in concrete experiences and contexts, such as perspective-taking and simulation. These moves were multifacete d, involving dynamic interaction between language, share d representations, and students’ bodies. Throughout knowledge sharing, simulation, and negotiation move s, shared understanding co-evolved along with shared representations. Non-verbal cues, including gaze and gestures, were also critical modes of participation. These results show that, in interdisciplinary conte xts, students often lack sufficient shared linguist ic resources to effectively communicate disciplinary ideas. Therefore, they employ other modalities, such as gestures and shared representations, to express ideas. Witho ut adopting this ecological analytical approach, th ese multimodal forms of communication would remain unexamine d, obscuring our understanding of how these s tudents construct shared meaning across disciplinary bounda ries. Simultaneously, we w ere able to articulate the game-like patterns of interactions that supported students’ constructi on of shared understanding. Th ey provide insight into how educators can scaffold and support interdisciplinary teamwork and construction of shared understanding. Navigating the complexities: Dynamics and tensions in interdisciplinary learning simulations Hanni Muukkonen, Anu Kajamaa In this paper, we explore how a simulation-based environment can support interdisciplinary learning by examining how higher education students engage in socio-material co-creation and how they reflect on tensions emerging in interdisciplinary learning. Understanding these sim ulation process dynamics (Hopwood et al., 2016) is essential for developing an ecological understanding of integrating epistemic and capacity building goals, which are linked to complex, real-world challenges in knowledge work. Our study site was a course in educational psychology, which was pedagogically designed as an organization simulation of three small human resources and consultation companies, i.e., the interdisciplinary student teams. The teams create d a business concept and an offer of human resource services to answer a call for tenders by a n imaginary customer. Tartas and Muller Mirza (2007) argued that “moments of tension and negotiation are key points in the elaboration of collaborative work” (p. 156); therefore, we investigated how tensions and responsibility taking were addressed in the simulation. This addresses the multiple layers of personal, collaborative, (inter) disciplinary, and educational-industry contexts contributing to navigating the simulation as an educational ecology.

The course design engaged students in a co-creation process, which called upon interdisciplinary capacities (Boix Mansilla, 2017) and contributions. The simulation presented a time-constrained proxy of self-organizing teamwork, which required resolving tensions arising through disciplinary differences in knowledge, emerging functional or dysfunctional knowledge practices, and various forms of responsibility taking. The course reading and individual weekly tasks were designed to suppor t conceptual and research-based understanding of organizational processes. The concurrent simulation aimed t o offer an experience of coordinating  and leading teamwork as well as facing and managing the related challeng es (Muukkonen & Kajamaa, 2024). The simulation stru cture offered a time trajectory for investigation, as student diaries were built based on weekly advancement. Across the simulation, teams were expected to contribute to various documents and presentations with collective efforts. Two teachers and the customer were holding the lectures and providing feedback. In each of the four iterations, the course particip ants were assigned to teams of 11-14 students so that there was representation of each disciplinary field. Diaries (N=84) were collected from students (48% of all participants) in the following fields: educational psychology (n=36), engineering (n=16), economics (n=12), humanities (n=10) and education (n=10). All data was collected based on informed consent from the participants. We used an analytical approach that builds on socio cultural theories to address the evolving team contributions, tensions, and student roles and responsibilities to uncover the strengths and shortcomings of the simulation as an educational ecology. The lea rning diaries were analyzed to investigate how stud ents reflected on the tensions related to 1) interdisciplinary engagement, 2) the roles and responsibilities in teams, and 3) the simulation as a context representing both educational and industry-typical knowledge work.

Our findings revealed how the simulation with diffe rent roles (e.g., leadership expectations for a com-pany CEO) and expectations for answering the customer’s call created an environment tonegotiate meanings and draw upon various expertise present. The observed t ensions focused on a lack of responsibility taking relative to role expectations and the need for (re)negotiation of team objectives, use of time, and resources with in the time constraints. A simulation can only offer a limited view into e ngagement with a customer and real-life interdisciplinary teamwork and its tensions. However, students described combined weekly thematic course literature reading and the experience of the simulation as advancing their epistemic and practical understanding of interdisciplinary collaboration. The tasks, co-creation, and knowledge practices of the simulation  as lived experiences were examined in parallel through the research literature, creating a pair of interacting  elements of the ecology (epistemic and practical). The reflections showed how the simulation process was interpreted uniquely by each student, narrating their personal expectations, previous exp eriences, roles, and engagement. Complexity was see n as a necessary quality of this educational ecology to invite both the lived experience in the simulation and the deepening conceptual understanding of the phenomena at hand. Group-based simulations in interprofessional medical educ ation: A micro-ecological approach for learning analysis and design Crina Damşa, Rachelle Esterhazy, Hilde Wøien, Liv Mathiesen The ability to integrate insights and ways of thinking from different disciplines is increasingly essential in higher education (Kidron & Kali, 2015). Achieving this req uires students to develop collaboration and interdi sciplinary learning skills, enabling  them to address topics beyond the scope of a single  discipline (Boix-Mansilla, 2017). This is particularly critical in healthcare educati on, where solving complex problems demands collabor ation across disciplines and professions (O’Keefe et al.,  2017). Our project explores how simulation-based learning design can support interdisciplinary learning in me dical and health education. We examine how elements  of a simulation scenario, designed as a mic ro-ecology representing a complex problem environment,  are activated to facilitate student interaction and interdisciplinary problem-solving. Understanding the dynamics of interaction and problem-solving across domains is essential for creating learning situations that prepare students to address complex, real-world challenges in knowledge work (Markauskaite et al., 2024). However, these types of micro-ecologies are challenging to analyze, as they depend on domainspecific knowledge, associated expectations, norms,  and a relational layer requiring advanced interact ions. Codesign of curricula specifically may also support t eachers’ agency in refo rm efforts by creating an arena where teachers can influence what is taught and how it is taught (Severance et al., 2016). Goldman and colleagues (2022) further argue that co-design is a context for both researchers’ and teachers’ professional learning and observe that “to support this learning, we first need to understand it–for all who participate” (p. 1). In this study, we investigate how design choices in simulating a complex problem influence learning within interdisciplinary student  teams, specifically whether these designs foster interdisciplinary collaboration or reinforce mono-disciplinary routines. Our study focuses on a project course in interprofe ssional medical education, pedagogically structured as a simulation of interdisciplinary professional teamwork. The participating programs include medicine, nursing, pharmacy, psychology, dentistry, and nu trition. The simulation scenario features a simulat ed patient case with complex health issues. Students work together to discuss the case, assess the patient, and develop a suitable treatment plan. To work effectively on a patient case, students are required to develop a common understanding of the health issues at hand and how they may be addressed through an integrated interdisciplinary approach. The data gathered comprises observations and video recordings of students while planning and engaging in the simulation, co-design meetings with the teacher team, data-reflection sessions, and the student groups’ solutions (treatment plan s) and reflections. The analyses employ an ecologica l metaphor as a framework for interpretation. This allows us to disentangle the institutional conditions and disciplinary norms and use them as the basis for making design choices for this interdisciplinary course. While t he teachers have a certain degree of agentic space, negotiations aiming to be inclusive of each discipline and mindful of formal and inform al constraints are key to the process. In addition, both individual and relationa l actions can be mapped in the design process, resulting in concrete design features.

At another layer of the ecology, of the student groups enacting this design, our initial findings reveal that interactions in the interdisciplinary setup occur primarily through verbal exchanges where students share, negotiate, and integrate ideas to formulate diagnoses, coordinate actions, or co-create treatment plans. During prolonged group discussions, students exhibit varying verbal behaviors, including group discussions, silent work, and occasional off-task moments. However, certain “key moments” emerge where interdisciplinary learning is significantly advanced. These moments are characterized by  students articulating their perspectives, making c ritical connections, and reaching integrative insights. These key moments are episodes of sustained knowledge exchange that provide opportunities for meaningful collabora tive interdisciplinary learning. Schwarz, Anat Yarden, Boris Koichu, Michal Tabach, Einat Heyd-Metzuyanim Disciplines taught in schools are compartmentalized  from the perspective of epistemology, practices, m ethods, language, and organization. In our study (Schwarz et al., 202 4a), we capitalized on Goldman et al.’s (2016) Disciplinary Literacy Framework for designing learning  goals and for providing discipline-specific tasks to show how an instructional reform inspired by this framework and a dialogic approach could lessen this compartmentalization. This reform stresses the importance of argumentation and dialogue in all disciplines and opens a new way towards the implementation of interdisciplinary pro grams in schools. It draws upon Bateson’s (1978) vi ew of multiple perspectives towards ecosystem management as an educational necessity and Dewey’s (1916) view concerning the integration of disciplines towards education for democracy. We implemented our designed interdisciplinary program across Mathematics, Science, and Philosophy. However, our research revealed that many obstacles have to be overcome to enable the emergence of interdisciplinary processes. In this paper, our aim is to show how the above ecological perspectives enable these obstacles to be surfaced out and handled. These obstacles in the implementation of the program consisted of diverse ecological aspects. From an organizational vantage point, we negotiated the implementation of the interdisciplinary program with four schools in the same municipality. Diverse stakeholders were in volved in the negotiation: municipality officers, parents, principals, and superintendents. They pointed out the need for a program that would raise strong interest and engagement among students. We stressed  that interdisciplinarity opens the door to solving problems of the world students care about, raising stakeholders’ motivation to engage in joint ecosystem management. The decisions taken with the stakeholders constitut ed a contract that removed some of the institutiona l constraints: The stakeholders decided that the prog ram would be run as an out-of-school series of five day-long activities (called ‘focus days’) around Interdisciplinary Societal Dilemmas (ISD) in buildings of the municipality. This decision resolved organizational constraints, such as scheduling coordinated lessons in several subjects. In a professional development program, teachers learned to animate dialogic activities in their disciplines and learned to orchestrate interdisciplinary activi ties. Their motivation increased in the course of t he program (Schwarz et al., 2024a). Thus, the willingness of teachers and other stakeholders to enable the implementation of interdisciplinarity helped in lifting organizational and institutional constraints. From a pedagogical vantage point, the design of the program was based on another ecolo gical aspect. Researchers in mathematics, science, and philosophy education initiated a dialogue to de fine the issues to be addressed and the activities to be designed to prepare students for interdisciplinary sessions. This dialogue, being itself interdisciplinary, resulted in  design principles for triggering dialogic interdisciplinar y argumentation that covered content, pedagogy, and organization. The se design principles include the ISD Formulation Principle, which focuses on foster ing students’ engagement in interdisciplinary dilemmas, and the Disciplinary Argumentation Principle, which conveys the necessity of disciplinary dialogic argumentation for the resolution of the ISDs. From an instructional design vantage point, dialogue was also a central instruct ional design principle. Following the dialogue among researchers in educati on in different disciplines, dialogue was necessary  between designers in order to provide resources, instructio ns, and a macro-script for teachers to implement the interdisciplinary session. Dialogue also took place between d esigners and teachers to train them to implement di alogic practices in their disciplines, tonegotiate the ma cro-script of the interdisciplinary sessions, and to distribute responsibilities among teachers. As a result, all activities, disciplinary and interdisciplinary, were designed to facilitate the enactment of dialogic argumentative and inquiry-based practices. From an analytical vantage point, the methodology we adopted to explore students’ learning also had an ecological flavor as we chose a narrative approach to analyze how interdisciplinarity emerged within c lassroom talk. A major finding was that although students maintained dialogic argumentation around ISD, interdisciplinary learning was rare, and guida nce was necessary for integrating knowledge from di fferent disciplines. We found that when the teachers were attentive to students’ unarticulated references to disciplinary ideas, they were able to subtly guide the emergence of interdisciplinary dia logic argumentation. Often, the teachers missed tho se opportunities and declaimed the integration of knowledge in a non-dialogic talk (Schwarz et al., 2024b). In the symposium, we will elaborate on our claims a nd suggest that the methodologies we developed could provide teachers with metaphors for subtly guiding interdisciplinary processes.
