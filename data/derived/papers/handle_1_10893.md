# Collaborative Writing and Knowledge Building

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines the teaching effect of knowledge building for writing among elementary school students in Taiwan. The experimental group involved in Knowledge Building (KB) to enhance writing, and the control group engaged in direct instruction for writing. The findings based on mixed-methods analyses indicate: (1) the KB class outperformed the control class on essay writing as a learning outcome, and (2) there are progressively more engaged collaborative and discussion activities to improve writing ideas during the KB process.

## Introduction

Writing is essential for expressing thoughts and developing learning and thinking skills, as recognized in various studies (Camacho et al., 2021). Recent research, ho wever, indicates a decline in youth writing skills,  partly due to internet slang and shortcuts (Ghory & Ghafory, 2021). This study compares two approaches to improve writing in children: knowledge building (KB) and traditiona l direct instruction (DI).  DI, established in the 1960s (Engelmann, 2014), involves explicit ly teaching skills through structured, sequential less ons. KB, however, is a collaborative method focusing on idea generation and enhancement within a community (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2010). It is aligned with writing as a creative, problem-solving activity (Bereiter et al., 2023; Chuy et al., 2012).

The study investigates how KB, specifically through discussions on the Knowledge Forum platform, affects essaywriting abilities in young students. To this end, this study asks the following questions: Is there any improvement in essay writing in the knowledge-building (as compared with the direct instruction) class (Q1)? How did the participants progressively improve the quality of note-writing ideas during their online discussion (Q2)?

## Method

In this quasi-experimental study, two fourth-grade elementary classes were compared: the experimental class (n=25) received knowledge-building pedagogy, and the control class (n=28) received direct instruction. Following the national curriculum, both classes learned reading and writing from the same Chinese language arts textbook over 11 lessons. The control class received traditional direct instruction, while the experimental class engaged in Knowledge Forum (KF), an online KB environment. The KB approach involved four pedagogical principles: (1) Real Ideas for Authentic Problems: Students were engaged in online discussion via posting initial note-ideas in KF to reveal problems of their understanding from reading the textbook lessons. (2) Idea Diversity: Through online discussions, students exchanged ideas and feedback, enriching their learning of textbook lessons. (3) Idea Elaboration: Students clarified and elaborated their thoughts by re-reading and revising notes, deepening their understanding. (4) Idea Integration and Improvement: Students synthesized diverse ideas into coherent ones, enhancing their learning. KF also provided writing scaffolds like sentence openers to facilitate discussion and idea development. These online activities were integral to sustained idea improvement and personal knowledge gains. The main data sources include: First, all participants from the experimental and comparison classes took pre-and-post writing assessments before and after the study. Two Chinese language arts teachers graded students’ essay writing by following a 6-point writing scoring guideline designed and validated by the National MiddleSchool Basic Competence Test Committee. Using Spearman correlation, the inter-rater reliability was ρ =.69. A T-test was administered to compare the differences between the two class conditions. Second, regarding the quality of ideas within collaborative note-writing during the online discussion, using each complete idea within a note written as an analysis unit, we adopted a coding scheme of constructive writing activity designed by Chan et al. (1992) to analyze the written text in each note into five different levels in the adopted coding scheme. Using Spearman correlation, the inter-coder reliability was computed as ρ =.78.

## Results and discussion

Writing assessment as knowledge building outcomes Regarding RQ 1, a t-test showed there was a significant difference in t erms of improvement scores in writing assessment (as KB outcomes) between the experimental and control classes (t=2.41**, p<.01). While students from both classes improved in writing scores, the e xperimental class (M=3.78, SD=0.15, for pre-test; M=4.17, SD=0.21, for the post-test) significantly outperform the control class (M=3.71, SD=0.18, for pre-test; M=3.86, SD=0.17, for the post-test). The result implies that the pedagogical interventio n of "knowledge building for writing" is more effective than the conventional "d irect instruction for writing" approach often used in teaching writing in Taiwan.

Quality of ideas in terms of collaborative note-writing and discussion online Regarding RQ2, in terms of percentage of notes written in each level per week, the findings revealed a decreasing percentage in level 1 (from 55.06% to 35.94%). At this level, the analysis showed that the major ity of students could only relate some key concepts/keywords from the articles of the textbook to their personal living experiences, yet they still need to make an idea improvement in their actual writing. Most of their notes written were also contextually unrelated to one another. On the other hand, the fi ndings showed an increased percentage in level-2 by almost 20% (from 32.91% to 49.22%). Although level-2 was not considered as a high level of constructive activity, it is deemed as essential as a skill for writing fluency. This is especially obvious when students were able to contextually relate to each other’s writing by using build-on notes. Below is an example of three students’ builton notes discussing about one famous person introduced in on e textbook lesson: “I think Ma-Kai is a historical figure, not only because he [as a doctor] see patients for free but more importantly he established the first Western hospital in Taiwan…we need to thank him” (S17); “I agree with you; I also think Ma-Kai is a great person” (S04); “I also agree with you two. Ma-Kai is indeed a kind person” (S12). There were no level-5 activities, but some increased trends in levels 3-4 (together from 12.03% to 14.84% in total). Although very few, some students were also able to move beyond mere knowledge-telling to questioning or reflecting on some ideas from the textbook content, for example: “Can fruit be awakened? No, I don’t think so. This is just author's figurative writing style” (S 04). Furthermore, some students were also able to demons trate lateral or associative thinking required for creative problem-solving. For example, in discussing the article “Painter in the Tea Mountain”, students were able to relate prior knowledge learned from previous articles: “The language of Lu-Bing flower stands for lighting others by consuming itself because its color is yellowish like fire” (S23). “I also like it. Its great spirit is just like Ma Kai” (S17). Overall, the findings imply that students could gradually move away from pre-factual confabulation to constructive activities of retelling, assimilating, and problem-solving, even though there is still room for improvement in extrapolating activity.

Concluding remark In conclusion, our research findings prove that applying KB pedagogy in writing instruction can effectively complement the downside of direct instruction. It not only greatly advances students' essay writing performance but also fosters their collaborative skills in problem-solving through Knowledge Forum technology, as well as their more engaging, self-directed learning ability in the online platform. Previous research has shown that teachers in Taiwan tend to consider direct instruction as a main pedagogical approach to enhancing students' writing (Hsiang et al., 2020). Our findings suggest that knowledge-building pedagogy as an alternative means to teaching writing can be helpful.
