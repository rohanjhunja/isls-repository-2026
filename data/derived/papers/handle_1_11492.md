# Human-Centered Perspectives on Computational Thinking and Problem Solving in the Age of AI: How to Investigate the Changing Educational Landscapes?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As emerging digital technologies and AI reshape education, Computational Thinking (CT) skills such as abstraction, problem decomposition, and algorithm design are crucial for learners to navigate this landscape. This symposium brings together a set of diverse studies that highlight the relevance of CT and problem-solving skills in the age of AI. The studies demonstrate how CT can be comprehensively addressed in learning sciences research at different educational levels from primary school to teacher education, in different learning designs spanning from individual programming tasks to collaborative activities, and through multimodal multichannel data. The discussion will focus on the relevance of CT as a core concept supporting learning with and about AI while preserving critical pedagogical values such as agency, creativity, and critical thinking with human-centered approaches.

## Overview of the symposium

In today’s  educational landscapes driven by artificial intelli gence (AI), our theoretical and empirical understanding of such transversal skills and competences as computational thinking (CT) and problem solving that are foundational for learners undergo significant transformation. CT is increasingly recognized as a specific form of problem solving that involves processes such as abstraction, decomposition, and algorithmic thinking. As digital technologies and AI tools reshape how we le arn, teach, and design learning environments, there  is a growing need to explore how these skills evolve in these contexts. Our symposium offers a timely and comprehensive exploration of CT and problem-solving skills in the age of AI, with a dual focus: (1) showcasing diverse theoretical and methodological approaches t o investigating these skills in various educational  settings, and (2) interrogating the role of these skills in A I-supported learning and teaching across different ed ucational levels.

CT refers to a competence that allows us to solve complex problems  with a creative problem-solving approach relying on computational concepts and cognitive processes such as abstraction, problem decomposition, algorithm design, and debugging (Romero et al., 201 7). Rather than simply coding  line by line, i t involves developing an approach to solve complex problems  by engaging in a reflective and creative analysis o f the situation, its representation at the abstract and c oncrete levels, and the operationalization of a sol ution that is produced with the help of metacognitive strategies  associated with CT, which can be applicable to different contexts and situations, not only in computer programming (Romero et al., 2024). While early research on CT predominantly emphasized  individual learning outcomes —such as individual task behavior in programming environment s (Guenaga et al., 2021) —and was often grounded in cognitivist perspectives (Agbo et al., 2021), recent studies have expanded this scope. Emerging work increasingly explores CT as a socially situated and collaborativ e process (e.g., Lai & Wong, 2022; Lee et al., 2019), drawing on constructivist and/or experiential learning theo ries (Agbo et al., 2021). Consequently, there is an  increasing interest in studying various aspects of human behav ior involved in learning CT, such as participation,  social interaction, and interest (Kafai et al., 2020) as well as collaboration and regulation of learning  processes (Emara et al., 2021). Despite this shift, there remains a need for more integrative studies that examine CT development in situ, capturing the interplay of individual, social, and technological dimensions. Addressing this, our symposium brings together studies that investigate CT across a range of educational contexts —from elementary classrooms to teacher education—and at different levels of analysis, i.e., from individual performance to group collaboration. By employing diverse methods, including multimodal multichannel data (e.g., log files, video, screen a nd audio recording), these studies contribute novel insights  into the dynamic, contextual nature of CT. Collect ively, they advance a more holistic understanding of CT as it unfolds in authentic, technology-rich learning environments— an essential step toward theorizing CT in the age of AI. Building on this broader view of CT as a dynamic, socially embedded, and context-sensitive competence, recent research has increasingly examined the evolv ing relationship between CT and AI. Studies have  not only suggested that CT and problem-solving skills positively impact AI literacy (Celik, 2023), but also that the relationship is reciprocal—AI can in turn support the development and application of CT skills (Guggemos, 2024). However, as AI technologies become more deeply embe dded in educational practices, they are reshaping c ore problem-solving CT processes such as  debugging (Sun et al., 2024 a). This transformation calls for deeper theoretical and empirical inquiry into how AI-driven learning environments differ from traditional approaches to CT instruction—not merely as tools but rather as co-agents in learning. Against this backdrop, our symp osium’s second aim is to explore this evolving theme and its pedagogical implications. In particul ar, we emphasize the central role of teachers not only in integrating AI meaningfully into their classrooms (Celik, 2023; Luckin et al., 2016), but also in fostering learners’ critical engagement with computational systems. The presented studies shed light on how educators can leverage CT as a framework to both teach about AI and teach with AI— highlighting human-centered pedagogical approaches that empower learne rs to navigate, critique, and co-create within increasingly algorithmic educational landscapes (Romero et al., 2024). To operationalize our two central aims, (1) understanding CT development across contexts  and (2) exploring its evolving conceptualization in AI-supported environments, we present five diverse yet conceptually connected studies. Together, they offer a rich set of methodological approaches and theoretical insigh ts. The symposium opens with a scoping review (Poster 1; Mergianian et al., 2025) that synthesized how log data can be utilized to assess CT skills at the individual level. This w ork not only identifie s trends and gaps in existing research, but also builds the foundation for understanding process-based assessments of CT. From there, we shift toward collaborative learning contexts that utilize multimodal data. Poster 2 (Pasupuleti et al., 2025) explores how CT concepts can be introduced through embodied play  in primary education, drawing on theories of enacti ve cognition to show how physical interaction and co-creation support the emergence of computational understanding in young learners. The study compares pre-and post-test for concept retention and epistemic emotions in two conditions, utilizing generative AI to support data collection with young learners. Poster 3 (Iwata et al., 2025)  brings together log and video data to examine how C T skills emerge within problem-solving interaction in an AI-powered robotics task in secondary education. This is done by applying a CT problem-solving model (Barendsen & Bruggink, 2019) to robotics and analyzing the interaction through a hybrid framework that integrates creative computational problem solving (Chevalier et al., 2020) with established CT dimensions (Selby & Woollard, 2013). Poster 4  (Şener et al., 2025)  further extends this focus on collaboration, analyz ing undergraduate students’ interactions while debuggin g in a task-specific learning environment through a mixed-methods design, combining screen, audio, observational, and interview data. The results indicate the significance of scaffolding in debugging to avoid tinkering behavior and encourage deeper negotiation. Finally, Poster 5 (Frøsig et al., 2025) shifts attention to the educat or’s perspective, addressing how generative AI tech nologies influence teacher agency and pedagogical design. While this final study does not explicitly focus on C T, it complements our symposium with the essential context for understanding how CT and AI integration depends on teachers’ ability to orchestrate human-centered, reflective learning environments, therefore addressing the second aim of our symposium.

Overall, these studies methodically build  a comprehensive understanding of how CT  and problem-solving can be approached in technology-enhanced learning environments by focusing on vario us age groups, task designs, and utilizing different data channels to triangulate the findings. They underscore the significance of combining technological innovations, embodied exper iences, and teacher agency to foster deeper understanding and meaningful engagement with computational concep ts in the age of AI. Our symposium also offers concrete insights for curriculum design, teacher training, and AI literacy development which will be addressed in the final discussion. Ultimately, we aim to reimagine what it means to think computationally—and critically—to cultivate CT and problem-solving skills to enable learners and teachers to n avigate, understand, and contribute to our increasingly algorithmic world.

Poster 1. A  preliminary scoping review of log data use in computational thinking assessment Cristiana Mergianian, Ninja Hienonen, Muhterem Dindar, Mari-Pauliina Vainikainen Computational thinking (CT) has typically been measured using traditional asses sments focused on the final product (Tang et al., 2020). However, acknowledging  only the outcome provides a limited perspective of knowledge and skills; therefore, it is important to  also consider the process based on information gat hered from, for example, the log data of carefully designed tasks (Grover et al., 2017). In this preliminary scoping review, the use of log data is examined in the assessment of CT at the individual level. The review was conducted according to the latest version of the PRISMA guidelines (Page et al., 2021). The main aims were to identify 1) the form of CT examined in the studies (e.g., block-based programming), 2) the education level at which  the studies were implemented (e.g., primary school), and 3) the specific indicators extracted f rom the log data (e.g., number of trials).

To meet the above objectives, the following search term was used in Scopus and Web of Science: ("computational thinking" OR programming) AND ("log  data" OR "log record*" OR "interaction log" OR "interaction data" OR "trace log" OR "trace data").  The criteria for inclusion were peer-reviewed journal articles published in English between 2006 and 2024. Further more, the included studies focused on evaluating th e performance of individual learners and clearly described the CT task(s), education level, and measures of log data. Studies were excluded if the collected log data wer e unrelated to task behavior of specific CT tasks (e.g., interactions with different types of course content in the Learning Management System of a programming course). The initial search produced 795 results with 127 repetitions between the two databases. This number was narrowed down to a total of 13 studies with at least 14 642 participants following screenings of the titles, abstracts, keywords, and full texts.  According to the preliminary results of the scoping review, the predominant CT form was text-based programming (n = 7), followed by block-based/visual programming (n = 5) and other forms (n = 1). Additionally, most of the studies were conducte d at the higher education level (n = 8). The remaining five studies all included participants in primary and middle school (i.e., grades 1-8 or ages 6-13), while three of the studies also examined participants in high school.  Interestingly, more than half of the studies (n = 9) explicitly included the term ‘computational thinking’ in the text, of which only two were conducted in higher education. In terms of log data indicators, most studies extracted information related to frequency, duration or sequence (Wang et al., 2022). Common examples of frequency indicators were the number of trials, code lines/blocks, code changes, errors, and variables. The duration was of ten represented by total time on task and sometimes  by time spent on specific task activities (e.g., writing code, fixing errors). Finally, sequence-related indicators appeared to be the least used and they were usually in the form of specified combinations of commands/behaviors. These initial results demonstrate the trends in the  research published in academic journals thus far and areas that might benefit from further investigation  in this type of publication  channel. For instance, additional studies at the high school level could be helpful as well as ones utilizing sequence-related indicators in contexts other than text-based programming. In the future, the literature se arch could be expanded to include conference papers for a more comprehensive perspective on existing research. This research contributes to the emerging discussion on how log data can be used in CT assessment and advances it by consolidating existing ideas in the literature. There is great potential for scientific contribut ion considering there are relatively few studies on bot h CT and log data. Moreover, a greater understanding of how to leverage log data for educational assessment wou ld support the design of individualized feedback and scaffolding in digital learning environments.

Poster 2. Hopscotch binary: Learning computational thinking through embodied outdoor play Surya Pasupuleti, Kristian Kiili, Marjaana Kangas This study investigates how embodied playful learni ng can support elementary school students in unders tanding binary digits and foundational computational thinki ng (CT) concepts. Drawing from embodied cognition — particularly the 4E framework that sees cognition a s embodied, embedded, enactive, and extended (Galla gher,

2023) the approach situates learning within physica l and interactive contexts. Playful learning furthe r enriches

this by enabling learners to co-create games that let them construct, refine, and r eflect on their conceptual understandings (Kangas, 2010).

To explore these ideas in practice, teachers of Gra des 4 and 5 are invited to a professional developme nt workshop preparing them to implement one of two ped agogical designs: (1) an embodied, playground-based lesson where students co-invent games to explore binary concepts (experimental), or (2) a classroom-based “CS Unplugged” binary numbers lesson — a widely used low-tech approach to teaching computational thinking (control) (Battal et al., 2021). The study employs pre-, post-and delayed post-tests to measure both immediate and retained understanding. Epistemic emotions — emotions that arise during the processing and gener ation of knowledge, such as curiosity, surprise, confusion, and boredom (Vogl et al., 2019)  — are captured at multiple points using the Epistemically-Related Emotion Scales (Pekrun et al., 2017). To be tter support young learners' self-reflection, the scales were augmented with AI-generated visualizations of emotional expressions. A pilot study (N = 17) was conducted to assess the feasibility of the embodied playful learning condition and the accompanying measurement tools. Most students had minimal prior knowledge of binary numbers, except for two outliers. Following a 60-minute playful session-comprising group-based game co-creation for the first half and playing their peers’ games in the second-students demonstrated a statistically significant i mprovement in their understanding, as indicated by post-test scores (Wilcoxon signed-rank test: W = 103, p =.02). Epistemic emotions were measured on a Likert scale (1 = not at all, to 5 = very strongly). Results indicated that while learners’ levels of su rprise remained unchanged, there was a statisticall y significant decrease in confusion and boredom, coup led with a marked increase in curiosity from the pr e-test to the activity phase. These emotional shifts persiste d into the post-test phase. Additionally, curiosity during the post-test was positively correlated with performance (r =.60, p =.01), while confusion and boredom were negatively correlated (r = –.50, p =.04; r = –.71, p <.01, respectively). Notably, two students reported elevated boredom during the activity; both had reduced curio sity, and one was the only participant whose post-test performance declined. Both were in the same group a s two high-performing peers, suggesting that group composition may have constrained their engagement: undersco ring the teacher’s critical role in facilitating productive play and participation.

Based on the initial results, it is anticipated tha t students in the embodied playful condition will demonstrate a stronger desire for knowledge explora tion, as indicated by increased curiosity and reduc ed boredom. Notably, some students in the pilot drew r epresentations of their physical games during the p ost-test (e.g., hopping to add, flipping cards), suggesting that embodied engagement may further support both p roblem solving and retention in the experimental condition. These findings inform the design of collaborative, low-resource unplugged activities and offer practical i nsights for integrating CT concepts through embodie d playful learning in primary education.

## processes in AI-powered robotics context

Megumi Iwata, Kateryna Zabolotna, Jari Laru, Kati Mäkitalo Robotics is an effective approach to develop CT (Ke ith et al., 2019; Lin & Mubarok, 2024), still it is one of the underrepresented contexts in the current CT researc h (Lai & Wong, 2022). AI-supported programming environments (e.g. AI code-generation, human-machine communication in natural language) are gett ing more common in both professional and educational context s, yet their influences on the human behaviors are not fully explored (Liu & Li, 2024). Barendsen and Bruggink (2019) describe CT problem solving as a shift between abstract problem solving and real-world application. CT problem solving in volves decontextualization, computational problem solving, and recontextualization, in which CT skills (abstraction, decomposition, algorithmic thinking, evaluation and generalization) are practiced (Kallia et al., 2022). Chevalier and colleagues

(2020) outline five phases of creative computational problem solving (CCPS) in educational robotics starting with

generating ideas in human language, then translatin g them into robot code. Anthropomorphic programming environments (e.g. block-based, drag-and-drop features, AI code generation) reduce learning barriers (Ezeamuzie & Ezeamuzie, 2024) but may also lead to trial-and-error behaviors without sufficient analysis and planning (Sun et al., 2024b).

This theory-driven study aims to examine the CT skills that eme rge in computational problem solving through group interaction. The study is guided by t he following research question: how do CT skills emerge in problem-solving processes in an AI-powered robotics programming workshop?  Using the video data of group interaction during computational problem solving, w e offer empirical examples of how CT skills emerged  in the different phases in robotics programming. We discuss CT problem-solving processes in the robotics contexts, and student interaction in the AI-powered programming environment. Participants were 17 ninth-grade students (15–16 years old) who attended two-day robotics programming workshop. They were divided into small groups of two to three, each receiving a robotic arm kit, a tablet device, and printed task instructions with code examples. Students worked on the tasks at their own pace, with instructors’ support if needed. Video data of group work and log files were analyzed using qualitative coding. We identified CT problem-solving episodes by reviewing video segments before and after each logged code execution. Using the CCPS model by (Chevalier et al., 2020), we  focused on three phases: formulating the robot beha vior, programming the behavior, and evaluating the behavi or. We then coded CT skills (abstraction, decomposition, algorithm design, evaluation, generalization, data, and debugging) in each CT problem-solving episode based on the CT definition by Selby and Woollard (2013), as well as related studies (Barth-Cohen et al., 2018; Palop et al., 2025).

The results showed that students spent less time formulating robot behavior. There were 71 examples of “formulating the robot behavior”, compared to  201 examples in “programming the robot behavior” and 182 examples in “evaluating the solution”. This indicates that students started programming without discussing robot behavior. Algorithm design was most frequently observed  CT skills (n=139) followed by evaluation (n=68) and abstraction (n=28). In the process of decontextuali zation, students used algorithm design, abstraction  and data, such as when they discussed which direction the robotic arm would move if they put negative value in the code. By using the gesture to point out the expected dire ction, students’ understanding of the negative valu e became concrete and visible. During computational problem solving, students discussed the structure of the code and the errors in the syntax, in which algorithm design, de bugging and data emerged. In recontextualization, e valuation was observed as students compared the robot’s actua l movement and their expectations. Human and robot languages were often used interchangeably in student interaction, such as when students used the command “bow 20 degrees” to describe the desired robot movement.

This study applied the CT problem-solving model by Barendsen and Bruggink (2019) to robotics. The processes of decontextualizing and recontextualizin g were observed in the shift between concrete (robot) and abstract (code) levels. Multiple CT skills appeared together in these processes: abstraction in decontextualization, algorithm design in computational problem solving, and evaluation in recontextualiz ation. AI-assisted programming allowed students to use natural language to communicate with the robot, reducing the need for translating between human and robot languages. This led to fewer instances of “formulating the robot behavior”, as students used command s to describe robot movement s. Students rarely engaged in deep evaluati on and optimization of solutions,  often moving on to a new task even if previous ones were incomplete or failed. As indicated in previous research (Sun et al., 2024 b), the simple interface and AI’s human-like communication encouraged students to try many commands without mu ch analysis. In AI-powered programming environments, the human acts as a navigator while AI generates the code (Liu & Li, 2024), making CT skills even more essential for evaluating solutions and understanding real-world-computational transitions. Poster 4. How beginner programmers fix bugs in groups Burcu Şener, Duygu Umutlu Collaborative problem solving is a fundamental 21st  century skill (OECD, 2017). Wing (2006) defines CT as “solving problems, designing systems, and understanding human behavior, by drawing on the concepts fundamental to computer science” (p.33). CT is a ty pe of problem-solving technique (Jonassen & Hung, 2006). Debugging is one of the practices used in CT (Brenn an & Resnick, 2012), and it is the process of detec ting, locating, and fixing flaws in a program (Araki et a l., 1991). Thus, debugging can be regarded as an ideal process to practice problem solving. On the other hand, although previous studies on CT or programming are ample, how collaborative debugging (CD) occurs has been rarely  investigated so far (Chung & Hsiao, 2020; Estler e t al., 2013). Also, the number of studies which employed a  process-oriented approach while examining CD is low. A few studies (e.g., Wu et al., 2019) focused on the CT processes in a collaborative learning environment; however, they did not analyze CD as a process.

This study aims to provide a detailed understanding  of novice programmers’ CD processes and design an effective CD learning environment. To this end, the research questions are:  (1) How do novice programmers engage in CD?; (2) What are the associations between collaborative problem solving, CT, and debugging phases of novice  programmers during CD?; (3) Is there a change in novice programmers’ use of CT practices while collaboratively debugging across weeks?

A qualitative-dominant convergent mixed methods design is employe d in this design-based research study (Creswell, 2017; Fishman, 2013). The study wa s conducted in the laboratory sessions of an underg raduate educational technology course, where pre-service teachers learn how to code on Scratch. Debugging ta sks were designed on Scratch by the researcher to investigat e CD behaviours. Purposeful sampling was used. Vari ous scaffolds such as exploration, expert modeling and role assignment were utilized. Data wer e collected through classroom observations, audio-screen recordings, and focus group interviews. Recordings were analyzed through focused coding by using literature-driven codes from these three frameworks: ECSAP for  collaboration (von Davier et al., 2017), Troubleshooting Framework for  the debugging phases (Jonassen & Hung, 2006), and Computational Thinking practices (Brennan & Resnick  2012; Selby & Woolard, 2013). Any emerging CD behaviours not included in these frameworks were ex tracted through open coding. After ensuring interra ter reliability analysis with a peer expert (κ =.885, κ>.810; p<.001), the obtained recording data was quantified through quantitative content analysis (Chi, 1997) a nd then, descriptive analysis and chi-square tests were conducted.

The data for the study were collected in Fall 2023 and analyzed in Fall 2024 as the first phase of a larger dissertational work. Audio-screen recordings (of 6 weeks) and interviews with one group of participants (N=3) constituted the dataset for the analys is. The analyses showed that there was a significan t but weak association between the collaborative problem-solving behaviours and troubleshooting phases (df=8, χ= 75.92, p<.000, V=.137). “Expressing agreement” and “expressing disag reement” was significantly more frequently used in the negotiation (NEG) and solution generation and verif ication phases (SGV), implying that the participant s mostly relied on basic expressions in these phases rather than using evidence for their arguments or identifying conflicts with their teammates, which were two of the descrip tors included in the ECSAP framework. Moreover, a significant but weak association was detected between CT practices and troubleshooting phases (df=12, χ= 82.30, p<.001, V=.143). A significantly frequent use of t inkering was detected in the SGV phase. Finally, it  was found that there was a weak but significant change in the use of CT practices across weeks (df=30, χ= 228.05 p<.001, V=.150). Despite its significant use in SGV phase,  its use decreased across weeks and reached zero in  the final week. Also, algorithmic thinking was significantly used in the final week, which might also have been due to the complexity of the task of the final week.

Findings showed that novice programmers might need various scaffolds (i.e. metacognitive tools) before they are engaged in debugging (Murphy et al., 2008). This could help both with their overreliance on t inkering and on less complex negotiation interactions, which could stem from a lack of expertise. Despite this, as they gain more experience in debugging over time, even novice  programmers can start using other CT practices tha n tinkering (Wu et al., 2019). In the light of the fi ndings and evidence from th e literature, design revisions were made in the learning environment (e.g., improvement of the tasks and weekly load, addition and removal of certain scaffolds). Frøsig, Margarida Romero It used to be a distinct human ability to communicate through conversation. However the latest generation of AI, the so-called Generative AI (GenAI), have shown the ability to do a probabilistic treatment and adapt to both the content given in a chat, as well as an evolving context during the human-machine interaction (Mishra et al., 2023). This conversational nature of GenAI is referred to as an opportunity, especially in the domain of lang uage learning, have documented this ability to be able t o increase student results in the field of English as a foreign language (Tai, 2024; Wang et al., 2023). But despite the potential of GenAI in different educational activities, it’s integration into the classroom can also disrupt educational practices due to its ability to foster an over-reliance on the generated content by students, making them miss out on opportunities to develop critical thinking, independent problem-solving, self-regulation, self-reflection, and metacognitive skills (Holmes, 2024). On the teacher side of the learning process there is a growing body of evi dence supporting the neg ative effects of GenAI on Teacher Agency (Ghamrawi et al., 2024; Holmes, 2024; Lepage & Coll in, 2024), considered as teacher’s beliefs leading them to take control of their design and orchestration of the teaching/learning activities. This study aims to preserve a human-centered approach to AI in education (Romero et al., 2024) and to recognize the need for teachers to exert their expe rtise and enact their agency within AI technologies. For this objective, the study engages teachers in a game-based learning activity that aims to support teachers and AI to cocreate learning experiences. This notion is explore d through the lens of Cultural Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) (Engeström, 2015). Such a framework can integrate both the activit y of subjects (teachers), objects (learning activities) and instruments (generative A I). Thereby mapping their mutual interactions (rule s and division of labor) and defining the various roles (in the community) of this interaction. In this case, the role o f teachers consists of defining a learning objective and setting up the context in which it takes place.  Thereby creating the basis for a hybrid intelligence (Akata et al., 2020; Dellermann et al., 2019), where the collaboration between the teacher’s input  and the AI’s output, works together to create a mor e effective and efficient learning experience.

By giving teachers the possibility to (1) act, (2) affect matters, and (3) make decisions or choices, as well as the possibility to (4) take a stance about how gen-AI should execute the learning activity it is expec ted that their agency is increasing (Vähäsantanen, 2015), which in accordance with Bandura's triadic reciprocal causation part of the social cognitive theory (1989) should also lift their self-efficacy and thus have a positive effect on their environment. Which in this case is the classroom and the students.
