# Epistemological Beliefs: A Missing Link in Funds of Knowledge/Identity Scholarship

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores the intersection between Schommer’s Epistemological Belief Model and the Funds of Knowledge/Identity (FoK/I) framework to better understand how teachers’ beliefs about knowledge influence FoK/I approach. Drawing on qualitative data from teacher interviews, the study highlights how naïve epistemological beliefs, such as viewing knowledge as fixed, certain, or exclusively derived from authority, may limit the recognition of FoK/I, while more sophisticated beliefs support the integration of students’ cultural, experiential, and practical knowledge into classroom learning. Findings demonstrate that teachers with constructivist and growth-oriented beliefs are more likely to scaffold learning through students’ FoK/I. Despite the richness of FoK /I scholarship, limited research has examined the role of epistemological beliefs in shaping how teachers utilize students’ FoK/I. This study addresses that gap by offering a conceptual framework that links epistemological beliefs with FoK/I, contributing to broader discussions on equity-oriented pedagogy and culturally responsive teaching.

Teachers’ beliefs strongly shape classroom practices and student learning (Briggs, 2019). Research on Funds of Knowledge (FoK) (Moll et al., 2006) and Funds of Identity (FoI) (Esteban-Guitart & Moll, 2014) highlights the importance of recognizing students’ cultural and experiential resources (Collins, 2020; Kang, 2022; Kim, 2023; Zhang-Yu et al., 2021). However, meaningful integration is often constrained by time, limited training, and deeply held teacher beliefs (Cho et al., 2019).

Teachers’ epistemological beliefs (EBs)—assumptions about knowledge and learning—shape how teachers value knowledge, design instruction, and engage learners (Schommer, 1994; Buehl & Fives, 2016). Constructivist-oriented teachers tend to support collaboration and student agency (Letina, 2022), whereas rigid views emphasize transmission and may overlook students’ lived experiences (Leal, 2011). Despite expanding FoK/I research, the role of EBs in shaping equitable practice remains underexplored. This study examines how do teachers’ beliefs about the nature of knowledge impact their ability to utilize students’ cultural assets to facilitate learning. Theoretical frameworks Schommer epistemological belief model This study is grounded in Schommer’s Epistemological Belief Model (1994), which views personal epistemology as a set of independent beliefs about knowledge and learning, including its structure, stability, source, learners’ ability, and speed of learning. These beliefs shape teachers’ instructional practices, with more rigid beliefs tending toward transmissive teaching and more sophisticated beliefs supporting constructivist approaches. In this context, teachers with more complex epistemological beliefs are more likely to recognize and integrate students’ funds of knowledge and interests, promoting more equitable and culturally responsive learning (table 1). ## Participants and sampling

I conducted interviews with five elementary school teachers, selected through purposive sampling based on their experience teaching in culturally and socioeconomically diverse upper elementary classrooms (4th and 5th grade). Participants had 5 to 19 years of teaching experience and were recommended by principals for their demonstrated ability to work effectively in diverse contexts  (table 2). After each interview, I reviewed and coded the data, conducting preliminary analyses to identify recurring themes and determine when data saturation was reached. Saturation occurred after five interviews, as consistent themes emerged and no substantial new insights were identified.

## Data analysis

Thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006) was used to examine interviews, surveys, and lesson plans. Data were transcribed, reviewed, and inductively coded from open codes to themes (Merriam & Tisdell, 2016), with Dedoose used to support organization and analysis.

Teachers’ belief of students’ FoK/I Teachers viewed students’ cultural, familial, linguistic, and experiential backgrounds as central to learning, shaping participation, behavior, and identity. They aimed to honor these values while establishing shared norms, reflecting recognition of FoK and FoI.

They also emphasized students’ everyday knowledge and skills—such as baking, gaming, and household tasks—as meaningful entry points for connecting to academic concepts. Linguistic, cognitive, and sensory diversity, including multilingualism and neurodiversity, were seen as assets that enrich peer learning and support understanding.

The findings show that teachers’ epistemological beliefs shape how they recognize and integrate students’ Funds of Knowledge and Funds of Identity. Teachers with more sophisticated, constructivist beliefs view knowledge as complex, evolving, and socially constructed, enabling them to draw on students’ cultural experiences, practical skills, and community knowledge to support engagement and critical thinking. However, persistent challenges — such as limited family collaboration, reliance on formal assessments,  and deficit-oriented assumptions—indicate that more rigid beliefs can constrain equitable practice. Because epistemological beliefs are dynamic and shaped through experience, teacher education programs play a key role in cultivating more constructivist or ientations. Embedding reflection on the nature of knowledge alongside FoK approaches can help teachers value students’ diverse experiences and enact more culturally responsive and inclusive pedagogies.
