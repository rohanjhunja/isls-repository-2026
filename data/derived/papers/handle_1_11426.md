# Strategies for Self-Regulated Learning with AI in an Undergraduate Research Methods Course

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Undergraduate research methods education faces numerous challenges with respect to student affect, interest, and conceptual understanding. We incorporated a set of artificial intelligence chatbots into an introductory psychology research methods course, designed to promote students’ self-regulation while learning to design their own research studies. Analyses of student-AI interactions and student focus group interviews revealed instances in which students used AI to exercise agency over their learning, deepening their understanding of a topic, and engaging in self-regulation. Findings also revealed instances of students’ overreliance on AI. This study has implications for the design of AI chatbots to support research methods education, and self-regulated learning more broadly.

## Introduction

Experimental research methods are central to many d isciplines—including psychology, social sciences, and the physical sciences—but alsonecessary for all to understand in order to reason about, and make informed decisions (Yacoubian, 2018). However, teaching and learning r esearch methods is complex. Students have difficult y articulating researchable hypotheses, identifying a nd operationalizing variables, selecting appropriat e statistical tests, and drawing conclusions based on evidence (D asgupta et al., 2014). Other challenges involve stu dents’ anxiety toward, disinterest in, and perceptions of the irrelevance of research (Earley, 2014). Self-regulated learning (SRL) strategies —which include various (meta)cognitive, motivational, emotional, and resource management strategies that orient learners toward goal achievement (Winne, 201 7)— can improve students’ motivation and academic perfo rmance (Schunk & Zimmerman, 2017; Zimmerman & Schunk, 2011). SRL strategies are used at different  phases of learning (Panadero, 2017; Theobal, 2021): In the preparatory phase, learners set goals based on the task to be achieved; in the performance phase, they  monitor their progress and adjust their strategies to advance toward their goals; in the appraisal phases, learners reflect on their performance to inform adaptations of strategi es for future performance. While SRL is beneficial,  it can require explicit training and feedback from instructors and peers (Theobal, 2021). Recent developments in artificial intelligence (AI)  chatbots present new opportunities for supporting SRL in research methods courses. With their ability to interpret and assess students’ behaviors and thinking, and provide appropriate scaffolding (Hilpert  et al., 2023; Molenaar, 2022), AI tools can complem ent human instruction, and personalize learning (Mohebi, 2024). Students, for example, may ask a chatbot to help  evaluate their understanding, to adapt its level of complexi ty, and to redirect its focus away from, or toward topics that students feel they need more or less practice.  However, we recognize the potential for students to overly rely on the chatbot at the expense of developing agency. In deed, research on AI-supported learning, specifically on ChatGPT-supported learning (Lo et al., 2024), suggests mixed effects on student outcomes. With these trade-offs in mind, this study asks: In what ways do students demonstrate SRL with an AI chatbot designed to support their learning of experimental research ? Answers to this question will inform how to desig n and integrate AI into research methods curriculum in ways that enrich students’ learning experiences.

## Participants and context

We collaborated with an instructor at a large, publ ic university in the Southern United States, and hi s 23 undergraduate Psychology and Social Work majors, en rolled in his course, Experimental Methods & Statistics. In this course, which introduces students to experi mental research methodologies and statistics, stude nts completed a semester-long project that involves drafting a proposal for an experimental research study.  Students were invited to  participate in two in-class AI-integrated Workshops. Workshop and co-design of chatbots We worked with the instructor to co-design and implement two 3-hour-long AI-integrated workshops, intended to support students in planning their studies and analyzing data. Through regular meetings with the instructor, we sought to understand the course’s learning goals, a nd students’ typical challenges. The instructor als o informed the design of the chatbot’s conversational tone and  guidelines for interaction that aligned with his o wn instructional approach. The result of these co-design efforts were chatb ots aimed at supporting self-regulation, understanding, and critical thinking in the context of different stages of experimental research. This study focuses on a chatbot designed to support  student construction of research studies, first us ed in Workshop 1 (implemented half-way through the term), then revised and used in Wor kshop 2 (implemented a few weeks before the end of the term). In Workshop 1: “Design Your Experimental Study,” the chatbot wa s designed to ask students questions that encouraged them to articulate hypotheses for their own research questions, identify independent and dependent variables, and d iscuss appropriate study design s to test their hypotheses. Based on preliminary analyses of transcripts of stu dents’ interactions with the chatbot in Workshop 1,  and on students’ progress with their research designs, we modified the chatbot and the curriculum for Worksho p 2. In Workshop 2, the instructor first demonstrated and explained effective strategies for conversing with the chatbo t. Then, students engaged with a revised chatbot to refine their study designs and reason about appropriate statistical analysis methods and potential conclusions.

Data sources and analysis Data included transcripts of student-AI interactions with the chatbot in both Workshops; focus groups conducted after Workshop 2; responses to a survey administered after Workshop 2; and written feedback from the instructor on students’ research design proposals.

## Findings

There were 18 students in Workshop 2 compared to 10  students in Workshop 1 who demonstrated agency (i. e., controlling or directing the conversation) in their  interactions with the chatbot. In Workshop 1 there  were a total of 42 instances (4% of total lines) compared with 1 26 instances (18% of total lines) in Workshop 2. In  these interactions, students asked the chatbot questions, challenged feedback from the chatbot, specified a task to work on, or asked the bot to behave differently. In Workshop 1 students engaged in more self-monitoring and evaluating behaviors (47%) compared with goal setting and adap ting behaviors (11%). In contrast, student interact ions in Workshop 2 shifted toward goal setting and adaptati on (48%), compared with evaluating and self-monitoring (23%). There were also cases in both iterations in which students asked the chatbot to engage in reaso ning for them, asking “Why is between subjects best for this  design?” or “Can you tell me if you think this wou ld align with my study and how it might impact the interpretation?” Students who participated in the focus groups further confirmed a shift toward goal-setting in Workshop

2. Students described Workshop 2 as more interactive, engaging, and useful for achieving personally meaningful

goals. They stated that their goals were to (1) refine their experimental research proposals; (2) develop conceptual understanding; and (3) practice with a variety of e xamples and test questions. Students adopted active  roles in their chatbot interactions, asking the chatbot abou t particular issues with their proposals (n=16 students) and discussing answers to AI-generated, hypothetical research problems (n=13 students). There were also cases in both Workshops 1 and 2 in which students used the chatbot to support self-regulating behaviors, such as by asking it questions to clarify their conceptual understanding of statistical methods. Students in the focus group reported seeking opport unities to deepen their understanding of statistica l methods and to test their understanding of research with AI-generated word problems. They praised the chatbot f or challenging them to think critically about their de sign choices and for reinforcing a conceptual rathe r than procedural understanding of statistical methods. As one student shared in a focus group, the chatbot: “really tested whether or not you have just the common knowledge [...] it was like [...] if you get just this [statistical result] and you don't have to do any of the work, can you still answer? It put the words behind the math, I guess.” Students also engaged in adaptation by asking the chatbot to modify its prompts and res ponses based on their needs. They asked the chatbot to provide problems around ANOVA interpretation, generate sample data for their research study, or help them apply a one-way ANOVA. Students in the focus group shared that being able to engage with various problems related to their research question supported their understanding. However, students also critiqued the chatbot for not offering increasingly challenging questions. One student in the focus group, for example, shared that she hoped “[the chatbot] would entice me to elaborate.” Students also engaged in adaptation when they could not understand the chatbot, and so asked the chatbot to explain concepts in different ways (e.g., If I was an 8th grader, how would you explain this to me?), help them identify where they were getting “confused or lost” or to “help [them] get started.” These examples show students’ abilities to adapt the chatbot’s output to best support their needs. Students needed support to evaluate AI feedback about their research proposals Students in the focus group reported seeking opport unities to clarify details about their experimental  research study proposals. One student, for example, shared that she “kept asking other questions to kind of follow up and give more depth like information to make sure [she]  was heading in the right direction with [her propo sal].” Students also demonstrated evaluating behaviors when interacting with the chat bot. For e xample, students clarified their studies to help the chatbot generat e more accurate recom mendations, challenged the chatbot’s critiques by explaining their reasoning, expressed uncertainty about their understanding, and prompted the chatbot to generate further explanations to clarify areas of confusion. However, several students ultimately chose inappropriate analyses for their final study proposals (n=6), some who over-relied on the chatbot’s inaccurate recommendations,  and others who chose analyses counter to the chatbot’s recommendations. In cases of overreli ance, students did not sufficiently clarify their s tudy design choices for the chatbot to provide accurate and rel evant recommendations, nor did the chatbot prompt f or clarification, or highlight potential problems for the student. ## Discussion and conclusions

This study identified ways that undergraduate stude nts engaged in self-regulated learning with a chatbot in the context of an experimental research methods course. We found that  students were able to take control of their learning by using the AI chatbot to help set goals, support self-monitoring, evaluate new information against their existing understanding, and adapt the direction and  approach of the chatbot’s help according to their needs, especially when given explicit instruction on how t o use chatbots effectively. At the same time, we id entified instances in which some students came to overly rel y on the chatbot’s help, or e lse to mistrust its output. These findings suggest opportunities for the design of ch atbots and surrounding curriculum to prioritize AI literacy, such as by guiding students to critically evaluate and cross-check the chatbot’s output. Future work might explore other factors that may have contributed to the observed SRL behaviors, such as students’ current SRL skills, prior experiences with research and AI, the instructor’s approach, and the classroom environment. Future wor k could explore how a chatbot can be designed to promote the emotional dimensions of learners’ self-regulation, such as motivation, attitudes, and self-efficacy.
