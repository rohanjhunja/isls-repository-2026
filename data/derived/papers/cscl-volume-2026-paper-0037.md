# Effects and Mechanisms of Learning Analytic-Supported Reflective Assessment for Promoting Metacognitive Regulation among Undergraduates

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Metacognitive regulation is critical for effective learning, yet many undergraduates lack the necessary skills to self-regulate. This study conducted an 18-week quasi-experimental investigation with 88 undergraduates in a Knowledge Building (KB) environment to examine how l earning analytics-supported reflective assessment (LAsRA) influences collaborative knowledge creation and metacognitive regulation. Group Communication Analysis (GCA) and Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) were employed to analyze students ’ discourse and regulatory networks. GCA results showed that the experimental group significantly outperformed the control group in knowledge creation, exhibiting highly cohesive discourse. ENA revealed that LAsRA facilitated a critical shift toward socially shared and deep-level metacognitive regulation. Rather than merely monitoring outcomes, students successfully constructed a dynamic collaborative cycle of "goal-setting—process monitoring —reflective evaluation — dynamic regulation." Findings suggest that a nalytics-supported reflective assessment provides an effective pathway to foster a community-level, self-improving regulatory environment, fundamentally enhancing students' metacognitive capabilities and collective knowledge advancement.

In today ’s knowledge-driven society, higher education increasingly aims to cultivate students' higher-order competencies to meet future challenges, including metacognitive regulation, inquiry, collaboration, and knowledge creation (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014; Snell & Lefstein, 2018). In collaborative learning contexts, metacognitive regulation is considered the core of effective learning, requiring learners to strategically coordinate cognitive, motivational, emotional, and behavioral processes to achieve task goals (De Backer et al., 2015; Järvelä et al., 2016). To cultivate these higher-order competencies, Knowledge Building (KB) offers a student-centered framework for collaborative inquiry (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). In KB environments, students are  not isolated individuals but active knowledge constructors who must assume collective cognitive responsibility for advancing the frontiers of community knowledge (Scardamalia, 2002). This advancement of knowledge requires students' deep, active participat ion across cognitive, metacognitive, social, and emotional dimensions, thereby extending the regulatory process from the individual's internal psychological level to the level of interpersonal interaction (Hadwin et al., 2011; Volet et al., 2009). Based on the level of social interaction, regulatory activities in collaborative contexts are conceptualized into three dynamically intertwined types: self-regulation (SR), co-regulation (CoR), and socially shared regulation (SSR) (Hadwin et al., 2011; Järvelä & Hadwin, 2013). Among these, SSR is the most critical for collaborative learning, extending regulation to the group level; it requires group members to interdependently negotiate shared task perceptions, establish collective goals, jointly monitor progress, and coordinate strategic adaptations to optimize collaborative performance (De Backer et al., 2015; Panadero & Järvelä, 2015).

Empirical studies suggest that high-level socially shared regulation is crucial for productive group engagement and sustained collaborative knowledge construction (Järvelä et al., 2016; Zheng et al., 2019). However, many undergraduates lack sufficient metacognitive awareness and skills to spontaneously initiate and maintain high-level SSR without the support of appropriate external scaffolding (De Backer et al., 2022; Raes et al., 2016). Collaborative inquiry is highly dynamic and complex; students must not  only process domain knowledge content but also jointly monitor collective understanding and progress, which imposes a high cognitive load on learners (Raes et al., 2016; Zheng et al., 2019). When facing cognitive conflicts or challenges, the regulatory behaviors of groups lacking effective regulation often remain at the individual SR or CoR levels (Lee et al., 2015), easily triggering epistemic emotions such as confusion and anxiety (D'Mello et al., 2014; Pekrun et al., 2017; Yang, Yuan, et al., 2024). To address these challenges, prior research has introduced external scaffolds, 264 such as collaboration scripts and metacognitive prompts, to guide students' regulatory processes, demonstrating positive effects on collaborative learning (Kim & Lim, 2018; Raes et al., 2016). Nevertheless, students often lack an intuitive perception of the dynamic evolution of collaboration, making it difficult to accurately identify the specific actions needed to resolve disagreements and advance collective knowledge. In such cases, their collaborative processes are prone to devolve into fragmented or short discussions lacking deep knowledge synthesis and substantive conceptual progress, forcing them to rely on low-level exploratory strategies (Yang et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2018).

Reflective assessment has been proposed as an effective scaffolding strategy to support iterative metacognitive behaviors (White & Frederiksen, 1998; Yang et al., 2016). Reflective assessment prompts students to exercise agency and responsibility, actively examining their cognitive and metacognitive processes through cyclical activities of goal-setting, monitoring, reflection, and regulation, thereby enhancing regulatory awareness and skills, and developing internal metacognitive structures (Lei & Chan, 2018; Yang, Chen, et al., 2020). In recent years, with the rapid development of learning analytics technologies, reflective assessment has been further enhanced and expanded (Siemens & Gašević, 2012; Tao & Zhang, 2021). As a commonly used learning analytics tool, KBDeX can visualize complex collaborative interactions and knowledge-building processes, helping students utilize visualized charts and data to conduct reflective assessments (Oshima et al., 2012; Yang, van Aalst, & Chan, 2020). On the one hand, visua lized collaborative data and analytics help students objectively evaluate group contributions, identify inquiry gaps, and intentionally create cognitive discrepancies between current inquiry states and desired goals (Yang et al., 2022). This not only effectively reduces the group's cognitive load when monitoring interaction processes but also transforms the confusion or frustration accompanying inquiry into positive epistemic emotions, such as curiosity, which drive deeper investigation (D'Mello et al., 2014; Yang, Yuan, et al., 2024). On the other hand, by integrating structured metacognitive prompts, learning analytics-supported reflective assessment (LA sRA) can guide student groups to engage in collective meaning negotiation and formulate evidence-based regulatory plans based on the interpretation of analytical data (Järvelä et al., 2015; Miller & Hadwin, 2015). Prior research indicates that this approach can effectively promote students' inquiry and the advancement of collective knowledge by alleviating associated cognitive burdens; moreover, even after the removal of learning analytics data and accompanying reflective scaffolds, students can continue to advance collective knowledge, suggesting that reflective assessment fosters self-sustaining metacogniti ve capabilities (Yang, Feng, et al., 2024). Therefore, the deep integration of learning analytics and reflective assessment holds the promise of empowering learners in highly complex collaborative environments, stimulating and sustaining a dynamic, community-level cycle of metacognitive regulation, thereby providing an effective practical pathway for shifting from individual regulation to high-level SSR (Zhang, Chen, et al., 2021). Building on this background, the present study conducts an 18-week quasi-experimental investigation to deeply explore the effects of analytics-supported reflective assessment on undergraduates' collaborative knowledge building and metacognitive regulation (Yang et al., 2022). Using a multi-method approach, this study utilizes Group Communication Analysis (GCA) to objectively quantify socio-cognitive processes and knowledgebuilding outcomes, and employs Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) to capture the network characteristics and co-occurrence structures of metacognitive regulatory behaviors. This study aims to address the following three specific research questions: (1) Do undergraduates with analytics-supported reflective assessment perform better in advancing collaborative knowledge building?  (2) To what extent does reflective assessment affect metacognitive regulation?  (3) How does reflective assessment support the mechanism of metacognitive regulation?

## Research context and participants

This study was conducted in a compulsory core course, Scientific Inquiry and Knowledge Creation, at a comprehensive research university in Central China. The course, spanning 18 weeks with two 45-minute sessions per week, aimed to develop undergraduates' foundational knowledge and skills in scientific inquiry, critical thinking, and metacognitive regulation. It adopted a blended learning environment combining face-to-face and online collaborative inquiry. A total of 88 third-year students from diverse acade mic backgrounds participated, most with limited prior experience in scientific inquiry and knowledge creation. A quasi-experimental design was employed, with an experimental class (n = 50) and a comparison class (n = 38). Students worked in small teams of 4 to 6 members to investigate pre-assigned inquiry topics, with 10 groups in the experimental class and 7 groups in the comparison class. All participants provided informed consent and were assured of confidentiality and voluntary participation.

265 KB design enhanced by analytics-assisted reflective assessment The instructional design integrated Knowledge Building (KB) principles —epistemic agency, reflective and transformative assessment, collective responsibility for idea improvement, and rise-above — into a three-component model to support collaborative knowledge creation and metacognitive regulation in a blended learning environment.

Component 1: Establishing a collaborative learning culture (Weeks 1–5). Students engaged in principlebased activities to cultivate awareness of collaborative inquiry and metacognition. Each student signed a learning contract that specified tasks, monitoring strategies, and weekly reflection topics. Guiding prompts (e.g., "Wha t I have and haven't known about the topic, and what I want to know") supported reflective collaboration. Small groups investigated topics such as maintaining motivation and persistenc e in scientific inquiry, created posters, presented their findings, and developed research plans to guide their inquiry. Component 2: Engaging in problem-centered inquiry (Weeks 4–8). Students conducted inquiries around selected core topics, posing questions, sharing ideas, and reflecting on discussions on Knowledge Forum (KF). Two types of scaffolds were provided in this phase: theory-building scaffolds (e.g., "My theory," "A bette r theory") to advance collective knowledge, and reflective scaffolds (e.g., "Our research topic," "Critical issues to explore") to support metacognitive development (see Figure 1). Students analyzed discussion notes and generated reflective summaries weekly using KF's visualization and integrated assessment tools. Component 3: Deepening inquiry with analytics-supported reflective assessment (Weeks 8 –18). As inquiry notes accumulated, the KBDeX tool was introduced in Week 9 to visualize the dynamic relationships of knowledge building. Students utilized the growth curve of cumulative degree centrality to monitor the progress of collective knowledge, and identified core concepts and promising areas for inquiry through the idea-network map. To support data interpretation, the instructor provided structured reflective prompt sheets (encompassing "our analysis," "our problems," and "our plan"), which guided students to identify cognitive gaps and plan subsequent inquiries. Meanwhile, they were encouraged to synthesize knowledge by creating rise-above notes and concept maps. In Week 13, a second round of reflective assessment was conducted, where students optimized their inquiry patterns by comparing their group's KBDeX data with that of the whole community. Furthermore, continuous weekly feedback on individual participation strengthened metacognitive monitoring and regulation throughout the entire process.

## Measures and analysis

To investigate the effects of learning analytics-supported reflective assessment (LAsRA) on undergraduates’ collaborative knowledge construction and metacognitive regulation (RQ1, RQ2), and the mechanisms influencing metacognitive regulation (RQ3), we collected 1721 Knowledge Forum (KF) notes f rom the experimental class and 706 notes from the comparison class.

Effects on Collective Knowledge Building (RQ1). Students’ online discussions were analyzed using Group Communication Analysis (GCA), an advanced computational linguistics method that models collaborative discourse as temporal-semantic networks (Dowell et al., 2019; Dowell & Poquet, 2021). GCA quantifies key socio-cognitive dimensions, including: (1) interpersonal interaction, measured by Overall Responsivity and Social Impact; (2) individual cognitive processes, indicated by Internal Cohesion; and (3) inform ation-level contributions, captured by Participation, Newness, and Communication Density. This approach allows fine-grained, data-driven measurement of knowledge construction processes and outcomes. Independent samples t-tests compared the experimental and comparison classes on these dimensions. Effects and Mechanisms on Metacognitive Regulation (RQ2 & RQ3). To examine metacognitive regulation, all KF notes were coded using a three-level framework adapted from prior research (Su et al., 2018; Zhang, Wen, & Liu, 2021). First, we identified the type of regulation: Self-Regulation, Co-Regulation, and Socially Shared Regulation. Second, we classified the process of regulation into Planning, Monitoring, Regulating, and Evaluating. Third, the focus of regulation was categorized into Task Understanding, C ontent Monitoring, Process Monitoring, Positive Emotion, Negative Emotion, Confusion, and Organization. Two researchers independently coded 1,000 notes (41% of the data), achieving high inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s κ = 0.85). We compared the proportions of metacognitive behaviors between classes and conducted Chi-square tests to examine significant differences.To explore mechanisms of metacognitive development, Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) was applied using ENA Webkit  (www.epistemicnetwork.org). ENA constructs dynamic networks visualizing co-occurrences of metacognitive elements, capturing both indivi dual and group-level structures and strengths of connections. Effects of analytics-supported reflective assessment on collective knowledge building (RQ1) GCA was used to examine the socio-cognitive processes of students’ collaborative knowledge creation in the experimental (n = 50) and comparison (n = 38) classes. Independent samples t-tests revealed that the experimental group significantly outperformed the comparison group across all GCA dimensions (p <.001, see Table 1), indicating a comprehensive positive effect of analytics-supported reflective assessment on collaborative knowledge building.

## Educational implications

First, fostering a collaborative and reflective culture is essential. Principle-based activities can encourage students to engage in socially shared metacognitive activities, enhancing collective knowledge construction. Second, reflective assessment supports meta-level dialogue, allowing students to monitor, evaluate, and regulate their inquiry effectively. Third, technology-supported analytics can provide real-time, embedded feedback, prompting students to engage in higher-order discussion and reflection. Educators should design learning environments that integrate analytics and structured prompts to scaffold metacognitive development.

## Limitations and future research

This study has limitations. First, it did not examine long-term transfer of metacognitive skills to other contexts. Second, the analysis relied primarily on online discourse, lacking multimodal data from face-to-face interactions. Future studies could incorporate AI-based analysis of video, audio, and other process-oriented data. Third, the scope of analytics was limited; future designs could integrate AI for automated, process-oriented reflective interventions across disciplines and learning contexts(Ouyang et al., 2023). This study demonstrates that learning analytics-supported reflective assessment (LAsRA) significantly enhances undergraduates’ collaborative knowledge creation and metacognitive regulation. Through analytics-supported reflective assessment, the intervention prompted students to shift from individual or superficial monitoring toward effective Socially Shared Metacognitive Regulati on. Epistemic Network Analysis revealed that L AsRA successfully fostered a dynamic collaborative cycle of "goal-setting — process monitoring — reflective evaluation —dynamic regulation." Within this cycle, students engaged in deep-level regulation by actively identifying gaps and evaluating ideas.
