# Making Processes Visible in Distributed Maker Communities

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores Make-in-Progress (MIP), a tool for learners to document and share photos of their making processes within a distributed community. We examine the initial set of maker posts to the MIP carousel in two concurrently running maker camp groups, along with video recordings of maker sessions and participant interviews. Through this analysis, we identify the elements of the making process made visible in these artifacts and the documentation styles and communication techniques employed.

## Introduction

In constructionist making, learners externalize their thinking by creating artifacts that are meaningful to them and their communities. These artifacts and their creati on processes make thinking visible and shareable. F rom a sociocultural perspective, lear ning is a process that emerges not only from the re lationships between the maker and the artifact but also from their interaction wi th other learners, personal histories, cultural con texts, the materials used, and the physical environment (Holbe rt et al., 2022). Thus, while complete construction s serve as a source of pride and motivation for the learner, i t is the iterative construction processes and entan gled sociocultural dynamics that are central to learning. Rough sketches, casual collaborations, early prototypes, and non-linear iterations make learners’ evolving understanding and identity visible to the community. Keune a nd Peppler (2022) found that shared portfolio sites and group documenting tools support collaboration and community building beyond immediate makerspaces. We  explore the potential for supporting these connect ions through capturing moment-to-moment making practices, challenges, and successes in distributed maker communities. In this paper, we share the initial design and implementation of Make-in-Progress (MIP), a tool for makers to document and share their process across multiple digitally connected maker environments. We examine students’ use of MIP to understand: How is the making process made visible through makers' posts? What social interactions and dynamics are facilitated through MIP’s public display of processes?

## Methods

Make-in-Progress (MIP) is part of a suite of tools designed  for the Connected Spaces project, which aims to support collaboration, mentorship, and community bu ilding across geographically distributed makers. As  part of the project's larger design-based research process, MIP was rapidly developed to meet an observed need to support the sharing of making practices across connected ma kerspaces. It consists of a web app for uploading p hotos, videos, and captions and an ambient display that sh owcases these uploads across the makerspaces. The tool was built and implemented during a two-week maker summer camp with two groups of middle and high school-aged students (28 total) from community organizations supporting local youth of color. The groups ran concurrently in separate spaces and were connected to maker mentors  across three additional locations. Data collection  included students' MIP posts (photos, videos, captions), vid eo recordings of maker sessions, six individual int erviews and one group interview. Posts were coded through multiple rounds using a bottom-up process to identify how learners share their making process. Findings from the posts  were triangulated with interview transcripts and r oom recordings to better understand the context in whic h makers created the posts. All names are pseudonym s in the findings and discussion below.

## Findings

When MIP was introduced on the third day of camp, students quickly began uploading photos and videos of their in-progress work. Cycling in a randomized manner, the display offers glimpses of the makerspaces in actio n: videos of quick functionality tests, photos of disj ointed prototypes, overhead shots of messy workstat ions, and selfies, along with finished artifacts. Students ga thered around the display to discuss each others’ p osts and projects based on facilitator prompts and of their own volition. During  the final group interview, a student expressed how the MIP gave a feeling of communal connection: “Even though we're two separate parts, we were together because we're all working on one thing.” O ut of 52 posts, only seven show finished artifacts,  while 45 posts display processes. Below, we outline four ways the MIP posts made students' p rocesses visible and some social interactions that emerged.

Documenting current stage and micro-accomplishments Many posts captured the current state of makers’ pr ojects, often highlighting micro-accomplishments using language such as “version 1”, “2.0”, and “updated” to indicate ongoing work. Others, such as Lyla, made a series of posts about seemingly small accomp lishments that over time, led to her final prototyp e. In one series, Lyla posted the disassembled wheels of her project along with a heart hand gesture, then a post of the wheels connected, then another displaying a test run of the motors, a nd finally a video of a functioning prototype. Alth ough the device worked, she captioned it “the struggle” cons idering the difficulties she overcame. In a post-workshop interview, Lyla explained that MIP “made us look fo rward to working on our project so we can take a pi cture of it.” Another maker echoed this sentiment in her int erview; “As I was finishing my project, I was like,  oh, that’s kinda cool. I want to show everybody and keep updat ing.” These posts and comments suggest that the pro spect of sharing the process with the wider maker communi ty motivated some makers to achieve small goals tha t add up to bigger successes.

Future-oriented documentation While some makers posted updates, others engaged in  future-oriented documentation. Nolan, for example, captioned a photo of his cat toy prototype with his  current work and future plans: “Today I managed to  make it work so tomorrow I will decorate it” followed by, “Today I’m decorating my cat toy and adding stability.” Nolan's posts illustrate not only his accomplishments but also his future improvements. Some students looked forward to further improvements after completing their prototy pes. After finishing his bike, Jonah shared a photo  with the caption, “Gonna work on a remote.” In an interview-style video made with one facilitator, Jonah speculated about additional features, saying: “No wheels. It's going to fly,” explaining, "This is just a prototype.” He added, “Maybe I might add a couple modes. Maybe you could pedal, or maybe it could also be electric.” Jonah and Nolan’s ideas illustrate a range of future-oriented thinking and imagining made visible through maker documentation with MIP. Documentation of the failures, missteps and challenges Some makers focused their posts on missteps or failures. After a few days posting about his micro-wins and next steps, Nolan faced a major setback. When he proudly  demonstrated his prototype to a group of makers, t he structure's base gave way and the device leaned to the side. Subsequently, Nolan posted: “With help I’m trying to stabilize it.” Notably, Nolan’s post remained posit ive and acknowledged his need for help. During the postinterviews another student explained, “We got to see everybody’s project and stuff and they were really showing everything.” This student’s exclamation highlighted how important it was to them for their community to see not only polished results but also the challenging parts of their process. Documentation of in-between moments The MIP Carousel can also document the nonlinear na ture of the making process where there is no visibl e accomplishment, mistake, or “progression.” We conceptualize these moments as ones that fall outside of step-bystep, recipe-style project descriptions l ike those on DIY sites like Instructables (Tseng & Resnick, 2016). For example, one maker shared a video saying “was just testing motors and gears. no additions today.” Simi larly, makers captured their workstations packed with material and electronics in various stages of assembly. The photos have no caption and materials seem to be resting af ter long and intense activity. These undefined post s show students' intention to share their rough processes in addition to particular stages of making.

## Discussion

Our analysis highlights the broader socio-technical system involved in sharing in-process work and shows how MIP enhances the visibility of making processes in a distributed maker community. Two key MIP features, posting flexibility and public display, were crucial.  MIP’s flexibility, allowing students to post and caption photos or videos about anything, enabled makers to share p rojects at various stages, including setbacks and c hallenges often overlooked in DIY communities that tend to em phasize product over process (Tseng, 2016). Instead, MIP showcases projects as they unfold, which participan ts found exciting and motivating, as indicated by t he wide range of posts about incomplete projects, future pl ans, challenges, and even undefined moments in betw een. Furthermore, the public nature of the MIP display provided makers with a constant audience giving the community a sense of shared process and collective building. In line with findings on other Connected Space tools (Bawankule et al., 2023), this public character gave the community a sense of what was possible and what they were building together. Students voiced appreciation for how thei r contributions were part of a larger collective ef fort, as the accumulation of posts helped foster a sense of belo nging to a community that extended beyond their individua l classrooms.
