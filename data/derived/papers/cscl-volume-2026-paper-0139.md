# Technological Competency: A Conceptual Model for Understanding the World Around Us

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This conceptual paper describes a framework for fostering “technological competency” (TC), integrating ideas from multiple disciplines including literacies, computer science, and computer supported collaborative learning as a social practice, data literacy, AI literacy, and justice-centered computer science education. TC is the ability to (1) ask questions about technology that are relevant both personally and collectively; (2) identify, collect, and curate data to answer these questions; (3) use appropriate tools to analyze this data; and (4) report and re-evaluate this data in discussion with relevant communities. Most importantly, this should be collaborative work between learners of all kinds-researchers, practitioners, students, and other stakeholders. We conclude with practical guidelines for designers of learning experiences: stakeholder collaboration, diversity and inclusion, critical approaches, and effective communication.

In the learning sciences, there are calls for improving data literacy learning, teaching future computer scientists to think more critically, and of course a growing call for incorporating AI literacy throughout the educational system. However, for practit ioners in K-12 settings, it can be difficult to wade through all of this information to arrive at practical solutions for teaching these concepts to the real students sitting in their classrooms every day. This conceptual paper describes “technological competency” (TC) - an understanding of how the technological systems and tools that impact our lives are shaped by the inherent social structures and human perspectives that underlay them. TC is a way of thinking about various literacies and learning approaches related to technology and its role in our lives. TC is the ability to (1) ask critical questions about technology and its impact that are relevant and important both personally and collectively; (2) iden tify, collect, and curate data to answer these questions in context; (3) use appropriate tools and techniques to analyze this data; and (4) communicate, evaluate, and refine the questions and answers in discussion with various relevant contexts.. By framing TC as both a conceptual model and a practical framework, we aim to support research-practice partnerships that connect disciplinary expertise with classroom needs. Aligned with the conference theme Partnering with Purpose, TC can serve as a shared foundation for cross-sector collaborations, enabling more intentional, equity-driven learning experiences that are rooted in both socio-technical understanding and everyday classroom practice.

## Theoretical perspectives

Literacy is often viewed as a collection of discrete skills that can be acquired step-by-step (Hamilton, 2010; Hardy et al. 2022). In the early 1980’s, literacy scholars including Shirley Brice Heath pointed out that observable literacy events, such as reading a book, are part of broader social interactions. The concept of literacy as a social practice (Hamilton, 2010; Perry, 2012) acknowledges that while literacy includes certain knowledge and skills, it is also defined by broader social and cultural implications. This also means that literacy can be viewed in different ways by different people at the same time, and that what it means to be literate may change over time as society and culture change.

A world powered by computerized systems results in a lot of data being generated and stored. As a result, Hardy et al. (2022) and Rubin (2022) called on education to broaden the view of data literacy from a conceptual data approach-teaching things like inference, variability, and visualization from specific content areas such as mathematics or biology-to also include a critical data literacy approach that positions students as not just consumers, but producers of data. This view empowers humans to not j ust calculate, but also critique, data practices and use their lived experiences to inform how/why they interact with these data. The explosion of generative AI in 2022 has only added to an already growing call to action for schools and teachers to include AI literacy in the curriculum. For example, Casal-Otero et al. (2023) conducted a systematic literature review and described the need for better assessment of students’ understanding of AI as well as a more intentional focus on the negative consequences of haphazard use of AI. While there is a growing sense 737 of urgency about teaching AI literacy, there is still confusion and debate about how to define it, let alone how it should be taught to students from kindergarten to high school. Related to these literacy concepts are ideas about restructuring how we teach computer science to our students. Vakil (2018) has called for a shift to justice-centered equity: to consider and critique the ethical uses of technology and computing, along with the rights and responsibilities of individuals and organizations within our society; to include opportunities for students to adopt disciplinary ideas in computer science while also creating environments that recognize and value the students’ own social,  civic, and political identities; and finally to connect computer science to political vision, acknowledging that computer science is important for the economic and political welfare of informed citizens.

While GenAI points to a new perspective on “learning with computers” (Salomon et al. 1991), there is a scramble to define AI literacy and best practices for design and use of such tools. In this landscape, there is a need for Principled Practical Knowledge (Bereiter 2014) to bridge the gap between theory and practice. TC is more than just an AI literacy framework. In fact, while TC is a concept that developed in response to questions about GenAI and how to teach young people about these tools, our goal is a  more robust model that candescribe ways to interact with computers and other humans in ways that are personally and communally productive. Considering concerns about technology over the last few decades (e.g computational thinking, CS for all, 21st centu ry skills, the Metaverse, virtual reality, and so on), it seems relevant to develop a more general approach that can accommodate the current and future technological landscape, whatever that may be. In the spirit of Wise and Schwarz (2017), we position TC within CSCL as a collaboratively developed and socially constructed practice that encompasses both humans and machines interacting in collaboration with each other. Our approach supports learners in engaging with real-world, complex aspects and questions about gen-AI and other technology through collaborative data practices and ethical reflection. Moreover, TC can guide the design of future CSCL environments that aim to foster critical engagement with  technology and promote learning.

Our concept of TC is influenced indirectly by many more perspectives that space prohibits us from detailing here-researchers such as Papert, Wing, Kafai, and more. TC is not a replacement framework, but a meta-orchestrating construct that integrates inquiry, data practice, analysis, and community-based reflection across different technological contexts.

Humans need TC to be more fully participating members in society. This broad idea builds from conceptions of literacy as a social practice, data literacy, AI literacy, CS education, and CSCL. Our current vision of TC includes four elements. (1) Ask critical questions: draws from traditions of critical literacy and justice-centered computer science education, which foreground issues of power, representation, and ethical responsibility in socio-technical systems. (2) Collect data: grounded in critical data literacy, emphasizing learners’ roles as producers and interpreters of data rather than passive recipients. (3) Analyze data: builds on computational thinking and AI literacy perspective, which help understanding how technological systems operate and how analytic processes shape knowledge construction. (4) Communicate and discuss: reflects on CSCL and literacy frameworks, positioning learning as socially situated, dialogic, and collaboratively constructed. TC is not a binary “yes/no” skill, but rather one tha t can be applied in better or worse ways. TC is also a skill that is accessible, in some fashion, to any learner at any level.

This discussion of TC emphasizes the importance of helping learners think critically about the technology and tools we use every day. As technology continues to permeate education, work, and daily life, it is essential that learning experiences foster inqu iry, reflection, and agency, and TC offers a lens for designing such experiences. If TC describes the competencies learners should develop, then designers of learning environments must intentionally structure opportunities for collaborative inquiry, data engagement, and critical reflection. From our initial conceptualization of this model, here are guidelines for designers of learning experiences/tools: (1) Stakeholder collaboration: Researchers, practitioners, students, and community members should work together to understand technology and its impact on their lives. (2) Diversity and inclusion:  Asking and answering questions about technology should prioritize diverse perspectives by including a wide range of team members. Such diversity can lead to more inclusive and complete outputs. (3) Critical approaches: All affected stakeholders should have the opportunity to be producers, not just consumers, and should be encouraged to think critically about how and why to use technology for learning and creating. (4) Effective communication: The questions and answers should be communicated in ways that invite reflection and critique, with a view to establishing discourse and improving collective knowledge-building, in both academic and other settings. 738 Bereiter, C.
