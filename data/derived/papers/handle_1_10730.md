# High Achievement in Middle School: Is it a Blessing or a Curse for Adolescent Girls and Their Relationship with Themselves as Learners?

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research in progress addresses how high-achieving middle school girls’ see themselves as learners in the face of the psychological paradox of high achievements and low self-belief. The purpose of this research is to provide a nuanced understanding of the tensions, pressures, and resistances at play (e.g. internal and external rewards, pressures, gender norms, racialized and ethnic cultural tensions) that shape girls’ sense of self. It employs the Listening Guide method, a feminist qualitative research methodology of psychological inquiry, specialized in discovery research through listening. Preliminary research described in this paper includes multiple interviews with two high-achieving middle school girls in a large city in the northeast of the United States. The contribution of this research in progress includes specific cultural, psychological, and relational patterns across the lived experiences of high-achieving girls that show the psychological logic of how they see themselves as learners.

## Introduction

Adolescent girls in the United States face hardship s in their psychological well-being, including depressive episodes (Center for Disease Control and Prevention, 2021)  and decreases in self-esteem. Throughout middle school, the percentage of girls who feel confident in who they are declines from 86% to 60% (Hinkelman, 2017) which stays consistent throughout high school. Addi tionally, Hinkelman (2017)  found that 46% of girls do not speak their minds to be liked by others, which has been found to indicate a state of psychological ris k through dissociation from their own thoughts, feelings, and knowledge about relational realities (Brown & Gilligan, 1992). These behavioral indications align with present cultural norms of gendered expectations for girls (e.g., being nonconfrontational and non-disruptive). For high-achieving adolescent girls, these issues are exacer bated, as about 30% of girls with a 4.0 or higher GPA average feel they are not smart enough for their career aspirati ons and an increased 62% of high-achieving girls do not speak their minds to others (Hinkelman, 2017). The alarming percentages raise questions about the impact of the apparent psychological paradox of achievement and self-belief, as well as the relational  nature of girls’ development within (middle) school s, especially in the context of high achievement, on their sense of self as learners.

This paper describes research in progress that aims  to provide new insights to the conceptual understanding of what happens to girls in school when high achievement becomes central to their lives, and how that shaped the relationship they have with themsel ves as learners. The purpose of this study is to understand the psychological processes and undercurrents of the girls' experiences and the impact of social and cultural tensions on their sense of self as learners. The research wi ll employ the Listening Gu ide (LG) method (Gilligan & Eddy, 2021), a feminist qualitative research methodology of ps ychological inquiry, specialized in discovery resea rch through listening. This methodology is uniquely sui ted to understand the interdisciplinary nature of t he paradox of achievement and girls’ sense of self, as it enables researchers to deconstruct shared narratives by individuals to lay bare their psychological logic. The anticipated  results of this research in progress  include specific cultural, psychological, and r elational patterns across the lived experiences of high-achieving girls that show the psychological logic of how they see themselves as learners.

## Background

In recent decades, there has been an overrepresenta tion of high-achieving girls’ participation in AP and college preparatory classes (Buchmann et al., 2008; Freeman, 2004), while research has more often prioritized student s who are scoring low in schools (Pepperell & Rubel, 2009). It has been reported that high-achieving girls' self-concept levels are paradoxically low (Preckel & Brüll, 2008; Rudasill et al., 2009) in contrast to their educational achievements and compared to other groups of studen ts. The r easons for this, while speculated on, remain unknown. Several perspectives on these issues, for example the imposter phenomenon  (Muradoglu et al., 2022) aim to understand the psychological paradox of  achievement and self-concept of girls. To further unravel the confounding paradox, research is needed that is rooted in the girls’ subjective understanding of their  experiences of their sense of self combined with an interdiscip linary lens of psychology, sociology, and education. The interdisciplinary nature of the research lens is essential to understand tensions of social and cultural narratives on gender and educational development within schools through individuals’ psychological experiences. Particularly, girls of color with intersecting identities have su ffered compounding consequences of these dominant c ultural narratives impacting the way their development is supported within schools (Nasir, 2011; Smith-Purviance, 2021). The theory on the psychological development of girls us ed in this research is derived from the work of Brown and Gilligan (1992). While this work is not p ositioned as a theoretical framework, it provides u s with a lens through which to observe the internal developm ent of girls’ voices and knowledge (of themselves) that is relevant to this research. This work explains that girls approaching adolesc ence have a relational acuity that is centered in their senses: they know about relations hips by what they see, hear, and thus feel and thin k. When transitioning through adolescence, the voices of ot hers, essentially the voices of society a nd culture, that often perpetuate ideas of so-called ‘ideal’ relationships and ‘ideal’ girls start to enter the girls’ internal worlds, of ten coming to a head with their own knowledge from thei r senses. Over time in adolescence, most girls stru ggle to balance these dual perspectives, often leading their own voice centered in their own senses, to go underground as it risks disrupting the social and relational status quo. The voices that go underground can, when time goes by and ‘ideal’ ways of being are internalized, be hard to connect to or be forgotten, in other words having been dissociated from. If we know that throughout female adolescence a knowin g voice goes underground or is dissociated from (Brown & Gilligan, 1992), in the context of relationships, how does it impact their sense of themselves as a learner, especially when schools are highly relational space s? Do the voices of others influence how a learner should behave, feel, think as a learner, and take over the knowledge of one’s self as a learner?

## Methods

This preliminary research employs the LG methodology, a feminist, qualitative, relational vo ice-centered approach to research that aims to understand psychological processes through discovery (Gilligan & Eddy, 2021). Research questions that address the ways in which p eople “live with, in and against conflict and chall enge” (Tolman & Head, 2021, p. 152), which can include topics that are taboo, highly emotionally charged and/or disrupt societal power structures and dynamics (Cruz, 2021), by illuminating people’s psychological logic are particularly appropriate to be answered through the LG method.  As the girls’ experiences are embedded in dominant social and cultural narratives and structures, the psychological logic of how they make sense of t hemselves in a challenging and contradicting context the LG is particularly useful. In this preliminary study, two high-achieving middle school girls were interviewed, from a large, socioeconomically, and culturally diverse city in t he northeast of the United States. Charlotte (pseudonym), 14 years old, went to a private middle school, and was interviewed twice (45 minutes each), in a public place of her choosing. Gemma  (pseudonym), 11 years old, went to a public middle school, and was interviewed once  (40 minutes), similarly in a public place of her choosing. The girls informed us about the research question “How do high-achieving middle/high school girls see themselves a s learners?”. The interviews and the consequent five steps of analysis, also known as ‘ listenings’, of the interview data were conducted according to t he LG method. Part of the steps of analysis is creating I-poems from interview transcripts, meaning to extrac t only the mentions of “I” and the attached verb to compose a poem. The  way the I moves throughout the interview, the rhyt hm, and disruptions of the I, without contextual information, helps us to reveal one’s psychological processes  and/or obstacles that lay bare one’s psychological logic, that might be hard to observe otherwise.

## Discussion and conclusion

This paper presents the preliminary analysis on high-achieving middle school girls’ experiences of themselves as learners and their psychological logic. Two distinct psychological processes of two high-achieving middle school girls in their relationship to themselves as learners are described. Firstly, a process of dissociation for Charlotte  is observed through her I-poems, leading us to question the possibility of this process occurring over time  for more girls in their educational journey as hig h achieving students. In top of that, the definitions of the "perfect stu dent" or “good student” echoes a cultural narrative that has penetrated our educational systems that predominantly focus on (standardized) results. This cultural narrative includes a patriarchal value-system (Way et al., 2018) including valuing mind over body and thinking over feeling. The prioritization of some experie nces over others contribute to a disconnection from  oneself and others. Also, the ideal of the “perfect student” or “good student” seems to create the tendency for high-achieving students to self-identify with the results they receive in school. T he self-identification with externally validated values and dissociation of one’s embodied experiences creates a dependency on others and detachment of  one’s own knowledge. This can create  about one's sense of themselves  when usual desired results are not obtained.  In contrast, Gemma’s I-poems indicated a predominantly embodied psychological experience of herself as a learner, defined by us as a voice of ‘resistance’.

This study’s future work includes elaboration of pa rticipants’ backgrounds and lived experiences to expand on the intersectional nature of these insights. The current analysis leads us to pose the following questions for the field to ponder: Can one hold on to a voice of resistance as a high-achieving student? Is it possible to hold on to being a learner that values both mind and body, thinking and feeling? If so, ho w can we support and foster a voice of resistance, that some high-achieving girls seem to disconnect from over time in middle school?
