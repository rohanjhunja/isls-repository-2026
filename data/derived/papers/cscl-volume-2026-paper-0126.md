# The Effect of Group-Awareness on Peer Feedback Quality in Pre-Service Teachers’ Collaborative Diagnostic Reasoning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This quasi-experimental study examined the effect of a group awareness tool on peer feedback quality in video-based collaborative diagnostic reasoning. In total, 54 pre-service teachers collaboratively diagnosed a teaching video in dyads and generated peer feedback for the acting teacher regarding the use of digital media in the classroom. Results suggest a rather detrimental effect on feedback quality when supported by the group awareness tool, indicating the need for additional scaffolding to utilize group awareness.

Peer feedback is considered crucial for teachers’ professional development  in all phases of teacher education (Hammerness et al., 2005). According to the classification of  Hattie & Timperley (2007), effective feedback comprises the definition of a clear goal, information about the progress on achieving that goal (Feed Back) and suggestions for improvement (Feed Forward).  Both feedback provider and recipient are usually on a similar expertise level during peer feedback. As a form of co-constructive teacher collaboration, peer feedback holds potential for enhancing  instructional quality, particularly the successful integration of digital media in the classroom (Drossel & Heldt, 2022).

Research suggests that diagnostic competences are prerequisites for delivering effective peer feedback on teaching (Prilop et al., 2024). In the teaching context, diagnosing can be understood as „the goal-oriented collection and interpretation of case-specific or problem-specific information to reduce uncertainty in order to make […] educational decisions“ (Heitzmann et al., 2019, p. 4). Whil e earlier research in teacher education predominantly focused on individual diagnosis, recent studies increasingly underscore the value of collaborative approaches (e.g. Csanadi et al., 2020). Collaborative Diagnostic Reasoning (CDR) conceptualizes the process of generating a joint diagnosis through engagement in both diagnostic and collaborative problem-solving activities (Radkowitsch et al., 2021). CSCL literature highlights that groups need instructional support to effectively engage in collaborative problem-solving (Vogel & Fischer, 2020). Research in medical education indicates that practi tioners often fail to share and elicit relevant information  while collaboratively diagnosing patient cases  which negatively effe cts the diagnostic outcome (Radkowitsch et al., 2021; Brandl et al., 2024). One concept connected to promoting collaborative learni ng that has received increasing attention over the past years  is group awareness (GA). GA refers to the perception of relevant characteristics of the group or group members, such as their knowledge or perspectives (Bodemer & Schnaubert, 2018). GA-tools visualize such information and implicitly guide collaborative processes. One particular potential of GA-tools is facilitating the recognition of conflicting assumptions within the group. Knowledge about conflicting viewpoints can then trigger social learning activities to resolve socio-cognitive conflicts, e.g. sharing of unshared knowledge or elaborating on content-related aspects of the task by activating internal collaboration scripts (Schnaubert & Bodemer, 2022; Engelmann & Hesse, 2011). The use of a GA-tool could help address the problem that sharing and eliciting relevant information is often neglected in CDR.

Research aim and questions CDR is a promising approach for improving teachers ’ professional development. However, only a few studies examined CDR in teacher education. Hence, little is known  about the effectiveness of scaffolds, like GA-tools, on the diagnostic process and learning outcome s (Brandl et al., 2024). In this study the learning outcome was assessed by examining features of a written peer feedback on teaching situations, since high quality feedback can be understood as the result of the engagement in diagnostic activities (Prilop et al., 2024). The following research questions were addressed: RQ1: What is the effect of a GA-tool during CDR on Feed Back quality? 698 RQ2: What is the effect of a GA-tool during CDR on Feed Forward quality? RQ3: What is the effect of a GA-tool during CDR on the feedback content?

## Method

In a quasi-experimental study N = 54 teacher education students (mean age: 22. 6 years; f = 36) were grouped in 27 dyads and assigned into two conditions. First, the students watched a scripted teaching video which showed the use of digital media in an English  lesson. Then, they individually engaged in diagnostic activities, namely evidence generation and evidence evaluation, which involved describing and analyzing the integration of digital media in the classroom. For each analysis, participants indicated their agreement with seven items on a four-point Likert scale with each item representing an indicator of digitally enhanced teaching based on the European Framework for the Digital Competence of Educators (Redecker, 2017; Özsoy et al., 2024). Subsequently, participants were connected via audio-chat and they collaboratively developed suggestions for improvement for the observed teacher based on their individual evaluations. In the control group, participants could only see their own evaluation of the indicat or of digitally enhanced teaching during collaboration. In the experimental group, participants could access both their own and their learning partners’ evaluation in an assessment grid revealing agreement or disagreement for each indicator. Subsequently, participants in both conditions individually formulated a written peer feedback for the observed teacher. A coding scheme was developed capturing the quality of the feedback messages based on the classification of Hattie & Timperley (2007) and the criteria of the Feedback Quality Index by Prins et al. (2006). Accordingly, we distinguished between the evaluation of the observed teacher behavior, the Feed Back, and suggestions for improvement, the Feed Forward. As quality criteria we chose the level of detail for specificity and explanation for both the Feed Back and Feed Forward. Additionally, we operationalized  the feedback content by the level of integration of the seven indicators of digitally enhanced teaching resulting in five categories (Feed Back Specificity, Feed Back E xplanation, Feed Forward Specifity, Feed Forward Explanation and Use of Indicators). Our coding scheme distinguished between three quality levels (0-2). Two raters coded half of the feedback message independently, taking the whole feedback message as unit of analysis. Discrepancies between the two raters were negotiated until a consensus was  reached. The interclass correlation coefficient ranged from.69 and.84 for each category before the negotiations for consensus. Descriptive results revealed that the control group achieved higher mean values in three categories. Regarding Feed Back specifi city, students who received no GA support described situations in more detail on average. Conversely, the experimental group demonstrated more depth in their Feed Back explanation. In terms of Feed Forward, the control group outperformed the experimental group in both their level of detail for specifi city and explanation. Concerning feedback content, the experimental group referred more strongly to the seven indicators of digitally enhanced teaching. However, partly medium effect sizes were found but none were  statistically significant (all t(52) < 1.4, p >.09, d <.36) which may be due to the small sample size. The specific use of the GA-tool, which showed (dis)agreement concerning the evaluation of instructional features, might not be sufficient to foster collaborative diagnostic activities, as the rather superficial revelation of agreement might have led to a n increase in quick consensus building (Weinberger & Fischer, 2006). The analysis of the recorded conversations will shed light on the collaborative mechanisms that influence the quality and the content of the peer feedback and will inform additional need for support during teachers’ CDR.
