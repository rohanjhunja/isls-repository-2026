# The Connected Spaces Dashboard: Fostering Peer-to-peer Collaboration Through Ambient Maker Profiles

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The Connected Spaces Dashboard is a tool designed to foster maker identity development, peripheral awareness, and the promotion of student help seeking behaviors. Developed iteratively across several years, prototypes of the Dashboard range from lo-fi solutions to in-house developed applications. Beneath these external embodiments, there are a number of frameworks and scaffolds developed to facilitate student interactions and engagement with the Dashboard as a rich identity resource. In this interactive demo, participants will have a chance to utilize these scaffolds alongside a specially tailored version of the Dashboard to develop peripheral awareness with their peers.

The Connected Spaces (CxS) Dashboard is a tool that combines aspects of Maker Portfolios with structures that support students’ peripheral awareness and participation (Fields et al., 2023, Chan & Holbert, 2020, Lave & Wenger, 2001), as well as the promotion of constructionist learning and the development and reexamination of students’ identities (Godwin, 2016). However, finding peers within a distributed community of makers to engage with, particularly when faced with roadblocks in the making process, is a persistent challenge (Aleven et al., 2003). This connection with others and the ability to see their work aligned with work of their peers, is also a strong driver of identity development (Kim et al., 2018). In response, we set out to address the need for peer-topeer collaboration, as well as ownership of the space as students adapt to a constructivist environment as opposed to direct instruction. Through a system of “affinities” (Kumar & Tissenbaum, 2022) as an alternative to badging, students are able to associate themselves with a number of maker concepts, regardless of whether or not they have gained some arbitrary level of competency sufficient to earn a badge. Through ambient awareness of their peers’ affinities (Alavi & Dillenbourg, 2012; Ogata & Yano, 2000), the technology facilitates students’ help seeking behavior (Aleven et al., 2003) so that they can locate other students in their community who may be willing to learn alongside them, especially when they encounter a problem. These patterns of behavior support constructionist ideas of knowledge generation through student tutoring (Roscoe & Chi, 2008), as well as acting as a component of a larger system of technology serving to create an extended community of practice across spaces and promoting boundary spanning connections with makers in disparate locations (Smith & Tissenbaum, 2023).

Informal makerspaces are spaces in which people with shared interests and of varying skill levels can gather to share ideas, knowledge and technology. Through the intentional design of their curators, these spaces have the potential to become communities of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991) by allowing students to collaborate with peers and mentors while also expressing their unique identities through personally relevant making (Bawankule et al., 2019; Keune & Peppler, 2017). Makerspaces can also provide unique opportunities for the use of technology to support student learning alongside deliberate scaffolding from instructors within the space (Pitkänen et al., 2020). One approach that is gaining particular prominence in promoting students’ sense of ownership, belonging, and competency in maker environments is the idea of “maker portfolios” (Chan & Holbert, 2020; Lui et al., 2019; Keune & Peppler, 2017), as documentations of students’ work and as adaptive representations of their identities. The Dashboard builds on prior work (Kumar & Tissenbaum, 2022) and seeks to combine aspects of maker portfolios with scaffolds for help seeking and peer mentorship. The Dashboard has been developed through a design-based research approach, and has been implemented in a number of settings, including four two-week long summer camps designed to foster computational identity. Tool overview The Dashboard is composed of three main components: an interface by which students can customize and update their personal Dashboard profile, a system of “Affinity” badges that students can tag their profiles with, and a “grid view” screen which is displayed ambiently within the makerspace environment and can also be viewed on a student’s personal computer. Through several years of iterations, the Dashboard has been implemented in a n umber of different ways, with systems added based on the in-situ needs of students, making the Dashboard a relatively fluid tool, centered around a few core theoretical ideas. Some of the changes that have been previously incorporated have included a virtual “help” signal with alert messages on the ambient display, integration with a “work in progress” photo carousel, reflection prompt “stickers”, and a number of scaffolded activities that provided students an opportunity to utilize the Dashboard as a reflective tool. Profile personalization/maker affinities After initial design experiments (Bawankule et al., 2023), we concluded that students really enjoyed and resonated with the ability to personalize their Dashboard profiles, prodding their neighbors to look at what they’d done, collaborating with each other, and growing attached to the representation, even indicating that they didn’t want their profile to be deleted at the end of the camp. When developing an in-house prototype, we slimmed down the customization feature but still left a large amount up to the student to be able to create an authentic representation of self. Alongside the ability to add pictures, colors, and descriptions to their profile, we incorporated a fluid set of affinities – badges that represented both maker concepts and social causes that students could use to add motivation to their projects. These affinities can be quickly modified based on direct student feedback, as well as input from mentors present within the space. Scaffolding included daily reflections on what affinities students chose to include, the act of seeking out other students with similar affinities, and prompts for students to analyze their projects and to add affinities that were related to their components, so that other students could seek help from those who were more experienced (figure 1).

Grid view/ambient display Through each iteration of the Dashboard, student profiles have been displayed in different ways, either ambiently or on a student’s personal computer. By displaying all profiles simultaneously ambiently in the room, students are now able to passively observe their peers’ particular skills and preferences, removing some of the barriers to collaboration within an informal learning environment (figure 2). Additionally, by being able to filter and explore profiles on their own computers, connected to photos of students’ works in progress through other Connected Spaces tools, students can identify connections between their own needs and other students’ capabilities. As a resource for teacher orchestration, the grid view provides a “quick and dirty” view of students’ interests, and allows a facilitator to point to a shared resource when suggesting student mentors or collaborators. The Dashboard’s grid view acts as a meeting place for making students’ maker identity visible, and is the first step in establishing peer to peer collaboration.

Dashboard in practice The Dashboard was implemented in maker education-based summer camps over a period of four years, in which students developed personally meaningful projects after a week of engaging with pre-built physical computing artifacts. Dashboard personalization and scaffolded reflection activities were incorporated into the curriculum, and Dashboard changes were recorded and analyzed, and student feedback was incorporated into the design of the next implementation. From our first iteration to the latest, the Dashboard underwent a number of changes, both within the technology, and in its situated implementation in the environment. We succeeded in incorporating maker portfolios to support students' identity development but recognized the tensions surrounding trying to develop students’ computational identities with the Dashboard, if it requires sacrificing their perception of the tool as a help seeking structure. However, based on our observations of the tool in practice, we thought it was possible that students’ legitimate representation of identity might make them feel a sense of authenticity and belonging to a community, and that this could potentially be productive when used with scaffolding that allowed for peripheral awareness of their peers, aligning with previous findings about Maker Portfolios (Chan & Holbert, 2020; Lui et al., 2019; Keune & Peppler, 2017).

As we conducted our various camps and programs while using the Dashboard, one question that frequently emerged in our discussions was the role of the instructor in the camp. One of our main goals for the Dashboard was to promote peer-to-peer collaboration and constructivist learning. When instructors short-circuit student questions to avoid momentary chaos, do we deprive students of the opportunity to grow? We considered the possibility of the instructor using the Dashboard as a reference point, a way to redirect student inquiries onto an alternative, more productive path, and to take advantage of learning gains from student tutoring (Roscoe & Chi, 2008). We saw several instances of the Dashboard being used to promote collaboration, but students still demonstrated resistance to our suite of tools, apparently because they were used to a traditional method of engagement with learning – they would come to the instructor before their peers. We attempted to implement a practice of “check the Dashboard before you ask an instructor,” but the scope of our camps made it difficult to fully pull away from traditional instruction, and students didn’t have enough time to adapt to the change in classroom culture.

Dashboard fidelity The affordances of the Dashboard concept varied greatly from iteration to iteration, and our rapid, design-based research approach allowed us to experiment not only with embodiments of design conjectures, but with the design conjectures themselves. When we observed that students weren’t responding well to the ambient display, we put it on the backburner and developed additional tools for other aspects of student participation. Because of this, the Dashboard has become a multi-faceted conceptual tool that can be presented in a number of ways. One of our major interests going forward will be what features of the Dashboard make sense to implement within a given environment. For example, our implementation in Google Slides had major benefits due to its easy implementation, flexibility, customization, and in-network connection to many school districts’ pre-existing technological networks.
