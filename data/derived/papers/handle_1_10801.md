# Patches on the Gap: Are Neuroscience Studies Helpful for Reducing Educational Inequality?

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Addressing the pronounced disparities highlighted by the COVID-19 outbreak, the study explores the implications and difficulties of utilizing neuroscience in educational programs aimed at mitigating socioeconomic status (SES) gaps in the context of South Korea. A literature review involving 20 selected papers and interviews with 10 scholars elucidates how neuroscience informs equity programs while also highlighting its limitations. Neuroscience studies can provide the basis, ideas for development, and evaluation for equity-promoting programs. However, lack of connections with sociocultural learning theories, limitations in context, and data collection issues hinder the integration of these studies with systemic programs. The findings underscore the need for contextualized approaches that bridge cognitive and sociocultural perspectives to design effective educational programs for equity.

## Introduction

Equity in education has long since cemented its pos ition as a key issue across the globe. Recent events, such as the COVID-19 outbreak, have accentuated the significance of t his issue,  throwing the existing disparities in educational achievement into harsh definition, and amplifying concerns for accessibility. Differences in socioeconomic status (SES) can lead to discrepancies amongst students in access to resources —spanning from parental support to broadband connection —which have persistent effects starting from an early age  (Sanchez, 2021). It follows that the development and implementation of educational programs to alleviate the effects of disparities in SES is imperative.

Research in the learning sciences field attempts to address equity issues through a systemic, sociocultural approach; however, elemental approaches  based on cognitive science  can also bring new insights for practice. Systemic approaches for equity in learning tend to focus on expanding the learning activity system, fo cusing on sociohistorical practices of the community (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016). They take a transformative, situated stance, putting a particular focus on context, which is important for bridging research and practice. An elemental approach can complement such research with additional insights on individual learning  characteristics. Combining these two approaches in a synergistic manner can lead to  deeper understanding of learning phenomena (Nathan & Sawyer, 2022), and thus may potentially facilitate the development of more effective learning tools, activities, or policies—in a word, programs—that help ameliorate the gaps induced by SES. Previous literature  studying the long-reaching effects of SES  through an elemental lens does give us valuable knowledge, but these insights are seemingly underutilized in  systemic program development. SES can be comprised of various factors such as income, lev el of education, occupation, urbanicity, or ethnici ty (APA, 2023). This ‘multidimensional construct’ impacts educational outcomes, as well as cognitive skills a nd brain development (Hackman & Kraemer, 2020). Neurocognitive performance related to executive functions and neural activation during working memory tasks are influenced by differences in SES and appear to medi ate academic achievement (Merz et al., 2019). Brain structures such as areas in the frontal and temporal lobes or the hippocampus and the structural integrity of white matter are also related with both SES and academic achievement (Noble et al., 2015). However, as Hackman and Kraemer (2020) point out, integrating such knowledge into actual programs in education is still in its infancy.  This may be due to the systemic properties of devel oping a socially embedded program. Delineating which aspects of the research can be applied in equity-oriented programs, and what limitations prevent possible integration, is c rucial for the synergistic combination of this knowledge with systemic program design.

The current study  examines the status quo of neuroscience research aimed at mitigating the effects of SES, to find possibilities for implementation and tens ions which prevent it. This can provide implication s for meaningful educational programs to ameliorate the effects of SES by elucidating how research can inform these programs and the limitations that need to be addressed. Our study thus explores the following research questions:

1) What are the implications of neuroscience studies for educational programs aimed at improving equity?

2) What are the difficulties of utilizing neuroscience studies in educational programs to reduce achievement

gaps?

## Data collection: Literature review and interviews

A review was conducted on journal articles that studied the educational achievement gap using ideas or methods from the field of neuroscience. Keywords in five categories, (a) neuroscience (‘neuroscience’, ‘brain’), (b) equity issues (‘gap’, ‘underachievement’, ‘inequality’, ‘i nequity’), (c) education (‘education’, ‘school’, ‘l earning’), (d) achievement (‘achievement’, ‘test’), and (e) socioe conomic status (‘family’, ‘SES’, ‘parents’) were us ed to perform database searches on SCOPUS and the Web of Science. Our search was limited to peer-reviewed journal articles published between 2004~2023 in English. Twenty papers were selected through a screening process from January to June 2023. To be eligible for our analysis, the paper had to have: (a) applied neuroscience research for its research questions, methods, or results; (b) focused on the influence of socioeconomic status on the education achievement gap or methods to reduce the gap; or (c) explored the influence of socioeconomic status on the brain. Our research is grounded in the context of South Ko rea; as such, to delve more deeply into the implications and limitations of neuroscience studie s for educational equity programs  in South Korea,  we interviewed 10 scholars familiar with the Korean context and affiliated with fields spanning educational sociology toneuroscience. 7 experts were formally trained in cognitive neuroscience, while 6 had a background in education. 3 scholars identified as scholars in the field of educational neuroscience. Hour-long semi-structured interviews were conducted between April 2023 and April 2024; experts were asked questions on the relationship between neuroscience and education research regarding the topic of achievement gaps. A particular focus was to elucidate how neuroscience research may inform educational programs aimed at achieving equity. Experts provided various examples from Korea and abroad, and additional relevant literature was reviewed per their recommendation. Analysis of literature and interviews The results of our literature review and interviews  were first analyzed separately, then synthesized i nto themes according to our two research questions. Interviews were coded using an open-coding based thematic analysis method, by first labeling segments of each interview, then grouping labels into subcategories  (Braun & Clarke, 2006). For instance, segments labeled ‘effectiveness of programs ’ and ‘evaluation using brain studies ’ were grouped into the theme ‘evaluating programs ’. The literature was also analyzed by determining the  type of program implemented in the study and the knowledge base that informed those programs, then connecting the literature with the themes acquired from the interv iews. Segments and themes were created for each research question; as a result, the themes ‘basis for equity programs ’, ‘informing program development’, and ‘evaluating programs’ were generated for the first research question, whe reas the themes ‘lack of connection with learning theories’, ‘limitations in context’, and ‘data collection issues’ were created for the second question. The coding and interpretation of the data was reviewed by all the authors.

## Implications of neuroscience research for educational programs

Basis for equity programs Neuroscience studies can provide the background for equity-enhancing programs by highlighting their necessity and providing scientific evidence for effective learnin g. Two studies related to developmental neuroscienc e research showed that environmental differences, inc luding socioeconomic factors, may lead to differences in neural development, which then incur lasting effect s on school readiness and academic achievement. The se disparities can appear early on, speaking to the ne cessity of programs that diminish such gaps. Experts also emphasized drawing upon knowledge about  learning acquired  from neuroscience studies to heighten the effectiveness of these programs; three experts mentioned that evidence from resear ch on marginalized groups’ brains provide s implications for mitigating the effects of marginal ization. Another scholar suggested that longitudinal database construction and use could enhance our understanding of the connections between biological and social factors, and thus help us provide better programs for vulnerable students. Informing program development Research in neuroscience also appears to assist the development of equity programs. The extant research can inform policies targeting varied actors: students, parents, and teachers. Two studies described programs meant for kindergarten students, effective for enhancing exec utive function and hopefully school readiness. Othe r studies targeted students in middle or high school, focusin g on music education. Some programs were meant to provide support for  parents. One interviewee, citing examples from teac her education  in Australia, also argued the necessity of teacher-oriented programs that help teachers implement strategies based on neuroscience research. Studies and experts also mentioned several areas that can be targeted with equity programs. Neuroscience research tended to focus on certain cognitive aspec ts, with two studies targeting executive function, another retrieval practice, and another growth min dset. Differing from the neuroscience papers on intervent ions for lessening SES gaps, the researchers we interviewed emphasized the impor tance of programs aiming to improve the literacy, numeracy, and socioemotional skills of disadvantaged students. Some experts explained that  these topics are especially pertinent to contexts such as South Korea, citing widening disparities in basic academic skills and issues such as mental health or bullying, in which disadvantaged students are vulnerable. Evaluating programs Finally, neuroimaging technology can offer a means of probin g neural level changes that educational programs might bring, thus providing a method for program ev aluation. One expert affiliated with the learning sciences mentioned the example of a participatory research program involving teachers, parents, students, and researchers in Canada, which showed brain images to elucidate t he effects of an educational program.  Neuroscience, more specifically neuroimaging techniques, can thus prov ide methods to visualize the effects of a program, perhaps even picking up on effects that would not be visible through other measurement techniques (Farah, 2018). Difficulties of applying neuroscience research to educational programs Lack of connection with learning theories The current neuroscience literature, though it often  attempts to provide methods for alleviating educat ional challenges, lacks connection with learning theories, particul arly theories that follow a sociocultural, situated perspective. All the studies we reviewed followed a n elemental, individualistic approach rather than a  systemic one—which, while providing rich understanding on principles of learning, may need to be connected with situated theories when being applied to contextual, complex educational programs aimed at equity. Several studies provided a detailed description of the participants  of the study, including sociodemographic informati on or poverty levels; however, they did not elucidate participants’ experiences regarding SES, often omitting important contexts that may be relevant to educational settings such as family environment or cultural norms. Theories that underpinned these studies  were also mostly related to cognitive skills; conversely, education experts mentioned the importance of using theories grounded in sociocultural perspectives to develop programs for equity.

## Limitations in context

Applicability in context also seems to be a struggl e when attempting to utilize  neuroscience research  for educational programs to reduce the achievement gap. Each study mentioned the limited generalizability  of its results in either its discussion or conclusion, a concern echoed by resea rchers from all fields. One neuroscientist explained that experimental studies consider only a very limited aspect of the context, which may not make their results applicable in real-world settings. From the perspective of psychology and cognitive neuroscience, another researcher claimed that the results of some studies  could have been due to the act of practicing the experimental task. In other words, the studies may show a “near effe ct” but may not have “far effects”  that transfer to other contexts. Another science of learning researcher described the difficulty of a process that she called “translation”, stressing the importance of making science relevant  for solving real world problems. Transferability t o concrete educational contexts, therefore, seems to be a key issue and limitation of current neuroscience studies.

## Data collection issues

Difficulties involving data collection appear to be a persistent hurdle when attempting to conduct research. Neuroscientists with experience collecting brain imaging data mentioned the high costs involved, particularly for functional magnetic resonance imaging(fMRI). The exorbitant costs of fMRI, which are high per participant and even more expensive when hiring  a technician on the weekends, bar the use of this technology in the education sector. In addition, several scholars pointed out t hat most neuroscience-related data is collected in limited areas, in certain (mostly Euro-or Anglo-centric) cultural contexts. A lack of systematic data collection and aggregation of prior data could thus be a limitation to utilizing neuroscience research in an East Asian context.

## Discussion

Educational programs and strategies to lessen achievement gaps can be informed by both education and neuroscience research; yet there are dissimilaritie s in the approaches of these two fields that make i t difficult to implement neuroscience research into educational pr ograms. The difference s between elemental and systemic viewpoints (Nathan & Sawyer, 20 22), in other words tensions between cognitive and so ciocultural perspectives (Danish & Gresalfi, 2018), seem to be the driving force behind the difficulties shown in this study. Because equity is a value-laden area deeply rooted in society, attempts to decrease the achievement gap need to consider situated, sociocultural theories to analyze the context in wh ich the program is to be implemented and design the  program accordingly. Yet neuroscience can also provide valuable information for these programs, as evidenced by various literature and the interviews presented in this stu dy. Then how might the elemental approaches of neur oscience be integrated with systemic viewpoints necessary in educational programs for equity? Perhaps the field of learning sciences could ameliorate the difficulties of integ ration outlined in this paper with further discussi on on communication between fields as well as methodological approaches such as those suggested in Nathan & Sawyer(2022). Further research highlighting the voices of experts in educ ational neuroscience, along with scholars and practitioners of various related disciplines, also seems necessary. Educational programs for equity could be a critical  area in which education and neuroscience can come together to bridge gaps in research and practice, o r elemental and systemic approaches. Utilizing rese arch from neuroscience, yet drawing from sociocultural learni ng theories, we can propose ecological programs con necting families, schools, and society—for instance programs promoting supportive environments for students both within and outside of school.  Educational neuroscience researchers already use various forms of data to investigate multiple levels of learning, and emphasize a long-term approach carefully connecting neuroscience fin dings to psychological constructs and classroom applications (Howard-Jones et al., 2016). These approaches can be taken further in the learning sciences; m ethods such as participatory design or design-based research can embed neuroscience and education research in concrete con texts when designing these programs, by adjusting both approaches in the given sociocultural situation, iteratively applying neuroscience knowledge and observing longterm effects, and enabling the collection of relevant, real-life data. In this way, it might be possible to connect cognitive and sociocultural views to create ‘synerg y in design’  (Danish & Gresalfi, 2018).  Such synergy could facilitate the implementation of neuroscience resea rch into educational programs in a n ecological manner that works to close the achievement gap effectively, rather than merely applying limited patches.
