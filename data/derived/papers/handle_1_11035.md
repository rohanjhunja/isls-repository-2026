# “Trust me, this is gonna be something”: Agency Through Un/Learning with Materials in Arts Integration

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Offering choice is not enough to support student agency, because we co-create with materials (Hood & Kraehe, 2017). This qualitative study used a materialist lens to understand how teaching artists supported agency through material engagement within arts integration. We found teaching artists expanded student agency by 1) facilitating material exploration as a pathway to un/learn material relationships toward perceptual expansion, and 2) using varied practices for reorganizing and troubling time to reshape activity and material relationships.

## Introduction and framework

Research on arts integration has assumed that teachers merely offering choice is enough to cultivate student agency. A more recent line of research in arts education, however,  has used a materialist lens to shift from theorizing agency as a social phenomenon to recogni zing how agency is co-constituted with socio-material conditions (Barad, 2007). This means that we can understand agency as a dynam ic, emergent process co-constituted through intra-actions between humans and materials (Barad, 2007). Art can be understood “as ways of being and ways of knowing” with materials (Kind,  2018). How teachers facilitate instruction can constrain or expand students’ “thinking with” materials  and knowledge-making practices; for example, a pencil can be perceived exclusively as  an extension of the body used to write, but it can also become an instrument to create rhythm or an armature for a sculpture. The expansive making practices in the arts offers unique opportunities for students to perceive material, multimodal, and sensorial dimensions of l earning that can require  un/learning perceptual and temporal constraints (Lewis, 2018). Yet, there is a lack of research on understanding a gency with materials in K-12 art settings. We use un/learning to describe  awareness of these ongoing differences in knowledge-making practices that can be understood through intra-actions with materials (Barad, 2007). The context of this study was a social design-based experiment (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016). The researcher and Chicago Partnerships in Education’s (CAPE) staff co-designed professional development and research activities focused on exploring the concep t of materialism with a cohort of teachers and teaching artists in 2022-2023. Classroom teachers and teaching artists designed and implemented inquiry-based arts-integrated curricula in a public school district. Through years-long inquiry, one main research ques tion was derived collectively: 1) How do teaching artists support students’ material engagement in ways that expand agency?

## Methods

This multiple case study (Stake, 1995)  used thematic coding to describe how four teaching artists  supported agency. Data collection included teaching artist interviews, member check convenings, and digital portfolios. Data analysis (see Table 1) involved one pass with provisional codes based on key areas from the literature review (un/learning and perceptual expansion) then two passes creating sub-codes using theming to integrate larger ideas, process coding to label conceptual actions, and in-vivo coding for striking language (Miles et al., 2020). ## Findings

Perceptual expansion: Infusing multiplicity and reframing material relationships Teaching artists supported perceptual expansion by 1) infusing multiplicity into knowledge-making practices and

2) reframing material relationships. In collaboration with classroom teachers, teaching artists introduced different

ways of learning with materials. In one project, students moved through un/learning their relationship to trash by collecting, reimagining, and restorying their own trash to make wearable sculptures and a dance designed around the water cycle  and ethics of waste. The teaching artist shared, “At first there's a resistance … some of the kids were actually like, ‘well, it's just trash’… And th en they start a cascade of al l these different possibilities … it's about the relationship with the materials and whate ver that might  be for them ” (Interview). The teaching artist facilitated students’  multimodal inquiry with materials connecting personal meaning and ecology through sculpture and dance. In another project, the teaching artist guided students to un/learn traditional representational practices by beginning with what is known with materials; for example, watercolors intra-act with time and the body through pause and pressure in ways that invite looseness, expression, and transmutation of emotion in music and poetry. The teaching artist described how this process created an accessible pathway to poetry, painting, and music: “Moving your hand across the page and seeing what happens … and that being the starting point rather than knowledge about the text, knowledge about poetry, knowledge about painting” (Interview). Teaching artists supported student agency to know with materials by expanding perceptions by focusing  on multiplicity and reframing material relationships.

Troubling time: Time to not “get it right” and to “make it their own” Teaching artists troubled timed by 1) reorganizing how time shapes activity t oward not “getting it right” and 2) facilitating extended time for material intra-actions for students to make projects “their own” through co-creative relationships with materials. In schools, efficiency and correctness are privileged, leaving not “enough time” for much else. We found that teaching artists restructured time as an important material for creating conditions to cultivate agency. Teaching artists provided  support for students to  engage in inquiry without constraints of “getting it right” and expediency. One teaching artist removed rigid time limits for completing project components to reduce a focus on expediency and expand material exploration so students could “make it their own.” One used short time limits, making it  impossible for students to achieve preconceived ideas of correctness. Lastly, one lengthened the time of material exploration by enga ging in repetitive practices to deepen students’ relationships to materials. Extended time for material engagement led to individualized inquiry processes for students to “make it their own.” For example, one teaching artist shared: “Once they find their hook … They're not questioning themselves as much because they have their own inte rnal vision there at that point, they are in their own time … They are 100. They are now” (Interview).

## Discussion

Building on studies connecting materialism and arts learning, this research contributes examples of how to support student agency and expansive ways of knowing through reframing material relationships and reorganizing time.
