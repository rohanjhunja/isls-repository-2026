# Growing into Collective Forms of Scientific Inquiry: The Dignifying Affirmation of Timid, Half-baked Ideas

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper we look at an informal science learning environment, the Choreographing Science Camp, where middle school youth, scientists, and choreographers engaged in scientific inquiry together. We attend to how timid, quiet idea proposals by youth were taken up by the group in dignity affirming ways that led to collective wonderings, creating space for the group to grow into their half-baked ideas and questions together.

## Motivation

Affect is a central part of science learning for bo th youth (Appleby et al., 2021; Keifert & Stevens, 2019) and scientists (Jabar & Hammer, 2015). When affect is t he center of analysis for learning, oftentimes loud  and energetic forms of participation are foregrounded. While high energy forms of participation can be generative and aspirational forms of participating for learning (Vea, 2020), this paper focuses on smaller, quieter forms of affect. Specifically, we focus on how these quieter affective stances get taken up in collective scientific inquiry. Authentic scientific inquiry involves contending wi th feelings of uncertainty, which can be uncomfortable, commonly provoke anxiety (Jabar & Ha mmer, 2015), and manifest in different affective st ances (Keifert & Stevens, 2019). Both scientists and youth sit in uncertainty as an important part of engaging in scientific inquiry (Phillips, Watkins, & Hammer, 2018). In traditional learning environments, not-knowing can be a liability, a state to be quickly resolved to knowing, even tho ugh instances of not-knowing can be generative for initiating and sustaining collective scientific inquiry (Watki ns et al., 2018). Thus, it is important to attend t o how ways of not-knowing and nascent ideas youth contribute can be taken up in science learning environments, because all too often they are quickly diminished when they can support important insights.

In this paper we look at an interdisciplinary and intergenerational informal science learning environment, the Choreographing Science Camp, where middle schoo l youth, scientists, and choreographers engaged in scientific inquiry together. We attend to how timid and quiet idea proposals by youth were taken up by the group in ways that affirmed youth participants’ dignity and led to collective wonderings, creating space for the group to grow into their half-baked ideas and questions together.

## Theoretical framework

Scientific and thoreographic research as inquiry In this work, we emphasize the socially situated na ture of scientific inquiry as an attunement to unce rtainty in question development through choreographic inquiry.  Rigorous choreographic inquiry can generate new questions, answers, and science ideas, and positioning movement as inquiry can expand learners ’ access to scientific sensemaking resources (Champion, 2018). We draw on post-modern improvisational traditions of choreographic inquiry known as physical research in which new ideas are developed through  the reciprocity of dancers creating physical proposals and responding to each others’ movement ideas with their own bodie s (Vogelstein, 2022). We also position dance as an in herently expressive and embodied art form that inco rporates ideas, movement, and affect into first person exper iences and reciprocity between dancers. It is a kin esthetic, cognitive, and affective way of knowing similar to the embodiment scientists leverage in their thinking (Meyers, 2012). Hence, a dance perspective attunes us to a r ange of expressible affects, and in particular, to those quieter and smaller forms of affect that may be missed in other contexts and inquiry practices. Affect, learning, and collective action We take up sociocultural and interactional work on affect that demonstrates the relations between affe ct, embodiment, learning, and collective action (Goodwi n & Goodwin, 2000; Vea, 2022) as relevant for scien ce learning. Based in methods of microanalysis of interaction, we draw on the Goodwins’s (2000) conceptualization of affect as the performance of stance embedded in larger sequences of action. Attending both to how interlocutors’ responses involve changes in intonat ion, gesture, body posture and timing and what they are responding to analytically foregrounds how affect is consequential to the social negotiation of “mutual orientation and action” (Ibid, p. 25). This is connected to the concept of emotional configurations (Vea, 2022), in which social actors negotiate the relationship between feelings, sense-making, and practice towards goals for collective action.

In this paper we illustrate how iteratively responding to timid affective stances and half-baked ideas verbally and choreographically led the group to “grow into” collective forms of scientific inquiry in ways that affirmed youth’s dignity (Espinoza et al., 2020).

## Context and methods

Choreographing Science is a design-based research project focused on engaging scientis ts and youth in choreographic and agent-based modeling inquiry by foregrounding embodiment in the process of scientific discovery. Our first iteration of the project took place in the Summer of 2022 as a two-week full-day camp where ten middle schoolers, two scientists, three choreog raphers, and three learning scientists came togethe r at a local theater to engage in embodied and creative scientif ic inquiry together. The structure of camp foregrou nded the use of choreographic tools to raise and explore que stions about the scientists’ research topics: the b iomechanics of walking and cellular repair of spinal cord injur y. These tools included, generating multiple physic al representations of ideas, creating improvisational choreography through sets of rules to follow, and breaking down complex systems into enactable rules and aggregate patterns. This paper focuses on a hotspot (Jordan & Henderson, 1995)—identified using content logs and analytic memos—that supported one of our design conjectures: youth-generated ideas would lead to scientific questions and choreographic explorations. In this episode, Da nielle (a youth participant) offered a suggestion f or thinking about the biomechanics of walking, “like a puppet.” Her quiet and timid affective stance was met with collective engagement in developing ideas about marionettes, l eading to an extended period of choreographic research and the generation of ideas about how signals are sent and interpreted in the body. Figure 1 traces cycles of proposals, responses, and emergent wonderings that began with Danielle’s half-baked idea, “like a puppet” (Figure 1.1) which led to a conversation about marionettes (Figu re 1.2-6), and deepening during their choreographic explorations (thickening spirals in Figure 1). We used interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to examine micro-interactional verbal and phy sical affect in the context of the group’s sensemak ing around the idea of a marionette as a way to explore biomechanics.

## Findings

Our findings show that attending and responding to quieter proposals sparked wonderings that were responded to verbally and choreographically in ways that affirme d youth participants’ ideas, questions, and feeling s. Tracing cycles of proposals, responses, and emergent wonder ings illuminated how the idea of “like a puppet” gr ew into scientific inquiry amongst middle school youth, sci entists, and choreographers in the domain of biomec hanics (Figure 1). This analysis demonstrates how (1) timid utterances of "half-baked" ideas drove collective inquiry; (2) choreographic tools provided a pathway to engage physically, cognitively, and affectively with science ideas and new questions; and (3) youth experienced this space as dignity-affirming. Getting into scientific inquiry: Responding to timid half-baked ideas to spark inquiry There were moments during the camp when youth enthusiasm was loud and highly energetic, and there were other moments when the affect of their participation was far more subtle. These subtle and quieter moments, which could have easily been interpreted as moments of lo w interest or little investment, presented opportun ities for generative idea exploration, and att uning to and responding to these subtler affective stances allowed for generative idea building. We argue that engaging in  uncertainty in scientific inquiry often entails ge ntly and timidly sharing a half-baked idea, the beginning of an idea, with an uncer tainty to what might come of it. We demonstrate that these seeds of ideas can only be understood if they are taken up respectfully and seriously. On the fourth day of camp, Dionne asked the group t o try and come up with new ways of modeling partner scientists’ science topics by starting with  ideas about spinal cord injuries. Danielle, a midd le schooler, responded by offering a "half-baked" idea for the other topic, biomechanics. Danielle raised her arm from the elbow and moved it as if it was swinging from a hinge while she shared her half-baked idea, "Uhhh like a puppet," (Figure 1.1). Her proposal that biomechanics was re lated to the idea of “like a puppet,” was an initial inkling spoken quietly and with an intonation that rose at the end like a question: “like a puppet?” Not yet ready to express a fully formed sentence, she began with “uhh” and u sed gestures to help formulate her idea. When Dionne asked her to clarify, Danielle elaborated by swinging her  arm once again from the elbow and adding thoughts about Pinocchio’s joints and whether his strings were rel evant. In this moment, Danielle grappled with how t o connect her idea to the science topic, and once she started, we began to grapple together. Dionne responded to Danielle’s proposal by getting out a large post-it to write this idea down, then asked Danielle if she could elaborate more about what she meant, and led the entire group in a 10-minute discussion of how a puppet might be related to the  biomechanics of walking (Figure 1.2). Nate, the sci entist who studied this topic, responded excitedly by sharing how he'd want ed to make a marionette for years because its strin gs could provide a generative way to think about the biomechanics of walking. Through this discussion the group came to wonder what the strings on a marionette might actua lly represent: bones, muscles, neurons, signals (Fi gure 1.3). As the ideas about marionettes as representations f or biomechanics in the body developed, Brianna ver y quietly asked, “what’s a marionette?” About 10 minutes into  the group discussion, Brianna’s quiet question act ed as a form of interactional repair, revealing that mutual  orientation to the concept of a marionette was sti ll in development. Responding to Brianna with both a quic k verbal definition and video (cakesinorbit, 2019) allowed unquestioned assumptions about what a marionette wa s and could do to be opened in our inquiry. Her que stion was taken seriously, affirming the dignity of the y oung participant, and the video led tonew ideas fo r how the whole group could explore this "half-baked" idea through dance/movement. Growing into scientific inquiry: Choreographically creating spaces for ideas to grow In response to the video Brianna’s question encoura ged us to watch, the choreographers proposed a move ment research activity: marionette duets. Paired as a puppet and puppeteer, duos faced each other and the puppeteer had to move the puppet without touching them. Since the re are many ways to respond to any given movement proposal, puppets' responses did not always match puppeteers’ intentions. For example, a puppeteer might create a proposal by flicking their wrist towards a puppet ’s right arm and the puppet could respond to such a  flick by moving their right arm in a way that matches the quality of a sharp wrist flick or lightly floating their arm up as a response to the gentle breeze the wrist flick creat ed. These duets were a form of choreographic resear ch that necessitated participants’ creative embodied, felt responses to each  others’ physical ideas. In this activity everyone's ideas were taken up and respected as puppets and puppeteers responded to each other. Through three cycles of duet research, youth played with many different ideas, with even the smallest of ideas being taken up and responded to. We observed participants developing fluidity and confidence in making proposals and crafting responses, growing into inquiry and affect. Participants initially com mented on the difficulty of this task during the pauses between p hysically proposing and responding. Over time, thes e pauses shortened and the kinds of movements proposed and r esponded to become more complex and compositionally compelling by varying the parts of the body, moveme nt textures, qualities, size and levels enacted. We  interpret the increase in fluidity between proposals and responses as a sign of increased confidence of participants. In figure 1, the spiral during this choreographic research thick ens, emphasizing the many cycles of proposals, resp onses, and wonderings about biomechanics that were generated and started to grow. Ultimately, new ideas about biomechanics were gener ated by co-developing feelings of surprise, difficulty, and wonder. It was challenging for pupp eteers to come up with new ideas for how to manipul ate their puppets and it was, at times, frustrating and surpr ising when the puppets responded in unanticipated w ays. For example, at one point Danielle was trying to get her puppet to slide her foot backwards to start walking backwards, but instead the puppet lifted her foot upwards into an arabesque-like position. Puppets sometimes found it hard to know how to respond to their puppeteer's proposals when there was uncertainty in the clarity of a pupp eteer’s proposed movement. This puppets’ uncertainty and pu ppeteers’ frustration and surprise raised questions  about variability in biomechanics: What does it mean for signals to be intentionally sent throughout the bod y and not always interpreted in intended ways? How are bodies set up to respond to these signals?

## Discussion and conclusions: The power of half-baked ideas for group inquiry

Attending to the nascent ideas of youth in this exp loratory science space created opportunities for co llective inquiry and facilitated prolonged engagement and co mplex thinking about the biomechanics of walking. I t also created a dignity-affirming space where responding to and taking up youth’s half-baked ideas, whether verbal or physical, was as a sign of respect for those ideas and for the youth who shared them. In sharing their  ideas and questions, Danielle and Brianna exercised a form of agency from which we infer that they felt this space was one in which their thinking mattered. The discussion and the choreographic activity that followed positioned all youth as valid and important contributors to the collecti ve investigation, and youth stated in post-interviews that they felt their ideas were valued and heard. Our marionette-biomechanics inquiry continued to develop over the course of the camp and became an important part of the bio mechanics inquiry. Youth mentioned the marionette i nquiry in their developing questions and understandings of how the brain, body, and spine are in conversation with each other, and Nate now includes a marionette activity in his undergraduate biomechanics course. This analysis demonstrates the importance of attuning to quieter forms of affect in youth participation in scientific inquiry and the power of half-baked ideas. While scientific inquiry entails negot iating uncertainty, it makes sense that some affective st ances in the face of such uncertainty would be quie t or timid. Thus, it is important to attend to these subtle forms of uncert ainty as seeds for ideas that can only gain epistem ic traction if and when they are treated with respect and given space to grow. This can be done by responding to learners’ timid and half-baked ideas both verbally and physically. We demons trated how extended choreographic research gave space for a group to grow into the generativity of these ideas. Connecting the expressiveness and reciprocity in choreographic research to scientific inquiry expand ed opportunities for authentic scientific inquiry b y honoring and affirming youth’s ideas and questions as an instrumental part of the science itself.
