# When Transgression Becomes Developmental – Ethical-Political Empowerment Through Expansive Learning and Transformative Agency

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning in times of climate change and ongoing inequality, requires ethical-political engagements that are transgressive of unsustainable norms, including those left in coloniality’s wake (Lotz-Sisitka et al., 2017; 2023). However, a question arises as to when such transgressions become developmental (cf. Williams et al., 2018). This symposium addresses this question through analysis of five formative interventions (cf. Engeström et al., 2014). The studies advance a decolonial dialectic of transformation, enacted as a process of expansive, transgressive learning and emancipatory agency from below, grounded and expanding from living heritage and place; reflexively returning to it. Here co-engagement in expansive learning actions strengthen African relationality (Ubuntu relations), and collective (emancipatory) transformative agency (cf. Engeström and Sannino, 2010; 2021; Lotz-Sisitka et al., 2023). Studies emphasise ethical-political expansion of the object of activity in expansive learning struggles to address environmental degradation, gender and racial discrimination, and exclusions of living heritage, youth, and indigenous cultures.

Paper 1: Exploring the dialectics of transformation and transgressive learn ing in an environmental youth commons expansive learning formative intervention Heila Lotz-Sisitka, Maletje Mponwana

## Introduction and context

This study focusses on an exploration of the dialecti cs of transformation and transgressive learning in an environmental commons expansive learning formative intervention s panning a number of years, and involving multiple formative interventionist researchers  (Lotz-Sisitka et al., 2021; 2024). The study particularly seeks to address exclusions of youth in the co-construction, and recognition of viable workstreams and learning pathways that recognize their environmental citizenship and transformative agency potential for river health mo nitoring, agro-ecological food production and participation in the  circular economy, and other environmental commons work necessary for decolonizing the skills system to be more inclusive of the excluded and marginalised. Exclusion of  youth in the formal education and learning system a nd in systems of work has been recognized as a critical and unsustainable problem in South Africa. Numerous studies describe the processes of marginalization, and their cultural and political h istories that stem from histories of discrimination  and poor quality education – a legacy of colonial and apartheid educational discrimination and exclusions. Nowadays there are numerous interventions to provide ‘opportunities’ for youth, but most of these are short term, ad hoc and lack substance and permanent benefits, as the economy is shrinking and not enough jobs exist to absorb youth, and not enough time and attention is given to the co-defining of these futures by youth (Du Toit et al.,  2018; Marock, 2008). This conundrum drives a need for transgressing boundaries holding this unsustainable situation in place. At the same time the country is experiencing many unsustainable patterns of environmental degradation, with risk and damage escalation from climate change  impacts becoming more severe and extreme in scale and impact. There is also a high level of food inse curity, food production promotion remains large-scale and monoculture dominated, and the waste reduction / circular economy is as yet un der-developed. Rehabilitation processes amongst other environmental commons practices  are also under-developed. Thus, there is scope for including youth in environmental commons innovation s that can potentially transgress the unsustainable  norms and realities sketched out above.

## Method: Formative intervention processes

In seeking to address the above conundrum and contra diction, this study was established as a form of fourth generation activity theory research (Engeström and Sannino, 2021).  Starting in 2019, the first phase of the study worked with multi-actor partners in the river health commons context to identify activity systems involved in the scaling of river health monitoring through youth em ployment programmes, citizen science tool s and associated ‘learn for the future’ programme for youth. Three h eterogeneous coalitions  (Engeström and Sannino, 2021) formed including 1) an NGO and university mediating tools development c oalition (focusing on citizen science tools and learning programme development and accred itation), 2) a policy system coalition (focusing on policy and leveraging resources for youth employment and t raining programmes using citizen science tools), and 3) a river health monitoring coalition made up of organisations engaged in this practice. A series of change laboratory type workshops (Sannino and Engeström, 2017) were held across these heterogenous coalitions, who agreed to collaborate in support of the primary activity system, namely ‘Enviro Champs’ (ECs) or youth who are employed part time to undertake river health monitoring  and other environmental commons activities. This led to employment of 800 young people, and development of a first set of training programmes, and improvement s in citizen science tools. But it also surfaced the depth of the complex contradiction that exists between the desire for inclusion and the realities of exclusion and precar ity, all of which  need ongoing engagement  by all of the coalitions.

Since 2022 these coalitions have continued to opera te as they seek to reconcile a policy contradiction between participation in water quality monitoring and a lack of inclusive strategy to address this. Their work has expanded in scope. In our research, we regularly capture map out emergence of transformative agency, expansion of the object and signs of ethical political transgressions in the coalitions’ work. For example, data shows that the mediating tools coalition now includes new partners  and resources to develop online courses for youth that have been enriched by operational research to examine yo uth-led EC learning pathways from their vantage points, living heritages, knowledges, aspirations and exper ience. A number of formative intervention change la boratory workshops have taken place with this group, and new  tools have been developed to support expansive l earning with youth groups, not only in river health monitor ing, but also in agro-ecological food production, and waste economy activity development.  The policy coalition  has also expanded, with more funding being release d for youth employment, with over 2000 youth now having b een part time employed through the social employment fund to undertake ‘green work’ and at the same time  to engage in the emerging ‘learn for the future’ l earning programme, but still within the complex contradiction of inclusion – exclusion noted above. The river health coalition group has also expanded to include more organisations and mentors in NGOs and in informal community mentoring set ups, and the focus of activity has similarly expanded to include activities such as waste management and rehabilitation, with m ore workstreams becoming visible, and more demand for EC involvement and inclusion, yet the employment si tuation of the ECs remains and is indeed (paradoxic ally) becoming more precarious. Change laboratory worksho ps and more general meetings across these coalitions are still occurring, but increasingly smaller groups wi thin the larger system of networked activity are engaging in expansive learning processes which are shared via v arious forms of interaction, including through onli ne forums and networking tools, meeting records and shared site visits.  The change laboratory workshops now also involve a larger group of formative intervention researchers, who are key to mediating the cross coalition links with ‘nexus building’ double stimulation tools being produced and used. Analysis and results: The key double stimulation tools, processes, workshops and key agentive actions emerging are documented, and form the focus of various project reports (Lotz-Sisitka et al., 2021; 2024), and are also the subject of a PhD study (Mponwana 2, in pr ocess). The PhD study is particularly focusing in on the central contradiction between inclusion intent, and paradoxical exclusion, and ho w this is generating various ethical political dilemma’s and expansions of the object from the vantage point of the primary activity system in focus, the Enviro Champs, and associated heterogeneous coalitions working on the partially shared object. This work is core to framing the decolonizing dialectic of transformation that we ar e interested in and for reflexively reviewing the w ork of the heterogeneous coalitions. For example, the mediating tool coalition is researching its prac tices related to the dialectic of on-site and online learning tools  and meaningful learning for ECs, and is questioning how to do this to address the core contradiction more substantivel y. The policy coalitions are also becoming more reflexi ve of problems around short termism of job opportunities, showing some transgression of the unsustainable norms and practices noted above, and ECs themselves are finding the need to engage  community members in their own struggles for transformative change. All of this becomes visible in the transformative agency actions that have led to various temporal and ethical-political expansions of the object of activity so far. What appears to be significant is that overall, the dialectic of transformation  in all of the heterogenous coalitions’ work, is motivated by the need to transgress the ethical political conundrums of excluded youth, and escalating environmental degradation  and food insecurity, and the complex contradiction that exists around inadequate forms of employment and recognition of v iable environmental commons work.  In short, evidence of ethical-political transgressions in the object of activity are revealing the ongoing decolonial imperative in expansive learning struggles where deep seated contradictions characterize the context of praxis and co-learning. A variety of forms of co-engaged transformative agency are possible and emer ging in the various heterogeneous coalitions work, but overall the struggle for resolution of this complex, wicked problem continues. Paper 2:  Expanding capabilities for sustainable livelihoods and empowerment: Ethical political expansions of activity in a rural women farmers group in South Africa Laura Conder-Aller

## Introduction and context

Also addressing wicked problems left in the wake of coloniality, this study examines the transformations emergent from an expansive learning (Engeström, 2015; 2016) process with a rural African women farmers group focusing in on their food production capability expansion and empowerment and the well-being of their local catchment or landscape. The study took place in the Lutengele vi llages along the upper reaches of the lower Mzimvub u Catchment near Port St Johns in the Eastern Cape pr ovince of South Africa. Women in southern Africa ar e significant contributors to household livelihoods t hrough their household food production practices and at the same time they are also one of the primary natural resource users in rural landscapes, and they also remain some of the most marginalized citizens in the formal eco nomy. H istorical and contemporary ethnographic and situational data revealed disjuncture between exist ing practice and the fulfilment of women ’s aspirations with regard to food security and social and ecolo gical well-being at large. As a result, central to this study were the concepts of aspirations and capabilities and the ro le that these played in transformative, transgressive learning processes via formative intervention research  and expansive learning  (Engeström, 2015). The Capability Approach (CA) provided impetus to consider how such development i nterventions or initiatives can focus on “expanding the freedom that deprive people from enj oying their valued beings and doings” (Sen, 1999, p. 3), in other words, what the women value or have reason to value.

## Method: Formative intervention processes

The first phase of th is formative intervention (Engeström et al., 2014) study set out to map the context in w hich the rural women participants’ small-scale food production activity was situated, their aspirations relevant to sustainable agricultural livelihoods, food security, well-being and lastly, the main factors or contradiction s inhibiting the women from attaining the aspired food production goals  at household level  (using 2nd generation CHAT tools). The initial phase of the expansive learning cycle I foc used on tensions and contradictions in aspiration-practice relationships and how these shape household food (in)security of the women farmers’ group. Twelve contradictions were identified from the hist orical and contemporary socio-cultural analysis of the homebased food production practices and agricultural ac tivity in relation to the research participants’ en visaged aspirations, which under further s crutiny were thereafter considered by the participa nts as critical capabilities to pursue during the collective and transformative lea rning process in the second phase of the study. In the second phase of the study, a series of second stimuli were  introduced in the form of conceptual and material tools and tasks with the aim to move participants along the expansive learning process. This led to the unfolding of the rural women’s collectively defined Capability Learning Pathways f or sustainable food production in the local micro-catchment or landscape, as means of catalysing the decolonial dialectic of transformation referred to above. Analysis and results: Through the various Change Laboratory workshops (Sa nnino and Engeström, 2017) and supporting mini-cycles in the last stages of the formative interventionist  research, participants’ co-learning and development was supported in a way that not only brought individual s together to co-design relevant solutions, strategies and working groups or committees, but also catalysed and amplified transformative agency and the expansion of food production capability, sustainable land use practices and  ultimately material and ethical political expansions of the object that led to the women’s empowerment.

The Capability Approach coupled with CHAT  in this study  deepened the contextual understanding of the agricultural activity system in light of the engendered power dynamics associated with women having access to productive resources, their culturally expected roles and responsibilities in the institution of th eir households, their families and the community at large. Most importantly, ethical political aspects of gerontocracy defined by their age and status in society arose as a key concern to be navigated  and transgressed. The Capability Approach as a lens to view agricultural development, social transformation and empowerment, provided ethical-political analytical tools to conceptualise participants’ aspirations, t heir true value and the capabilities necessary for such aspirations to be realised in a context filled with  socio-cultural historicity, and political power relations and dynamics faced especially by women. This addressed the question of how expansive learning processes can shape conversion factors for turning available resources into functionings that enhance household food secur ity capabilities and ecological well-being, while also potentially transgressing ethical poli tical tensions identified. The methodology of expansive learning and formative interventionist research design, with supporting mediating tools, prov ided for a positive intervention in the attainment of capabili ties (or functionings) in relation to the participant’s aspired livelihoods. This helped to improve their well-being as well as their ability to navigate through the various gendered power dynamics  identified. Especially for the  young women participating  the process enabled them to transgress normalized relat ions of gerontocracy, and to form new more empowering relations, reflecting material and ethical political transformations of the object. Women were not only able to pursue attainment of ma terial and cognitive changes but they were also able to exercise greater ethical political freedom to exercise their agency-in other words, they had more space for living into the freedoms that they envisaged, and aspired to be, do and become. Through this process, rural women were more empowered to transgress unwanted and unsustainable oppressive no rms through transformation of their collective activity and soc ial relations, on their own terms, thus contributing to working out how to realise a decolonial dialectic of transf ormation through expansive learning with rural women in some of the most marginalized areas in the country.

## Introduction and context

Sometimes when you are unfortunate, they gang rape you. (CP GP interview, PH) This extract of data shows the ethical political dialectic of transformation needed in some women’s lives to escape the need to collect firewood for cooking in an incr easingly deforested landscape. The ‘Improved Cook S tove’ (ICS), a social-innovation technology, was introduced to respond to deforestation and scarcity  of firewood in Malawi, as it uses less wood, and has potential to reduce the risk to women noted above. But, this technology was not being taken up and used by rural women such as those in the extract above.  Why was this? And what could be done?

Drawing on the possibilities of a decolonial, femin ist dialectic of transformation amongst marginalise d women (similar to Conder-Aller’s study above), this study sought to investigate and expand learning within and between activity systems working with the improved cook stove (ICS) intervention in Malawi. The study focused on how existing learning interactions among  ICS actors can be expanded using expansive learning  processes, mobilised through Boundary Crossing Change Laboratories (BCCL)  (Sannino and Engeström, 2017) typical of 3rd generation CHAT studies, to potentially inform more  sustained uptake and utilisation of the ICS  technology.

The study was conducted in three climate change hot spot districts in Malawi: Balaka, Dedza and Mzimba, with specific case studies in each of the three administrative re gions of the country. Chapita Village case  study is in Balaka district, in the Southern region; Waziloya Makwakwa Village is in Mzimba district in the Northern region; and Chilije Village in Dedza district in the Central region. The focus of the research in these areas was on women’s learning and agency, as it relates to their inclusion in the socio-technical transition to ICS, their empowerment, and the relationship of this to climate change adaptation intentions.

## Method: Formative intervention processes

In order to engage the potential  for transformation in the study areas, the study was divided into two phases. Overall, the study worked with the methodological t heory of Expansive  Learning from CHAT (Engeström and Sannino, 2010; Engeström 2015; 2016) and Sannino’s (2015) theory of transformative agency through doub le stimulation which was emerging at the time (cf. San nino, 2022). The study aimed at in-depth understanding of uptake and utilisation of ICS  and associated contradictions impeding uptake and u se of ICS as a potential empowerment technology. The first phase involved collection  of ethnographic data to more deeply understand the context of the problem including existing learn ing approaches and historically shaped impediments to women’s agency (cf. quote above). This informed the  second phase, which focused on expansive learning processes in the  Chapita and Waziloya Makwakwa case studies. The stu dy used a formative intervention (Engeström et al., 2014) approach, which  focused on supporting the women, and other actors to manage the challenges they were facing and work out the proble matic situations in their joint activity. Using a dialectically generative and action-oriented approach, the study sought to co-generate solutions to contradictions with women using the ICS, and with associated actors who were supporting and supplying the ICS.  Through this app roach, the study was able to catalyse transformative outcomes (Jalasi, 2020).

## Analysis and results

The major findings of the study  from an ethical political perspective indicate that broadly, uptake and utilisation of ICS is problematic, hence  unsustained. The findings indicate that the majorit y of end-users in Chapita and Chilije case studies  switched between the traditionally used three stone fire (using more  wood) and ICS (using less wood), or abandoned the ICS, which was not the case in Waziloya Makwakwa study. The underlying causal mechanisms that appear to explain and influence end-users’ actions in all the case studies w as the search for convenience during the cooking activity  amongst women users, and associated political ethical motives for inclusion and safety. Further, findings  revealed that earlier / initial learning interactions between users and suppliers of the ICS were unidirectional, and top-down. The top-down approaches were practiced by  suppliers of ICS, NGOs mediating ICS into use, and funding organisations, showing exclusion of women users in the co-construction of the socio-technical innovation; again reflecting a contradiction of inclusion and exclusion. Early findings of the study indicated that most of  the learning taking place in ICS implementation introduced by disseminators was informative, not transformative. It was also in adequate, particularly for  the women end-users.  Ethical political transgressions were needed as the  main factor shaping these exclusions was poverty, and a tendency of rural women to rely on donor-driven projects, which produces ethical political a nd material contradictions that need transgressing. T he dialectical processes of tran sformation, shaped by changeoriented and transgressive expansive  learning processes  in the formative interventions, as carried out in t he Chapita and Waziloya Makwakwa case studies,  expanded learning interactions among ICS actors, and evoked and support ed their transformative agency and enhanc ed their reflexivity (Jalasi, 2020). These processes were identified as being crucial in development and in sustaining learning and change in the uptake  and utilisation of ICS innovation, and in addressing associated ethical political challenges such as the dominance of top down power relations in donor driven projects, and gender based violence vulnerabilities noted above. Overall, the study found out that it was possible, but not easy to expand ethical pol itical dimensions of the object of activity amongst interacting activity systems.

Important to understanding the decolonial dialectic  of transformation noted above, the study shows tha t putting the agency and empowerment of the (most marginalised) end-user (the woman who cooks on the stove) in the centre of socio-technical transitions through context-based problem resolution and rigorous and deliberately mediated processes of participation and learning can catalyse transformative agency from below.  Such processes must allow for multi-voicedness and must take power relations into account, and explicitly work to catalyse transformative agency, reflexivity, collaboration and learning capacity of those most marginalised, and other ICS actors for more sustained uptake and utilisation of the ICS socio-technical innovation. This can help to address the ethical political challenges experienced by such women, and environmental challenges at the same time. ## Introduction and context

The cases above all point out transformation in education should not be reduced to ‘situatedness’ but rather that transformation is a dialectical process of movement that is grounded in situated experiences, but is emergent from these – a process which occurs through mediated expansive l earning actions using double stimulation tools that are educative and catalytic of agentive action (Engeström, 2015; Engeström and Sannino, 201 0; Sannino, 2015; 2022), with implications for teachers education and curr iculum. This paper focusses on the latter, and shares research into design and development of models of p rocess that may be more practical for teachers to u se with learners in classrooms  in curriculum praxis, than the more extended and co mplex forms of expansive learning shared by Authors 1-5 above which have been developed in community lear ning settings. As can be seen from Paper 1-4 above, such settings offer useful insights into situating learning in African education settings, pointing to the significance of dealing with the inclusion – exclusion contradictions, living heritages, cultural meanings, indigenous knowledges, and experience. These all show the need to take substantive account of peoples cultural histories and experiences, including the ethical political conundrums that affect their lives and activity in framing a decolonial dialectic of transformation in the lea rning sciences. In contexts where colonial forms of education that prioritise transmission of abstracted concepts  in pedagogy above dialectics of transformative lea rning, we foreground the importance of situated action learning. But we recognize that this is not adequate for transformative learning, and therefore worked out a dialectical learning process model for applic ation and testing with teachers, and for guiding pedagogical methods such as use of storytelling to catalyse the dialectic of transformation through expansive learning.

The wider context in which our research is situated is one where decolonial praxis is being worked out in contexts where indigenous knowledges and living heritage is generally excluded from formal curricul a and modern institutions (schools), and where pedagogies are not well attuned to engaging local matters of concern in creative resolution of the many challenges that are found in schools, failing to engage learners interest in learning (O’Donoghue and Sandoval-Riviera, 2023; Bhurekeni, 2020). Education also fails to fully connect these through enabling an ethics of care for self, community and society, hence we have developed a teachers handboo k and learning platform informed by background research and research on indigenous knowledge and ethics-led learning (Van Staden et al., 2023) which we build on in the two formative interventions described below. Our starting point was relating the expansive learn ing cycle (Engeström and Sannino, 2010) to curriculum settings  to develop a model of  process for curriculum  activated E ducation for Sustainable Development (ESD) (Figure 1). The development of this open process model after Anne Edwards (2014) allowed us to explore the  dialectics of transformation  for mediating change in  and out of practice with a  curriculum pedagogy for ESD Handprint action learning by teachers in online course-mediated learning to change t ogether. They used associated knowledge mediated task sequences  with learner-led change project interventions in formative intervention 1, advanced further through the story sharing pedagogical process explored in f ormative intervention 2.

## Method: Formative intervention processes

Our study progresses along two lines – a formative intervention with teachers on an online course, and a formative intervention with teachers and learners in schools. The second formative intervention elabo rates on findings on pedagogical process modelling developed through the first formative intervention. Formative intervention 1: ESD Change Project development with te achers via an online course:  This formative intervention focusing on online mediated teacher education was undertaken to explore the dialectics of transformation in school curriculum settings of Education for Sustainable Development (ESD). The research approached dialectics of transformation as co-engaged deliberative processes of developmental cha nge that candevelop through learning in and out of practice. Two intermeshed arenas of change are explored, teachers working in curriculum change and their students mediated in  learner-led change projects. The study used the double stimulation tool of an action learning sequence mod el of process (Figure 1) to guide the socio-cultural learning processes of change project development in curriculum related social-ecological contexts where teachers were supported to develop curriculum activated ESD chang e projects. Co-engaged formative intervention work was undertaken alongside and within two in-service teacher professional development programmes in southern Africa which supported teachers to surface heritage knowledge, expanding this to ESD change projects in lessons. A key focus was  uncovering and recovering heritage knowledge practi ces as grounded learning  mediation tools for curriculum activated learning towards more just and sustainable futures. Formative intervention 2: Story sharing with teachers and learners in schools-To develop an ethics-led heritage approach to storytelling through a dialectics of story and practice, the formative intervention departs from the dominance of traditional folklore as morally loaded storytelling. It moves towards children’s experiences and childhood stories as personal, shared experienc es that have substantial implications for the promo tion of an ethics-led heritage education. Story sharing i s considered here a s a culturally distinct pedagogical tool for deliberative action learning. Using the dialectics of practice and  deliberative action learning theoretical frameworks developed out of formative intervention 1 and its background research  (Figure 1), this formative intervention process with children and teachers ori ents towards co-constructing story sharing pedagogies to coconstruct ethics-led heritages and dialectics of transformative practice.  The formative intervention utilizes the story cycle framework, which views story sharing not as a linear narrative, but as dynamic cycles that evolve over time. According to Parks (2023), this approach allo ws us to explore identity  development (including the development of ethical reasoning) in educational re search by using a focus group  type setting to collect large amounts of narrative data from multiple participant s and then understanding  commonalities in the data. This approach to research and learning aligns with our s hared interest in working  with Vygotsk ian sociocultural learning and the dialectics of transformation in qu alitative research that  allows reflexively examining and expanding people’s learning and experiences from their perspectives (Vygotsky, 1987).

## Analysis and results

Formative intervention 1:   This work developed relating the  expansive learning cycle after Engeström (201 6; Engeström and Sannino, 2010) to mediated learning in curriculum  settings towards learner-led design research interventions in learning lab settings  after Cerinsek et al. (2021).  Through interactions with the teachers and teacher educators working on the online courses, and observations and documentation of the ESD Change projects developed, we have developed a grounded model of pr ocess and mediating tools for ESD with  associated knowledge-scapes (wider scope and ecology of knowledge than normally used in curriculum settings). These knowledge-scapes are dialectically related and are found in and between encapsulated concepts in the curriculum and the heritage knowledges of teachers and learners, as well as in other sources of knowledge of local contextual matters of concern (e.g. from local NGOs etc., or through river monitoring experiences etc.) for teachers to use in lesson planning. The research  continues with teachers as design research partners  in small-scale teacher-led curriculum activated ESD change projects  with teachers then working in and out of practice to support learnerled ESD change projects. The latter is elaborated via a second formative intervention with  teachers and learners shared below, which develops especially the aspect of heritage-led learning as foundation to the dialectic of transformation in curriculum and change projects  as an ethical political decolonial education transf ormation praxis. The relationship between these two types of change projects is significant for the dialectic of transformation and for establishing an ethical political praxis of decolonial education process that can also engage environmental concerns in socially just ways.

We reasoned that further depth of engagement is nee ded at both levels, with our research continuing to probe teacher education change projects  at different, yet relationally connected levels of the dialectic of transformation.  We are now observing dialectically related transformation processes at and between three levels of ESD Change projects: 1) teacher educator led  change projects that enable and support 2) student teacher-led ESD Change projects  which then 3) enable and support learner-led ESD Change p rojects in schools.  We have observed that for depth of analysis of a decolonial  dialectic of transformation in schools, we should work with teachers and learners on in-depth inquiries into their ESD change projects and support development of methods for this (see formative intervention 2 below).

Formative intervention 2:   All of the formative intervention s above, including this one,  show that the historical and theoretical contexts of the dialecti cs of practice in Southern Africa are complex and d ifficult to articulate. This condition is expanded into postcol onial Southern Africa by the continuities of coloni ality that flourishes beneath the thin veil of a ruptured inherited democratic process, ongoing but often highly contradictory efforts to include while excluding, not least throu gh an abstracte d curriculum whose main objective was the acquisition of skills that reinforced the existence  of an exclusionary social hierarchy that assigned learners to predetermined roles in society. Story sharing is a culturally distinct pedagogical tool for deliberati ve action learning (widely used in African cultures), and in this study was developed through a co-engaged formative intervention with teachers, children and communitie s. Findings of the formative intervention show that s tory sharing is a powerful transformative educational approach that is engaging, relevant to learners lived experiences, interactive, and it promotes empathy, diversity of reasoning, and ethical decision-making, offering learners locally relevant avenues for ethical political expansions of their object of activity. IN SUM:  Looking at all of the above studies from a decolonial dialectic of transformation  perspective, where formal learning processes, as well as education curricula tend to be abstracted and disconnected from the learners’ cultural experiences, and community learning processes tend to be top down and paradoxically exclusionary, one candeduce that “a more subterranean transformation  is occurring in education: namely, a shift towards  a more collaborative, participatory, and inclusive method of teaching and learning” (Longo, 2023, p 14).  The studies all show that expansive learning can assist with this i n all of its generations, and in more or less forma l learning settings. Central to this dialectic is the situatedness in heritage knowledge and experience, expansion thereof, and reflexive returning to renew these knowledges and experienc es through strengthening scope of engagement as well as depth of engagement and ethical-political expansions of cultural praxis and activity.
