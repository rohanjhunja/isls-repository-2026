# “Dirty Waters”: Science Teachers’ Perceptions of Discussing Politics in Science Learning Spaces

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Science education has historically tended to call itself apolitical or neutral and consequently been unwelcoming to conversations about how science is and has always been politically charged. More recently, scholars underline that science learning spaces must begin to intentionally integrate the way political actors shape how societies respond to present day science issues (e.g., climate change, health) so that students are better prepared to be civic leaders as scientists or other relevant stakeholders. Using narrative interview data from a study exploring influencing factors for what and how science teachers in Guyana teach about socio-political dimensions of science issues, this submission explores the why for the way teachers conceive of the relationship between politics and science learning. We use this to consider what would be required of teachers, of students, and of society to effectively use political discourse as an avenue for developing criticality towards historical healing, resilience, and community.

## Introduction

The need to address how science is  directed politically is upon us as present-day issues like climate change, environmental degradation, genetic engineering, and  health are influenced by political actors across t he world (e.g., Scientific American, 2016). It is increasingly a disservice when we ask our students to treat science, as it is commonly done, as an objective discipline best taught through rote memorization and application of rules (Yerrick et al., 1998). Youth will be expected as scientists  or other relevant stakeholders to make decisions a bout complexities that include the ac companying social-cultural, economic, and political factors that affect and are affected by scientific problem-solving efforts.

Science as a political phenomenon is not new. The attention placed on the sciences in the United States of America, for instance, has been  a political pursuit  as far back as the 1800s as it was then grounded in nationalistic agendas. Montgomery (1994) describes America as having been considered a “vast living mu seum waiting to be explored” emphasized by the favored d isciplines of the time: astronomy, geography, and n atural history (p. 37). Science and exploration were entangled for a century as expeditions to take possession of land and resources remained steady.  Later, d uring the Second World War, the United States initiated r eforms to both science curricula and informal science efforts (e.g., designing essay contests, talent searches, scien ce clubs, and inaugurating the National Science Fair) to encourage youth to see the value of science with the clear message that science would help win the war (Terzian, 2008).  Then, during the Sputnik Era, federal funding was allocated for American scientists, mathematicians, and engineers to work alongside educators to develop relevant instructional materials and initiatives for specifically improving STEM-related capacities and ultimately winning the arms race (Bybee, 2013). Science teaching is and has historically been inadvertently in service of some political objective. Despite this history, science teachers are often averse to connecting politics and science in the classroom. Roth and Désautels (2002) highlight this problem of an apolitical science education using a question that science educators often use to frame their pedagogical decision-making: “How do we create the conditions for students to experience and acquire the scientists’ way of knowing?” (p. 2). They go on to explain that this question has an implicit expectation that youth will develop a critical mindset that will enable them to develop into critical social actors from simply existing within these conditions. This expectation is misguided. The development of criticality necessitates discursive engagement with controversy  in the classroom and through the curriculum (Roth & Désautels, 2002). Roth and Lee (2002) propos e that authentic scientific literacy  means being able to participate in dialogue about how scientific knowledge  and the pursuit of it  is “tied up in complex actor networks that continuously shift and adapt to local pressures and activities” (p. 88) i.e., is a political process. Though there are examples that explore teaching sci ence in the way we suggest, the focus is largely on its liberatory value (e.g., Dos Santos, 2009; Bazzu l & Tolbert, 2019; Morales-Doyles, 2019), the positive impact it can have on students (e.g., Barton & Osborne, 2002; Uphadayay et al., 2020; Raveendran, 2018), the troubling impact it can have on teachers (e.g., Tobin, 2002; Rodriguez, 2017), or guidelines for best practices (e.g., Oulton et al., 2004). There is little discussion of science teachers’ own conceptions of what constitutes politics and ho w this is reflected not only in their understanding of the relationship between politics and science learning, but also of the society they belong to.  Using narrative interview data collected for a stud y exploring influencing factors for what and how science teachers in Guyana teach about socio-political dimensions of science issues, we explore here the why for the way teachers conceive of the relationship b etween politics and science learning. Put simply, we ask, what do Guyanese science teachers consider as politics, and how does this affect their stance on the role of politics in science learning?

## Methodology

Semi-structured interviews adapted from Seidman’s (2013)  three-part interview series were conducted with Guyanese secondary school science teachers. Intervi ews in this tradition engage participants in explor ing their life experiences related to a relevant topic, exploring the concrete details of their lived experience with the topic, and reflecting on the meaning of those experiences (Seidman, 2013). Questions were designed as pre-set openended questions so that participants were able to r econstruct experiences instead of answering questio ns directly (Seidman, 2013) and so that “unexpected leads” could be followed (Glesne, 2011, p. 134). The “figured worlds” construct (Holland et al., 1998) and positioning theory (Davies & Harré, 1990, Van Langenhove, 2017) were used to  explore Guyanese science teachers’ orientation as s ocio-political actors and relate it to their conception of science as a socio-political discipline. Positioning theory offered a means to “trace and map processes of identity construction” insofar  as revealing the way teachers position themselves in relation to the social forces that constitute their figured worlds of science teaching and of science (Boyland & Woolsey, 2015, p. 65). Teacher conceptions of what constitute politics were embedded throughout responses. Six participants were used for this analysis  from a larger data set of 25 science teachers acros s seven secondary schools with a single city in Guyana  (see Table 1). These participants were chosen because they were each from a different public school and all held high-level positions (e.g., Head of Department) in addition to their science teaching roles. ## Findings

All participants defined politics in three ways: (1) as related to government, (2) connected t o racial tensions, and

(2) as related to power  in the forms of authority or wealth. When referencing politics, all  participants generally

spoke on politicians or “leaders” such as elected officials (e.g., the President) or policy makers (e.g., Ministers). More specifically, participants would reference the actions or actors of one or both major political parties that run Guyana at a federal level: the People’s Progressive  Party (PPP) or the People’s National Congress (PNC). These references were followed closely by discussions of ethnic tensions in Guyana as each political party is associated with a following divided along ethnic lines; the PPP is considered the political party of Guyanes e citizens of Indiandescent while the PNC is that of Guyanese citize ns of Africandescent. To illustrate  this division, all participants narrated personal experiences of ethnic tensions that have surfaced in and out of the classroom at any mention of decisions, inclinations, or initiatives of party officials. There was also consensus among all participants that politicians, regardless of party allegiance,  are motivated by self-interest that is usually a desire for power in the forms of influence and wealth.

Given the tensions of politics, four of the six par ticipants said that they do not get involved in pol itics. Mrs. Bea said, “I particularly don’t like to pay attention t o politics. I ignore it as much as possible because  I feel that all politicians are bad.” Chandra described politics as “dirty waters” and went on to explain how she has been called a racist because of her pro-government views which she holds because she is  of Indiandescent and the party holding office is the PPP. Because of this, s he does not encourage political discussion in or ou t of the classroom. Similarly, Mary Jane Paul provided a story of how even decorating her classroom in green decorations or wearing the color green can be a risk because she is of Africandescent and the political party color of the PNC is green. She risks unintentionally making a political statement of all egiance, which may alienate students or create a strained environment in her multiracial cl assroom. The other two participants who were not averse to discussing politics described only really doing so outside of the classroom with friends or others. In terms of the relationship between politics and s cience learning, only Mrs. Bea was able to provide classroom scenarios where she would directly reference decisions being made by politicians related to the science content at hand. She describes her aim as providing students with al l of the information they ask for so that they candraw their own conclusions about inequities tha t result from governmental decision-making. The other participants did not see a reason to discuss politics in the classroom because they either (1) did not want to incite racial tension, (2) did not think students would ca re enough, (3) did not see a connection between the agendas of science and the agendas of politics. In terms of student engagement, Skipper explained that once you begin talking about politics in the classroom, even the body post ure of students’ changes, and they become unwilling to participate in any meaningful way. Chandra described needing to be careful what is said in the  classroom as not to seem as though she is peddling any particular agendas. Rishi and Aristotle, notably teachers in Junior secondary schools, did not think students were critical think ing or knowledgeable enough to be able to participa te in discussions related to politics.

In terms of the relationship between the agendas of  politics and that of science, a ll participants highlighted how science is eclipsed by the self-interested agendas of politicians. Rishi said, “…you have politicians who are scientists. But when they get i n that arena, science is not their game anymore. Th ey’re in politics now…the best one who influences, that’s wh at they getting their satisfaction or they’re getti ng the promotions with, n ot supporting science…”  Science is largely seen as being in an advers arial relationship with political agendas rather than having a hand in the development of those agendas or being shaped by those agendas. Mary Jane Paul explains the way distrust in the gov ernment causes people to dismiss scientific challen ges addressed by the government. Aristotle stresses that science as it relates to making decisions for the greater good is not synonymous with taking and maintaining power, which is the main int erest of politics. Mrs. Bea was the only participant who considered  knowledge of science  as important for  illuminating the damage self-interested parties are causing to the environment. More generally, five of the six participants saw science as inherently good, and politics as bad.

## Conclusion and implications

Politics, as felt by participants in this study, falls directly under a more recent association th at has become prevalent with politics: “duplicity, corruption, dogmatism, inefficiency” (Hay, 2007, p. 4). Hay (2007) goes on to say that to label something as political is often t o “distance oneself from it” (p. 5). He emphasizes that though there is a lack of global consensus for what constitutes politics, politics has generally changed over the last five centuries from being concerned with “the resolution of collective action problems and the delivery of public goods such as security, social cohesion, and societal wel l-being” to a preservation of the state though “stabi lizing, insulating, and crystallizing the political power and authority of a person or group” (Hays, 2007, p. 8). Given the ways participants in this study see polit ics as occurring within the sphere of government and as a process of sustaining self-interested motives, it would be a challenge to expect teachers to include politics in their science classrooms in the ways earlier descri bed because this would require understanding politi cs as a pursuit of the collective good i.e., the initial co nception of politics that has transformed in the last five centuries (Hays, 2007). This dissonance is worth deep exploration if we hope science teachers will begin to build in social justice agendas in service of historical healing, resilience, and c ommunity into their designed curricula. Developing a common understanding for what counts a politics may be important because otherwise, at least in a context like that of the participants in this study, asking teachers to discuss politics in the science classroom may translate as asking them to solidify a racial allegiance.
