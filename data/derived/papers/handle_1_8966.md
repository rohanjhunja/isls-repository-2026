# Examining Instructors’ Adaptive Equity-Oriented Pedagogical Competency: A Validated Measure That Promotes College Students’ Success

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study validates an Adaptive Equity-Oriented Pedagogical Competency (AEPC) assessment, a measure of college instructors’ teaching effectiveness. AEPC integrates core elements that have together been shown to improve student learning by over a letter grade. These elements include: 1) Clarifying learning outcomes, 2) Aligning formative assessment with learning outcomes, 3) Identifying students’ competencies, needs and interests, 4) Understanding equity barriers to meeting outcomes, 5) Adapting based on needs and barriers, and 6) Iterating: Reflecting upon pedagogy to support continuous learning. Developed under Wilson's (2005) validity framework and calibrated using a partial credit Rasch model, the AEPC measure has both high reliability (0.94) and significant correlation with independent reviews of instructional quality. This measure includes materials that are often included in portfolios used in part to evaluate college teaching, such as teaching philosophy and practice statements, learning outcomes, formative assessment, and reflections on teaching (Seldin, Miller, Seldin, 2010). Unlike course evaluations, this measure shows no gender or URM assessment bias. A learning progression of inclusive practices shown to improve student success provides a measurement foundation for the AEPC assessment.

## Introduction: Problem statement and research questions

Research shows that college teaching directly impacts student achievement and retention in STEM, especially for underrepresented students (URM; Seymour & Hewitt, 1997; Sax et al., 2018). It is, therefore, beneficial to equip STEM instructors with adaptive equity-oriented peda gogies that have been shown to increase student lea rning. The purpose of this study is to develop and validate an instrument to measure the progression of adopting adaptive equity-oriented pedagogical competencies (AEPC). This measure can be used for professional development since it helps instructors reflect and respond to students’ learning needs. This measure was taken as an assignment by student-instructors who were enrolled in STEM pedagogy courses that taught adaptive equity-oriented pedagogies. The student-instructors were graduate and undergraduate students who served in an instructional capacity under faculty direction and were enrolled in pedagogy courses at an R-1 university. We have not found other equity-oriented teaching measures in higher education that were validated using Item Response Theory (IRT). We found college-level,  observation-based, equity-based checklist measures  of universal design for learning (CAST, 2018) and cult urally responsive pedagogy (Hammond, 2014). These measures ask observers to examine whether instructo rs use formative assessment, explain course content  in different ways, and promote participation for all students, but these measures are not validated using IRT and we cannot find reliability estimates. We explore the following 3 research questions:

1. Is the AEPC measure valid and reliable?

2. Does this measure have gender and/or URM bias?

3. What are the consequences of using the assessment?

This study contributes to the learning sciences by offering an assessment that defines a learning prog ression of inclusive behaviors and practices that have been shown to improve student success. The assessment enables us to see where instructors are on a developmental learning progression so that we can further support their learning of inclusive practices. This information can enable professional developers to support instructors as they implement equity-oriented practices measured by the AEPC assessment. Adaptive Equity-Oriented Pedagogical Competency Adaptive equity-oriented pedagogy (AEP) is a framework for adapting teaching to address equity barriers to learning based on student data (e.g., formative assessment, observations, surveys) (Phuong et al., 2017; Phuong ICLS2022 Proceedings 1645 © ISLS & Nguyen, 2019). Effective AEP practice is characterized by instructors’ use of the 6 competency elements listed below, which build on each other and reflect a mastery learning model.

1. Clarify learning outcomes, equitable course policies, and prerequisite knowledge needed for a course

2. Align formative assessments and activities with outcomes

3. Identify students’ competencies, interests, and needs

4. Understand equity barriers and contextual challenges to meeting outcomes

5. Adapt teaching practices based on these needs and barriers

6. Iterate: Reflect upon pedagogy to support continuous learning, adaptation, and growth

## Methodology

We engaged in the four building blocks of the Bear Assessment System (e.g., construct map, item design, outcome space, and measurement model) (Wilson, 2005). We designed 6 items that aligned with construct map levels that reflect the AEPC learning progression. We defined an outcome space by creating 0-7 point scoring rubrics for 5 of the items and a 0-1 point scoring rubric for the 6th item. The construct measures varying degrees of AEPC on an ordinal scale, with higher positions on the rubric implying that criteria of lower positions have been met. Table 1. To answer research question 1, we fitted a Partial Credit Rasch Model (Masters, 1982) and estimated person locations on the construct and the item difficulty parameters.

Model 2: PCM with Latent Regression. To answer research question 1 and examine external validity, we conducted a latent regression to account for measurement error when examining the effects of covariates such as fidelity of implementation scores and gender.

## Research Question 1: Validity and Reliability

Construct Validity.  The Wright Map is an empirical representation of the construct map (Wilson, 2006).  The Wright Map displays the difficulty of each item, ov erall and by scoring rubric level, in relation to student-instructors’ AEP competency. When items are built to align directly to construct map levels, the scoring rubric levels are expected to band together on the Wright Map. Such empirical banding demonstrates a coherence that contributes to strong construct validity. Based on the Wright Map in Figure 1, we see relative banding for each level since each color tends to gr oup together. We notice that the banding is ordered in the way we hypothesized in the construct map. Respondents who tend to score high on the construct tend to score high acro ss items. This mean location increase supports construct vali dity. According to the Wright Map, the items appear to cover most of student-instructors’ competencies. Nonetheless, some respondents are above the highest items. In other w ords, the most competent respondents need more challenging items for optimal measurement.

Reliability. We estimated Warm’s Weighted Likelihood Estimate (W LE) for the AEP competency of each student-instructor. The reliability estimate o f the instrument is 0.94, suggesting the instrument  has high consistency in measuring AEPC. We examined infit st atistics to ensure that items were behaving appropr iately and that there was alignment between what was expected from the PCM and what was observed. An item with an infit statistic above 1.33 is exhibiting more rando mness than expected, suggesting that respondents wi th high proficiency scores tend to score low on that item or vice versa. None of the items had high infit statistics. External validity. To assess external validity, we conducted a latent regression and regressed studentinstructors’ AEPC on their fidelity of implementation scores, when controlling for gender. For each unit increase in the fidelity of implementation score, the student-instructor’s location in the logit scale is estimated to increase, on average, 1.571 logits in position when controlling for gender (p<0.05). When a student-instructor scores higher on the fidelity of implementation score, there is a higher likelihood that the student-instructor will have, on average, demonstrated higher degrees of AEPC. Moreover, researchers’ review of student-instructors’ teaching materials and observations of their teaching tended to align with student-instructors’ competency estimates. These results further support this measure’s validity. When controlling for fidelity of implementation scores, gender does not significantly predict a student-instructors’ AEPC at the 5% level.

## Research Question 2: Investigating Gender and URM Bias

For all items, we found no evidence of Differential  Item Functioning (DIF), or item bias, between male s and females because no item by gender interactions (i.e., DIF Contrast) were both statistically significant and above the generally accepted threshold (i.e., 0.426) in m agnitude (Paek & Wilson, 2011). In fact, none of ou r DIF contrasts were of above 0.426 in magnitude, so even  had larger sample sizes resulted in statistically significant results, they would not have met the generally accepted threshold indicating a problematic level of DIF. In other words, for all levels of item difficulty, male student-instructors do not have significantly higher item scores than female student-instructors who have the same AEP Competency score, or vice versa. Using the same criteria, we also found no evidence of DIF between URM and non-URM student-instructors.

## Research Question 3. Consequences of Use

Benefits. We collected validity evidence on the consequences of the assessment’s use. One of assessment’s goals is to help student-instructors document equity-oriented practices and demonstrate teaching effectiveness in ways that go beyond course evaluations. We examined the degree to which this occurred. We found evidence th at student-instructors documented and improved their AEP practices. Student-instructors stated that the instrument ICLS2022 Proceedings 1647 © ISLS helped them improve their instruction and student learning. Many student-instructors also discussed how the AEP framework helped them promote inclusive learning en vironments. In response to a think aloud exercise, one student-instructor stated the following when asked about what motivated his thought processes and responses to the assessment: “I relied heavily on the AEP methods because many of the principles of AEP agreed with my own teaching philosophy and I had found that based on my experiences with the students the AEP methods were the most effective.” Multiple student-instructors also expressed that this assessment helped them apply AEP to adjust teaching practices they were passionate about (e.g., inquiry-based learning, modeling expert problem s olving, growth mindset-oriented praise). Student-instructor s’ AEPC assessment reflections suggest that the ped agogy course impacted their models for effective teaching and learning. Preliminary research also suggests that success on the AEPC measure is associated with greater student achievement.

## Discussion

Based on prior research (Phuong et al., 2017; Fink, 2013), we theorized that the highest AEPC level would be the most difficult to reach because it required instruc tors to demonstrate and act upon all lower-level el ements. In theory, reaching the highest level requires ongoing planning, observation, active reflection, and adaptation based on data, which are the traits an instructor with hi gh AEPC mastery would demonstrate. The levels and ordering of the construct and learning progression are supported by the Wright map and mean location increases within all the items of the instrument. We crafted multiple items that tap into all levels of AEPC. Based on the Wright map, these items targeted participants at different competency levels. Limitations. This study faces challenges with generalizability since this instrument was administered in a graduate-level course in which student-instructors were provided with opportunities to learn and reflect on their adaptive equity-oriented practices and had little to no classroom-teaching experience.

## Significance

This study contributes to the literature on communities of practice, groups of professionals who have shared goals and interests such as improving student learning (W enger, 1998). We provide a way to assess the learni ng progression within communities of practice focused on equitable teaching practices and interactions with students in STEM. While we understand that such communities can promote learning of new pedagogies, there has not yet been a validated IRT measure on inclusive pedagogie s in higher education that can empirically inform t heir progress.

If validated with a larger sample and elevated in t he teaching evaluation process, this AEPC measure could help reduce instructor evaluation bias and in centivize instructors to focus more on high-impact equityoriented teaching practices that drive student learning. Using item response theory, this study presents a clear and rigorously validated teaching measure that leverage s artifacts that are already collected in higher ed ucation evaluation. For example, the AEPC items incorporate  a teaching philosophy and practice statement, teac hing materials (e.g., syllabi, formative/assessment), and evidence of student learning. This measure identi fies instructors' developmental progress within a mastery model of learning based on AEPC elements. Using the AEPC measure in this way can prioritize equity in profes sional development and in conversations around effe ctive teaching practices for all students.
