# Exploring Complex Systems through Multiple External Representations: High School Students’ Exploration of the Mechanobiology of Venus Fly Traps

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Complex systems thinking is a critical yet underrepresented competency in high school STEM education. This study investigates how students developed systems understanding through a two-day mechanobiology unit on the Venus flytrap, integrating multiple external representations (MERs). Using surveys and interviews, we analyzed how students engaged with complex system characteristics and transferred these ideas to other systems. Findings show that students developed cross-scale explanations and applied systems thinking beyond the learning context. Results underscore the effectiveness of MERs to scaffold reasoning across micro-and macro-levels and make abstract system properties tangible. This study informs the design of MER-based learning environments for STEM-integrated education.

STEM-integrated learning in K-12 education is essential for developing STEM literacy, yet high school curricula often remain siloed in discrete subjects  with little emphasis on crosscutting connections  (Yoon et al., 2024). Emerging interdisciplinary fields  like mechanobiology, which investigates how physical forces affect living things, offer rich opportunities for real-world interdisciplinary learning  (Nelson, 2022). We used Multiple External Representations (MERs), including a live electrophysiology experiment, an agent-based model, and a Rube Goldberg machine, to scaffold complex systems thinking (Figure 1). By providing complementary investigations of the trap closure phenomenon, MERs can help students visualize  interactions across scales and to understand the invisible mechanisms underlying how the plant responds to forces in its environment to produce emergent behaviors (Ainsworth, 2018; Olympiou et al., 20; Rau, 2017). We implemented a two-day unit where students examined the predatory response  of the Venus flytrap through these MERs, which were designed to convey the following key complex systems principles: many interacting parts, decentralization, non-linear interactions, emergence, and adaptation. We investigated how this design support ed students’ understanding of these principles and their ability to transfer these ideas across contexts  (Bielik et al., 2023). We were guided by the following questions:

1) How do Venus flytrap MERs support students’ learning of complex systems concepts?

2) To what extent did this intervention lead students to transfer systems-level thinking across contexts?

Figure 1 Models Used to Stimulate Complex Systems Thinking in Relation to the Mechanobiology of the Venus  Flytrap 1392 To examine how this instructional design supported students’ reasoning about complex systems, we adopted a mixed-methods exploratory design that combined survey and interview data to address the research questions. Our instructional approach integrated and made explicit ideas from biology, physics, and engineering, and was organized around five critical complex systems principles: 1) many interacting parts, 2) decentralization, 3) nonlinear interactions, 4) emergence, and 5) adaptation. To make the underlying mechanisms visible and to support cross-scale reasoning, the unit employed MERs as interactive learning tools with associated scaffolds.

## Context and participants

The unit was delivered during regular class time across two double-period sessions (~120 minutes each) and sequenced with concreteness fading—students moved from working directly with the organism to abstracted representations (computer-based, then the tangible, physical model). Prior to experimenting with the MERs, students learned through a short introductory lecture that two mechano-electrical stimuli of the trigger hair within approximately 20–30 seconds generate two action potentials (APs) that close t he trap, while additional signals from struggling prey subsequently trigger digestive gene expression before the trap reopens and the cycle repeats. STEM teachers and researchers collaboratively co-designed the three dynamic models as part of a larger ongoing mechanobiology education project.  Students first engaged in the live flytrap experiment to observe the plant’s predatory behavior through touch-induced, electrical signaling. They then explored an open-access agent-based model to visualize the underlying ion dynamics and then interacted with a digitally fabricated Rube-Goldberg model that externalized threshold-triggered closure in a tangible and collaborative way (Figure 1). Each of the three models offered distinct affordances and limitations.  Together, the MERs complemented one another to support students’ multi-level reasoning.

Data sources and analysis Two complementary datasets included 16 individual, semi-structured post-intervention interviews and 28 pre-and post-surveys. Using a constant comparative method (Glaser, 2008), we analyzed interview responses for evidence of complex systems reasoning based on the five critical principles, focusing on connections between micro-level mechanisms and macro-level outcomes (Table 1). This resulted in a Cohen’s Kappa of κ = 0.645 when two raters were trained in the resulting codebook and independently coded a subset of the data (n = 37 or 41% of all coded instances). Interview-derived themes were next triangulated with pre-and post-survey responses to corroborate students’ emerging understandings of mechanobiology and complex systems. 1. Many Interacting Parts/Scale

Students identify micro-level parts (e.g., ions, cells, signals) and macro-level parts (e.g., trigger hairs, enzymes, prey) and explain how these interact to support function.

“There's six different hairs located on the Venus Flytrap and a fly or a bug triggers [it to close].” Student 8  identifies two macro-level parts in this response: trigger hairs and prey.

2. Decentralized Control

Students note differentiated roles (e.g., hairs, ions, enzymes) that cooperate without one part directing others.

“It's like the hairs, they have a job and then the calcium and the energy there has a job… none of them tell each other what to do. They help each other out.” Student 15  articulates that while the parts work together, they have their own specific “jobs”.

3. Nonlinear interactions

Students mention signal accumulation/decay, thresholds, limited time windows (~20s), or sufficient force to fire an action potential.

“And if that calcium becomes greater than a certain threshold… within a certain time period …and then it closes.” Student 9  explains the accumulating calcium signal (i.e., micro-level component) is needed for the flytrap to close.

4. Emergence

Students link many small interactions to outcomes such as closure, enzyme release, or nutrient extraction “The Venus flytrap closing … that's the sum of all the little interactions...the hair, the calcium… And then if there's enough of that, it causes the fly trap to close and then that accomplishes its goal of catching prey.” Student 9 describes the behavior of closing to capture prey as emergence in the flytrap.

5. Adaptation (ADAPT)

Students describe guarding against false alarms (energy saving), counting touches/action potentials, evolutionary adaptation to low-nutrient soil, or regulating enzyme production as signals persist.

“It adapted to its environment where there wasn't a lot of nutrients in the soil… it has to calculate whether to release more or less depending on how much is triggered. ” Student 11 describes the flytrap evolving over time to adapt to low nutrient soil and to also catch prey by counting the number of hair triggers.

Using MERs to learn about complex STEM-integrated concepts Forty percent of interviewees said two or more models together were most helpful. They explicitly linked evidence across MERs. For example, Student 8 mentioned: “I like the computer model that actually shows the calcium buildup and the threshold line and how there’s some decay. And I like how the physical model represents that decay through the water dripping down. There’s a small hole where the water drips down, so [if] there isn’t enough buildup within a period of time, [then] there’s no action.” This reasoning demonstrates how Student 8 linked evidence across MERs, connecting micro-level calcium signaling visualized in the computer model with macrolevel water flow dynamics represented in the physical Rube Goldberg machine. However, since most learners still leaned on one representation, this suggests challenges in connecting abstract concepts across representations. Improving understanding of flytrap mechanobiology We identified 89 coded instances aligned to the five complex systems principles in interviews. Adaptation was referenced most often: all students articulated at least one adaptation aspect, and 50% named two or more. Emergence was also strong (94% identified ≥1 aspect; more than half specifically named trap closure as emergent). By contrast, perfo rmance on Nonlinear Interactions varied (31% named one aspect; 19% named two aspects-as defined in the codebook), and Decentralized Control appeared least frequently. Still, a few students attributed decision-making to the trigger hairs, indicating a partial yet unstable grasp of decentralized control rather than a fully distributed view of system coordination. All students could name many interacting parts  across levels and relate them to the mechanism for trap closure. On average, interviews included at least two macro-level elements (e.g., trigger hairs, trap “mouth,” prey) and one micro-level element (e.g., ions, membrane potential, electrical signals). Moreover, 50% identified four or more interacting parts from the micro level to the macro level, indicating an ability to coordinate more abstract variables that underlie closure. Overall, these findings demonstrate students attained an in-depth understanding of flytrap mechanobiology. Notably, this depth was achieved even though 20% of students had reported limited to no prior understanding on the pre-survey (e.g., uncertainty about what causes closure). Transferring complex systems thinking to familiar contexts Across student interviews, we documented 33 instances where learners transferred complex systems thinking beyond the flytrap. Seven students explicitly connected complex systems thinking to how schools function and to classroom experiences. Student 16 emphasized teamwork in engineering class: “You definitely have to work together as a team to achieve anything…when you work on projects with other people, sometimes it's really hard…This definitely helps… to get different perspectives of something complex.” This response illustrates how Student 16 transferred the idea of decentralized control from the flytrap system to human teamwork, recognizing that progress emerges from coordinated efforts without a single controlling entity. Further, Student 11 described how school-level variables, though they look insignificant, could have consequential downstream effects, i.e. 1394 emergent behaviors: “We have all these… super strict schedules that we have to follow. And if we don't follow them, it's bad for our attendance and attendance matters for college [admissions]. It's all these little things we don't think of as being relevant at all that…make a big difference. ” Student 11’s reflection extends complex systems reasoning to schooling, showing awareness of nonlinearity and emergence, how small, seemingly insignificant actions (e.g., missing routines) can accumulate to affect larger institutional outcomes like attenda nce and admissions. These excerpts foreground decentralized control, nonlinear interactions, and many interacting parts. Others extended these ideas to biological and ecological contexts, such as crocodiles adapting to environments and bees supporting plant growth through pollination. Here these students connected the survivorship strategy of the flytrap that they learned about, which was to evolve to eat animals due to poor nutrient access in the acid ic soils, to other evolutionary/biological processes that they had learned about. Moreover, students connected MER-informed mechanisms to everyday life. Student 15 stated, “...the [Rube Goldberg machine] and then [the agent-based model] shows us how different results can be, or the same conclusion can be made from different experiments… we can find a correlation between Venus flytraps and your everyday life, or these complex systems.” This perspective signals an ability to spot mechanisms —parts, accumulation/ decay, thresholds—outside biology. Pre-survey responses further suggested that many already held intuitive but underdeveloped systems-level ideas, citing examples such as ant colonies, Rube Goldberg machines, broken glass and working collaboratively on a classroom project.  Taken together, these findings  highlight the fluidity with which students transferred various aspects of complex systems knowledge to multi ple domains. This study introduces a high school mechanobiology unit that is organized around three complementary MERs that can elicit coherent complex systems reasoning and transfer. Students most frequently  articulated emergence and adaptation, named interacting parts across levels, and explained threshold closure via accumulation/decay of calcium signaling, yet decentralized control was less prominent. These patterns align with research on MERs supporting abstraction and relational understanding (Ainsworth, 2006, 2018; Rau, 2017, 2020) and with work showing agent-based models make micro-macro mechanisms visible (Jacobson & Wilensky, 2006; Yoon et al., 2017). Positioning MERs in a  relatively new interdisciplinary domain, such as mechanobiology, extends prior systems-learning studies (Bielik et al., 2023) by demonstrating how MERs can support students’ reasoning across crosscutting biological and physical concepts and practices (NGSS Lead States, 2013). This work highlights mechanobiology as a promising context for interdisciplinary STEM learning. Because it integrates physics, biology, and engineering around concrete phenomena, the field  offers a unique approach for making abstract systems principles tangible and making STEM-integrated learning explicit and relevant to current research. In particular, the Venus flytrap’s mechano-electrical signaling provided a compelling anchor for bridging scales, and the coordinated use of MERs enabled students to visualize and connect how micro-level processes generated emergent, macro-level behaviors. This suggests that intrinsically integrated fields, like mechanobiology, can play a critical role in updating STEM curricula through an interdisciplinary lens, one that enables students to engage more deeply with systems-level thinking However, these findings are not generalizable, as the study was conducted in a single school context with a relatively small sample and short duration. These design choices, though, also allowed for close observation of students’ reasoning processes in a natural classroom setting. Moreover, our analysis revealed areas for improved instruction. These include designing scaffolds that foreground micro–macro linkages and integrating prompts that direct attention to accumulation/decay and thresholds. This emphasis might mitigate  the difficulty students experienced in wrestling with the characteristic of decentralization. Furthermore, spending more classroom time engaging students via in-depth explorations of the MERs—for example in “looking under the hood” in the agent-based model and remixing the code, to redesigning the Rube Goldberg machine—would likely lead to deeper learning. Nonetheless, these findings indicate that even short interventions can open opportunities for STEM-integrated learning when supported by MERs.

Our future research will build on these findings by examining how immersive learning environments can further support students’ understanding of complex systems, particularly in strengthening areas such as decentralized control and cross-scale reasoning. Virtual reality (VR) envir onments offer  engaging platforms within dynamic, multi-scale biological systems, enabling them to observe and manipulate otherwise invisible mechanisms in real time  (Enyedy & Yoon, 2021). We intend to  explore how adaptive and interactive environments can support students in navigating between micro-level mechanisms and macro-level system behaviors, as well as how such approaches can be effectively integrated and made accessible into classroom practice across diverse STEM contexts.
