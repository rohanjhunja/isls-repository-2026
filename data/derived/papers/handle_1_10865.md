# Making the Self: Community and Identity in Generative Role-Playing Game (RPG) Based Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study uses constructionism, social constructivism, cognitive constructivism, transformative learning theory, DSMRI theory, self-determination theory, and situated learning theory to analyze the impact of generative RPG-based learning in a university literature course. The findings reveal the interplay of these theories in shaping student identities, motivation, and engagement, highlighting the importance of leveraging identity within the learning process. Game-based learning transforms the ways students engage with literature and develop their identities.

## Introduction and background

Game-based learning (GBL) is popular in STEM courses, bu t it has not had the same success in the literature classroom (Pugh &  Ramey, 2022). Role-playing games (RPGs) provide broad opportunities to  engage student identities. RPGs such as Dungeons and Dragons provi de further benefit to the instructor of the literat ure more generally because they focus on worlds, identities, conflicts, and narratives. Students engaged in this type of roleplay become members of a literary community. Our study was grounded in constructionist theory, s ocial constructivist theory, cognitive constructivi st theory, transformative learning theory, DSMRI theor y, self-determination theory, and situated learning theory. Constructionism focuses on the idea of gaining knowledge through creation. Cognitive constructivism claims the process is individual, as opposed to social constru ctivism (Powell & Kalina, 2009). Transformative lea rning theory focuses on changing a student’s inflexible frame of reference to a frame that is more open tonew ideas and able to be changed by different experiences (Mezirow, 2009). In DSMRI theory, identity is a non-linear, complex system that can be changed by outside influences th at can impact the beliefs, self-perceptions, purposes, and perceived action possibilities that a person has. (Kaplan & Garner, 2017). In self-determination theory, the concepts of extrinsic and intrinsic motivation are defined as the reasons for human participation in c ertain activities (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Pr inciples from situated learning theory focus on the  environment (Anderson et al., 1996).

## Methods

Our network analysis investigates the complexity of  relationships between student experiences aligned with the theories used to design a semester-long RPG-based learning activity in a university literature course. The gaming aspect of the course happened in four pa rts. First, students were asked to create a fiction al future Earth and an alien civilization on a distant planet. Then they were asked to create fictional characters within this universe. The characters were placed in groups and asked to create backstories  explaining how they came to be friends. Finally, those groups created scenarios, and games were played at the end of the semester with the aid of students who took on the roles of game masters.

We collected reflection papers from 39 students, wh ich we coded in the MAXQDA Analytics Pro qualitative analysis software according to 56 codin g categories for principles derived from each of th e learning theories. Then we conducted correlation analysis re sulting in a symmetrical correlation matrix contain ing only values significant at the P < 0.01 level from which we constructed a network map in the UCINET network analysis software. We conducted Girvan-Newman analysis to identify clusters and set the no de sizes according to betweenness centrality values.

## Findings

Our analysis resulted in a network map of principles from theory correlated at the p<0.01 level with four GirvanNewman clusters at a robust confidence level of Q=0.618. The black triangles cluster is the largest. I t is situated centrally, along with the gray diamonds cluster, and includes three principles from constructionist learning theory, three principles from self-determination theory, two principles from DSMRI, and two principles from transformative learning theory.

Analysis reveals the DSMRI theory principle of expl oring or changing purposes and goals can allow students to enjoy the benefits of principles from o ther learning theories. Through personal exploratio n, students engage in the constructionist theory principle of c onstructing things and doing design work. By partic ipating in this constructive process, students develop intrins ic motivation through immersion, a self-determination theory principle. Additionally, the DSMRI theory principle of exploring or changing purposes and goals emphasizes the development of intrinsic motivation through enhance ment of one’s own competence. The se two principles, the development of intrinsic motivation through immersi ng oneself and gaining competence, show the largest betweenness centrality node, along with the DSMRI p rinciple of exploring or changing purposes and goal s (168.83, 182.67, and 201.3 3 respectively). GBL experiences grounded in learni ng theories encourage the active development of identity and knowledge construction.

Through the mediation of the tinkering and exploration node, students constructed their knowledge of the discipline and developed an identity in the discipline. The opportunity to tinker and explore content within the classroom benefits a student’s identity development because it allows them to enact the D SMRI theory principle of exploring and changing their beliefs, knowledge,  and reality. Students developed confidence in ques tioning their own beliefs, part of self-determination theory. By gaining confidence in them selves and becoming comfortable, students can then engage in collaborative knowledge construction. Engagement in this collaborative construction of knowledge, which is a principle of social constructivism, helps students develop more wellrounded schemas to integrate diverse ideas and viewpoints into their understanding of the topic.

## Implications and conclusion

Multiple learning theories are needed to understand  the user experience of generative RPG-based learning. Designers must deliberately leverage identity throu ghout the process. After the game is done, identity  must be leveraged once more, and in-game identity transferred to their identity as readers of literature. Here the hope is to transform them into lifelong gamers/readers.

The ramifications for research cannot be understate d. Humanities classrooms have been slow to adopt game-based learning. When so many principles from so man y different theories collide in practice during generative RPG-based learning, interesting and unexp ected results emerge. What resulted was the erasure  of the line between the creator and their creation, the pl ayer and the game, the reader and the literary work, subject and object. When breached, these binaries open to reveal creative possibility, critical curiosity, community, and joy.
