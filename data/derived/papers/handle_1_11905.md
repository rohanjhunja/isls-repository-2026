# Leveling Up Inclusive and Collaborative Learning Through Experiential Game Design Model

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We present the Experiential Game Design Model as a student-centered approach to fostering collaborative learning through game design. Rooted in experiential learning theory and Vygotsky’s emphasis on social interaction, this model addresses common challenges, such as conflict and disengagement, by focusing on four core components: knowledge, identity, culture, and values. Students collaboratively plan, design, and revise games. Teachers guide students through iterative development and reflective practices. Emphasizing culturally responsive pedagogy and shared goal-setting, the model offers a framework where students can express unique perspectives and engage in meaningful and collaborative learning.

## Introduction

Disengagement in collaborative learning often stem s from interactions between students, teachers, and the ir environments (Dinç & Classen, 2024; Isohätälä  et al., 2020). To address these challenges, we introduced the experiential game design model, which  integrates knowledge, identity, value, and culture  to enhance collaboration. This approach empowers students as active participants, reducing conflict, fostering engagement, and promoting inclusivity. Through the design process,  students reflect on personal and cultural narratives, coconstruct learning objectives, and set shared goals via “socially shared regulation” (Järvelä et al., 2013, p. 269).

## Conceptual framework: Experiential game design for collaborative learning

Although Kiili’s (2005, p. 18) “experiential gaming model” aimed to support learning, it lacked emphasis  on social interaction within the design process. Our framework builds on experiential learning theory (Kolb, 2015) and Vygotsky’s view of social interaction as essential for higher-order thinking (Vygotsky, 1978). It positions game design as a social process where students build knowledge through action and reflection (Roland, 2017) and collective meaning-making by drawing on diverse perspectives.  Integrating culturally responsive pedagogy (Richards et al., 2007), the model fosters inclusive space s that connect educational content to students’ lived experiences. By embedding personal and cultural narratives into game design, the framework deepens reflection and strengthens student connection to the material. The model aims to mitigate disengagement through hands-on problem-solving and reflective collaboration, which promote engagement and reduced disconnection (Yılmaz & Griffiths, 2023). Given that conflicts often arise in peer and student-teacher interactions, learning environments must prioritize  agency, divers ity, and inclusivity  (Pérez Cortés & Kessner, 2023). Collaborative goal-setting enhances social performance (Järvelä & Hadwin, 2013), and co-creation between students and teachers nurtures relatedness, competence, and autonomy (Halverson & Peppler, 2018). Game design supports collective understanding and identity formation through the ex ploration of personal and cultural narratives  (Dinç, 2022). Ultimately, experiential learning ’s dynamic and reflective nature supports  deep learning through hands-on experiences and values the learning process over outcomes (Gross & Rutland, 2017; Kolb, 2015). Core components of the experiential game design model Knowledge in this model is content-driven and process-oriented. Students draw on prior knowledge and acquire new information relevant to their game’s themes and  mechanics. Through knowledge sharing, they exchange ideas, deepen understanding, and engage with content. Identity exploration allows students to integrate personal experiences and goals into the game design. Elements like character creation, storytelling, and in-game choices promote self-reflection and strengthen their connection to the project. Culture shapes students’ perspectives and approaches to collaboration. The model emphasizes cultural inclusivity by encouraging students to embed cultural narratives, practices, and symbols into their games. This promotes the exploration of cultural diversity and helps build shared cultural understanding. Values guide design decisions and shape themes and ethical considerations. The model encourages students to incorporate values  into their projects, sparking dialogue around ethics, social responsibility, and community. An example scenario might be choosing fair trade products over cheaper alternatives Phases and principles of the experiential game design model The initial stage, planning, focuses on goal-setting, ideation, and narrative development. Stude nts define their game’s purpose and direction, brainstorming ideas t hat reflect personal, cultural, and shared values. As Dondlinger (2007, p. 28) notes, key “design elements, such as narrative context, rules, goals, rewards, multisensory cues, and interactivity,” are vital to effective game design. Teachers facilitate this phase by guiding students to express their ideas clearly.  Building on the planning stage, the designing phase involves developing the game. Students apply knowledge and integrate identity, culture, and values into the design, transforming ideas into game mechanics, visuals, and narratives. Students draw on one another’s perspectives and expertise. Iterative testing and experimentation are encouraged, with teachers offering technical support, resources, and feedback. In the revising phase, students and teachers review the game’s alignment w ith initial goals and values, reflect on its inclusivity, and make refinements. This phase foste rs collective reflection, allowing the integration of diverse cultural perspectives and values.  Throughout all phases, students may keep reflective  journals or participate in guided discussions to document personal growth and cultural insights, reinforcing the model’s reflective foundation.

Connection-centric design (student-centered): S tudents embed their cultur es, identities, knowledge, values, and personal experiences into the game design process. By incorporating elements from their backgrounds, students develop a stronger connection to the content and a sense of ownership (Young et al., 2015). This fosters engagement and a collaborative atmosphere where all voices are heard. Collaborative storytelling (collaboration): Storytelling is central to collaborative game devel opment (Lassheikki, 2022). By sharing personal and cultural narratives, students co-create a ric her, more inclusive game that blends diverse perspe ctives. This principle promotes collective decision-making, empathy, and appreciation for different vie wpoints. Balance of challenge and skill (motivation): Sustaining motivation requires a careful balance be tween challenge and skill (Hamari et al., 2016). Designing games that accommodate varied  abilities ensures that every student can contribut e meaningfully. This balance promotes mutual respect  and a more inclusive, motivating learning environme nt. Iterative development (iteration): T his principle highlights continuous improvement thr ough prototyping  and testing. Each iteration builds technical, creative,  and interpersonal skills while strengthening group  dynamics. “Design is an iterative knowledge-building cycle” (Cennamo & Kalk, 2019, p. 6), which  enhances the process and the product. Reflective practice (reflection): Reflection is woven throughout the process, encouraging students to evaluate their decisions, insights, and growth (Majgaard, 2014). Documenting and discussing these reflections at each stage deepens understanding of content and collaboration, making the experience more meaningfu l and personally transformative.

## Scholarly significance and future research directions

This experiential game design model emphasizes active participation, reflective practice, cultural inclusivity, and student-centered learning. By engaging students as co-designers, the model fosters ownership, empathy, and deeper connection. Future research could validate and refine the model  by examining its impact on collaborative learning outcomes, cultural competence, and students’ sense of identity. Exploring diverse game design tools and methods may help optimize the model across different contexts, age groups, and subject areas.
