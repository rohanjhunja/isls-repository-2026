# Learning with Latinx Futurisms in Computer Science Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning environments for Computer Science (CS) education need to contend with the ways technological growth is positioned as an inevitable future. Justice-centered approaches to CS education aim to challenge the values that undergird ‘technological inevitability,’ through paying attention to ethics, political visions, and learners’ politicized identities. In this paper, we offer insights from imagining justice-centered CS education through the lens of Latinx Futurism, which centers imagination and world-building from the perspective of people in and of the U.S., Latin America, and the Caribbean.

## Introduction

If the future is based on technological innovation,  then perhaps there is a need to ask new questions about what the future will be. This positions us to ask: whose futures are being imagined? Based on what paradigms ? These questions bring us closer to the heart of equity is sues facing Computer Science Education (CSEd) today --White, Western futures are imagined on the structures of c apitalism and unfettered “progress,” driven forward by the dominance of Silicon Valley in the technological in novation sphere (Hong, 2021). Critical perspectives on CS learning, then, must take up the specific ways that  technological futures envisioned within Silicon Va lley imaginaries harm people in the present, reifying globalized inequities along racialized, gendered, socio-economic, and (dis)abled axes.

In this paper, we take up Latinx Futurism (LF) as a  specific intervention for CS learning environments to take up justice-centered, critical perspectives. Latinx Futurism ex ists within the specific cultural, ethical, and political ways of being that inform  both who is envisioned in the future as well as the types of relationships that can exist. Thus, it challenges dominant White and Western futurisms and speculation, reimagining the values and rationale (Santo et al., 2019)  embedded in current technological innovation. Justi ce-centered perspectives on CSEd challenge how we design the learning environme nt for CS, towards centering the ethics, identities  and political visions that can be made explicit to challenge normative ways of engaging in CSEd (Vakil, 2018). Thus, we frame our discussion in  this paper by discussing the generative intersections between LF and justice-centered approaches to teaching CS (Kafai et al., 2019; Vakil, 2018; Yadav et al., 2022). We argue that these intersections illuminate dimensions of justice-centered CSEd that could be explored in future lear ning environments as bases for design or conversation.

We situate our conversation of Latinx Futurism within an expansive, transnational conversation of “who counts” as Latinx, which for us means that we recognize this identity for its potential as a coalitional tool. While accounts of the term Latinx position it solely as a tool for academics in the U.S. (Brammer, 2019) or as a marginal term within U.S. -based Latinx communities (Lopez, 2020), our use of it here is in line with growing calls for gender-inclusivity and its origins in LGBTQ+ communities (Gamio, 2016). Further, we use this name to disrupt “the colonial omniscient force of Spanish and the perceived homogeneity of the Latinx population” (Gamio, 2016, p. 12).

## Background

Despite Latinx people being the second-largest ethnic group in the U.S. and experiencing s ignificant growth between 2010 and 2020, their participation in compu ter science remains troublingly low, limiting acces s to the field's numerous career opportunities  and social advantages (Gangas et al., 2024). Villeg as Molina et al. (2024) highlight that Latinx students in computer science face barriers such as a low sense of belonging, cha llenges related to identity, and a lack of supportive commu nity environments. Their study emphasizes the impor tance of Culturally Relevant Computing as a key pedagogical practice but notes a limited focus on integrating m edia computation. The findings from Villegas Molina et a l. (2024) highlight a critical need to rethink how computer science education is approached for Latinx students. The barriers these students face —ranging from a lack of belonging to insufficient support systems rooted in  their cultural values —underscore the limitations of current educational models that focus on  inclusion without addressing deeper structural ineq uities. Another critical finding is the lack of family-centered studies, despite the strong cultural empha sis on familismo within Latinx communities, which is known to provide high levels of perceived support (Alvarado, 2023). Exploring the role of familismo could be vital in enhancing Latinx students' engage ment and retention in computer science education (Garza, 2017). Furthermore, the lower emphasis on media computat ion and the lack of research on Latinx experiences in computing indicate a missed opportun ity to explore more culturally relevant pedagogies.  This suggests the potential of leveraging LF, which inte grates media computation while staying culturally r esonant. Latinxfuturisms often center on family and community, aligning with the value of familismo that provides crucial support for Latinx students.

This perspective aligns with Vakil’s (2018) argument that simply increasing representation in computer science is insufficient. There is an urgent need for a justice-centered approach that not only includes marginalized students but also critically engages with the political, social, and ethical dimensions of technology. By integrating culturally relevant frameworks like Latinx Futurism, we can create learning environments that are not only inclusive but also transformative, allowing students to see themselves as both creators and critics of technology. Justice-centered CS education CS learning is often framed as important for the social and economic welfare of historically nondominant students and their communities, including but not limited to students and communities of the United States, yet this promise largely obscures the struc tural formations that create inequity to begin with  (Vakil, 2020). Building on these narratives, a dominant approach to framing the impo rtance of diversity in CS education draws on multic ultural discourses of equity and inclusion, creating “pipel ines” into tech industries,  and increasing the num ber of underrepresented students of color and women in these fields (Cannady et al., 2014). However, these methods ultimately work in service of a White-supremacist, neoliberal framing, that places an emp hasis on CS for the needs of economic and militaristic growth (Vossoughi et al., 2016). A justice-centered approach to CS education challenges the corporate technology sector on moral, epistemological, and political grounds (Vakil, 2018), while actively resisting discourses that rationalize the importance of learning CS in terms of “national sec urity,” “defense,” or other ideological constructions that conceal nationalistic goals of war and empire (Vakil, 2018). This is the tradition we build our argument for LF in CS education on; we argue that to truly begin to ‘benefit the welfare’ of historically marginalized communities across the globe, there must be an emph asis on interweaving political critique into CS learning environments, towards creating worlds that do not enact the same types of physical and onto-epistemological violence and extraction that exists  under neoliberalism in the current moment (Morales-Doyle & Gutstein, 2019; Vakil, 2018). We organize our argument for Latinx Futurist just icecentered CSEd using Vakil’s (2018) call for a more critical and justice-centered CS education, where he charted three elements that, when made explicit, could push  forward a more critical approach to CS: ethics, identity, and political vision.

Representations of ethics in the curriculum should move learners from merely acknowledging that technology and CS have societal implications to ena cting a deeper understanding of the political impli cations of technologies (Vakil, 2018). Rather than being confi ned to notions of personal responsibility and choic e, here ethics are situated in larger conversations about t he role that new technologies, and CS as a discipli ne, play in reproducing, sustaining, or resisting social, racia l, and political hierarchies. This reformulation of  ethics in the curriculum should allow students to engage in critiquing the unethical abuses of technological power and explore the meaning of learning CS to contest and mitigate such abuses for social justice. Identity approaches to CS should be expanded from focusing mainly on students developing disciplinary identities as participants in CS as a field to expl ore “how to design learning environments that are r esponsive to students’ multiple social identities, including their civic and political identities” (p. 37). Here, political identity is understood not as alignment with partisan politics or involvement in formal democratic processes, but more broadly as an awareness of and commitment to addres sing societal power dynamics and inequalities (Nasi r & Kirshner, 2003; Yates & Youniss, 1998). It also inc ludes a sense of agency to envision and work toward  “a new and different world” (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2010,  p. 192). In the context of CS, political identity may signal the extent to which students are aware of both the benefits as well as the negative consequences of ne w technologies, a commitment to using scientific and technical knowledge to improve the human condition,  or a general awareness of how computer science as a discipline is interwoven with history, culture, and ideology. Articulating a political vision for CS challenges the taken-for-granted assumption that CS is primarily important for global economic competitiveness and n ational security (Vakil, 2018; Vossoughi et al., 2016). This view positions learners of CS as future workers, ma inly encouraged to streamline their education towar ds career opportunities in technology companies. A political vision for CS education anchored in justice is unwa veringly motivated by ethical and m oral commitments, where CS is enacted as a critical  praxis of peace and freedom in opposition to the development of technologies that advance the goals of militarism, occupation, surveillance, and expansionism.  Learners are thus encouraged to pursue CS as part of and connected to larger struggles for justice and liberation. In making the ethics, identity, and political vision explicit, CS learning environments can attend to the broader context in which they exist, challengin g dominant economic and militaristic rationale, and  forging a more equitable future.

Latinx Futurism(s) Visions of the future are often found in the realm of speculative and science fiction, though the domi nant imaginaries held in these genres often work to main tain whiteness and erase people of color globally (Thomas, 2019). The need to counteract this erasure is not only a  matter of representation within a genre, but of wh o is imagined to exist in the future at all. Afrofuturis m was one of the first interventions to the White-dominated imaginaries (Taylor, 2024), which push ed speculative genres not only to consider including Black and Afro-centric aesthetics in the future, but what it would mean to world-build around Afro-centric ways of being (Nelson, 2002). In conversation with this, Indigenous, Latinx, As ian, and Global South futurisms each add to the conversation to continue in this tradition of imagi ning futures from the standpoints of non-White, non-Western cultures (Taylor, 2024). These more specific instantiations of futurisms a re an assertion as much as an aesthetic: Latinx people, Black people, Indigenous people, and the Global South exist in the future (Taylor, 2024). We thus discuss Latinx futurisms as forever in conversation  with these other voices, particularly  Black and In digenous futurisms (Taylor, 2024). We, as Latinx people base d in the U.S., use LF here as a way to be specific about the cultural lineage that we draw from, including criti quing the ways Latinx cultural l ineages are implicated in anti-Indigeneity (Chon et al., 2021) and anti-Blackness (Hernandez, 2022). Latinx futurisms repre sent the future as imagined from the specific point of view of people from Latin America, the Caribbean, and diasporic La tinx communities (Merla-Watson & Olguin, 2017). Core ideas in LF challenge dominant narratives of progress and technological development, especially examining the  implications of indigenismo, mestizaje, and coloni ality, while opening up possibilities for more radical and transformative visions of the future (Merla-Watson & Olguín, 2017).

We employ LF here in a similar vein: to expand the imaginaries of what attention to ethics, political visions, and identities can mean for CSEd within th e specific world-building work. This work is already being taken up in studies of technology and Latinx commun ities more broadly, such as Cortez et al. (2024), w ho conceptualized AlgoRitmo Literacies as practices ro oted in a Chicanx lens, emphasizing critical reflec tion, ingenuity, and speculative imagination. They foreground how these literacies involve narrative movidas (Espinoza et al., 2018) that disrupt dominant frameworks of whiteness and cis-heteropatriarchy in AI, envisioning alternative Chicanx futures. Participants in their study engaged in reimagining historical events, portraying Columbus' arrival as an act of violence met with Indigenous resistance, thus shifting from a "discovery" narrative to one highlighting horror. By embracing fantastical storytelling and h orror aesthetics, participants moved beyond convent ional retellings, integrating decolonial perspectives ali gned with Indigenous temporalities (Cortez et al., 2024). These discursive shifts are strategic and subversive, cha llenging established social, political, and economi c norms. Through collaborative world-building, participants explored redesigning their city with Indigenous technologies, envisioning futures grou nded in cultural histories. This aligns with LF, wh ich, like Afrofuturism, reclaims speculative genres to center marginalized perspectives (Merla-Watson & Olguín, 2017). In previous studies within the learning sciences, (Ojeda-Ramirez et al., 2023; Ojeda-Ramirez & Shaw, 2024), have analyzed how Latin futurisms media actually engage with Community Cultural Wealth (Yosso, 2005) of Latinx youth towards re-storying and reimagining futures on their own terms. Specifically, through analyzing popular culture films such as Marvel’s Black Panther, which contains elements of both Afrofuturism and Latin American Indigenous futurisms, researchers, teacher s and students can enact polit ical critique of dominant futurities shown in media, while also rooting alternative visions in their own cultural knowledge. Such alternative futures, in the context of CSEd, are created throug h interrogating the dominant narratives about techn ology and the purpose of technological innovation (Ojeda-Ramirez & Shaw, 2024). For instance, we can see that narratives of technological innovation are weaponized against Latinx environmental justice organizing in south Te xas (U.S.A.),  as communities push for more oversight o n the environmental impact of Space Exploration Technologies Corporation’s (SpaceX) launch facility (Perez, 2023). Latinx Futurism for reflecting on ethics in CS Latinx Futuristic media, like Alex Rivera’s Sleep Dealer and the speculative comics by Faierman & Zela, delv e into the ethical implications of technological adva ncement, particularly its impact on marginalized La tinx communities. These narratives foreground critical i ssues of labor exploitation and displacement, chall enging the notion that technology is inherently neutral or ben eficial. In Sleep Dealer, the concept of cyber-braceros reimagines the historical exploitation of migrant l aborers during the Brace ro Program, where Mexican workers were subjected to harsh conditions in the U.S. Rivera’s speculative future envisions these cyber-braceros remotely connected to machines through bioelectrical nodes, performing labor from afar while enduring physical exhaustion and the threat of death from power surges. He re, “the machine” becomes a symbol of technological systems that extract labor without granting rights or protections, mirroring the real-world exploitation faced by Latinx immigrants in a digital age wher e technology is wielded as a tool of control.The fi lm also extends its critique to psychological exploitation. Luz, a char acter in the story, sells her memories by uploading  them for others to consume, leaving her emotionally drained.  Surveillance systems monitor her uploads to verify  their authenticity, pushing her to reveal  her most intimate experiences. This depiction criti ques the invasive nature of digital surveillance technologies that disproportio nately target marginalized communities, turning per sonal lives into commodities while deepening alienation.

Similarly, Samaras’ comic addresses the issue of fo rced displacement by depicting Latinx immigrants being sent into outer space under the guise of seeking a better life—a stark metaphor for contemporary narratives of forced migration and exclusion. The emotional to ll of this relocation is vividly portrayed as onloo kers watch the ships depart while "several bands along the lau nch area played celebratory selections from some in sipid playlist—I expected to hear ‘When the saints go marching in,’ but I don’t think they played it." The crowd includes immigrants who chose to remain behind, their faces reflecting a mix of "suffering and nervous joy." Families too fearful or defiant to leave dread their impending relocation to segregated “homelands,” described as "new ghettos where they would be sent to work, live, and die—scheduled arrivals that were weeks away. Their faces lacerated me" (Faierman & Zela, 2021). This narrative critiqu es techno-utopian promises by exposing how technological solutions can mask deeper systemic injustices, perp etuating displacement and exclusion under the guise  of progress.

Engaging with these Latinx Futuristic narratives al igns with the neo-Luddite approaches discussed by Logan (2024) and exemplified by Olguín (2016). Acco rding to Olguín, Sleep Dealer offers a critique of how advanced technologies can perpetuate labor exploita tion, using speculative media to explore the socio-political dimensions of technological systems. By highlightin g the exploitation of cyber-braceros and the psychological toll on characters like Luz, the film invites a critical examination of how technology candeepen social hierarchies rather thandismantle them. This form of media anal ysis becomes a powerful tool to interrogate the eth ics and social consequences of technological advancements, positioning speculative storytelling as a form of c ultural resistance.

Logan, on the other hand, extends these critiques into the realm of the Learning Sciences by advocating for a Luddite praxis that goes beyond traditional AI literacy. He argues that mainstream ethics discussions around AI are often co-opted by corporate in terests, turning complex social issues into superfi cial checklists that fail to challenge underlying power structures. Instead, Log an proposes that the Learning Sciences should empha size a critical interrogation of AI's socio-technical systems, focusing on how these technologies reproduce inequalities, particularly for marginalized communities. By engag ing learners in the critical analysis of speculativ e media, as Olguín demonstrates with Sleep Dealer, and framing educational practices around understanding and resisting exploitative technological systems, both scholars converge on a vision of education that prioritizes humandignity, agency, and social justice. This approach encourage s learners to not only question technological devel opments but also to imagine and build more equitable futures.

Latinx Futurism for reflecting on identity in CS In CS education, considerations of identity need to go beyond representation, equity, and diversity to substantively include “how matters of identity shape learning pro cesses and trajectories of engagement with CS conce pts and practices.” (Vakil, 2018, pg. 33) Taking up identity allows for linking learners to the larger social sphere(s) they inhabit, and recognizing how those identities shape  and are shaped by their participation in CS learni ng environments. This allows us to see how political and civic identities are intertwined with racialized and gendered identities, in that learners interact with their communities and cultures as much as they do with the broader sociopolitical spheres. Thus, all of these identities (a nd more) are at play as learners engage with CS. La tinefuturism offers visions for how learners can bring together their multiple politicized identities to engage with CS learning, towards imagining and building technologies that can support them and their communities. To illustrate how this may be done, we first discuss how a Latinx/e identi ty is political, followed by examples of how Latine futuristic works that highlight the ways that identity, politics, and technology come together as a re-envisioning of what CS education can be.

In considering Latinx/e as a political identity, we move away from biological or racial ac counts of Latinidad towards understanding who counts as Latin x, when, where, and why (Willis, 2020). In the case of the term Latinx with “x” in particular, Pelaez Lopez (2 018) conceptualizes this as a wound, invoking the m ultiple violences against immigrants, Indigenous and Afro-descendiente, queer, and femme people, within and b eyond Latin America and the U.S. Yet, this term is politicized in the U.S. because of how it is positioned as a neo-colonial term (Orbea, 2015). As we stated in the start, we take up this term b ecause it “disrupts the colonial omniscient force of Spanish and the perceived homog eneity of the Latinx population” (Gamio, 2016, p. 12). So, in discussing the usefulness of LF, we take up a co nversation of Latinidad for how it allows for solid arity across experiences within the multiple sites of colonizati on, struggle and liberation globally. Further, life  experiences across the spectrum of Latinidad can be valuable for thinking about the role that technology can play in our lives. In LF work, we can see the ideas of solidarity and politicization in tech being taken up in Carnaval, where the main character states that "Even when the  machines took our place, none of them could do wha t my family did" (Taveras & García, 2021). In this state ment, he is recognizing that attempts to erase his family of laborers would never be complete because technology would not be able to replace them. He follows this with the statement that "Just like cycles, we came back. Uni dos" (Taveras & García, 2021). In emphasizing the togetherness of the community, and the cyclical nat ure of oppression that Latinx people face, Carnaval presents a vision of solidarity that can be seen as a politicized intervention on the individualized nature of computer science education. Similarly, Worldbuilders (Estrada & Hinojosa, 2021), follows Esme, a video game coder and designer who is hired to write code for a bio-technological machine. Esme is hired for the game m echanics she imprinted onto her previous job as VR-AR developer, making a game full of love for the co mmunity, that is why she was chosen to be the new coder to create an algorithm t hat optimizes the resources of the ecosystem in whi ch these plants, animals and humans live. Esme is tasked specifically to “write the code that balances the needs of all—the flora, fauna, and people that make up Ma'ya'ab." In  the collective work, we can see how connection to the environment and community is central to Esme’s work, and her knowledge of technology is used in servic e of these larger aims. In both of these examples, we can s ee that the characters’ identities as Latinx people influence how they interact with technology. For example, both of these characters emphasize the importance of collectivity rather than individualism in their work, which serves as an intervention on tech nology’s assumed individualistic framing.

Through these examples, we can see how CS education is implicated in future-making. How Esme takes up her knowledge of video game design to enact comm unity-based ecological care is at once a political, ethic al, and cultural commitment to using her knowledge for the community. In turn, we can also see how LF offe rs a vision for how identity matters to CS education. Le arners’ identities are a mediating factor in how th ey take up CS, while normative CS learning environments often reproduce existing injustices instead of recognizing the ways that learners seek to have their experiences, commu nities, and identities matter. As we discussed here, the ways LF envisions identity expands on justice-centered goals of having political identity recogni zed in the same ways as racial, gendered, (dis)abled, identities. In the next section, we explore political visions of CS education wherein Latinx peoples bring about their own futures, pushi ng back against the “marginalization, limited acces s to resources, and colonial policing of knowledge [which] have historically suppressed Latine voices, silencing their epistemic contributions” (Santos, 2006).

Similarly, in the Latinofuturistic comic Emisaria (Blas & Rosales, 2021), we encounter a future where Earth is uninhabitable, and scientists stationed on  an international space station discover a new plan et populated by an advanced pacifist race. Here, the protagonist  persuades the council against colonizing the newfo und civilization, instead assuming the role of an emiss ary to facilitate communication based on mutual res pect. This narrative opposes traditional colonial models of ex ploration and dominance, instead advocating for dia logue and coexistence. A pivotal scene encapsulates this shif t in mindset: "Simply going to another inhabited pl anet and claiming it as our own is definitely not something we should do." The struggles that we see here are of consequence t o CS education, especially in imagining what ends CS can be used for. Such visions in LF encourage La tinx youth to reflect on alternatives to dominant n arratives of technology. Another example is the comic  Crossroads of Infinity (Bravo, 2021), which imagines a hyperloop transportation system across the Americas, connecti ng Mexico City to Ontario, Canada, and crossing the  U.S. in mere minutes. This technological marvel, as Bravo (2021) describes, is a “landmark achievement, the first major milestone and catalyst for the era of global hyper-transportation…Human connection across all communit ies, nations, and continents became not only accessible,  but unavoidable.” The hyperloop underscores techno logy's potential to break down physical and political borders, ma king mobility and family unity central technologica l goals. The poignancy of this narrative is illustrat ed in scenes where, as vessels depart Earth, celebr atory music mixes with the complex emotions of families witnessing the launch. For some, it’s a moment of trauma, while for others, a defiant hope emerges. This vision of tech nological infrastructure transcends conventional bo rders, emphasizing connection over control.

Across these works, we see the imagination of Latin x communities combine with political critique to envision technological futures where they are not o nly present, but at the forefront of change. Throug h each of these examples, we can see how embedded in LF stori es are spaces for technology to be directed towards liberatory goals. As vision for justice-centered CS education, we argue that LF offers mult iple ways of thinking about the goals of technological innovation beyond typical “progress” oriented narratives, and is ther efore a powerful starting point for future learning experiences.

## Discussion and conclusion

Across these works we discussed the relationship between technology and LF for CS learning, but this is only one potential vision for how these can interact. In LF,  Latinx youth find resources to contest oppressive systems and envision futures rooted in liberation, resistance, and community resilience. These cultural production s provide them with philosophical tools to critically engage with and reshape computer science, steering it toward a discipline that values justice and equity. Through LF, they cultivate a political vision that reclaims  technology’s potential to be a force for collective empowerment,  pushing back against the forces that  have historically used technology as a tool for oppression.

Importantly, while any of the contexts we described  from the visions of the authors and artists could be spaces for learning CS, they are not explicitly marked as such. In future work, imagining what theses spaces might look like will be crucial, and is in line with curr ent work in imagining what otherwise CS education m ight look like (Jones & melo, 2021). This work of bringing together principles and articulating a vision for justice-centered CS learning provides an orienting vision for what c ritical, justice-oriented CS learning environments can look like.
