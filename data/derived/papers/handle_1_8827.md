# “I’m Still Learning”: Experienced Mathematics Teachers’ Development of Adaptive Expertise Amidst the COVID-19 Pandemic

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In 2020, the global COVID-19 pandemic disrupted education across the country, requiring a re-organization of instruction on a large scale. This study examines the experiences of highly-committed secondary mathematics teachers in a large urban U.S. public school district as they shifted their instruction online for an entire year. Using the framework of adaptive expertise – processes and dispositions that can enable teachers to extend their knowledge to novel situations – this paper contrasts the cases of three teachers’ development of adaptive practices over time. Drawing on data gathered from semi-structured interviews, participant observation, and content analysis, we discuss how teachers’ engagement in the processes of identification and negotiation contributed to their adaptive orientations. By documenting how highly-reflective educators adjusted their instruction to respond to unprecedented teaching demands, this study contributes to the field’s understanding of adaptive expertise in mathematics teaching.

In March 2020, school districts across the country spontaneously moved all instruction online as the COVID-19 pandemic spiked. Teachers who had spent years honing their craft in the classroom were forced to learn a different job. As a field, we know little about the processes through which experienced teachers continue to learn to adapt their existing knowledge tonew and novel situations. This study explores the ways that secondary math ematics teachers developed adaptive expertise to meet the demands of their various novel teaching contexts. To do so, this paper builds on an existing four-year partnership with a highly-selective professional development organization for mathematics teachers, most of whom teach in a large urban school district in the Western United States. These teachers demonstrate strong commitments to their ow n and their students’ learning and occupy resource-rich professional networks. By working closely with math ematics teachers and documenting their responses to teaching in the midst of the pandemic, this study contributes to educational practice and research. Practically, this study offers examples of the different ways teacher s developed adaptive expertise in their unique lear ning environments. Theoretically, this is a case study of adaptive expertise as a process of mathematics teacher learning, contributing to the field’s understanding of how te acher learning environments can be designed to supp ort the development of experienced teachers’ adaptive exper tise in post-pandemic learning contexts. Specifical ly, this study examines the question: How are well-resourced, experienced secondary mathematics teachers in a l arge urban school district transforming their teaching during the COVID-19 pandemic?

## Theoretical Framework and Prior Work

Adaptive expertise We take a situative view of teacher learning, a subset of sociocultural theories rooted in Vygotsky’s (1978) early work (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Because of its attentio n to learners-in-context, it is possible to leverag e this perspective to investigate teachers’ transformations and adaptations, in the context of the COVID-19 p andemic. Teachers were thrust into mandated online teaching, bringing about entirely new demands that required them to move beyond the application of existing knowledge. Faced with a novel situation, teachers had to make a situational adjustment (Becker, 1964) – rather than  completely reinventing their practice, teachers ma de adjustments, changes, and choices between situationally feasible alternatives, choosing actions that best meet their professional demands and expectations as well as align with their professional, ethical, and personal commitments. This kind of problem solving supports the construction of knowledge, making this context a compelling place to study teacher learning.

The unique context of pandemic teaching lends itse lf to specifically examining teachers’ development of adaptive expertise. Drawing on ideas about adaptive  expertise in teaching (Crawford et al., 2005), ada ptive expertise can be described as flexible and innovative and marked by the ability to extend current understandings to meet the demands of novel problems or atypical contexts. This stands in contrast to what has been described as routine expertise (Hatano & Inagaki, 1984), marked by speed, efficiency, and automaticity but does not necessitate flexibility or adaptability tonew situations. Adap tive expertise is a stance on knowledge and practice: adaptive ICLS2022 Proceedings 464 © ISLS experts approach new situations flexibly and learn throughout their lifetimes (Bransford et al., 2000). Furthermore, adaptive expertise is not only a way to describe ho w teachers have changed their practice, but it also  motivates their own learning, as they recognize when rules and principles do not apply to situations, seeking in stead to develop new practices (Gott et al., 1992).

Crawford et al. (2005) describe several necessary d ispositions and skills that teachers must possess i n order to develop adaptive expertise, adaptive practices – an instructional practice that is evolving in order to meet the demands of a novel situation – and to be considered adaptive experts. Particularly relevant to this study is an inclination to acknowledge and even push the limits of one’s ability while also assessing the adequacy of current knowledge or practices for meeting the needs of par ticular problems. Cognitively, adaptive experts loo k for evidence to inform their plans, seek causality as well as feedback, monitor the results of their decisions and their own learning, and assess their own and the field’s knowledge for addressing the situations at hand. Th ese dispositional and cognitive tendencies support a ba lance between efficiency and innovation in teaching. On the one hand, by having and relying on developed routines, adaptive experts can move through predictable aspects of their work smoothly. On the other hand, by sensitiz ing themselves to the feedback that things are not going as expected, they can seek new forms of practice roote d in well-understood principles. This conception of  teacher learning implies that knowledge is flexible and contextual and that teachers’ ongoing learning is responsive to a changing context and dynamic community instead of being a set of skills or static knowledge. Adaptive expertise in teaching and learning has bee n studied in a multitude of contexts: exploring different conceptions of mathematics student understanding (Hatano & Inagaki, 1984); characterizing reasoning and problem solving processes that enable adaptive experts to continue to learn in adult professional settings (Bransford et al., 2000; Feltovich et al., 1997; Gott et al., 1992); and describing the epistemic/dispositional aspects of adaptiveness and the habits of mind involved in science teacher professional development (Crawford et al., 2005). This body of work has evolved over time to recognize that the content or characteristics of learners in any context are not static over the course of teachers’  careers. However, this research fails to discuss h ow adaptive expertise is developed.

Identification and Negotiation In keeping with the goal of understanding teachers’  developing adaptive expertise from a situative per spective, we draw on Horn et al.’s (2008) conceptions of iden tification and negotiation in their discussion of p reservice teacher learning. Specifically, this paper is in alignment with their view of identity: Identity, as an analytic construct, provided access  to both individuals’ dispositions and the environments they encountered. Because accounts of identity necessarily provide for the description of individuals, it helps us see the peo ple we are trying to understand. At the same time, identities are constructed through culturally  available descriptors, narratives, and archetypes, embedding and linking the individuals in the contexts around them. (p. 62) Viewing identity as both individual and contextual provides insight into the ways teachers’ pre-pandem ic, inperson teaching identities evolved during mandated online teaching as they adapted their existing know ledge through processes of identification and negotiation. Identification is the process through which teachers integrate new understandings into their existing identities while negotiation is when teachers modify their existing identities based on new understandings. Together with adaptive  expertise, the processes of identification and neg otiation serve as a rich theoretical basis through which to investigate experienced teachers’ transformation du ring the COVID-19 pandemic.

The ideas of identification and negotiation are similar to the cognitive, adaptive processes of assimilation and accommodation (Piaget, 1970). In their discussi on of identification and negotiation, Horn and coll eagues

(2008) extend Piaget’s ideas in their work with pre-service teachers. Liking identity to Piaget’s conc ept of

cognitive structures, they consider the ways preservice teachers maintain or transform their identities in the face of external elements. This work has since been taken up by many scholars – primarily the idea that teacher identity formation involves the interweaving of the individu al and the social (eg. Beltman et al., 2015; Stenbe rg et al., 2014). Together with adaptive expertise, the processes of identification and negotiation serve as a rich theoretical basis through which to investigate experienced teac hers’ transformation during the COVID-19 pandemic; this analysis seeks to connect teacher identity – namely  identification and negotiation – with the ideas on  adaptive expertise to gain understanding on how adaptive expertise can be developed. ## Methods

This study extended a four-year ethnographic study of secondary mathematics teachers in urban public schools, conducted in partnership with a professional development organization (PDO). Teachers were recruited from the PDO, resulting in a convenience sample of 8 teachers (Gerson & Damaske, 2020).

## Research Context

In mid-march 2020, the first documented COVID-19 ca ses appeared in the United States, including the ci ty in which the PDO teachers work. Several days later, di strict leaders emailed teachers that schools would close for two weeks, initially with no mandated online learni ng. In the midst of this initial closure, teachers in this study were notified that the closure was extended through  the end of the 2019-2020 school year and that cont inued online learning was mandated. In July 2020, all teachers in the district were notified that the 2020-2021 academic year would remain virtual indefinitely. The effects  of COVID-19 on schools have had a global reach – internationally, teachers found themselves in similar positions as teachers in the US and in our study. Unique to this study, some of the burden regarding safety as well as untenable working conditions in the shift t o online teaching was eased by the strong local teachers union as well as effective district leadership. One teacher, Amber (1), who was the union representative at her school described the extensive time the union spent negotiating over the summer for continued online instruction in the 2020-2021 school year with school administrators and district leadership: “I think we didn’t get everything that we asked for, but we never will. I think we ended in a place that was doable for what we were about to start” (Interview 2, August 2020) – indicating the strength, influence, and support of the union. Furthermore, district leaders hip prioritized teacher and student safety throughout the pandemic. The teachers in this study did not experi ence some of the same uncertainties that others acr oss the country have, such as having access to vaccines to go back to school in person or not having access to adequate professional development to learn new technologies (Cornish, 2021). The specific sources of uncertaint y that remained – like how to be a good online math teache r – presented opportunities to study the developmen t of adaptive expertise. For example, one of the focal teachers, Linda, explained “the fact that [reopening during the 2020-2021 school year] hasn’t been a part of the na rrative-it hasn’t been a huge piece of the convers ation has made everybody else feel better and feel like they could focus more” (Interview 4, March 2021). This supportive local context freed up emotional stress and physical time for teachers in this study to focus on transforming their teaching, making it an ideal location for the study  of adaptive expertise. Further, this particular st udy context is compelling in the way that everyone felt like an amateur, or outsider. In interviews, experienced teachers discussed feeling like first year teachers all over again due to the overwhelming novelty of mandated online teaching. This dynamic provided a space ripe for exploration as traditional boundaries of expert-novices in research were blurred.

## Participants

The participants for this study were determined through theoretical sampling and convenience sampling (Gerson & Damaske, 2020). First, participants were strategi cally recruited from the PDO. According to an exter nal evaluator’s report, PDO teachers typically experien ce higher satisfaction with professional learning, greater retention, and stronger student survey and test results than a matched control group. PDO teachers are passionate about and committed to their work, collaborate regu larly, reflect on their instruction, and are seen a s leaders in their schools. Recruiting exclusively from this PDO  ensured that the teacher participants would be exp erienced and well-resourced.

In May 2020, teachers from the PDO were given the o ption to volunteer to participate in the study. Of the 80 teachers in the PDO, 8 elected to continue to participate. Due to this participant selection, we conceptualize this study as a best case study (Yin, 2017) for sev eral reasons. First, the eight teachers in this stu dy were all experienced teachers with 15 years or more teaching  experience. They were seen as leaders in their sch ools or departments and were highly-reflective, continually seeking out opportunities to learn. Second, these teachers had a strong community of support through the professional development organization. They all reported collaborating regularly with both school-based teacher teams and colleagues in the organization. Lastly, the teacher s in this sample were members of a strong teachers union and feel supported by a data-driven district superintendent. By conceptualizing this sample as a best case sample, the findings from this study can be extended to con sider the contextual circumstances that enabled participating teachers to develop adaptive expertise.

## Data Collection

Data for this study included interview transcripts  from all 8 participating teachers, fieldnotes from participant observations of 3 of the 8 teachers’ de partment meetings, and content analysis of relevant announcements from the district and national media coverage of education during COVID-19 (Altheide & Schneider, 2012). In this semi-structured interview  study, we conducted iterative cycles of data colle ction and ICLS2022 Proceedings 466 © ISLS analysis, starting with Interview 1 in May 2020 (Em erson et al., 2011). While the study concluded with  a final interview in June 2021, the data for this paper are drawn from four interviews across 8 participating teachers from May 2020 to March 2021, as well as content analysis  (Altheide & Schneider, 2012) and participant observation (Emerson et al., 2011). In between interviews, data  from previous interviews, content analyses, and pa rticipant observations were analyzed and used to inform subsequent interview protocols.

## Data analysis

Across-teacher learning analysis Aligned with our ethnographic approach (Emerson et al., 2011), data analysis centered participants’ own meanings. After each round of interviews, we identified teachers’ sensemaking via open coding, consolidating themes across the interviews (Charmaz, 2008). For instance, a common (and novel) dilemma emerged around engaging kids in synchronous online lessons: should teachers require that students’ cameras stay on? On one hand, cameras leave students vulnerable, since peers see into their liv ing spaces or screengrab awkward moments to post on  social media; yet without students’ faces, teachers struggle to gauge engagement. Discussing such dilemmas provided insight into developing adaptive expertise as teach ers deliberated on what made the most sense for dif ferent teaching situations. Cross-case comparisons enabled me to identify clusters of responses (e.g., choosing primarily cameras off) and to extrapolate conditions in which different instructional responses make sense. Within-teacher learning analysis Alongside group-level analysis, we analyzed individual teacher interview data and fieldnotes from part icipant observations of department meetings to understand how each teachers’ local, school-based contexts supported the development of adaptive expertise over time. We hypothesized that different teachers’ constraints and affordances make identification or negotiation more sensible, t hus shaping their learning. For each teacher, we co ded for statements that provided insight into their pre-pandemic teaching identities. For example, when descri bing his reliance on humor during in-person teaching, Ezio described “I'll toss out a corny math joke and I'll laugh at my own joke, and then the kids will laugh… so that's h ow my classes normally are” (Interview 2, August 20 20). When we coded for identification, we looked for evi dence that teachers were integrating new understand ings gained from mandated online teaching into their existing, or pre-pandemic, teaching identities. When coding for negotiation, we looked for evidence that teachers had shifted or modified their pre-pandemic teaching identities based on new understandings.

Comparative Case Analysis Throughout the data collection and analysis process, we looked at the data across all 8 participants. For this paper, we focus on three of the teachers – Linda, Brad, and Ezio – because we found that they all adapted differently. By looking across these three cases, we can get a sense of how adaptive expertise is developed. Specifically, we will discuss how Linda limited her development of adaptive expertise by not engaging in meaningful identification or negotiation, how Brad developed adaptive expertise primarily through identification, and how Ezio deve loped adaptive expertise through negotiation.

## Findings

To explore the ways experienced teachers developed adaptive expertise, we investigated the ways that t eachers responded to the dynamic and novel context of online teaching during the COVID-19 pandemic, illustrated in the following three cases. In this study, we found that the teachers’ learning  and development of adaptive expertise was contextual and dynamic. Specifically, some teachers were able to develop adaptive expertise by engaging in identity identification or negotiation while others  teachers’ development of adaptive expertise was li mited. For each case, we will describe the teachers’ pre-pandemic, or in-person, teaching identity. We will then describe the teachers’ engagement with identification or negotiation in their attempt to adapt their current understandings. We will conclude with a description of the ways each t eacher developed adaptive expertise or was limited in their development.

Case 1: Linda, Limited Development of Adaptive Expertise During the study, Linda did not engage in identification or negotiation because of discordance between elements of her pre-pandemic teaching identity and the demands of online teaching. In interviews, Linda often d iscussed how the physical space of her classroom contributed to her pre-pandemic identity: “I have built a very intentional learning space that has particular things on the wa lls. It has particular arrangement of the tables” (Interview 3, November 2020). She often referenced the importance of physical materials in her teaching – “I teach very driven ICLS2022 Proceedings 467 © ISLS by the stuff on the table kind of thing” (Interview  2, August 2020), referencing yardsticks, string, s cissors, and other physical materials she typically had available on student desks. Her reliance on the physical resources in her classroom was rooted in her pedagogical commitment to learning being physical, embodied, and experient ial. Being unable to access these resources during online teaching left Linda at a loss for how tonegotiate the demands of online teaching.

In interview transcripts, we found no evidence that  Linda engaged in identification by integrating new understandings into her existing pre-pandemic identity. Because the physical space and materials were no longer available to her in virtual teaching, she couldn’t integrate new understandings into this element of h er existing identity. This was evidenced when she said “I’m rea lizing how much they’re not learning because they’r e not marinating in the environment” (Interview 3, November 2020), or when she discussed not knowing how to teach trigonometry: I am absolutely at a loss for what the heck I’m going to do. Really, really confused because for the last four or five years, we start trig by actua lly building a trig table on the board and they don't know that's what they're doing. But then, at their tables, they're given a bunch of index cards with different angle degrees marked on it, and they're going to mark that angle and then they build a right triangle. And they measure, and we just record all of these ratios and build this giant thing by hand up on the board and then start analyzing it. And it's like, I can't get kids to draw stuff because I don't know what equipment they have. They have no protractors. They have no rulers. (Interview 3, November 2020) Linda described confusion about how to teach trigon ometry because she didn’t have the physical materia ls of protractors and rulers available to them.

When looking for data that supports negotiation, we looked for evidence that Linda modified her existing identity based on new understandings. However, Linda often framed negotiation as not worthwhile. For example, she expressed a lack of desire to invest time in learning new technologies: “I just don’t care that much about it to invest umpteen oodles and oodles in learning like every new widget, whatever” (Int erview 3, November 2020). Further complicating her dilemma, “the idea of having this co-constructed, giant thing that we do in person, right? … I don’t want to let go of it entirely because trig is confusing” (Interview 3, November 2020). Her pre-pandemic identity was rooted in the physical materials in he r classroom and tied to her conception of being a g ood math teacher. At the same time, it was incompatible with her online teaching practices. Therefore, Linda was unable to reconcile her pre-pandemic identity with the demand s of online teaching by engaging in identification or negotiation, ultimately resulting in limited development of her adaptive expertise. She recognized this limitation when she explicitly described how unsuccessful she felt teaching online: I feel now that I know this is not me at my best, I know this is not-the Linda in the little box is not as good as Linda in person… I’ve never felt so incredibly inadequate and I don’t, I don’t know that I would go so far as to say a fraud but c ertainly so far from who I want to be as a teacher. (Interview 3, November 2020) Case 2: Brad, Developing Adaptive Expertise through Identification Before the pandemic, facilitating whole class discu ssions was a significant facet of Brad’s identity. He valued conducting whole class discussions driven by studen ts’ current understandings, motivating his monitori ng of student work and intentionality in guiding students’ discussions. He was still able to act on this part of his identity during online teaching: “Kids… still feel like they still have a teacher in front of them, and still allow[s] the teacher to do teacher moves in terms of looking at their responses, filtering the ones that we can have a discussion about, pausing them, and then we’re going to discuss it” (Interview 1, April 2020). While he was successful i n incorporating new practices and adapting his discussion practices through identification, Brad did not engage in negotiation – he was able to adapt to online teaching without negotiating his pre-pandemic teaching identity. To investigate the ways in which Brad engaged in id entification, we looked for evidence that he had integrated new understandings into his existing identity of building and eliciting student understandings in whole class discussions. We found that Brad continually d eveloped new understandings of a technology platfor m, Desmos, and integrated those new understandings to maintain his existing identity. Desmos is a free technology that hosts digital classroom activities designed for students to “explore [math] concepts more deeply, collaborate with their peers on problem-solving, and apply knowledge creatively as mathematicians” (Desmos.com). Teachers can also develop their own classroom activities cat ered to their specific students and content while u tilizing the tools built into Desmos for student exploration and collaboration. In person, Brad reported only using Desmo s for specific lessons that were few and far betwee n. In interviews, Brad described the new ways he utilized  Desmos to meet the demands of online teaching incl uding enabling certain features like non-math check-ins a nd modifying the in-person curriculum to make it mo re accessible to students at home, spending 10-16 hours a week converting in-person lessons to desmos. For example, he said: What does it look like lesson planning is basically  thinking from what my lesson would look like in class and they can get into Desmos and enabling features that I think would make it easier for students, cutting out questions that I think wo uld only be able to be done with same manipulatives or say if they were in a group settin g where I could be supporting them a little more or things like that. (Interview 1, April 2020) He continued to explain the ways that Desmos connec ted his in-person teaching identity with the novel context of pandemic teaching: “that’s been my mindset, is how can I keep my lessons engaging, make [the students] learn while also figuring out the best ways to allow me to still make it feel like a classroom” (Interview 1, April 2020). Brad’s pre-pandemic teaching identity involved teac her moves like looking for, listening to, and discu ssing students’ thoughts and understandings, and transforming all of his lessons into Desmos and learning how to utilize more features enabled him to preserve this existing identity. Brad’s engagement in identification contributed to the development of his adaptive expertise – he extended his knowledge to meet the demands of onlin e learning. Namely, he spent time incorporating new understandings of the applications of Desmos to make it possible – even easier – to facilitate the type of discussion he valued in person: It's like when you're hovering around a class-Desmos is actually easier to facilitate discussion than when you have a clipboard and you're like, “Ooh. James, number two you said something really cool, that's really creative.” I'll call on James for question number two. I can see it right in front of me at all times, all the things kids ar e not understanding and things that kids are understanding and things that kids are solving in unique and creative ways. While he adapted his teaching practice, he did so in ways that left his existing identity intact. Supported by Desmos as a mediating tool, Brad was able to adapt his practice to meet the demands of online teaching in ways that aligned with his identity rooted in eliciting and giving feedback on student understanding. Case 3: Ezio, Developing Adaptive Expertise through Negotiation In our final case, we found that Ezio attempted to engage in identification and deve loped adaptive expertise through negotiation. Prior to mandated online teaching, Ezio deeply valued discovery-based learning. At the same time, his teaching identity included not being a technology person. He reiterated this many times in i nterviews, saying things like “technology is not my thing” (Interview 1, May 2020). For example, in Interview 3, Ezio says “I was never the tech person. I was the old school-I had no problem-give me a chalkboard, I feel per fectly comfortable with a chalkboard and chalk” (Interview  3, November 2020). Ezio was settled in who he was as a teacher, and salient to him, this did not include incorporating technology. Initially, Ezio attempted to engage in identificati on by recording in-person style lectures and resist ing incorporating new technology: “This virtual world is new to me, so it was just safer. Just stick with what you do. Go ahead, lecture the whole time, lecture the whole hour” (Interview 3, November 2020). However, he came to recognize that maintaining this aspect of his identity ultimately limited his ability to adapt to online learning in a way that made him feel successful: And even though it wasn’t effective, I guess as the days or weeks wore on, then it started getting to me. Okay, this isn’t working. You’re not doing your job, you’re just lying to yourself when you’re doing that. (Interview 3, November 2020) Ezio felt particularly unsuccessful in the ways online teaching did not meet his expectations rooted in his value of discovery-based learning – he did not feel like he was meeting the needs of his students or demands of  online teaching.

Namely, he extended his knowledge to include new technology in response to his realizing that online teaching was making him feel unproductive. For example, he said “I’m us ing Desmos a lot more in my classroom… I just feel more productive now. I have something to build from. Not that I’m doing everything perfect in Desmos, but I’m learning as I go along” (Interview 3, November 2020). Initially, Ezio identified as “not a tech person” a nd attempted to engage in identification to adjust his practices in line with this identity. However, he then negotiated this aspect of his identity to become the “tech pioneer” of his school, further adapting and transforming his practice to meet the demands of online teaching.

## Discussion

This study provides insights into how the field can  better understand experienced teacher learning thr ough the concept of adaptive expertise. Theoretically, this paper contributes to a richer understanding of adaptive expertise by examining its development through the lens of identification and negotiation. Practically, this study highlights the ways that supportive district leadership, stron g union presence, and resources for collaboration p rovide a context for experienced teachers to reflect and adapt tonew and novel situations. While we certainly hope that the findings are not used to inform teacher learning du ring a future international pandemic, we conceptual ize this study as an extreme case (Yin, 2017) for understand ing how teacher learning environments can be design ed to support the development of adaptive expertise in less extreme, post-pandemic learning contexts. In this spirit, the findings contribute to the fields’ understanding of teacher learning processes by investigating the different ways that Linda, Brad, and Ezio engaged in identification or negotiation in response to the dynamic and novel context of online teaching. Aligned with our situative view of teacher learning, each case highlighted the contextual and flexible nature of teachers’ knowledge. Specifically, we have described how Brad and Ezio developed ad aptive expertise by engaging in identification and negotia tion while Linda’s development of adaptive expertis e was limited.

We found that all teachers in this study were supp orted to attempt adaptations to online teaching by the insulated support of district leadership as well as the influence and presence of the teachers union. By removing many external uncertainties, teachers were able to devote their focus on their teaching practice. This  kind of support can be valuable in creating learning environments ripe for ongoing teacher learning; whether in a global pandemic or not, teachers negotiate countless institutional and local demands. We show that experienced teachers’ sensemaking and learning can be better supported by alleviating these stresses. There is also a lot to be learned from our focal teachers. Brad and Ezio both developed adaptive expertise in different ways as they incorporated Desmos into their online teaching practice. Although Desmos aligned with Brad’s existing identity, supporting his identifica tion, Desmos did not align with Ezio’s existing ide ntity. His attempts to integrate online teaching practices into his existing identity made him feel unsuccessful,  motivating him to seek out something different and engage in negotiation. Similarly, Linda too felt unsuccessful in engaging in identification. Her lack of engagement in the process of negotiation indicated that this was a place in which she may have benefitted from additional support.

Taken together, our three focal cases illustrate how teacher learning can be studied and supported through the lenses of identification and negotiation and un derscore the centrality of identity. On the one hand, when teachers’ identities are thoughtfully considered, t eachers can be supported by resources that align wi th their ICLS2022 Proceedings 470 © ISLS identities to foster identification. On the other hand, teachers may alsoneed to be supported tonegotiate aspects of their identity that are incompatible or unproductive with their particular teaching context. It is important to note that the pre-pandemic identities described in this paper are not the teachers’ only identities. Our co nception of identity as socially and contextually constructed (Horn et al., 2008) suggests that the identities of these teachers are multifaceted and dynamic. Indeed, the teachers in our study both developed adaptive expertise and were limited in this development with respect to the identities described here, but they also exhibited entirely different adaptive practices with respect to other facets of their teaching identities. Further research is need ed to explore the interplay of the multiple elements of experienced teacher identities and how they contribute to ongoing teacher learning.
