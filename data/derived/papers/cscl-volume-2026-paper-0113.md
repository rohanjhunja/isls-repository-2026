# At the Intersection of Cognitive Engagement and Critical Thinking in AI -Assisted Learning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examined how cognitive engagement levels, defined by the ICAP framework, relate to critical thinking in student-AI interactions. Analyzing 784 student questions submitted to a GPT-4-powered chatbot in an introductory biology course, we coded each question for ICAP level and applied Newman et al. ’s (1995) critical-thinking indicators. ANOVA results revealed significant differences across ICAP categories: Constructive questions yielded the highest critical-thinking ratios, followed by Active and Passive. However, Constructive questions were mostly clarifying or confirmatory, rarely exhibiting evaluative reasoning. These findings suggest that higher ICAP engagement predicts higher critical thinking, but students often remain at explanatory rather than evaluative levels when interacting with LLMs. The findings shed light on the need for instructional scaffolds that promote both cognitive engagement and epistemic vigilance.

As generative AI tools become increasingly integrated into education, researchers emphasize the need to move beyond answer-seeking toward deeper learning and critical thinking. LLMs can provide instant, tailored responses, but their educational value depends heavily on how students engage with them (Jošt et al., 2024). A key concern is that students may over-rely on AI-generated information and accept responses uncritically, hindering higher-order thinking (Darvishi et al., 2024). In LLM-based learning, students—not instructors —drive the questioning process, making question depth a central determinant of outcomes. In our prior work (Ding et al., 2026), student questions to ChatGPT were coded using ICAP, finding that higher-performing students asked significantly more Constructive questions while lower-performers asked more Passive ones. The present study extends this by examining whether different ICAP question types also correspond to differences in critical-thinking quality, using Newman et al.’ s (1995) framework. We address two research questions: (1) How does question type relate to critical thinking quality in s tudent-AI learning interactions? (2) What patterns of critical thinking indicators emerge within Constructive questions?

## Literature review

Wittrock’s (1992) Generative Learning Theory (GLT) holds that learning depends on what learners mentally construct rather than passively receive. Building on GLT, Chi and Wylie’ s (2014) ICAP framework classifies cognitive engagement across four modes, Interactive, Constructive, Active, and Passive, each representing an increasing degree of generative processing, with empirical support showing that higher-mode engagement produces stronger learning outcomes (Menekse et al., 2013; Morris & Chi, 2019). Student questioning is a key driver of this engagement. Explanation-seeking questions reflect higher-order reasoning, while factual questions signal surface-level processing (Graesser et al., 2003), mapping directly onto ICAP’s Constructive/Interactive and Passive/Active modes, respectively. Structured scaffolds are needed to shift students toward deeper questioning (King, 1994; Chin & Osborne, 2008), and this is equally true in AI-mediated contexts, as Cheng et al. (2025) found that prompt quality significantly shapes student performance with GenAI. Critical thinking is essential in these contexts precisely because LLM outputs, though fluent, may be inaccurate or biased (Cain, 2024). Learners must practice epistemic vigilance, assessing the reliability and coherence of AI-generated information rather than accepting it uncritically (Sperber et al., 2010; Bielik & Krell, 2025). Calibrated trust is the goal: neither overtrust that yields passive acceptance of misinformation, nor excessive skepticism that forecloses productive AI use. This study was conducted in an introductory biology course with an enrollment of 140 students at a southeastern U.S. university, of which 85 voluntarily participated in the study (58.8% female, 39.2% male, 2.0% non-binary; 46.1% White, 24.5% Black, 7.8% Hispanic, 4.9% Asian). After the first exam in week 5 of the 16-week course, students discussed their incorrect answers with a custom GPT-4o biology tutor chatbot, and a total of 784 pairs of interactions were generated.

659 Each of the student question s was coded using an ICAP rubric (Ding et al., 2026a) as Interactive, Constructive, Active, or Passive, then independently coded using Newman et al. ’s (1995) critical thinking framework. The framework consists of ten positive indicators (e.g., relevance, justification) and ten negative indicators (e.g., repetition, uncritical agreement), which can yield a CTRatio where higher values indicate greater critical reasoning. Two trained research assistants (RAs) first independently coded a randomly selected 10% of the questions and met with the first author to discuss any discrepancies and then coded another set of 10% randomly selected questions and reached a high consistency in their coding. The remaining 80 percent of the data was divided in half, and each RA coded one half independently.  Due to the fact that only t hree Interactive questions were asked by students, they were excluded from the analysis. ANOVA with post hoc LSD comparisons examined CTRatio  differences across ICAP categories, followed by a deeper indicator-level analysis of Constructive questions.

ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of question type on CTRatio, F(2, 781) = 32.70, p <.001, partial η² =.14. Constructive questions had the highest mean score (M =.57, SD =.54, n = 129), followed by Active (M =.34, SD =.56, n = 279) and Passive (M =.07, SD =.39, n = 373). Post hoc LSD comparisons confirmed all group differences were statistically significant (p <.001).

Deeper analysis of the 129 Constructive questions (387 total codes) revealed that most were characterized by relevance (R+) and clarification-seeking (A+), indicating students stayed on topic and sought to resolve ambiguities. About half introduced new problem-related information (NP+), yet nearly one-third contained judgments without justification (JS−), proof/examples (J+) appeared in only 0.8% of questions, and critical assessment (C±) was never coded, suggesting students engaged generatively but rarely reached evaluative reasoning (see Table 1).

Constructive prompts produced the highest mean critical-thinking scores, indicating that higher cognitive engagement was associated with deeper reasoning. However, the mean CTRatio for C questions (M =.57) is still modest. Most Constructive questions were relevant (R+) and clarifying (A+), demonstrating that students were actively verifying understanding and addressing ambiguities. Yet nearly one-third exhibited judgments without justification (JS−), providing proof/examples (J+) was one of the least used codes, and critical assessment (C±) never appeared, suggesting a tendency toward reasoning-seeking rather than reasoning-producing discourse. These patterns align with Graesser et al. (2003) that learners often use explanation-seeking questions to confirm knowledge rather than generate new inferences. Our results suggest that even when students constructively engage with the LLM, their engagement does not always reach higher levels of critical thinking.

This finding implies that Constructive questioning may plateau at an explanatory level rather than extending toward evaluative reasoning or critique. This could be because students treat GenAI as an authoritative source (Ding et al., 2026b), making their questioning confirmatory rather than scrutinizing. Given the hallucination effect of LLMs, it is dangerous to interact with LLMs without epistemic vigilance. These dynamics underscore the dual challenge of fostering cognitive engagement while cultivating skepticism toward AI-generated explanations, highlighting the need for future work to examine how to design instructional training that will engage students cognitively with LLMs for their learning and foster students’ epistemic vigilance.
