# Exploring Pre-service Teachers’ Co-Design of Learning Environments with Generative AI: Effects of AI Support on Instructional Design Quality

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines the impact of support provided by generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) on the quality of pre-service teachers’ instructional design (ID) outcomes – lesson blueprints. An experiment was conducted with one control and two experimental conditions: 1) producing ID outcomes with a generic GenAI bot (ChatGPT), and 2) producing ID outcomes with a Co-instructional designer bot customized with a single ID model (Systems approach). Participants included 78 pre-service teachers from diverse fields such as behavioral sciences, physics, and architecture. ID outcomes were blindly analyzed by experts in ID and evaluated using a pairwise comparison technique, where two ID outcomes are holistically compared to determine which one is better. The findings reveal a significant improvement in the Co-instructional designer condition compared to the control, suggesting that a GenAI bot can support pre-service teachers in ID when customized with an ID theory.

Aims of the study Some educational researchers suggest viewing teachers primarily as designers and treating teaching as a design science (Goodyear, 2015; Laurillard, 2012). This perspective stems from the understanding that, to increase the chances of student learning, teachers need to carefully design learning environments (e.g., define learning objectives, design and sequence learning tasks, design support mechanisms) (Laurillard, 2012). However, empirical research has found that teachers rarely engage in an explicit instructional design (ID) process or rely on ID theories, such as ID models (Bennett, 2017; Kerr, 1981). Hence, the teacher’s role as a designer of learning environments exhibits a paradox: on the one hand, it is considered critically important; on the other hand, it is largely neglected in practice. This can be explained by different factors, including, but not limited to, a lack of formal training in ID (Kerr, 1981) and the time-consuming nature of the ID process (Dick et al., 2015). In this study, we aimed to explore whether GenAI can support teachers in their often-overlooked but essential role. Collaborating with GenAI during instructional design GenAI has the potential to assist teachers in ID by providing structure, ideas, and resources, as well as formulating learning objectives and suggesting relevant teaching methods (Choi et al., 2024). However, it remains unknown whether GenAI support will benefit pre-service teachers, given that they are novices in ID and might struggle to critically evaluate the output of GenAI (Estaiteyeh & McQuirter, 2024). ## Research question and hypothesis

Our research explores how different types of GenAI support (no support vs. generic GenAI vs. GenAI bot customized with one ID model) influence the quality of ID outcomes. The research question addressed is: What are the effects of different types of GenAI support on the quality of ID outcomes? We hypothesized that the quality of ID outcomes produced in the Co-instructional designer condition would be higher than those in the control and ChatGPT conditions, given that the bot was built to guide the design process in a systematic manner. Participants. Participants were pre-service teachers, pursuing their Master’s degree in Teaching across different domains (e.g. behavioral sciences, architecture, physics). A total of N = 78 participants took part in the study, with a mean age of M = 26.46 (SD = 6.12, Mdn = 24). Female participants comprised 58.97% of the sample. Materials Task Participants were tasked with designing a two-hour lesson about a historical figure of their choice, focusing on any phenomenon associated with that figure (e.g. Socrates and the Socratic dialogue). The task was intentionally designed to be open, allowing participants to define their own learning goals and objectives. Script All participants received a nine-question script to help them focus on the design phase, which included questions like, “What is the overarching aim or purpose of this instruction?” and “What specific outcomes should your students achieve?” Co-instructional designer bot The Co-instructional designer bot was developed using ChatGPT Plus and the GPT Builder. The Dick and Carey ID model (Dick, 2015), also known as the Systems Approach, was selected as the ID theory. This model consists of ten steps for designing instruction, beginning with identifying learning goals and writing performance objectives. The bot guided future teachers through the steps of the model and suggested relevant ID components. Participants were expected to provide feedback on the suggestions, discuss each component, and give final approval before proceeding to the next step. A bot-led co-design process was chosen due to participants’ limited familiarity with ID.

## Data analysis

Three ID experts independently evaluated the outcomes without knowing their associated conditions, using the comparative judgement method (Verhavert et al., 2017). They compared pairs of ID outcomes holistically to determine which was better, using the Comproved tool (https://comproved.com/en/). Raters 1 and 2 each completed 260 comparisons, while Rater 3 completed 83, resulting in an inter-rater reliability of 0.74, indicating substantial agreement. Through Comproved, all 78 blueprints received a quality score. A one-way ANOVA was conducted to examine whether quality scores differed significantly across conditions (independent variable: condition; dependent variable: quality score), followed by post-hoc tests to identify specific group differences. A one-way ANOVA showed significant differences between conditions (p = 0.027), with post-hoc tests revealing higher-quality ID outcomes in the Co-instructional designer condition (Exp. 2) compared to the control. in the Co-instructional designer condition compared to the control, confirming the hypothesis, while no such effects were observed between the ChatGPT and control conditions. This may be due to the participant-led nature of the ChatGPT interaction, which relied on users’ prior knowledge. In contrast, the Bot condition followed a scripted, feedback-based process that may have supported all participants more evenly. Further research is needed to examine how ID model choice and interaction type (participant-led vs. bot-led) influence the quality of codesigned learning environments.
