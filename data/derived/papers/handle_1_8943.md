# #WhiteRibbonAgainstDictatorship: Youth Participation in Thailand’s 2020-2021 Political Movement

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
A large number of Thai high school students participated in pro-democracy protests on all levels: from organizing rallies to small symbolic actions such as wearing white ribbons during 2020-2021. We engaged 691 Thai students from diverse backgrounds to write a letter to a recipient on the other side of the political divide to examine this phenomenon. Participants were asked to reflect upon their hopes and fears for Thailand's political future and explain why their civic engagement is personally, socially, and politically important. Utilizing the ladder of youth participation and other succeeding frameworks in the field, we explored the high school students' perception of their youth participation: their aspirations, justification for their participation, and responses from the adults in their lives. We find that while the students have clear and well-articulated reasons for their participation, they are hindered and resisted by their parents or teachers who disagree with their political views.

## Introduction

Current Thai youth (aged 12-18) have grown up in this political change and polarization. Resentment towards the junta government had been brewing for almost a deca de. This dissatisfaction exploded in protests, many  at university campuses ('Student Flash Protests,' 2020). Student-led protests revived not only at the uni versity but also at the high-school and middle-school levels. These students expressed their solidarity with anti-government protests creatively, such as wearing white ribbons and raising the three-finger "Hunger Games" salute during the national anthem (Wongcha-um, 2020). A hashtag that translates as #WhiteRibbonAgainstDictatorship was used nearly 1 million times on Twitter in August 2020 (I n Pictures:'Thai Students "Hunger Games" Protests,' 2020). The situation escalated after a mass rally on October 14, 2020, when the government declared a state of emergency in Bangkok. A series of warrants and arrests of stu dent leaders inflamed youth anger and pushed high-s chool students to be more politically active ('The names of 15 student protest leaders', 2020). While the United Nations (UN) has fully recognized young people's right to freedom of expression in the 1991 Convention on the Rights of the Child and scholars have developed theoretical models for yout h participation, there is a paucity of research that focuses on Thailand and its current political insurgency. This study examines youth participation in Thailand and embeds their experience within the conceptual framework of youth participation and the broader scholarship on youth and politics in Asia. We assigned a non-graded home work called "A Letter to Bridge the Divide" to approximately 700 high school students representing Bangkok and three regions in Thailand. These students were asked to write letters to friends, teachers, or adults with different political views. We have worded the assignment's prompt so th at those who disagree with the protests can also wr ite to fellow students to explain their views. Participants' students will reflect upon their hopes and fears for Thailand's political future and the reason why the movement is vital to them. These key experiences may involve but are not limited to past and current political turmoil, social inequality, generational gaps, and students' future aspirations.

## Literature Review

Youth Participation Checkoway (2011) defines youth participation as a p rocess of involving young people in the institution s and decisions that affect their lives. It requires active engagement and the real influence of young people, not a passive presence or a token role under adult agencies. This  study utilizes Hart's (1992) "Ladder of Participat ion" framework to determine how much adults in Thai soci ety engage with young people, classifying participa tion hierarchically across eight levels using each rung of a ladder as a representation. Hart (2008) later emphasized that framework is about the degree to which adults and institutions enable children to participate (p.23). Thus, the ladder portrays different degrees to which children are allowed, enabled, and supported (often by adults) to initiate civic actions and make decisions in carrying them o ut with others. In addition to Hart's original ladd er, we also utilize Reddy and Ratna's (2002) work which extends  Hart's ladder framework beyond the "Model of Nonparticipation'. They suggest two additional bottom rungs of non-participation to Hart's model: "Hindrance" when ICLS2022 Proceedings 1377 © ISLS adults intentionally or unintentionally undermine c hildren and discourage them from participating, and  "active resistance," in which adults actively work against children's participation. The reasons for active re sistance resonate with Checkoway and Richards-Schuster's (2006) argument that young people are too often displaced and disengaged from the process by adults, even in their schools and communities, because adults may feel that young people should not be involved in decision-making processes because they cannot do so. "It is difficult when adults view young people as deficits rather than resources or show 'adultism' which flows from the assumption that adults are better than young people, and entitled to act u pon them without their agreement because of their a ge" (Checkoway, 2011, p. 342). Adultism can lead young people to question adults' legitimacy and suppressi on. Moreover, viewing young people through a deficit lens can stifle their potential to change. Thailand 2020-2021 Youth Participation Much research remains to be conducted on Thailand's 2020-2021 youth participation in politics. McCargo (2021) has conducted one of the first scholarly surveys on the current wave of youth participation. More in-depth studies by Sinpeng (2021) and Lertchoosakul (2021) shed lig ht on different aspects of the movement. Sinpeng (2 021) analyzes the use of Twitter in the current political movement. She argues that social media is mainly used to air grievances and categorizes the tweets into six topics: democracy, youth rights, education, government opposition, the economy, and the Thai Monarchy.  On the other hand, Lertchoosakul (2021) interviews university and high school students at protest sites. She finds that di ssatisfaction with the authoritative and conservati ve education systems, corruption, and inequality in Thai society are the main reasons driving the protests. Lertchoosakul (2021) also emphasizes that Thai youth participation is motivated by grievances against political institutions, particularly the monarchy. Our study extends previous scholarship by engaging Thai students at schools--not the protest sites nor Twitter, whose opinions may not represent all s tudents. It highlights Thai high school students' p olitical participation and embeds this experience within the conceptual framework of youth participation and the broader scholarship on youth and politics in Asia.

## Methodology

This study was a qualitative research project. We reached out to six teachers from six different public schools in various parts of Thailand, who agreed to participate in the study and assigned "A Letter to Bridge the Divide" as non-graded homework to their high school students. The assignment asked participating students to write a letter to teachers, parents, adults, or peers who hold different political views while reflecting upon their hopes and fears for Thailand's political future and why the movemen t is vital to them. The teachers were responsible f or disseminating and collecting the assignment in thei r classrooms. The students have the right to opt-ou t of the research or leave the homework blank without conseq uence. Students' names, genders, and protected characteristics were anonymized, and thus the recruitment process did not exclude participants based on gender, class, or race. The homework was assigned to roughly 1,200 students from the six schools. Since the homework was non-graded and anonymized, 691 students turned in the assignment. To analyze the letters, document analysis was emplo yed to examine the specific language used by the participants in the study to understand their hopes  and fears for Thai political insurgency (Creswell,  2002). We addressed the coding based on the conceptual framew ork used for the study (Saldana & Omasta, 2017). Specifically, we used behaviors and characteristics of Hart's (1992) Ladder of Participation and Reddy and Ratna's

(2002) Adult-Children Engagement as initial codes t o develop themes. We looked closely at segments of the

letters related to the participants' 1) self-percep tion of current political situations, 2) experience  with civic engagement, 3) experience with generational clashes and adult engagement, and 4) hopes and fears for t he Thai political future.

## Findings

This section investigates how Thai high school stud ents perceive their political participation. It is important to note that not all students support the protests. Roughly around 27% of the letters indicate their neutral stance by writing: "I'm not taking sides;" "I'm neutral and reasonable;" "I understand that a lot of my friends want to express their stances. I think it's their right to do so. I don't think we can always agree, but we have to respect each other. It's natural in a democratic society." Less than 3% are supportive of the government. Thai high school students view their freedom of exp ression as one of their inalienable rights. Two hundred nine students (30%) explicitly mention their "rights" and "freedom" to political expression. For example, a student from eastern Thailand argues, "FREEDOM OF  SPEECH [capitalized by original source] is a human right by law and international standards. It means that we can express or communicate our thoughts and opinions through words, action, or writing." Since freedom of expression is their fundamental right, the students just want ICLS2022 Proceedings 1378 © ISLS the space to speak their minds. For instance, a Bangkok student states, "We are not asking for very much. What we're doing is exercising our rights. And I believe this right is not causing anyone any trouble." Moreover, the students believe that the policymaker s should listen to their concerns because they are taxpayers and thus can hold politicians accountable for their actions. Though most young people do not yet pay income taxes, they point to VAT (value-added tax) t o contribute to the country's economy. Forty-three students (6%) explicitly mention taxes in their letters. According to these students, since they pay taxes, they have a right not only to question how the government spends public funds but also to demand better infrastructure and welfare from the state. The students believe that their taxes should be used to improve people's well-being. Still, the current government is not spending these funds transparently or effectively. Since young people are almost universally denied th e right to vote, Thai high school students believe they can exercise their rights through symbolic actions, such as the Hunger Games three-finger salute or wearing a white ribbon. Since their civic participation is primarily symbolic, the students feel that they are  not harming anyone and are well within their rights. For exampl e, a student writes, "Wearing a white ribbon and th e threefinger salute have no physical effect, do not break any rules or laws, do not hurt anyone, involve no weapons." Many students expressed dismay at the resistance to  their political participation and what they feel a re violations of their fundamental rights by adults in  their lives. Three hundred five students (44%) exp erienced generational clashes and/or misunderstandings with their parents and teachers. At the fundamental leve l, Thai students feel that it is only "natural" to have a v ariety of political opinions. A student from Bangko k asserts, "Having different political opinions is normal because everyone has different ideas and viewpoints." Similarly, a student from the Northeastern region writes, "It is  normal that even when [people] are brought up in t he same environment, they can have different views only in different periods." Some students have cited a rule from the Ministry of Education that allows political expression in schools and question why their teachers are not abiding by these regulations. In their letters, the students recount how teachers and school administrators have infringed upon their rights to political expression. For example, some teachers reportedly threaten to deduct grades or have explicitly banned the three-finger salute in school. A teacher from eastern Thailand even allegedly sl apped a student for wearing a white bow to class. While the students are demanding real changes from the government of General Prayut, their expectations of their families and teachers are different: the students are not trying to change their elders' minds. Instead, young people want the adults in their lives to keep an open mind and lis ten to what they have to say. For example, a student writes, "We want the people who disagree with us to understand us. Not for them to switch sides, just to understand what we are protesting."

## Discussion and Conclusion

The students' beliefs in their unalienable rights and their criticisms of the Prayut regime motivated and justified their political participation. Our findings underscore that the students were not merely acting out or  misled into holding rallies or wearing white ribbons. We thus argue that Thai students were operating at high levels of Hart’s (1992, 2008) and Reddy and Ratna’s (2002) youth par ticipation models-despite adult resistance and unwillingness to communicate (further discussed below). The students can be placed on one of the highest le vels of Hart's (1992, 2008) model of engagement: "children initiated and directed." The contents of the letters indicate that Thai students' beliefs in their fundamental rights and experiences fueled their actions-meani ng that they were initiated by the students themsel ves. The students believe strongly in their freedom of expression and rights as democratic citizens. Therefore, they should voice their opinions and concerns against what they perceive as inappropriate, whether corruption, ineffective use of tax money, or unjust democracy. As mentioned above, this political participation was also by students in many forms: from large protests, such as the ones outsid e the Ministry of Education in August 2020 (Lertcho osakul, 2020), to everyday symbolic actions, like raising the three-finger salute and wearing white ribbons. We thus gain insight into students' political parti cipation beyond traditional forms. Studies in vario us countries have challenged the dichotomy between "apathetic" teenagers and activists (Fisher, 2012; Harris et al., 2010). These scholars find that young people in Australia and the United States engage in "everyday participatory practices" that reflect their political views-suc h as recycling, signing petitions, and supporting o r boycotting businesses. Our findings concur with these arguments. Thai students wearing white ribbons to schools or raising the three-finger salute during the national anthem have incorporated their political participation into everyday life. They have found a new way to participate in Thai politics. Those who participated were not all passionate activists who organized rallies and gave speeches at protest sites but were not politically apathetic. The different forms of participation further substantiate that the students were operating at the "child initiated and directed" level. These political actions weren’t something mandated by an authority figure. Students had the independence and decisionmaking abilities to take action in ways that were within their limits and means, regardless of the economic situation, availability, and comfort level.

A high number of stude nts writing of misunderstanding and generational cl ashes (44%) are made to feel inadequate and/or unfit to v oice their political opinions by the adults in thei r lives, thus being "hindered" from participation. According to the students' letters, adults viewed them as "children"; therefore, their role was to study, not solely participate in political movements. This resonates with Reddy and Ratna's (2002) findings when some adults believe that children don 't have the capacity to participate and hence canno t make informed choices for their actions. Adults under th e "active resistance" category tend to hold a stron g position against youth participation and mobilize their resources to lobby against it. This is seen in the case s of teachers deducting marks from politically active students or  even going as far as being verbally and physically  abusive. Overcoming this resistance will be a necessary step toward bridging the generational gap. A 16-year-old student nicely sums up solutions for the current generational clashes, "We should talk with facts and reasons that would lift the country up. Both sides should listen without bias. Reasons should come before feelings. [...] Age shouldn't be a factor. No matter how old you are, we should be able to express our opinion freely." Our study holds significance in showcasing the motivations of student political participation, particularly in Thailand. The messages from the Thai students' l etters are universal and almost too apparent. These  young people find issues with the status quo, believe that their rights to express their discontent are irrefutable, and are driven by their aspirations to secure a better future for themselves. Therefore, those in power everywhere should at least heed, if not welcome young people's political opinions and let them truly have a voice in society.
