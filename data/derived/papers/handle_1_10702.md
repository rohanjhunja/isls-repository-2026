# Teacher and Student Perceptions of Co-Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Co-learning is a novel hybridization of teacher professional learning and student out-of-school learning, wherein students and teachers collaborate and learn together. Because of its novelty, both questions and skepticism exist about co-learning as a useful professional learning model for teachers and an out-of-school learning model for students. We believe it offers great promise, and thus, this study is a first step at exploring teachers’ and students’ experiences with co-learning to define its strengths and limitations. In this paper, we share insights gained from teachers and students about their experiences co-learning during the Cellular Construction Workshop.

## Introduction

We have developed a hybrid teacher professional learning and student STEM out-of-school learning experience, the Cellular Construction Workshop (CCW), that models an integrated STEM learning environment. The CCW is a two-week summer workshop that provides an innovative in troduction to cellular engineering, computational thinking (CT), and engineering design by  utilizing robots as a model of cells. The novel piece here is that  high school teachers and students work together as co-learners throughout the workshop. In this co-learning model, adults are not the providers of all knowledge, the roles of teacher and student are blurred, and all are engaged as active learners.

A teacher-student co-learning model that integrates CT, biology and engineering design practices We start from the premise that authentic, integrated science-engineering experiences promote active engagement and a rich understanding of CT, engineering design,  and biology for teacher and student participants. The theoretical perspective that underlies our approach is situated learning, which considers learning a process of enculturation into a community of practice that develops knowledge, skills, and identification with the community (Lave & Wenger, 1991). In this perspective, knowing and learning are fundamentally social. Students and teachers gain knowledge and construct their interests and id entities through meaningful engagement, experience,  and practice (Aschbacher et al., 2010).

The co-learning model connects high school science teacher s with students to collaborate and learn together. Kermish-Allen and colleagues (2015) described a similar mod el, which they termed non-hierarchical learning in informal settings. In co-learning, knowledge is co-constructed in a “dynamic, multi-directional process involving all participants” (Kermish-Allen & Kastelein, 201 7; 2018). Co-learning draws from situated learning (Lave & Wenger, 1991), Place-based education (Gruenewald & Smith, 2008), Knowled ge Building Theory (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006), and finally, Funds of Knowledge (Moll et al., 1992). The co-learning process acknowledges that all participants (students, teachers,  and workshop instructors) have differing expertise  and backgrounds and that each has something to learn and teach others (Kermish-Allen & Kastelein, 2017; 2018). Colearning allows participating teachers to observe s tudents in an out-of-school learning setting, which may enable them to develop a deeper understanding of where and  how students experience difficulty in a learning s ituation. Moreover, the opportunity to interact with students in this way may help teachers better understand their students’ cultural expression of and perspectives on learning and gain insights on how to teach students from backgrounds different than their own (Ladson-Billings, 1995; Gonzalez et al., 2005; Villegas & Lucas, 2007). Co-learning also allows teachers to experience the pedagogy as learners, which will provide insights into the joys and chall enges of learning in this integrated STEM project-based learning environment. Finally, co-learning helps teachers gain confidence to bring new pedagogies into their own classrooms to engage students in more authentic practice. For participating students, co-learning provides an opportunity to observe and interact with teachers in an out-ofschool setting, which may alter or shift  their perceptions of teachers. In addition,  co-learning provides an opportunity for students to participate and be reco gnized (by their peers and teachers) for their comp etence in STEM activities, which is key for developing science/STEM identities (Carlone et al. 2011). While co-learning shows promise as a professional and out-of-school learning model, there is a dearth of research studying this type of experience. We know very little about the co-learning experience, i.e., what is it like for a teacher to be a co-learner with students and no longer be in a position of authority? What is it like for a student to be a co-learner with teachers? Is the shift in power dynamics troubling for teache rs or students? Are teachers able to make the transition from teacher to learner? This study is the first step in exploring teachers’ and students’ experiences in a co-learning professional and out-of-school learning program. The research question s of primary interest in this study are: (a) What are teachers’ perceptions of co-learning? and (b) What are students’ perceptions of co-learning?

## Method

This qualitative case study includes data from end-of-workshop surveys and follow-up semi-structured interviews with teacher and student participants. The data that we share are drawn from a larger stud y that documents and explores the influence of the CCW experience on teachers’ and students’ learning and STEM identity development. The data for this paper include findin gs that focus specifically on participants ’ reflections and insights about their experience with co-learning.

## Context and participants

The CCW was held in June 2023 on the campus of a university in Northern California. Participating students were 10th or 11th graders attending a public or public charter school  from a local school district and were individuals who identify with communities historically marginalized in STEM. There were 15 student participants, 14 of whom agreed to participate in the study. Participating teachers taught a STEM subject at a public or public charter school in one of seven local school districts. There were five teacher participants in the CCW, and all five teachers agreed to participate in the study. Co-learning teams were comprised of three students and  one teacher. Team membership varied across activities, so teachers and students had the opportunity to collaborate wit h different individuals throughout the two-week workshop.

## Data collection and analysis

Surveys were administered to participants on the la st day of the workshop to solicit their input on th eir experiences. The survey was completed by all five teachers and all fourteen students. Teacher and student participants were emailed following the workshop to request their participation in a 30 –45-minute interview via Zoom. Interviews were conducted with all five teachers and eight of the 14 students. Interviews were transcribed and then imported into ATLAS.ti for analysis. We used structural coding (Saldana, 2 021) to categorize participants’ responses to survey and interview questions, follow ed by in vivo coding (Saldana, 20 21), which prioritizes and honors participants’ voices, to code the survey responses and the interview transcripts.

## Findings

What are teachers’ perceptions of co-learning?

Teachers were asked to respond to the following question on the end-of-workshop survey: “How do you feel about having teachers and students participate in this le arning experience together? What do you think worke d well? What was challenging?” In addition, to probe further into teachers’ percep tions of their co-learning experience, we followed up with the same question during the post-workshop interview. We identified three key themes in teachers’ survey and interview responses: (a) teachers found it to be a unique and enjoyable experience, (b) teachers were comfortable learning with and from the students, and (c) teachers found it challenging to stay in “student-mode” while co-learning.

Co-learning is a unique and enjoyable experience Overall, teachers expressed favorable views of the co-learning experience. On the end-of-workshop survey, one teacher wrote: “It was interesting! It was both challenging and fun ! Very different to sit back and be a learner!” During the post-workshop interview, Janet described her experience in the following way: “Working with kids is fun. It is very different, sitting back and being like equal. That was different.” Co-learning provides an opportunity to learn with and from the students While one might expect that a teacher would not be comfortable learning with and from students, this was not the case with this group of teachers. On the post-workshop survey, one teacher said: “I loved learning from the students. They come in with so much knowledge and everyone had a common goal.” Another teacher stated: “This is the first time I have been in a workshop with th is model of learning alongside students. I think it  was an interesting way to approach problem solving in areas that I didn't have experience, because they were able to be experts and teach me more about programming, while I could help with my biology background.”  During the interviews, Shannon described how she was very comfortable not being the expert: “…Some teachers might feel like they don’t want the students to know more than  them in terms of programming, and that like, they might feel uncomfortable in the workshop because of that…but I don’t. I don’t have that problem. I’m totally fine being like, ‘add it in,’ ‘you teach me.’” Francine shared similar views as Shannon, saying, “I enjoyed…being able to collaborate with students in a way that it felt lik e I was a student as well. And I think there was po wer in that, in the students like advising me on things.” Much like Francine, Michael felt that being on equal footing with the students was beneficial not only for him but also f or the students as well: “So it wasn’t really like a teacher-student relationship where they thought I had all the answers, or like I had to like take charge of the group…I felt like they were pretty empowered to, like, mess up and not know how to do things and not be that awkward around me.” Staying in “student-mode” during co-learning is challenging Four teachers described instances where they debated whether or not to stay in “student mode” as a fellow co-learner or to exert their  authority as the adult/teacher in the group. While working with her group on the final robot programming challenge, Janet encountered this  dilemma: “…so maybe sitting back too much. I don’t know…there was some issues with deciding what robot  [to use], and basically, the person who was going to be the leader didn’t step up. And I’m just like, Do I do it? Do I not? Like, what do you do?”  For Shannon, a similar issue arose wherein she felt forced to step back to  not over-assert herself into the group’s working process: “…I kind of had to disconnect a little bit because I didn’t want to come out and be really forceful to be like ‘You need to do this!’…But, that was a little hard for me to just be like they’re not listening to me. But I wasn’t in teache r mode, so I had to let that go.” In Francine’s case, she actively fought against students’ expectations that because she was a teacher, she would know the answers: “I found myself using my teacher comments when I no ticed that student-to-student collaboration could have been better…There was an instance where the student would only talk to me because they saw me as like the adult. But then I caught myself saying, just like, ‘Oh, I think so and so has an idea as well, like, have you asked them?’” What are students’ perceptions of co-learning?

Students were asked to respond to the same question s the teachers responded to on the end-of-the-workshop survey and in their follow-up interview. We identified t hree key themes in students ’ survey and interview responses: (a) having a teacher on the co-learning team was helpful, (b) learning with teachers can be enjoyable, and (c) the co-learning experience changed their views of teachers. Co-learning with teachers is helpful Students felt it was helpful for their learning to have the teachers actively participating in the co-learning groups. As one student stated: “It worked very well because they helped us, but also, we helped them.” Another student said: “I think having a teacher work with students is a v ery good idea. The teacher would introduced  [sic] their already known knowledge of biology and the students would express their ideas and nuances. It was a good matchup.” Another student stated:  “Teachers also have a different perspective and can  really help during projects. ” Similarly, a third student stated: “…it was nice having a more knowledges [sic] person actively in our team.” Co-learning with teachers is enjoyable As one might expect, some students expressed some initial hesitancy at the thought of working with the teachers. However, as Emma stated during her interview, once the initial awkwardness passed, she really enjoyed working with the teachers: “But I think after a while, once you warm up to eac h other, it's really fun to work as a group with a teacher…. I think once we warmed up, I really enjoyed that…It was awkward, but after a while, it became fun.” For other students, part of the enjoyment of the co-learning experience simply came from learning more about the teachers and their lives: “Getting to know, like from where they came and wha t they do, where they work at. It was nice.” (Andy).

Co-learning changed their perceptions of teachers A perhaps unexpected  finding from the students’ responses  was that the co-learning experience changed their perceptions of teachers. As stated by Lily: “I think my overall view of them just changed a bit … I learned that they were more human, I guess.” Similarly, Anna stated: “To be honest, working with teachers like it made me see them like, not as teachers like, also, like, I guess someone you can talk to like as a friend, I guess, like it's kind of weird. But it's also kind of cool.” For Isaiah, the co-learning experience was one in which he not only saw the teachers differently but actually stopped thinking of them a s teachers: “…there was a point where I didn't really like still think there were teachers. Like for-for me, it was still like we're all just here learn ing the same thing. This is new to all of us.”

## Discussion

These findings provide valuable insight into the teacher-student co-learning experience. Both teachers and students embraced co-learning, resulting in the boundaries between teacher and student being “blurred into one cohesive community of actively-engaged learners” (Kermish-Allen et al., 2015, p. 3). For both teachers and students, their co-learning experiences may impact their future teaching and learning experiences. Co-learning may prompt teachers to develop a deeper understanding of where and how students struggle in a classroom setting, promoting deeper empathy with their students and reflection on how to address these issues in their future teaching practice. For students, their new found perceptions of teachers may help them develop stronger connections with future teachers (and other adults). This may be especially importan t in fostering ongoing participation in STEM and building stronger STEM identities.  This study was meant to be just the first step in e xploring co-learning as a professional learning model for teachers and as a STEM out-of-school learning model for students. While these findings have provided some initial answers, future research should continue to explore how to best structure and facilitate this type of co-learning experience so that it is a fruitful learni ng experience for both teachers and students.
