# Build Resilience in Knowledge Building Communities: Teacher’s Growth Mindset Oriented Feedback to Support Grade 5 Students’ Yearlong Science Inquiry

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines students’ social-emotional experience with knowledge building pedagogy. The goal of this study is to understand how student interest, persistence and growth mindset oriented feedback sustain students’ inquiry and discourse. The data source includes video-recordings and transcripts of nine classroom meetings. Deductive and inductive approaches to data analysis were applied to analyze the class recording data. The findings suggest that teacher’s growth mindset oriented feedback in the knowledge building community engage students social emotionally to face and overcome inquiry challenges to achieve community knowledge advancement.

## Introduction

Education in the 21st century needs to play a proactive role in preparing and supporting young students to become virtuous individuals who can think and take actions  in ways that can adapt to  changes and deal with complex challenges. Students’ attitudes and responses toward  various learning challenges may shape students’ mindsets (or habits of mind), which  can have a longer impact on their personal development, positi vely or negatively, outside of school and into their adulthood  (Yeast & Dweck, 2012). “Resilience—or whether students res pond positively to challenges —is crucial for success in school and in life” (p. 3 02). Yeast and Dweck (2012) define being resilient as having “any behavioral, attributional, or emotional response to an academic or social challenge that is positive and beneficial for development (su ch as seeking new strategies, putting forth grea ter effort, or solving conflicts peacefully)” (p. 303).

Knowledge Building (KB) represents a pedagogical innovation that cultivates children’s ability to deal with authentic challenges through collaborative, id ea-centered work (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014).  Sustaining effort for idea improvement is much more challenging than simply generating ideas, so educators should develop a disposition in students to keep working on idea improvement (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). The principles of Knowledge Building pedagogy are designed to challen ge students to work collaboratively and persistentl y on advancing their knowledge and understanding. Such efforts require students to exercise resilience. Yeast and Dweck (2012) argue that a growth-mindset can lead students to be resilient in the fa ce of challenges and adults’ message to students can affect their mindset. Teach er can offer feedback to students in a knowledge building community (KBC) in a way that support  students’ growth-mindset and resilience. Therefore, how a teacher in a KBC offers feedback to the students  who are new to KB to engage them socially and emotionally with the new classroom changes and challenges, is worth being investigated. Therefore, the current study investigates how a grade 5 teacher offered growth mindset oriented feedback to sustain students’ KB inquiry and discourse.  The research question is: How does the teacher scaffold and offer feedback to students to support their cognitive and social emotional engagement to sustain their effort and deepen their knowledge building inquiry?

## Classroom context

Mr. B has over 20 years of teaching experience  and works in an elementary school  the northeast U.S.  He had implemented the KB pedagogy and technology in his science classes for the past three  years. Students of two fifth-grade classes participated in this study. Each clas s had 23 students. Prior to each year’s classroom observation, parental and children’s consent forms were sent to parents and students. Only the student s with consent were included in this study.

The students studied 2 units  in this school year:  Water and Ecosystems. At the beginning of each unit, there was a “kick-off” event. After the kick-off event in the Water unit, Mr. B introduced knowl edge building pedagogy and the Idea Thread Mapper (ITM) to studen ts. At the beginning of their knowledge building jo urney, Mr. B did a direct teaching to share with students about their local water system. Students posted their ini tial wondering questions about water on ITM and formed t hree big wondering areas: “Where does fresh water c ome from”, “How do people get the water they ne ed”, and “How do humans affect water? How to treat water?”  The classes did water transportation experiment and water filtration experiment in this unit. During the water unit, the school invited a guest speaker to give a presentation on local water resources reserve, transportation, and filtration. Mr. B led students to have metacognitive meetings after their experiments and the presentation. Data sources and analyses Mr. B’s science classes  were videotaped and nine  class talks were chosen to be transcribed and analy zed. Deductive and inductive approaches to data analysis  were applied to analyze the class recording data.  The following steps were followed to complete the data analysis of class recordings. First, nine classes’ video recordings were transcri bed verbatim. The video files of 9 class recordings ranged from 19-30 minutes. And the word count for the recording transcripts of all 9 classes is 31,620 words. Second, the researcher  read the recording transcriptions. This round the researcher  read each class recording transcript carefully to get some general ideas of the common themes that respond to the research question.

Third, the researcher re-read the class transcriptions by focusing on coding  Mr. B’s talks exclusively. Then the 10 class transcriptions were printed out t hat only had Mr. B’s talks in them re-reading and coding was done. Each class transcription was read for at least another five times to code Mr. B’s talks. Fourth, the raw codes from both Mr. B’s talks were further reviewed and linked to develop salient themes. For Mr. B’s talks, the final coding scheme are 14 codes, and these codes were conceptualized into 3 themes. The last three steps above were repeated enough times to make sure that the data analysis was thorough and bias free as much as possible.

## Findings

The findings suggest that Mr. B offered instructions and feedback in a KBC that oriented students to have growthoriented feedback that led to resilience. Mr. B  had offered consistent instru ctions and feedback that focused on establishing and strengthening students’ beliefs th at they were capable of being open-minded and respectful to their peers and their ideas, being non-judgmental and able to face failures and take risks; Furthermore, Mr. B consistently and persistently offering students gro wth-mindset driven feedback that continuously challenge students to think harder, think deeper, and strive to make idea improvement and inquiry advancement. Theme 1: Discussing and modeling classrooms norms The first theme was identified through qualitative coding of Mr. B’s scaffolding and feedback that he used to establish and follow through social norms for knowledge building that resulted in four major codes. Nurturing an open and non-judgmental attitude toward diverse ideas Mr. B made effort to nurture an open and non-judgmental attitude in students towards diverse ide as through creating a sense of safety that allowed students to feel safe and comfortable to contribute ideas, to not afraid to be judged and not to judge others. In the first class, Mr. B told students that when they had metacognitive meetings, students’ participation “doesn’t go in any particular order, we don’t follow an order” and students do not need to raise their hands in order to talk. He said, “You can just, as long as no one’s talking, you can shout out”. Modeling a non-authoritative stance toward knowledge that welcomes student input Mr. B modeled a non-authoritative stance towards knowledge in the class room and he frankly shared with his students that he did not have all the answers to students’ questions, and it is their job to figure out their “wondering areas”.  Mr. B also told students that he did not hold an au thoritative role but was equal to students in terms  of doing knowledge building inquiry. For example, when  students discussed about how underground pipes transported water, Mr. B responded, “I don’t know  what the process is. If I was going to tell you, I’d be lying. I don’t know what the process is”. On the other hand,  Mr. B conveyed and practiced the idea that student s should have equal opportunities to contribute to a metacog nitive meeting. When Mr. B saw that some students  have not contributed, he would make suggestion for giving opportunities to those students to talk. Practicing respectful disagreement and constructive criticism Mr. B required students to respect peers’ input by asking them to “be polite to one another” in the first class. Mr. B explicitly taught students how to respond to idea s they agree or disagree.  He encouraged students t o build on the ideas that they agree and asked students to politely express disagreement. He said, “It could be that Wxxx says something that you disagree and you would say somet hing polite, like I -, I-, I disagree with what you say, or I humbly disagree, or based on what I know I disagree with you”. Conveying a high expectation of challenge-driven inquiry Mr. B communicated with students that they were hel d to high expectations to do knowledge building. Mr. B shared with students that one general rule of participating in metacognitive meeting is that they should not repeat what other people already said. If students repeated what was already said in a metacognitive meeting, the teacher would point that out and remind students, “Okay, we ’ve already said that. Remember the game we’re play ing is that you cannot repeat”.

Theme 2: Offering inquiry-oriented feedback The second theme was identified through qualitative coding of Mr. B’s effort in scaffolding and offering feedback that supports students to advance their knowledge building progress that resulted in four major codes. Complimenting on student questions and ideas while encouraging continual inquiry Mr. B complimented students’ input by offering soci al-emotional feedback that positively support students ’ knowledge building inquiry. He often purposefully c omplimented on student questions and ideas. For exa mple, when a student shared that he researched how “the R omans built systems for moving water called aqueduc ts”, Mr. B complimented, “That’s very cool to know.” Mr.  B also offered compliments through agreeing studen ts’ input. For example, when one student shared his won dering areas, he said, “One of my  questions was: how did water get to your sink?” Mr. B responded, “I think we all want to know that.” Reflecting on gaps/weaknesses for further improvement Mr. B offered non-judgmental feedback to help students reflect on the  gaps or weakness in their thinking when he observed that students had misconceptions or partially correct ideas or incomplete understanding. In the first cognitive meeting in ecosystem unit, one student br ought up a misconception about the definition of ec osystem. She asked, “If an ecosystem would be the same without non-living things?” Mr. B did not directly tell the students that she had a misconception, but responded, “Well,  let’s think about that. Are there any ecosystem on  earth that have nothing, either living or non-living?” Sharing authentic experience of inquiry involving challenges, uncertainty, and excitement about progress Mr. B took opportunities to share his genuine reflection with students on the class progress to strengthen students’ social-emotional engagement with knowledge building. By th e end of the first cognitive meeting in water unit after extensive amount of classroom discussion on t he topic of “where water comes from”, Mr. B shared with students, “what has happened now for me, is I have more questions than I do answers. And I bet in many  ways you feel the same way. You have more questions about this than you do answers.” Sharing and learning from failure experience Mr. B shared his and other’s failure experience in doing hands-on experiments and challenged students to make extra effort in conducting experiments. When talking about doing the balloon experiment in order to prove air has weight, Mr. B said he and his colleague struggled to successfully conduct the experiment and he said to students, “So you have to be very careful about how you do the setup.” Theme 3: Responding to and challenging students to improve The third theme was identified through qualitative coding of Mr. B’s effort in scaffolding and offerin g feedback in supporting students to persistently improve ideas that resulted in five major codes. Challenging students to extend their thinking on an idea Mr. B challenged students to extend their thinking on an idea to challenge students to become persiste nt in continuously deepen their knowledge building inquir y. In the second metacognitive meeting in water uni t, students talked about water usage. When a student s aid water could be used for crops, Mr. B asked the students to think further on how specifically water can benefit agriculture. In the metacognitive meeting after students did their experiment on transporting water, one student  said he changed how he thought about water got tra nsported after doing the experiment. Mr. B asked, “Can you just elaborate on that? You originally thought it was just pipes and you did the experiment. What did you, what changed?” When students talked about “conserve water”, Mr. B challenged student to think further, “Conserve water. And why are we traying to conserve water?” Challenging students to examine an idea Mr. B challenged students to persistently examine t heir ideas when he noticed the ideas were incomplet e or inaccurate. For example, when a student talked abou t water evaporating because “it gets heated and tur ns kinda into, like, air and it goes up into the clouds as water vapor”, Mr. B challenged the student, “So are you suggesting the water cycle in the north-east only works in the summer and spring?” Leveraging students’ curiosity and thinking with hypothetical questions Mr. B used hypothetical questions to trigger studen ts’ curiosity and thinking to engage them in persis tently deepening their knowledge building inquiry. For exa mple, when students talked about rainwater being used to grow crops, Mr. B asked students, “What would happe n if it was a drought? To those things out there? W hat would happen?” When students discussed elements in a food chain, Mr. B asked students, “So if one of t hose in the food chain gets taken out of the food chain, what happens?” Challenging the class to come up with design ideas for experiments Mr. B also engaged students in knowledge building i nquiry by challenging them to figure out experiment  design ideas on their own. In the metacognitive meeting on  states of matter, Mr. B asked students to come up with experiment design ideas that could prove air has bo th mass and weight. He said, “I have a similar proposal for you today. I need you to figure out how we can prov e that air has both mass and takes up space. How co uld we prove that?” Working with students to clarify/revisit inquiry goals Mr. B engaged students to persistently deepen their knowledge building inquiry by asking the community to keep their inquiry focus. In the metacognitive meeting a t the initial phase of water unit, the class talked  about local reservoir. Mr. B asked student s to focus back to the question that was still bein g discussed when they tried to bring up other topics. He said, “So can we go back?  The question was how does water get into a reservo ir? Someone suggested perhaps pipe. Can anybody think of another way water gets into a reservoir?”

## Discussion

Mr. B promoted resilience in the KBC because he praised students “for the process they engaged in—their effort, their strategies, their focus, or their persistence ” (Yeast and Dweck, 2012, p. 311). Mr. B consistently offered growth-mindset oriented feedback throughout the KBC’s scie nce inquiry journey. Such feedback provided opportunities for students to build their resilience to sustain their  effort in the face of challenges. Community resilience gradually got built into the KBC to help  students face and overcome inquiry challenges, and  therefore sustain the community’s knowledge building effort a nd collaborative knowledge advancement. Mueller and Dweck's (1998) research findings suggest that stude nts who got praised for being hardworking in the le arning process improve their efforts more than the students who only got praised for being smart. Mr. B established classroom norms and expectations for students to an ticipate the attitude that they should hold as they  conduct knowledge building inquiries. And he rarely stopped  at praising students’ input but always went beyond to challenge students whether there was more that students could think about or dig deeper.
