### Abstract
This conceptual paper explores integrating critical theory and complex systems theory to address systemic inequities in education. ## Background

This conceptual paper will explore literature at th e intersection of critical theory and complex syste ms theory. Complex systems theory provides conceptual and analytical tools to investigate emergent phenomena arising from dynamical interactions among interdependent elements in complex systems. The critical theory literature provides perspectives and analytical tool s through which to not only understand systemic ine quities and injustices, but to situate educational researchers as agents of transformation. After overviewing each of these areas, we will explore them through a thematic review leveraging the complex systems methodology of network analysis. We argue that learning scientists, educators, and leaders must work towards integrat ing complex systems and critical theory to address epistemic and social injustice issues.

## Perspectives on complexity, justice, and conceptualizations

Complex systems and critical theories offer holistic perspectives for understanding wicked problems in education, yet their integration remains limited (Century & Ca ssata, 2016; Zellner & Campbell, 2015). Complex sys tems theory represents a unique paradigm aligning with a ctivity theory (Engeström, 2022) and embodied/embedded cognition (Newen et al., 2018). Critical theory examines power relations and promotes justice (Freire, 1974/2013; Kincheloe, 2008), while critical pedagogy applies t hese principles to education (Giroux, 2013). Over t he past 20 years, AERA journals show increased use of critical theories bu t limited adoption of complex systems theories. Learning sciences handbooks initially focused on students learning about systems, with recent versions discussing systems theories for understanding learning (Esmonde & Booker, 2017). This paper aims to interrogate literature at the intersection of critical theory, complex systems theory, and conceptualizations in education.

## Thematic review procedure

We conducted a thematic analysis of synthesis liter ature using the complex systems analytical techniqu e of network analysis. We selected and reviewed literatu re focused on "critical theory," "conceptualization s," "complex systems," "complex dynamical systems," "complex systems and syntheses," and "dynamic systems" in the context of education, teaching, and learning fr om databases including ERIC(EBSCO), APA Psycinfo, Education Source, and Proquest. We excluded non-relevant articles and non-syntheses, and gathered more literature by investigating relevant sources cited in our original articles and articles that cited them. Our corpus of 39 articles (39,2536 words) was imported into MAXQD A Pro Analytics and coded for emergent themes withi n various categories. Each article was coded independently by each author,  resulting in a final codebook with 155 codes covering 3,133 segments. We calculated freque ncies and correlations for all code pairs, creating  a symmetrical correlation matrix used to construct a network m ap. Girvan-Newman cluster analysis (Girvan & Newman, 2002) and node resizing according to betweenness centrality values (Thurner et al., 2018) were used to identify leverage points.

## Findings

Our analysis included interdisciplinary articles fr om the learning sciences and educational research. Of these, 56.4% discussed complex systems, but only 2.6% incorporated critical theory or conceptual change. A complexity epistemology/ontology was present  in 61.5%, constructivism in 18%, and critical theor y in 15.4%, with 5.1% positivist/post-positivist. Complexity stances indicate potential a lignment with critical perspectives. In articles without complex systems theory, 41% emphasized comp lexity without using systems theory. In the spirit of complex systems theory, we used a complex systems approach to analyze academic publications through network analysis. Using correlations of pairs of coded themes at P < 0.001, we constructed a network map with five GirvanNewman clusters at Q=0.34 and set node sizes by bet weenness centrality. The largest cluster (red circles) relates to social justice and critical theory, using critical perspectives, social constructivist theory, and a critical paradigm. It sees injustice as emergent from complex systems without explicitly using complex systems theory. The secondlargest cluster focuses on complex systems theories  and principles, using a complex systems paradigm. Social justice issues are absent. The third largest cluster focuses on teacher beliefs and their relati onships to practices, describing but not analyzing them as complex system s. The fourth largest  cluster includes learning theories and lacks a distinct paradigm or disciplinary focus, potentially bridging the critical and complex systems perspectives. The remaining cluster involves students learning about complex sy stems, using complex systems methodologies and constructivist pedagogies, but lacks social justice issues or solutions.

## Discussion and conclusion

Our network analysis found distinct clusters, suggesting silos among communities using critical theories, complex systems approaches, and learning theories to study educational change. Communities could benefit from integrating each other's perspectives. T he critical community could benefit from complex sy stems theory and methodologies, as they often describe power and int ersectionality as complex without explicitly using complex systems. The complex systems community aligns with critical paradigms but could be strengthened by the critical emphasis on actively fostering justice and equitable human flourishing (Morrison, 2008). Betweenness centrality measures (Thurner et al., 2018) identified potentia l leverage points for integrating perspectives, suc h as usi ng ethnographic or discourse analysis, interdisciplina ry work, critical pedagogy, social constructivist t heory, and rejecting positivism. There is an enormous opportunity for learning sciences research and work at the intersection of complex systems theory and critical theory. Much  progress has been made in understanding and addres sing issues of justice, marginalization, and oppression in learning contexts, but we believe that these issues can only truly be addressed through approaches grounded in complex systems theory. The most pressing issues are wicked problems emergent from complex systems, perhaps from many yet-unidentified interdependent complex systems. Only by deeply understanding these systems can we d evelop strategies through which to apply the sustai ned pressure on key leverage points needed in order to change these underlying complex systems.
