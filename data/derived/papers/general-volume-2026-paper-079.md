# Implementing and Advancing Computational Action in the Context of a Near-Peer Mentorship Model within a Maker Camp

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This three-part dissertation investigates the emergent theory of computational action within the context of a multi-year summer maker camp. The first two papers define the first two pillars of computational action and investigate how they can be influenced by the design of informal maker camps. The final paper will investigate the implementation of a near-peer mentorship model within a camp, to present case studies of each of the middle-high school mentors’ experiences. It will investigate the design decisions within the mentorship model that influenced mentors’ computational action. This final paper will extend the theory of computational action and how it can be utilized to develop a sustainable community of practice where the ownership of computational learning shifts towards the learners’ communities.

Scholars and practitioners over the last few decades have been preparing learners to navigate the rapidly developing, technologically driven world (Guzdial, 2022). In this effort, Wing (2006) proposed computational thinking, which despite its significant influence, does not consider how students’ computational learning should enable them to utilize technology meaningfully towards their lives and communities (Kafai & Proctor, 2022). In response, computational action (Tissenbaum et al., 2019) focuses on learners using and creating with computing to develop their computational identities and digital empowerment. Following a constructionist approach (Papert, 1980), computational action suggests that students should create artifacts with their identities and communities in mind, and thus, engage with computing as producers in personally relevant ways. My dissertation is embedded in the computational action-driven Connected Spaces (C/S) summer maker camps, where middle-high school participants created projects with computing. I investigate their experiences to understand the influence of camp design on their computational identities and digital empowerment. Goals of the research My dissertation will consist of three individual journal articles, exploring students’ experiences of two pillars of computational action – computational identity and digital empowerment – and subsequently inform the theory through my findings. Table 1 outlines the first two articles, which will investigate C/S camp design elements from 2022, 2023, and 2024 camps. For the doctoral consortium, I will primarily discuss the third article, and its potential contribution to my dissertation, to computational action, and how it will inform my research trajectory. In the third paper, I will investigate the unique near-peer mentorship model implemented in the C/S 2025 camp. I will investigate the computational identity and digital empowerment experiences of the four middle-high school mentors as they assisted their middle-school peers towards making mobile apps, and as they worked with the research team to co-design and facilitate activities that enabled mentees to design meaningful apps. I will answer the research questions: 1) how did the experience of being near peer mentors and co-designers influence mentors’ computational identities and digital empowerment? and 2) what were some effective methods of positioning students as near-peer mentors in a maker camp? At the end of the 2025 camp, I conducted and recorded semi-structured interviews with the four near-peer mentors. I audio-recorded daily focus-group debrief sessions where the mentors and I discussed the activities of the day, what went well, and how we could prepare for the upcoming days. The mentors participated in a day-long training session before the camp, which was recorded; each camp day was recorded, and student and mentor artifacts were visually documented. Conducting holistic coding (Saldaña, 2021) of this data I will create a quintain case study (Stake, 2005), to present individual case studies of each mentor’s experience. These individual cases will inform a collective case study of the mentorship model to investigate the curriculum design decisions that supported mentors’ computational action experiences. The theories of computational identity and digital empowerment developed in the first two papers will inform the coding and themes within this final study. Each of the four mentors demonstrated unique mentorship approaches. For instance, Jade took on the role of the lead mentor among the group, Jasmine was intent on establishing sense of belonging and fun within the camp, Aria was the youngest and had no previous experience with C/S, and Talia often took on the role of a student rather than a mentor. By presenting individual case studies of the mentors, I will investigate how these varied lived experiences and approaches influenced their computational identity and digital empowerment in the camp. Through these individual case studies, I anticipate finding themes about the mentorship model that influenced the mentors’ experiences, such as working as a team of four, co-designing curriculum that enabled students to make meaningful apps, and participating in the mentor training. I will use these themes to present features of the mentorship model that impacted the mentors’ computational action experiences. Contributions This dissertation’s contributions will be two-fold: 1) developing and establishing two pillars of the computational action theory (computational identity and digital empowerment), and 2) investigating the multi-year implementation of the computational action driven design of the Connected Spaces summer camp. The third article in particular will investigate computational action within the context of a constructionist mentorship model. While the theory of computational action emphasizes students becoming producers of and with technology, the third article extends this approach, to understand how computational action can be implemented to establish a sense of belonging and sustainable community of practice among new and returning students. Designing AI-mediated Feedback for Knowledge Building: Comparative Inquiry and Design-Based Research Bunichi Otaki otaki.bunichi.23@shizuoka.ac.jp Shizuoka University, Graduate School of Science and Technology Abstract: This dissertation examines how generative AI mediates feedback and shapes knowledge-building processes in higher education. Through three connected studies, it analyzes how students and expert educators engage with AI in academic writing and collaborative inquiry and how these interactions influence their epistemic framing. Grounded in dialogic feedback frameworks and Knowledge Building theory, the research identifies differences in the structure and processes of AI-supported knowledge-building dialogue and explores how generative AI can be integrated into project-based learning environments that support collaborative idea improvement. Together, the findings reconceptualize feedback in AI contexts and provide analytic and design insights for fostering epistemic cognition and sustained knowledge building. Recent work has reframed feedback from information transmission to a process-oriented practice grounded in interaction and meaning-making (Winstone et al., 2022). In this view, feedback emerges through iterative dialogue in which learners interpret, negotiate, and use information. This perspective aligns with dialogism, which conceptualizes knowledge as emerging through interaction among people and artifacts (Bakhtin, 1981). Generative AI introduces a new form of participation in this process by providing immediate and elaborated responses, yet prior research shows that while learners value its efficiency, they question its contextual and relational sensitivity (Otaki et al., 2026).

To examine how AI can support deeper inquiry, this study adopts Knowledge Building (KB) theory, which frames learning as the collaborative improvement of ideas (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2014). From this perspective, productive inquiry is sustained through discourse practices such as proposing ideas, evaluating their promise, and engaging in reflective dialogue (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2016). However, recent work suggests that AI-generated responses require critical scrutiny, as they may be incomplete or weakly reasoned (Chen & Zhu, 2023). This raises a central question: how does generative AI shape the interpretive work and discourse processes that sustain knowledge building?

Goals of the research First, the dissertation examines how students and expert educators differ in their epistemic framing when engaging with GenAI, focusing on how expectations and interpretive practices shape dialogic feedback. Next, it analyzes how these groups differ in the structure and processes of AI-mediated, knowledge-building dialogue during complex problem-solving. Finally, it explores how GenAI can be integrated into project-based, KB-oriented courses and identifies design principles for learning environments that support sustained collaborative inquiry. Together, these goals position GenAI as a mediational resource for sustaining epistemic agency and collective idea improvement in knowledge-building environments.

The dissertation employs complementary methods across three studies. Study 1 adopts a quantitative ethnography approach (Shaffer, 2017), combining focus groups with students and educators to examine differences in epistemic framing when engaging with AI feedback. Study 2 investigates AI-mediated collaborative problem-solving using discourse data from student pairs and expert educators, analyzing how ideas are constructed and developed through interaction. Study 3 is a design-based research intervention embedded in a semester-long undergraduate course, tracing how students iteratively engage with AI in sustained inquiry. Across studies, network-based approaches, including Epistemic Network Analysis and KBDeX (Oshima et al., 2012), are used to model the structure and evolution of discourse. This multi-method design enables a process-oriented understanding of how AI participates in knowledge-building activity.

## Data and preliminary findings

S tudy 1 (Otaki et al., 2026) revealed contrasting epistemic framings of AI-generated feedback. Students emphasized efficiency, clarity, and emotional safety, positioning AI as a low-risk resource for initial improvements. In contrast, educators foregrounded contextual understanding, relational continuity, and ethical trust, highlighting AI’s limitations in addressing nuanced and affective aspects of feedback. These differences indicate that AI is interpreted through distinct epistemic lenses, shaping how it is incorporated into feedback practices.

Study 2 extended this analysis to collaborative problem solving. Students frequently organized their reasoning around AI-generated ideas, producing coherent but externally guided arguments. Their discourse networks showed limited integration between evaluation, problem reframing, and meta-dialogue. In contrast, expert educators engaged in iterative cycles of defining, reframing, and evaluating problems, using AI selectively to test and refine ideas. Their discourse exhibited more integrated epistemic structures linking evidence, critique, and reflection. These findings suggest that differences in epistemic framing shape not only how AI is used, but also how inquiry is organized.

Study 3 examines how generative AI can be integrated into sustained inquiry within a KB-oriented course. Preliminary observations indicate that productive use of AI emerges when it is positioned as one resource among others, rather than as a primary driver of activity. This highlights the importance of designing learning environments that support the coordination of AI, peer interaction, and instructional scaffolding. Expected contributions This dissertation contributes to a deeper understanding of how GenAI mediates dialogic feedback and knowledgebuilding practices in higher education. By examining students and expert educators across writing, argumentation, and project-based inquiry, it clarifies the epistemic differences that shape human–AI interaction and demonstrates how these differences influence the structure and depth of knowledge-building dialogue. In the Doctoral Consortium, I hope to contribute to discussion from a theoretically grounded perspective on feedback and KB theory, methodological perspective in network analyses including ENA and KBDeX, and practical insights gained through facilitating project-based learning environments, in ways that help deepen our community’s collective inquiry.
