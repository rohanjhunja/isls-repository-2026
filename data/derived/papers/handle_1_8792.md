# Examining Learning-by-Teaching Effect in a Secondary Math Course

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The present study aims to identify which phases during learning by teaching play the critical roles and to investigate how student ability and pairing types mutually influence students’ performance in each phase and the overall achievement. To this end, learning-by-teaching was applied with the form of reciprocal peer tutoring in a seventh-grade math course. Students’ achievement scores, preparing-to-teach worksheets and verbal records during tutoring were collected for future conversational and quantitative analysis.

## 1 Introduction

It is well-studied and well-supported that teaching can be an effective strategy to facilitate learnin g (Fiorella & Mayer, 2016). Teaching encompasses more complex mental processes than onl y learning for oneself (Duran, 2017). According to Chi’s Interactive-Constructive-Active-Passive (ICAP) framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014), learning increases when students behave higher-leve l cognitive engagement. Particularly, the act of ex plaining can strengthen deeper and longer learning effect th an solely preparing to teach (Fiorella & Mayer, 201 3). Furthermore, interacting, such as questioning and a nswering, helps tutors avoid the proclivity for knowledgetelling and stimulates them to generate reflective knowledge-building (Roscoe, 2014). Learning-by-teaching has also been implemented in classroom practices w ith various forms, such as cooperative learning, peer tutoring, reciprocal peer tutoring (RPT) and so forth. Ample studies had re vealed the benefits of those tutoring activities on students’ academic achievement (Alegre, Moliner, Maroto, & Lo renzoValentin, 2019). However, in addition to overall ad vantages, there is still a lack of classroom study that further investigate the respective effect of each phase wit hin tutoring process (Fiorella & Mayer, 2016). Henc e, the present study not only implemented RPT in a seventh-grade math course but also applied Kobayashi’s (20 21) three-phase teaching framework to explore students’  learning patterns and outcomes in each phase (Figu re 1). That is, throughout RPT sessions, all students were  required to prepare for their own teaching and the reafter to tutor each other through explaining and interacting. Two questions are examined in the study: ¥ Q1: To what extent did preparation, explanation and interaction influence overall learning achievement? ¥ Q2: How did student ability and pairing types influence tutoring performance and overall achievement? ## 2 Methodology design

The study was conducted in a seventh-grade math cla ssroom at a public middle school in Taiwan, involvi ng 23 students (11 females and 12 males). Written informe d consents were obtained from all the students and their parents. A total of six RPT sessions were implement ed: three training sessions and three formal sessions with a three-week break in between. Each RPT session was c arried out after the math teacher’s one-to two-wee k instruction for the topic. The math topics covered in the six sessions were (1) Intro to Exponents and Scientific Notation, (2) Factor and Multiple, (3) Greatest Common Factor and Least Common Multiple, (4) Laws of Exponents, (5) Simplifying Algebraic Expressions, and (6) Algebraic Equation. Prior to the beginning of the study, the students had been assigned to fixed pairs or triads by the teacher. Because each tutoring phase also encompassed two sub-phases — heterogeneous RPT and then homogeneous RPT, every student had two different pairs or triads (i.e., one for heterogeneous RPT  and the other for homogeneous RPT). Accordingly, ten pairs and one triad were includ ed in heterogeneous RPT, and six lowachieving pairs, four high-achieving pairs and one high-achieving triad were contained in homogeneous RPT. Figure 2 outlines the involving phases, clas s periods for implementation, and required materials and instruments. For each session, all students took the pretest after the teacher completed instructing in the topi c. The pretests contained a combination of multiple  choice, calculation and word problems. During problem-choosing phase, students picked out their problems from the pretest paper and filled in the registration forms so their partners could see and prepare to teach those problems. Then, during preparing-to-teach phase, students were required to write their teaching preparations (i.e., problemsolving processes and math concepts or formulas used in the problems, etc.) on the preparing-to-teach worksheets. In each RPT session, students were expected to prepare four problems totally (two for each of heterogeneous RPT and homogeneous RPT). Afterwards, in tutoring phase, students worked in pairs/triads and took turns being tutors or tutees. The first 15 min were heterogeneous RPT, then students re-grouped and turned to homogeneous RPT for the next 15 min. At last, all students completed the posttest. Question types and difficulty level of the posttests were the same as those of the pretests. All tests were graded from 0 to 100. Figure 2

## Procedure of Every RPT Session

Pretest scores, posttest scores, and students’ preparing-to-teach worksheets from six RPT sessions were collected. Verbal interaction from all pairs and tr iads during heterogeneous and homogeneous RPTs  in three formal sessions were recorded and transcribed. Data  analysis is in progress: marking rubrics for preparation quality and qualities of verbal explanation and interaction  are in design; quantitative descriptive analysis, correlation, multiple regression, and ANOVA are expecting to carry out for answering the two research questions. Although taking with small sample and might filled with unmanageable confounding variables, the present study made attempts to evaluate learning-by-teaching effect of every RPT phase in a real classroom setting.
