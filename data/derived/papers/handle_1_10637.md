# From Abstract to Tangible: Leveraging Virtual Reality and GeoGebra for Playful Math Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

From Abstract to Tangible: Leveraging Virtual Reality and GeoGebra for Playful Math Education Lea Anne Daughrity, Candace Walkington, Max Sherard, Prajakt Pande, Theodora Beauchamp, Anthony Cuevas ldaughrity@smu.edu, cwalkington@smu.edu, msherard@smu.edu, ppande@smu.edu, theob@smu.edu, acuevas@smu.edu Southern Methodist University Abstract: This study investigates the use of GeoGebra, a Dyna mic Geometry Software for math learning in Virtual Reality using Head-Mounted Displays. Investigators provided a rich environment for learner engagement through a novel application of these tools, including hand manipulation of mathematical objects within VR and play-based learning strategies. This paper focuses on distinguishing between mathematical and non-mathematical play in this VR environment and understanding the role each takes i n the learning process. Key findings highlight how mathematical play in an immersive VR environment using DGS allows mathematical misconceptions to surface, allows stud ents to explore mathematical ideas, and allows for new kinds of opportunities to build off of play experiences.

## Introduction

Virtual Reality (VR) technologies can be an innovat ive method for enhancing educational experiences wi thin mathematics education. These tools can allow learne rs to embody mathematical ideas (Johnson-Glenberg & Megowan-Romanowicz, 2013) in a collaborative space, discovering new ways to “play within” a dynamic, virtual world designed to immerse learners with mathematica l objects and relationships. Our study involved the  use of head mounted displays (HMDs) for middle school math tutoring. By enabling learners to employ natural hand movements to gesture and manipulate virtual objects in VR, we sought to curate an educational experience that was naturally engaging and playful. For this study, we describe mathematical play as a process where individuals cr eate and test mathematical ideas, often encounterin g failures (Williams-Pierce, 2019; Williams-Pierce & Thevenow-Harrison, 2021). There is potential for the unique affordances of VR to promote different kinds of spo ntaneous and non-spontaneous mathematical play. This longitudinal study aims to understand the use of HM Ds for promoting mathematical play in middle school  math tutoring, where the tutor can continuously and mean ingfully engage with the students and the students can collaborate. The research questions are:

1. What kinds of spontaneous mathematical play and non-mathematical play do middle school students

generate in a VR math tutoring environment?

2. What kinds of interactions unfold as tutors guide students to engage in pre-planned forms of mathematical

and non-mathematical play in a VR tutoring environment?

## Literature review

This research examines the intersection of mathemat ics, playful learning, and technology using VR lear ning spaces.  Recent research has indicated that playful  mathematics has the potential to enhance engagemen t and understanding (Williams, 2022; Pound & Le e, 2022). Resnick’s (2017) research emphasizes the transformative power of play in learning, suggesting that playful experiences can catalyze creativity, exploration, a nd deeper cognitive engagement in mathematics. Dynamic Geomet ry Software (DGS) allows students to engage dynamically with mathematical objects such as shape s and figures (Hollebrands, 2007), and can allow pl ayful engagement with mathematics. The DGS we use in the present study is GeoGebra, a free online platform t hat brings together capabilities for geometry, algebra, spreadsheets, graphing, statistics, and calculus (Hohenwater & Fuchs, 2004).

When combined with DGS tools like GeoGebra, VR beco mes an environment where students can actively connect to math concepts (Ahmed & Junaini,  2020; Bujak et al., 2013) while collaborating through play with peers. With the introduction of VR in the math classroom, a new realm of possibilities has emerged. ## Research design and approach

Participants underwent a maximum of seven tutoring sessions, lasting an average of 34 minutes. There w ere 46 total group tutoring session videos in this study. There were four tutors and four virtual rooms. Students’ assigned tutor and classmates were the same throughout the seven sessions.

## Data collection method

All tutoring sessions were recorded from the vantage point of the designated adult avatar whose sole purpose was to be the main recording within the virtual environment of the DGS on the HMD. Each video was then transcribed. Several videos were randomly selected, and all researchers on the team were assigned to code a sample. The team then gathered to discuss the results and collaborat ively devised a plan for coding each session using previous concepts and newly learned information. Videos were  then coded using new insights. This coding include d instances of mathematical and non-mathematical play.

Operationalizing and coding instances of play To better understand the different forms of play observed during this study, the researchers created a codebook to help guide them during data analysis. This analysis  included pinpointing the stretches of video (from all 46 tutoring sessions) considered play. From there, the researchers could narrow down the types of play (spontaneous and non-spontaneous; mathematical and non-mathematical) that existed to understand better and  define them for the codebook.

Table 1 Codebook Word Definition Mathematical Play This is when a teacher encourages the student to act out a scenario or engage in an activity that includes movement (or theatrics) so the student can better understand a math concept being taught. This can include a game where the teacher uses vocabulary words as part of the activity. Another example is when the teacher and/or students build spontaneous shapes (spontaneous but distinct, like a dog or a pyramid) with blocks, discuss new concepts, or use them to introduce basic math tool manipulation.  If math is not discussed as part of the build or tied to the shape, it is simply play. Students can also lead mathematical play by challenging a classmate to help them or compete against them in an activity based on learning math concepts. If a math learning is involved at any point during the play, it becomes Mathematical Play.

There were 30 out of 46 videos that were coded with mathematical play. Non-mathematical Play Non-mathematical play includes activities that are not based on math learning. An example of this might be thumb wars. While this does have movement and some theatrics, it is not focused on learning math concepts. Another example is when the students move to different places in the room. While this may be a gray area as it needs to be learned to view math perspectives from different angles, it is non-mathematical play if movement isn't directly tied to a math activity.

There were 25 out of 46 videos that were coded with non-mathematical play.

## Results

The findings focus on two of many instances of playful experiences during their tutoring sessions.  In Figure 3 (a) and 3(b), we see two students being instructed to play “Simon Says” with their virtual bodies by the tutor, in order to practice the concept  of mathematical rotation. The students are asked to  turn their bodies 90 degrees counterclockwise. The students become confused about the meaning of  “clockwise” and “ counterclockwise,” showing how this instance of play was able to surfa ce mathematical misconceptions. This led to the tut or giving instruction on properties of rotation.

This example of play leverages the presence of dyna mic and in some cases large-sized mathematical objects in a virtual environment (here, a coordinate plane), and elements of each are unique to the af fordances of VR. Figures 3(c) and 3(d) show two students discussing what the y wanted to build with cube blocks, and one of the students spontaneously decides to build a pyramid. The other student and the tutor reply that a pyramid requires triangles rather than cubes, and the stu dent who wanted to build the pyr amid decides to build a cube instead. He was never encouraged to do either, showing spontaneity. We also noted instances of non-mathematical play, such as students climbing the virtual tree outside of th e classroom or students racing each other through t he environment.

## Discussion and conclusion

In this research, we identified instances of mathem atical and non-mathematical play in a virtual reality environment for middle school mathematics tutoring. These instances of play were either initiated by the students themselves, or purposefully implemented by tutors t o reach a variety of pedagogical goals. What was st riking about these and other instances of play coded in th e VR environment was the degree to which both stude nts and tutors appreciated that the VR environment could al low them to do things that are impossible or unlike ly in the real world-such as teleporting around their space, resizing gi gantic mathematical figures to fill the room, or moving around together on a world-sized coordinate plane. Although the facilitation o f mathematical and n onmathematical play was not in our original plans for the tutoring intervention, these “fantasy” elements of VR gave rise to play quite naturally, and both the tutors and the students led the implementation of mathematical play into the VR environment.

We highlight that future tutoring interventions in VR could be designed to more explicitly capitalize on opportunities for play, and to imagine fantasy elem ents relating to VR that students might find compel ling. Our findings support other research sugges ting that play in a mathematics learning environmen t can support engagement and understanding (Williams, 2022; Pound  & Lee, 2022), but extend these findings to the uni que affordances of a VR system. Further, although resea rch on DGS (Hollebrands, 2007) doe s not often forefront opportunities for play, the dynamic nature of these  systems with the real-time measurement and feedback may, when implemented appropriately, allow important opp ortunities for mathematical play. We thus point to VRbased DGS as an important future avenue for research on mathematical play.
