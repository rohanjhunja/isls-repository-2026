# Re-envisioning Assessment Challenges and Opportunities in the Generative AI Era through CSCL

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract

Assessment in higher education faces unprecedented challenges, as generative AI now enables students to produce polished assignments in seconds. Many institutions have responded by reverting to pen‑and‑paper exams, a solution that mitigates some concerns but introduces other problems narrowing educational aims. This workshop provides a platform for collaborative exploration and dialogue of how CSCL can offer fresh perspectives and pathways for addressing these challenges and creating opportunities. Grounded in CSCL theory, design, and analytics, this workshop examines conceptualizing AI as a dialogic, collaborative partner; designing innovative assessments; assessing processes rather than products; and using collaborative analytics to support learning in AI‑enriched environments. Key issues for inquiry include: purpose of assessment, design and fine‑tuning of AI‑supported assessment, AI-powered multi-modal analytics and feedback, equity and diversity, researcher–teacher collaboration, ethics-privacy, and public discourse. Together, we work towards re‑envisioning assessment, synergizing research and practice, building collective knowledge and strengthening CSCL's impact and significance in the Gen AI era. Organizing team We are a group of CSCL researchers with shared interests in examining how CSCL can offer perspectives and directions for addressing assessment issues in higher education arising from the use of generative AI. Carol Chan conducts research on knowledge building, focusing on socio-cognitive-epistemic dynamics, reflective assessment, analytics, and teacher professional development. Xiao Hu’s research is on learning analytics, automated assessment and feedback, and AI in education. Her research includes collaborative writing facilitated by real-time analytics dashboards, multimodal learning analytics in collaborative maker activity, and analytics-supported writing in AI environments. Baruch Schwarz's research in CSCL focuses on argumentation, dialogue, and reasoning, particularly examining the roles of technology in boosting collaborative argumentation. Stephen Fiore conducts research on Human-AI teaming, focusing on collaborative cognition, including knowledge building when collaborating with AI, and the role of artificial social intelligence in assessing collaborative learning. Jun Oshima examines how knowledge-building discourse among undergraduate /graduate students can be supported by Generative AI, and examines their interactions with AI from a network-science perspective. Wenli Chen conducts research on human-AI collaboration to augment human learning by fine-tuning generative AI informed by learning sciences theories, developing AI-powered multi-modal learning analytics to assess students’ collaborative learning in classrooms. Manoli Pifarré' examines and designs dialogic, technology-enhanced pedagogies that foster co-creation processes focusing on how interactive technologies—empowered by artificial intelligence and multimodal learning analytics—can assess, visualize, and support group-based creative problem-solving. W orkshop description and goals We are facing a global assessment crisis in higher education with the widespread use of AI tools: students can generate polished assignments in seconds, fueling surface learning, plagiarism, and metacognitive laziness, and leading to reliance on ChatGPT (Fan et al., 2025). Public discourse highlights the seriousness of this shift—for example, Bloomberg (May 27, 2025) asked, “Does college still have a purpose in the age of ChatGPT?” Many universities have reverted to in‑person, pen‑and‑paper exams, which mitigate some risks but create others, curtailing the valuable educational goals of inquiry, problem-solving, and collaboration. This workshop is premised on the idea that CSCL is well-positioned to offer directions for rethinking assessment in higher education in the AI age (Schwarz et al., in press). We build on decades of CSCL research on assessing collaboration (Cress et al., 2021), extending to CSCL theory, design, and analytics for assessment practice. Traditional assessments emphasize final products (e.g., essays) that AI can replicate easily. Conversely, CSCL foregrounds collaborative processes (e.g., co‑construction, shared regulation) and CSCL designs such as argumentation, dialogic interaction and knowledge building, can offer new ways to scaffold and assess AI‑human collaboration. Beyond polished output, CSCL analytics can make learning‑collaboration processes visible for feedback and actionable improvement (Wise et al., 2021). This workshop provides a venue for sharing CSCL research and practices to address challenges and opportunities, reflecting on what has been accomplished and examining potential for further inquiry and impact. Our goals are to (a) identify and problematize assessment challenges brought by GenAI through a CSCL lens, (b) examine emerging CSCL theories, pedagogies, and analytics for AI‑enriched assessment, and (c) reflect on assessment practice both as higher‑education instructors and CSCL/learning sciences researchers. We seek to synergize efforts from workshop participants to build collective knowledge, create networks, to address these challenges and to strengthen CSCL’s impact. Theoretical background and themes Theoretical perspective. Since its emergence in the 1990s, CSCL has reconceptualized learning from individual knowledge acquisition to co‑construction of knowledge (Ludvigsen & Steier, 2019). Current concerns about students copying AI‑generated work reflect a continued reliance on surface‑level, transmission‑based conceptions of learning and assessment. Using a CSCL lens, Gen AI use in homework need not be prohibited; instead, it should be scaffolded to support agency, co‑construction, and shared regulation (Järvela, et al., 2023). The knowledge-building model posits collaboration as collective work on continually improvable ideas (Chan & van Aalst, 2018; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). Similarly, AI‑human collaboration can be conceptualized as iterative idea improvement rather than task completion. We need to shift from policing to scaffolding students to leverage AI as a dialogic partner, using AI‑generated transcripts as evidence for learning and collaboration. CSCL knowledge‑building perspectives could provide a theoretical foundation for unraveling the nature of AI‑human collaboration while enriching CSCL theories in AI age. Design‑pedagogy‑technology perspective. CSCL offers powerful approaches for aligning assessment, learning, and collaboration. Research in assessment has shown that assessment can be understood as assessment of learning (evaluating completed work), for learning (supporting improvement), and as learning (making thinking visible) (Wiliam, 2017). Beyond assessing completed work, CSCL approaches—argumentation, collaborative problem‑solving, knowledge building, and maker work, etc.—provide rich opportunities for formative assessment that support collaboration and complement traditional exams/papers. Assessing group interaction and quality of talk, increasingly supported by AI (Hu et al., 2022), offers important indicators of learning. Reflective assessment enables the assessment of collaboration while also scaffolding interaction and idea development (Yang et al., 2024; van Aalst & Chan, 2007), and this could now be enriched with AI. In this way, AI becomes part of an assessment ecology that supports collaborative learning rather than merely reproducing assignment products. Analytics perspective. Contemporary CSCL analytics research now makes it feasible to assess processes beyond easily replicable products. AI‑enhanced collaborative analytics can trace participation, interactions, and revisions through log data, revealing whether students are engaging in meaningful knowledge construction or simply copying outputs (Ouyang et al., 2023). These process‑level data can be fed back to teachers and learners through dashboards to support monitoring, reflection, and improvement. CSCL researchers are well-positioned to design AI-enriched analytics that make collaboration processes and idea development—previously invisible— now visible (Ba et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2022). This process‑focused view emphasizes ongoing work tracing student engagement and activity rather than post‑hoc grading through concurrent, embedded, and transformative assessment. Such analytics assess not only what students produce but how they collaborate—with AI and with one another—to co-construct and to build knowledge. Issues for Discussion: This workshop provides a platform for participants to share ideas as we inquire into how CSCL could address challenges of assessment for new opportunities in the AI era. Grounded in theory, design, and analytics and ecological-ethical considerations, several areas of inquiry include: ● Focus of assessment and expanding theories. Assessment needs to shift from evaluating products to processes–examining how people learn and collaborate with AI. Emerging research on human–AI collaboration highlights cognitive, metacognitive, motivational, and socio-emotional processes, yet more work is needed—for example, on how students take agency and engage in dialogue with AI when tackling assessments to advance learning. Questions also arise about what knowledge, skills, domain-specific competencies, and collaboration processes should be assessed in AI-mediated assessment -- How can AI’s roles shift from merely providing content to supporting co-construction, regulation, and reflection? These shifts call for expanding CSCL theoretical perspectives and engaging with related fields on assessment. Continued inquiry is needed to extend CSCL frameworks to better capture the complexities of learning and collaboration in AI-mediated assessment. ● Collaboration and AI-assessment designs. Building on CSCL designs, researchers can work on productive assessment tasks to replace traditional ones easily emulated by AI and designing AI-enriched collaborative environments. Questions include what we are assessing, what the assessment activity involves, and what resources—peers, AI, and technology—are required. Reflective assessment can prompt students to reflect on their work with AI for ongoing improvement. As well, automatic assessment features can provide teachers with timely information to assess student engagement and to make “on-the-fly” decisions about what support is needed. Design experiments conducted on AI-assessment designs while examining the emerging human-AI collaboration process would help advance both research and practice.. ● Examining AI-supported multi-modal data analysis. Collaborative analytics, combined with AI, can play a central role in assessing learning-collaboration processes via multi-modal data analyses. These could include automatic scoring and annotating student texts and conversations, applying expert-designed rubrics for evaluation, and identifying emerging patterns of behaviors. Information shown in dashboards can support teacher assessment and promote students’ reflection on ongoing work. These analyses open the door to assessing human–AI interactions and designing assessment tasks that capture these dynamics. How these tools and analytics can enhance assessment validity, scalable analysis, providing meaningful insights into collaborative learning processes are important questions. ● Equity and diversity issues. The advent of AI in assessment raises important questions about equity and diversity. AI use may widen existing gaps if students have unequal access to technology, differing levels of guidance, or varying digital literacy. AI technology itself can be biased due to an imbalance in data availability. At the same time, well-scaffolded AI use and mindfully-designed AI tools have the potential to narrow disparities by providing feedback and more accessible pathways for demonstrating student understanding. We need to consider how assessment design and practices can ensure fairness, how AI-based tools can be designed to reduce inequities, and what forms of support learners need to participate equitably in AI-mediated assessment environments. ● Research–practice integration. CSCL-AI assessment for impact involves research-practice synergy developing practical tools and strategies that teachers can use. This involves understanding teachers’ needs and communicating complex findings in accessible, actionable forms. Inquiry such as interviews with teachers about their successful experiences with AI-informed collaboration and assessment could enrich the integration. Supporting higher-education teachers in adopting new views of assessment, collaboration, and AI use—and aligning CSCL research with classroom realities—remain to be investigated. ● Privacy, ethics, and limitations. AI‑supported assessment raises concerns about data privacy, ethical use, and system limitations. Collecting process data—such as interaction logs or conversational traces—requires careful attention to consent, transparency, and responsible data use. Ethical challenges include helping students use AI responsibly, preventing misuse of analytics, addressing bias in AI‑generated judgments, and ensuring students are not harmed by algorithmic errors. Ongoing scrutiny of AI limitations is needed to ensure new CSCL-AI assessment practices remain trustworthy, fair, and beneficial. ● Engagement with the public. The rise of generative AI has sparked widespread public debate, offering CSCL researchers opportunities to engage broader audiences. Public engagement involves helping students, parents, policymakers, and other stakeholders understand the challenges and opportunities AI brings to assessment. By clarifying misconceptions, sharing evidence, success stories, and highlighting responsible uses, CSCL researchers can contribute to more informed public discourse. We propose CSCL offers powerful lenses and tools for rethinking assessment challenges and how learning and collaboration can be assessed in AI‑rich environments. This workshop provides a venue for sharing research, practices, and emerging ideas and future trends on AI‑enriched assessment, including the role of AI as a collaborative partner for knowledge building, process‑focused assessment, assessment design, multi-modal analytics, equity-ethics issues, teacher-researcher collaboration, and public engagement. Together, we will identify ways to address current challenges, examine theories and designs for CSCL-AI enriched approaches, build collective knowledge and reflect on implications for our roles as CSCL researchers and educators. Participants will engage in reflective inquiry, interactive discussions, and community building to reconceptualize assessment and to develop future collaborations on CSCL research for impact. W orkshop description and goals We are facing a global assessment crisis in higher education with the widespread use of AI tools: students can generate polished assignments in seconds, fueling surface learning, plagiarism, and metacognitive laziness, and leading to reliance on ChatGPT (Fan et al., 2025). Public discourse highlights the seriousness of this shift—for example, Bloomberg (May 27, 2025) asked, “Does college still have a purpose in the age of ChatGPT?” Many universities have reverted to in‑person, pen‑and‑paper exams, which mitigate some risks but create others, curtailing the valuable educational goals of inquiry, problem-solving, and collaboration. This workshop is premised on the idea that CSCL is well-positioned to offer directions for rethinking assessment in higher education in the AI age (Schwarz et al., in press). We build on decades of CSCL research on assessing collaboration (Cress et al., 2021), extending to CSCL theory, design, and analytics for assessment practice. Traditional assessments emphasize final products (e.g., essays) that AI can replicate easily. Conversely, CSCL foregrounds collaborative processes (e.g., co‑construction, shared regulation) and CSCL designs such as argumentation, dialogic interaction and knowledge building, can offer new ways to scaffold and assess AI‑human collaboration. Beyond polished output, CSCL analytics can make learning‑collaboration processes visible for feedback and actionable improvement (Wise et al., 2021). This workshop provides a venue for sharing CSCL research and practices to address challenges and opportunities, reflecting on what has been accomplished and examining potential for further inquiry and impact. Our goals are to (a) identify and problematize assessment challenges brought by GenAI through a CSCL lens, (b) examine emerging CSCL theories, pedagogies, and analytics for AI‑enriched assessment, and (c) reflect on assessment practice both as higher‑education instructors and CSCL/learning sciences researchers. We seek to synergize efforts from workshop participants to build collective knowledge, create networks, to address these challenges and to strengthen CSCL’s impact. Theoretical background and themes Theoretical perspective. Since its emergence in the 1990s, CSCL has reconceptualized learning from individual knowledge acquisition to co‑construction of knowledge (Ludvigsen & Steier, 2019). Current concerns about students copying AI‑generated work reflect a continued reliance on surface‑level, transmission‑based conceptions of learning and assessment. Using a CSCL lens, Gen AI use in homework need not be prohibited; instead, it should be scaffolded to support agency, co‑construction, and shared regulation (Järvela, et al., 2023). The knowledgebuilding model posits collaboration as collective work on continually improvable ideas (Chan & van Aalst, 2018; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). Similarly, AI‑human collaboration can be conceptualized as iterative idea improvement rather than task completion. We need to shift from policing to scaffolding students to leverage AI as a dialogic partner, using AI‑generated transcripts as evidence for learning and collaboration. CSCL knowledge‑building perspectives could provide a theoretical foundation for unraveling the nature of AI‑human collaboration while enriching CSCL theories in AI age. Design‑pedagogy‑technology perspective. CSCL offers powerful approaches for aligning assessment, learning, and collaboration. Research in assessment has shown that assessment can be understood as assessment of learning (evaluating completed work), for learning (supporting improvement), and as learning (making thinking visible) (Wiliam, 2017). Beyond assessing completed work, CSCL approaches—argumentation, collaborative problem‑solving, knowledge building, and maker work, etc.—provide rich opportunities for formative assessment that support collaboration and complement traditional exams/papers. Assessing group interaction and quality of talk, increasingly supported by AI (Hu et al., 2022), offers important indicators of learning. Reflective assessment enables the assessment of collaboration while also scaffolding interaction and idea development (Yang et al., 2024; van Aalst & Chan, 2007), and this could now be enriched with AI. In this way, AI becomes part of an assessment ecology that supports collaborative learning rather than merely reproducing assignment products. Analytics perspective. Contemporary CSCL analytics research now makes it feasible to assess processes beyond easily replicable products. AI‑enhanced collaborative analytics can trace participation, interactions, and revisions through log data, revealing whether students are engaging in meaningful knowledge construction or simply copying outputs (Ouyang et al., 2023). These process‑level data can be fed back to teachers and learners through dashboards to support monitoring, reflection, and improvement. CSCL researchers are well-positioned to design AI-enriched analytics that make collaboration processes and idea development—previously invisible— now visible (Ba et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2022). This process‑focused view emphasizes ongoing work tracing student engagement and activity rather than post‑hoc grading through concurrent, embedded, and transformative assessment. Such analytics assess not only what students produce but how they collaborate—with AI and with one another—to co-construct and to build knowledge. Issues for Discussion: This workshop provides a platform for participants to share ideas as we inquire into how CSCL could address challenges of assessment for new opportunities in the AI era. Grounded in theory, design, and analytics and ecological-ethical considerations, several areas of inquiry include: ● Focus of assessment and expanding theories. Assessment needs to shift from evaluating products to processes–examining how people learn and collaborate with AI. Emerging research on human–AI collaboration highlights cognitive, metacognitive, motivational, and socio-emotional processes, yet more work is needed—for example, on how students take agency and engage in dialogue with AI when tackling assessments to advance learning. Questions also arise about what knowledge, skills, domain-specific competencies, and collaboration processes should be assessed in AI-mediated assessment -- How can AI’s roles shift from merely providing content to supporting co-construction, regulation, and reflection? These shifts call for expanding CSCL theoretical perspectives and engaging with related fields on assessment. Continued inquiry is needed to extend CSCL frameworks to better capture the complexities of learning and collaboration in AI-mediated assessment. ● Collaboration and AI-assessment designs. Building on CSCL designs, researchers can work on productive assessment tasks to replace traditional ones easily emulated by AI and designing AI-enriched collaborative environments. Questions include what we are assessing, what the assessment activity involves, and what resources—peers, AI, and technology—are required. Reflective assessment can prompt students to reflect on their work with AI for ongoing improvement. As well, automatic assessment features can provide teachers with timely information to assess student engagement and to make “on-the-fly” decisions about what support is needed. Design experiments conducted on AI-assessment designs while examining the emerging human-AI collaboration process would help advance both research and practice.. ● Examining AI-supported multi-modal data analysis. Collaborative analytics, combined with AI, can play a central role in assessing learning-collaboration processes via multi-modal data analyses. These could include automatic scoring and annotating student texts and conversations, applying expert-designed rubrics for evaluation, and identifying emerging patterns of behaviors. Information shown in dashboards can support teacher assessment and promote students’ reflection on ongoing work. These analyses open the door to assessing human–AI interactions and designing assessment tasks that capture these dynamics. How these tools and analytics can enhance assessment validity, scalable analysis, providing meaningful insights into collaborative learning processes are important questions. ● Equity and diversity issues. The advent of AI in assessment raises important questions about equity and diversity. AI use may widen existing gaps if students have unequal access to technology, differing levels of guidance, or varying digital literacy. AI technology itself can be biased due to an imbalance in data availability. At the same time, well-scaffolded AI use and mindfully-designed AI tools have the potential to narrow disparities by providing feedback and more accessible pathways for demonstrating student understanding. We need to consider how assessment design and practices can ensure fairness, how AIbased tools can be designed to reduce inequities, and what forms of support learners need to participate equitably in AI-mediated assessment environments. ● Research–practice integration. CSCL-AI assessment for impact involves research-practice synergy developing practical tools and strategies that teachers can use. This involves understanding teachers’ needs and communicating complex findings in accessible, actionable forms. Inquiry such as interviews with teachers about their successful experiences with AI-informed collaboration and assessment could enrich the integration. Supporting higher-education teachers in adopting new views of assessment, collaboration, and AI use—and aligning CSCL research with classroom realities—remain to be investigated. ● Privacy, ethics, and limitations. AI‑supported assessment raises concerns about data privacy, ethical use, and system limitations. Collecting process data—such as interaction logs or conversational traces—requires careful attention to consent, transparency, and responsible data use. Ethical challenges include helping students use AI responsibly, preventing misuse of analytics, addressing bias in AI‑generated judgments, and ensuring students are not harmed by algorithmic errors. Ongoing scrutiny of AI limitations is needed to ensure new CSCL-AI assessment practices remain trustworthy, fair, and beneficial. ● Engagement with the public. The rise of generative AI has sparked widespread public debate, offering CSCL researchers opportunities to engage broader audiences. Public engagement involves helping students, parents, policymakers, and other stakeholders understand the challenges and opportunities AI brings to assessment. By clarifying misconceptions, sharing evidence, success stories, and highlighting responsible uses, CSCL researchers can contribute to more informed public discourse.
