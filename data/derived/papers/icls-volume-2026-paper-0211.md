# Sentiment Analysis in Parent-Child Conversations: Relations Between Emotional Tone and Parental Beliefs in Informal STEM

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Widespread motivational disparities in pursuing STEM careers may originate from negative STEM attitudes that emerge early in development. Children’s attitudes are likely shaped by parent al beliefs, conveyed through parent-child interactions. While prior research emphasized STEM-related content of these interactions, less is known about their affective nature. Here, we examined the emotional tone of parent-child interactions across STEM and non-STEM contexts and tested whether tone was predicted by parents’ beliefs about the role of talent versus effort in STEM success. Using text analysis software (LIWC), we analyzed conversations from 195 parent-child dyads for positive and negative tone. Results showed that non-STEM tasks contained more negative tone than STEM tasks, and spatial tasks elicited more positive tone than math tasks. Additionally, p arents who valued talent over effort expressed higher negative tone during STEM interactions. These findings highlight the importance of considering affective features of parent-child conversations when examining socialization of STEM attitudes.

Persistent declines in students’ interests in STEM fields over recent decades have contributed to a shortage of individuals entering STEM careers (Wiebe et al., 2018). One mechanism behind these motivational differences may be students’ negative attitudes regarding their STEM abilities. Emotional attitudes towards STEM fields are reciprocally linked  with academic achievement, such that  negative (or positive) emotions can exacerbate (or buffer) motivational and performance disparities in STEM (Pekrun et al., 2017). To better  understand these motivational disparities, it is essential to examine when STEM attitudes emerge. Foundational attitudes supporting STEM emerge during preschool years  but become increasingly stable over time, suggesting they may be particularly malleable during early years (Wiebe et al., 2018). By third grade, affective evaluations of math already predict performance, and math anxiety negatively correlates  with achievement (Wu et al., 2012). During these formative years, caregivers play a crucial role in fostering children’s skills and engagement in STEM through direct scaffolding or encouraging child-directed exploration (Crowley & Callanan, 1998). Children’s emerging attitudes are  also shaped by caregivers’ own beliefs, given the amount of time they spend together in early childhood (Barger et al., 2022). Together, these findings highlight the need to elucidate mechanisms by which parent-child interactions transfer parental beliefs to children’s developing STEM attitudes and performance. Two of the mechanism s through which parental beliefs may shape children’s developing attitudes and skills are the informational and affective natures of parent-child interactions during learning experiences. Research on parent-child STEM  talk prior to formal schooling has primarily focused on the informational  content of parental input. For example, the amount of number talk has been linked to children’s early math skills (ClinganSiverly et al., 2021), and parents have been shown to scaffold scientific reasoning through guided exploration in museum settings (Crowley & Callanan, 1998).  However, less is known about the affective  nature of these interactions and how they might contribute to children’s epistemic affect toward STEM (Jaber & Hammer, 2016). Thus, affective tone of conversations is likely key to how parent-child interactions shape motivation and learning. We operationalize this affective dimension as sentiment, defined as the expression of positive or negative evaluative language in communication (Taboada, 2016). Sentiment in learning-related dialogue has been linked to motivational outcomes. For example, in older primary school students, sentiment expressed in online tutoring conversations predict ed math identity, a composite of self-concept and attitudes toward math (Crossley et al., 2018). Building on this work, our goal was to characterize the affective content of parent-child interactions in STEM and non-STEM contexts before formal schooling.

1537 From this perspective, understanding the affective tone of parent-child interactions is only part of the picture; it is also essential to examine what shapes that tone. Expectancy-value theory indicates that parental beliefs are interconnected with children’s affective memories, which can then influence children’s own attitudes (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). For example, Barger et al. (2022) found that w hen parents had more of a growth mindset ideology, their responses valued effort in reaction to their child’s math performance.  Conversely, when parents used more responses focusing on talent, children had more anxious feelings towards math, and their math performance was poor (Barger et al., 2022). Still, little is known about how such beliefs may relate to the affective nature of parent-child conversations during STEM tasks. To investigate this, we used Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC), a text-analysis tool that has been widely used and validated to quantify emotional expression in clinical (Olson et al., 2024) and parent-child work (Risi et al., 2024). LIWC indexes sentiment via its Tone categories, which have not been examined in parent-child STEM contexts to our knowledge (Boyd et al., 2022). The present study had two aims. First, we compared sentiment (positive vs. negative tone) in parent-child conversations across STEM (math, spatial) and non-STEM (verbal) tasks. Given limited prior work, we made no directional prediction about STEM vs. non-STEM differences. Second, we tested whether parents’ beliefs about talent versus effort predicted conversational tone across tasks, hypothesizing that stronger talentover-effort beliefs would be associated with less positive and more negative tone during STEM talk. We used LIWC-22 to map transcript words to psychosocial dictionary categories, and we targeted the pre-school period to illuminate foundations of children’s emerging STEM attitudes and skills.

## Participants and procedure

Participants were from a Midwestern city in the U.S. comprised of 19 5 parent-child dyads with children aged 3 to 6 years. Of the children, 93 (47.7%) identified as female, 8 6 (44.1%) as male, and 1 6 had missing data. T he majority of parents were mothers (N=1 62; 83.1%), one participant identified as neither male nor female, and 16 participants had missing data. Child participants varied in age (M=4.7 8, SD=0.63) and gestational age at birth (M=35.7 weeks, SD=4.82 weeks, range=22 to 42 weeks), with 108 children being term-born (37 weeks gestational age or more). Average parent education was 16.4 years, corresponding to a college degree (range=12 to 18 years). Average household income was $130,487 but had wide variability (range=$13,000 to $700,000). person in the lab during a single visit, and the rest completed the study online via Zoom through four 45-60 minute Zoom sessions. Online visits were split into four separate sessions to reduce potential effects of fatigue from the screen. Other than the format of administration, all other procedures were conducted identically. P arent-child dyads participated in a Three-Bag Task, an observational assessment developed for the NICHD Study of Early Child Care and Youth Development (MacWhinney, 1998). Parents and children engaged in three tasks, each involving either a sorting and counting toy activity (STEM, math task), an animal puzzle to solve (STEM, spatial task), or a picture book titled Corduroy (non-STEM, verbal). The parents were instructed to interact with their child as they normally would at home. Out of the 195 dyads, all completed the puzzle task, 185 completed the toy activity, and 190 completed the book task.

Coding and emotional tone All parent-child interactions were video-recorded during the Three-Bag Task sessions. A transcription service was used to generate initial transcriptions of conversations. Trained research assistants verified the accuracy of the transcriptions using video recordings and segmented the speech into distinct utterances based on pauses and intonation patterns. A second research assistant independently checked all transcripts. Disagreements were resolved by having the two transcribers go over the text together.  Transcripts were reformatted using the CLAN (Computerized Language Analysis) software suite, following CHAT conventions as part of CHILDES (Child Language Data Exchange System) within TalkBank (MacWhinney, 1998). To examine the overall sentiment of the conversations, we used the most recent version of the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC-22) software. LIWC compares words from text input to its internal dictionary, calculating total word count and proportion of words belonging to categories such as Affect or Tone (Boyd et al., 2022). The Tone  dimension includes positive (tone_pos) and negative (tone_neg) words reflecting overall sentiment, though not all convey emotion. As examples, delicious and beautiful qualify as positive tone, whereas not good and funeral fall under negative tone (Boyd et al., 2022). Because our focus was on general sentiment, 1538 analyses centered on these positive and negative tone categories.  Transcripts from the Three-Bag Task were uploaded into the software  and divided by each task (math/pie sorter, spatial/puzzle, and verbal/book). This enabled us to gather positive and negative tone values for each of the interactions separately and  compare data through linear mixed models using Jamovi software. These tasks, including the book Corduroy, were chosen because of their familiarity with children of this age and usage in prior studies (Barnes & Puccioni, 2017).  The book transcripts included portions of the text which could introduce inherent sentiment from the story. We chose to retain this text because parents varied meaningfully in how they read the book—often skipping sections, adding interjections, or modifying wording (e.g., one dyad replaced Corduroy with Cotton Candy at the child’s request).

The goal of this study was to examine emotional sentiment (tone) of parent-child interactions during STEM and non-STEM tasks using a text analysis software  (LIWC) and relate these differences to parental beliefs about the role of talent compared to effort for success in STEM. Our first question was how the emotional tone of parent-child conversations differs during STEM (spatial and math) and non-STEM (verbal) tasks. Results showed that parent-child conversations during the non-STEM task were more negative and less positive compared to parent-child conversations during the STEM tasks. These challenge compared to the book task for the parent and child (Jaber & Hammer, 2016). Alternatively, it is possible that the specific book read during the verbal (non-STEM) task elicited more negative emotions due to the story content involving conflict of a bear trying to find his lost button. We also found that the level of negative tone in conversations during the spatial (puzzle) task compared to the math (toy) task did not differ. Yet, the spatial task did have higher levels of positive tone than the math task during parent-child conversations. According to Sorvo et al. (2017), young children might be more motivated in spatial-related STEM contexts rather than math-related STEM fields. This is noteworthy given that mathematics is typically emphasized in formal schooling, whereas spatial reasoning often is not. The difference in positive tone between the spatial and math tasks during these parent-child interactions could play a key role in the development of poorer math attitudes as children get older.

Our second research question was how parental beliefs regarding the role of talent versus effort in STEM success vary in relation to emotional tone across the STEM tasks. Results showed that parents who valued talent more than effort had more negative tone during the STEM task conversations. Consistent with expectancy-value theory, this could suggest a relation between parent beliefs and parent-child affect during interactions, which could predict exacerbating STEM attitudes in later development (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). Barger et al. (2022) illustrates this by finding that parents with less of a growth mindset had children with higher math anxiety. Since Barger et al. (2022) conducted this study in school-aged children, we extend these findings by focusing on the period before formal schooling.

Future work could compare variation in tone with different genres of children’s books to see how content can influence affect  in parent-child interactions. Additionally, longitudinal work could examine how sentiment around STEM context changes and if positive tone during earlier interactions is a possible buffering factor. Importantly, expanding on Wigfield & Eccles’ expectancy-value theory (2000), how might emotional sentiment during these parent-child conversations relate to children’s own STEM beliefs? Although not central to our research question, older children’s interactions were characterized by higher levels of positive tone. This pattern aligns with prior findings showing that conversations with 3-to 4-year-olds include more emotional talk (such as grateful or nervous, belonging to a subset of tone) than those with younger children, likely reflecting children’s advancing emotion knowledge and parents’ use of a broader emotional vocabulary as their children’s language and understanding of emotions develop (Lagattuta & Wellman, 2002). We also found that children of higher gestational age engaged in interactions with a more positive tone. Parents of preterm-born children may hold lingering concerns about developmental vulnerability, and this might be reflected in an overall more negative tone. Prior studies indicate that mothers of preterm infants often perceive their children as at risk for delays (Stern et al., 2006). Our findings suggest that such differences in parental perception and interactional tone may persist beyond infancy. In sum, the findings from our study highlight the possible importance  of emotional tone in parent-child interactions during informal STEM learning contexts  and its relations to parental beliefs regarding talent versus effort in their child’s performance. Although prior research has primarily focused on content of parent-child conversations, we have offered a unique angle to show that the level of emotional tone and parent beliefs during parent-child interactions are related.
