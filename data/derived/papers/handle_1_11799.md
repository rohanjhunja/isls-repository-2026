# Speculative Biotechnologies: AI as a Catalyst for Biodesign Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how integrating generative AI and speculative design into biodesign education can enhance students’ ability to envision and articulate sustainable and socially just futures. Conducted in a Colombian university with design students, the study employed three speculative scenarios—fatalistic, optimistic, and practical futures—alongside continuous reflection. Findings indicate that generative AI helped students visualize biotechnological solutions, such as hydrogel barriers and biosustainable leather production, enabling them to connect speculative futures with tangible societal and ecological applications. This process encouraged critical engagement, ethical awareness, and strengthened students’ identities as ethical designers. Digital AI-generated artifacts bridged gaps in students’ knowledge of biological systems, making abstract concepts accessible and empowering them to imagine impactful, community-driven solutions. By integrating speculative design and AI into biodesign education, this study demonstrates the potential of such approaches to support interdisciplinary learning, develop critical design agency, and prepare students to address complex global challenges through innovative, localized solutions.

## Introduction

AI has revealed its potential as both a learning aid and a medium for critical speculation, encouraging students to redefine their relationship with technology to contest dominant narratives and tap into their communities’  cultural practices and needs (Co rtez et al., 2024). Similarly, by engaging in critical and speculative design (Dunne and Raby, 2013), students are prompted to imagine and create artifacts and social infrastructures that challenge social and technological norms, envisioning preferable fut ures in which science and technology supports socia l justice, environmental sustainability, and community well-being (Matuk, 2023; 2024). Educators who incorporat e these frameworks guide students not only in critiquing cu rrent systems but in actively shapi ng alternative futures. As AI further integrates into educational contexts, eq uipping students to navigate its strengths and limi tations becomes essential. Similarly, innovations in life science education have incorporated Biodesign (Walker & Kafai, 2021), where organisms can be modified to create customiza ble and functional products. Therefore, a critical engagement with both AI and Biodesign tools can fos ter an awareness of its ethical, cultural, and envi ronmental impacts, ultimately empowering students to use these technologies for inclusive, sustainable futures. Existing research on learning through speculative design, Dunne and Raby (2013), positions design as a medium for questioning societal norms and envisioni ng more just futures. Studies in design education h ave revealed that methods like critical reflection (Iivari et al., 2024), design futuring (Sharma et al., 2024; Vent äOlkkonen et al., 2024), and culturally-grounded frameworks (Holbert et al., 2021) empower students to explore ethical, cultural, and social implications of desig ning for the present and the future, often developi ng critical perspectives on technology. More specifically, lear ning scientists have shown how Biodesign education,  which involves learners exploring the possibilities of ma king with living organisms, can be motivated by per sonal interest, cultural histories and issues of socio-political relevance (Walker et al., 2023). In paral lel, generative AI tools have been used in design education to help students prototype and envision their designs, helpin g them navigate the first steps of th e design process (Zailuddin et al., 2024). Moreover, since biological organisms are different from other materials used in design becau se of their unpredictability and the precise requir ements for their manipulation (Walker & Kafai, 2021),  the out comes of a biodesign process and its societal consequences might be difficult to envision. Thus, pairing gener ative AI tools with speculative approaches to  Biod esign, i.e., having students imagine what they could design thro ugh the use and manipulation of living organisms, with generative AI tools could help students to envision, contextualize and further develop their designs. To better understand the affordances of integrating  generative AI tools in design education and its affordances to learning, we designed a speculative design unit within a Colombian university course fo r design majors. In this unit, students collaborated with generative AI to create fatalistic and optimistic futures, as well as a future for their own biotechnological designs. Through the collection and analysis of students’ artifacts made in class, such as AI generated images and sustained reflections throughout the activity, we investigate the following RQ: How does the integration of generative AI in sp eculative design activities influence students' abi lity to envision and articulate sustainable and socially just futures? In what ways does engaging with generative AI in a biodesign curriculum shape students’ identities and  agency as ethical designers and problem-solvers? This research has implications for the de sign of learning experiences that involve learning-through-design in general, and specifically to research aiming to pair student s with generative AI tools for the early stages of envisioning their designs, as well as for biodesign education.

## Background

To understand learning-through-design, we draw from its connection to construction ist theories of learning (Papert, 1991), which emphasize that engaging in ma king, building, or programming can significantly en hance learning experiences. This approach sugge sts that creating tangible artifacts —whether digital or physical —not only facilitates the construction of knowledge but also fosters a deeper relationship between the designer and the object. As Kafai and Resnick (2012) assert, design and learning were viewed independently, with design focusing on end products and constraints, while learning cen tered on processes and knowledge construction. Howe ver, these fields have gradually converged, sharing a fo cus on the "construction of meaning" as a core proc ess. In the context of design, this meaning-making involves linking elements of an artifact wit h aspects of its context, forming a cohesive unity. Constructionist learning,  as advanced by Kafai and Resnick (2012), further h ighlights that building external artifacts supports meaningful learning by enabling students to connect personally with their work, often enriching the learning process itself.

Learning through speculative and critical design Speculative design, as introduced by Dunne and Raby  (2013), positions design as a method for questioni ng societal norms and envisioning alternatives. Unlike  traditional design practices focused on product cr eation, speculative design seeks to foster "critical thought translated into materiality" (pg. 35). This approach encourages creators to think beyond probable futures, instead engaging with the possible and preferable to design  scenarios aimed at social justice and inclusivity. Through sp eculative design, students can conceptualize artifacts that challenge existing norms, using the medium as a bri dge to reimagine human and material interactions.  This orientation shifts focus from commodified design ob jects to tools for imagining just and sustainable f utures, grounding educational design within a framework tha t fosters both critical engagement and aspirational  thinking (Morales & Fargo, 2020).

A key theme emerging from recent studies on critica l and speculative design in education is the promotion of ethical and sociocultural awareness. In studies by Iivari et al. (2024), students engaged  with design methodologies that encouraged them to reflect on the societal implications of technology; they found that although immediate impacts on ethical awareness were hard to  measure, the structured process of reflection enab led children to connect design work with real-world ethical challenges over time. The thematic overlap in these studies highlights the importance of ethically-grounded design education in fostering students' socio political awareness. Together, these studies show that by including ethi cal reflection in the design process, students beco me more critically engaged with technology and its social c onsequences, enabling them to create meaningful, et hical solutions within their future professions.

Another central theme in critical and speculative d esign research is the role of identity formation and agency in learning. Studies by Sharma et al. (2024) and Arada et al. (2023) demonstrate that design education can support students in developing a sense of identity as active creators and critics of technology. Sharma et al. showed that by engaging in long-term design projects, children could adopt diverse roles such as designers, makers, and activists. This role adoption contributed to a sens e of self-efficacy, as students explored their capabilities wit hin different contexts and gradually saw themselves as technology protagonists. The process of designing, making, and critically analyzing artifacts allowed students  to experience multiple identities, high lighting design education’s potential in supporting students’ self-perceptions and self-confidence in technology-related fields. Arada et al. (2023) introduced the framework of Cri tical Speculative Design Pedagogy, which provided m iddle school students with structured opportunities to en vision restorative futures addressing social injust ices in technology use. This framework fostered agency and a sense of empowerment among students, as they were encouraged to imagine alternative futures where the ir communities thrived through socially just applic ations of technology. With the framing of technology as a med ium for community-driven solutions, Arada et al. (2023) highlighted the role of design in helping students build identities as empowered individuals who can advocate for positive social change. The agency developed through these frameworks is instrumental in shaping how students perceive their ability to impact the world and their communities, reinforcing the importance of identity formation within critical and speculative design pedagogy.

In the learning sciences, a key reference for this study is the work of Campos (2024), which illustrat es how students can combine criticality and speculativ e imagination to address local challenges rooted in  their community experiences. By engaging with environment al and social issues, students are empowered to cre ate solutions to problems relevant to their lives and t heir “limiting situations”—defined as "situations of oppression that limit one's capacity for being" (p. 1). For example, they might address the lack of artistic spaces that amplify youth voices by using AI to envision alternatives grounded in reality. In this context, speculative design becomes a gateway for critically analyzing present realitie s while imagining sustainable futures. Although not explici tly framed within design education, Campos’s work empha sizes civic imagination —“the capacity to imagine alternatives to current social, political, and econ omic conditions (...) even if those alternatives tap the fantastic” (Jenkins et al., 2020, p. 5) —within a Latin American setting. This perspective c losely aligns with our understanding of speculative design as a practice t hat encourages students to intentionally envision a rtifacts and social infrastructures extending beyond their immediate possibilities. Biodesign as a provocative space for critical design Learning environments that center Biodesign, an int erdisciplinary field that “integrates living organi sms into designed solutions, often with the aim of improving  sustainability and ecological performance in novel  ways” (Myers, 2012; Gogugh et al., 2021) h ave recently been studied in the learning sciences,  this because of its affordances to rethink life science education towar ds a more active-learning focus, but also because the opportunities it brings to broaden the scope of making education, allowing teachers and learners to consider living organisms as materials that can be modified and tha t can be used to make personal and community releva nt artifacts (Walker & Kafai, 2021). Due to this, Biodesign has also been shown to be an ideal point of entry towards reflections on socio-scientific issues, both at a discipline-level, with learning questioning the ethics, afford ances and concerns of scientific use and prototyping with  living organisms, but also in a transdisciplinary and sociopolitical breadth, with learners reflecting on the potential use of biologica l materials to address community issues (Walker et al., 2022) Moreover, in a review of the Biodesign projects of the Biodesign challenge, the most influential platform and program for educational biodesign international ly, Walker et al. (2023) showed that participants c onnected with their projects through three different rationa les, either through personal interest: designing artifacts from their own or their communities’ benefit, through, cultural history, connecting with local histories of practice and designing to address problems with local relevancy, and sociopolitical relevance, in which biodesign is motivated by a broader social or political concern. All this research has shown that Biodesign is a highly provocative context for students to reflect on societal issues, using biological materials as a means to rethink society. This aligns with existing educational approaches to speculative design, in which learners engage with design by reimagining social infrastructures around technologies and materials (Wong et al., 2020); therefore, is not surprising th at existing research about Biodesign and its possibilities have already integrated speculative design at different scales, either by bringing experts in biological sciences and desi gn to imagine the future homes (Gough et al., 2021)  or workshops in which youth imagine synthetic-biology based food products (Walker & Kafai, 2021). Integrating AI into design curriculum For this study, we also draw from recent research t hat has explored the use of artificial intelligence  in design education, specifically in courses aimed at discipl inarily forming students as designers. Regarding th e use of Generative AI by such students, most studies have been case-based, examining the affordances and limitations of incorporating generative AI into their design proce sses. Approaches have varied, including measuring a nd comparing pre-and post-creativity levels and cognitive loads between groups using generative AI in their designs and those who did not. These studies have found that students working with AI experienced lower cognitive load while producing more creative outputs (Chandrasekera et al., 2024). Similarly, other case studies in which students input design datasets, such as images and meshes, i nto AI systems to generate novel designs have demon strated a synergistic relationship between AI and designers. This collaboration, wherein datasets are used to produce object prototypes, has been shown to stimulate crea tivity (Kavakolgu et al., 2022). Likewise, pairing design students with generative AI tools that create images, such as DALL-E, has revealed an advantageous relationship, with instructors noting that students feel more com fortable advancing through the initial phases of th e creative process. Students appreciated the ability to expres s some of their ideas through interactions with the  tool, specifically highlighting the affordances of pairin g with AI during early ideation. However, they also  expressed concerns about becoming overly reliant on AI (Zailuddin et al., 2024).

## Context and participants

This study was conducted at a private university in  Colombia, within a 16-week virtual undergraduate course on biodesign held from August to December 2024. The course was co-taught by two professors from the faculties of Design and Biology, each bringing unique expertise:  one specializing in product design and biodesign w ith a creative emphasis, and the other in biology, microb iology, and plant pathology. The class consisted of  17 undergraduate students from Colombia, including 4 m en and 13 women. The curriculum aimed to foster speculative design and biodesign thinking, using ge nerative AI tools such as Canva, Midjourney, and Ad obe Firefly to encourage students to envision solutions  for pressing environmental and social issues proje cted for the year 2050. Therefore, the course included three pri mary activities with generative AI, each designed t o build students’ ability to visualize and contextualize sp eculative futures. The prompting strategies were sc affolded by providing a single instruction to generate a prompt  while deliberately avoiding guided specifications on how to formulate it, encouraging independent exploration and creative problem-solving.

1. Fatalistic future: In the initial activity, each student created their  own AI-generated image representing

a fatalistic vision of 2050, using self-developed prompts. This exercise, held on the first day of the course, was intended to gauge each student's baseline abilities with generative AI and to initiate critical thinking about environmental and societal challenges.

2. Optimistic future: Three weeks later, students completed an optimistic  future activity, which required

them to generate three AI-generated images individually. Here, students refle cted on potential positive transformations, contrasting the initial catastroph ic scenarios. Thi s exercise, conducted after several weeks of lectures and initial research, helped stud ents consider social and environmental benefits of future technologies and analyze the outcomes collaboratively within their groups.

3. Designed practical futures with biotechnology: The third activity occurred after students had developed

specific biodesign projects. Each student generated  three images individually, now focusing on a grounded, realistic representation of their idealiz ed future where their biodesign solutions had been successfully implemented. This activity challenged students to refine prompts for precise visual communication and encouraged reflection on bioethical, regulatory, and socio-cultural implications. After these activities, students attended a lecture on the environmental (Li et al., 2023) and societal (Hogan, 2021) impacts of AI in which the water consumption of data centers, their locations in places like Mexico, and the labor infrastructure supporting generative AI was discussed. The lecture aimed to encourage students to critically reflect on the ethical implications of AI use and its broad er consequences. We aimed to foster a critical appr oach to AI literacy that nurtured skepticism toward the indiscriminate use of these technologies, emphasizing both individual and societal-level ethics in their application (Logan, 2024). This component was considered essential, as our goal as instructors was to develop designers who are mindful of the consequences of the tools they employ.

## Data collection and analysis

Data collected included student-generated AI images, reflective prompts, group discussions, and final interviews conducted at the end of the course. The final inter views provided additional insights into students’ e xperiences and reflections on the design process, AI usage, and their evolving understanding of biodesign and spe culative thinking.  For analysis, thematic analysis was appl ied, guided by methodologies from Nowell et al. (20 17) and Fereday & Muir-Cochrane (2006). According to thematic analysis, both deductive and inductive approaches were employed, with initial themes emerging from the pre defined course objectives and subsequently refined through repeated examination of student artifacts, reflecti ons, and interview responses. The thematic analysis began with a familiarization process, where researchers repeat edly reviewed the AI-generated images, student reflections, and interview transcripts to note initial themes. C oding was then carried out inductively from the dat a while referencing deductive templates based on key course themes, allowing patterns to emerge organically and ensuring alignment with the speculative biodesign framework. Codes were clustered to form cohesive themes that reflected students' evolving engagement with biodesign and AI, a nd these thematic connections were re-evaluated for coherence, with peer debriefings ensuring reliability. Overall, the thematic analysis aimed to capture how students' perceptions and design approaches shifted through e ach activity, exploring themes of en vironmental consciousness, ethical reflection, and community im pact. In this paper, we focus our analysis on Ana a nd Michelle, two design majors, to demonstrate how stu dents progressed throughout the proposed activities. We selected them as we found them r epresentative of the themes identified in the overa ll data, while also offering distinct perspectives and instances of these themes  through their differing speculative design project s developed during the course.

## Results

Our analysis identified three key themes: Expanding  Social and Environmental Awareness, Envisioning Transformative Futures Through AI, and Developing E thical Agency and Designer Identity. We also observ ed a correspondence between these themes and specific moments in our curriculum. Accordingly, we present each theme in connection with a particular moment of our  curriculum design. This does not imply that the th eme was exclusive to that moment or activity but rather that the chosen moment serves as an illustrative example to describe the theme and its relationship to the curriculum.

Fatalistic future: Expanding social and environmental awareness One central theme observed was how engaging with ge nerative AI broadened students' understanding of environmental and social issues, helping them recog nize interconnected problems and envision comprehen sive solutions. The first activity —creating a “fatalis tic” vision of the future —prompted students to confront the ecological impacts of current practices directly.  In her fatalistic scenario, Michelle used AI to dep ict “vast, stagnant water bodies choked with green algae and n o sign of life,” representing eutrophication caused  by excessive fertilizer use (see Figure 1, Left). Refl ecting on this image, she wrote, “The AI helped me visualize what uncontrolled fertilizer use cando, not just t o a small pond but to entire water systems.” This a ctivity highlighted for Michelle the potential devastation linked to intensive agriculture and fueled her curiosity to explore regenerative practices in the later activities. Ana  centered her fatalistic vision on the Doña Juana l andfill near Bogotá, visualizing a “mountain of endless waste with layers of plastics, flies, and clouds of toxic gas” (see Figure 2, Left). She explained, “Growing up near this site, I’ve seen the health issues it causes —the AI brought it all together visually, like a message for change.” Her reflection demonstrated how AI amplified her awareness of waste management’s social and ecological impacts. T hrough the image, Ana connected environmental degradation to community health issues, enhancing her appreciation for sustainable design solutions in subsequent tasks.

Generative AI content facilitated deeper engagement  with issues students felt passionate about due to personal connections, allowing them to not only des cribe but visually amplify the scope of these probl ems. This process encouraged Ana and Michelle to adopt broade r perspectives, framing sustainability and social j ustice as interconnected rather than isolated issues. As Walk er et al. (2023) explain, cultural history serves a s a motive through which youth connect to their biodesign init iatives, tying their designs to solutions for issue s with longstanding relevance in their communities. We found t hat prompting students to envision fatalistic futur es helped them confront the consequences of the misuses and excesses of current technologies, fueling their later disposition toward critical design and grounding their need to imagine and design alternative solutions. As illust rated in Michelle’s reasoning, a specific affordance of AI f or this activity lies in its ability to visualize l arge-scale consequences of material misuse—such as the impact of fertilizers on ecosystems —and to extrapolate the long-term effects of harmful practices like waste dispos al. These fatalistic futures not only deepened stud ents' connection to specific issues but also established these scenarios as provocative and urgent contexts for biodesign exploration.

Optimistic futures: AI-generated images as compasses toward better tomorrows The second activity, which required students to cre ate optimistic visions for the future, allowed them  to conceptualize transformative biotechnological solut ions. Working with AI to generate positive future s cenarios helped the students refine their approaches and env ision better futures. Michelle’s optimistic prompt described a “small Colombian village with a leather factory operating sustainably, surrounded by clean lakes and forests” (see Figure 1, Center). Her AI-generated image portrayed clean water bodies adjacent to leather production facilities, a stark contrast to her earlier fatalistic vision. She reflected, “AI allowed me to put this future in  front of me—to see what a community can look like when industry ad apts.” Through this exercise, Michelle connected environmental health with community prosperity, ima gining sustainable leather practices that incorpora ted local resources while avoiding harmful chemicals. The AI visualization reinforced her belief that “eco-friendly practices can preserve both our environment and our local economies,” illustrat ing her developing sense of sustainable design principles.

Similarly, in her optimistic scenario, Ana’s vision  involved “glass-domed structures in lakes and rivers filtering out harmful algae and pollutants” (see Fi gure 2, Center). She described the structures as “a  way to give back clean water to communities” and noted that “seeing it visually in the AI image helped me see this design as a real solution.” The AI-generated images allowed her to experiment with sca le and integration into the natural landscape, helping her see her design as both impac tful and achievable. Ana’s response indicated an ev olving view on biotechnological interventions as solutions that are not only feasible but necessary to address community health and environmental restoration. Visualizing their preferred futures through generative AI supported students in exploring how speculative design could contribut e to reshaping local and global contexts. This proc ess also underscored for both Ana and Michelle the significa nce of “realistic but hopeful” designs that provide  tangible benefits to both communities and ecosystems.

Through this activity, we found that generative AI helped students envision more just and sustainable futures as achievable. Michelle reflected on the “e mpowering effect of seeing sustainable futures that  I could actually imagine coming true,” highlighting  how AI boosted her confidence in biodesign’s potent ial for social good. Similarly, Ana observed that “AI allowed me t o see my ideas materialize,” reinforcing her belief  that biodesign could address urgent local challenges, su ch as water contamination. Both students emphasized  the usefulness of these tools in visualizing futures where they could exert positive agency through their designs. Ana wrote, “From my perspective, it could be used to visualize final ideas, particularly in the case of photos and videos that can solidify our projects.” This aligns with a key premise of recent approaches to speculative education: that youth should be empowered to dream of a society mor e just than the one current systems have failed to deliver (Mirra & García, 2020). Specifically, we observed that imagining more equitable and sustainable futures through AI-generated images appeared to support students in ex pressing a sense of agency, which was further explo red through their designs. A useful metaphor for this i s to think of these images as a compass, guiding th eir designs as a means of bridging the present with the better futures they envision. ## Discussion

This study highlights the educational potential of integrating generative AI and speculative design into biodesign education. Engaging students in iterative activitie s across three speculative scenarios —fatalistic, optimistic, and practical futures—paired with continuous reflection revealed shifts in their design agency, ethical awareness, and ability to envision sustainable solutions. Building  on Iivari et al. (2024), who emphasize the importa nce of reflection in design, our findings show that sustained reflective practice fosters critical engagement with emerging technologies. Scenarios supported by generative AI enabled students to explore the ethical, environmen tal, and social dimensions of their designs while visualizing realistic contexts. For example, AI helped Ana and Michelle articulate complex biotechnological solutions, such as hydrogel barriers for irrigation systems and biosustainable leather production, connecting speculative futures with tangible possibilities. As Walker et al. (2023) noted, biodesign often draws on cultural histories and socio political relevance; similarly, our findings demonstrate how generative AI helps design students to envision bio technologies that address community and ecological challenges. This empowerment aligns with Mirra & Garcia’s (2020) view of speculative education as an approach to engage in social dreaming towards a more just society. Our findings extend this by showing that AI-generated imagery supports students in envisioning socially just and sustainable futures, even when working with unfamiliar materials like biological systems. AI helped studen ts integrate biodesign projects into broader social infrastructures, as seen in Michelle’s vision of gu ava-based leather prod uction and Ana’s algae-purifying structures. These findings reflect Sharma et al.’s (2024) work on speculative design fostering identit y formation and agency, enabling students to see themselves as active creators and problem-solvers. Notably, generative A I and speculative design proved especially valuable i n biodesign education for students without expertis e in life sciences. For design students, AI served as a tool to connect with complex biological materials, empowering them to envision realistic solutions and strengthening their designer identities. Framing the design process through three speculativ e scenarios encouraged students to explore their ideas iteratively and contextually. The fatalistic future activity grounded their understanding of sys temic challenges by confronting ecological and social consequences of current practices. The optimistic f uture encouraged them to reimagine systems, envisioning transformative solutions that bridged community needs with ecological sustainability. The designed future task  integrated these insights into practical biotechno logical solutions, supported by AI-generated imagery. This structured progression alig ns with Vent ä-Olkkonen et al. (2024), who emphasize staged design activities in f ostering critical thinking and creativity. Moreover, this study has inherent limitations. Conducted as a case study in a Colombi an university, the findings are situated within a specific cultural and educational context, which ma y limit generalizability. Additionally, the small s ample size restricts broader conclusions about the l ong-term impacts of this approach. Overall, this study underscores the transformative potential of combining generative AI  and speculative design in biodesign education. Sta ged speculative activities supported by reflection nurture students’ ability to envision sustainable futures, develop their agency as ethical designers, and bridge disciplinar y divides. These findings resonate with Matuk’s (20 24) vision of speculative design as a tool for social justice and environmental sustainability, suggesting that such frameworks can empower students to actively shape a more just and sustainable world.
