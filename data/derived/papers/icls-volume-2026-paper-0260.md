# Designing with Care: Exploring the Embodied and Affective Dimensions of Collaborative Noticing

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper brings together three cases to examine the question, what happens when we approach the design of research contexts to explore professional noticing with care? Through video-based interaction analysis, a pattern emerged within the researcher-participant interactions. When researchers engaged with care, research contexts became sites of collaborative sense-making that supported participants to come tonew insights about their practices. Essential to the development of these research contexts was the emic nature of the researchers’ connections to the partnerships–each researcher had a personal connection to the professional practice. Together, these insights point to possibilities for the study of a researchers’ noticing practices, which we argue is integral to the analysis and design of future research partnerships and contribute to meaningful and care-full learning and research.

Over the last decade scholars have been “extending the conceptual territory of research on teacher noticing” (Rosebery et al., 2016, p. 1575). In doing so, scholars have called for an increased attention to methodological choices–selection of data sources, units of analysis, analytical approaches, and exploration in broader contexts – to deepen our understanding of responsiveness that is equitable and just (Louie, 2018; 2022; Walkoe & Luna 2020; Kang, 2022; Vossoughi et al., 2023). In this paper, we explore noticing, or the ways in which professionals attend to relevant phenomena, reason about observation phenomena, and subsequently decide to act (Sherin et al., 2008; Erikson 2010) across three different contexts. In particular, we explore the ways in which w e approached the design and analysis of research records with care. We draw on sociocultural framings and foreground care as a methodological practice to explore affective dimensions of noticing for equity across three different settings – Special Education, Dance Education, and Improvisational Music. In each of these projects, the researcher has an intentional, emic, or personal, connection with the participants and professional practice. Case 1 was developed by Shivani, a former Special Educator to explore and support the development of novice Special  Educators practices. Case 2 was developed by Lindsay, an educational researcher and dance educator and dancer, to study dance teachers working in K-12 schools. Case 3 was developed by Ananda, a researcher in c ollaboration with a close family member-having a familial connection to high-level improvisational jazz musicians and communities was integral to the development of this lasting research partnership. As such, we routinely drew on our own experiences and relationships to design research activities alongside communities we sought to learn with and from. We view our responsibility as researchers to engage in our own dynamic, noticing process and to approach and relational process, we orient our methodologies around care and ask, what happens when we approach the design of research contexts to explore noticing of one’s professional practice with care? To answer this question, we engaged in a collaborative process where we compared noticing for care across the three cases described here.

## Centering participants’ practices

In contrast to the historical legacy of extractive, one-sided research practices (Vossoughi et al.,2023), we view designing research as co-learning contexts for both participants and researchers as an act of care. We draw on Nel Noddings to theorize care as “feeling with the other” and as a process of “engrossment” (1984 p. 30). Furthermore, for participants to share their meaning-making of their own experiences and practices, researchers must create contexts within which participants candevelop those understandings (De Fina & Perrino 2011; Vossoughi & Zavala 2020). Similarly, researchers must draw on their knowledge and understanding of participants’ value systems and ways of being to engage in the reflexive practice of making relevant meaning from partici pants’ contributions (Wilson, 2020). Thus, to design research contexts to explore professional noticing in care-full ways, we expand traditional noticing methods by centering the affective and embodied ways of knowing and doing that are central to particip ants’ professional practices. In doing so, we join scholars that have begun to examine the 1782 ways that teachers committed to equity attune to the affective, embodied, and relational dimensions of their practices (Melander 2018; Coles & Helliwell 2025; Rubin & van Es 2025). To explore noticing across contexts, we draw on research that theorizes noticing from Goodwin’s conceptualization of Professional Vision (1994). From this perspective, noticing is  a socially situated practice, embedded in the larger cultural, historical, and political contexts of the teacher and learner (Louie, 2018; 2021 Russ & Luna 2013; Wager, 2014, Hand, 2012; Roseberry et al., 2016). Where noticing research methods often ask participants to reflect on what they noticed about a particular event, time and practice, we sought to frame this task in ways that centered the larger sociopolitical dimensions and patterns of their noticing. Understanding that these dimensions are not often named explicitly but embedded within participants’ sense-making, we sought  to be in and continue to develop authentic relationships with participants (Kang, 2022). Furthermore, rather than an internal, mental process, we view noticing as an interactional and relational process in which professionals attend and respond to specific phenomena “by coming into direct contact with the environment through active exploration that involves contributions from the body as a whole, not just her eyes” (Sheiner 2021; p. 13). Case 1 comes from an interview-based study exploring teachers’ noticing practices with students of color with disabilities. As a former special educator and current co-designer of a Special Education credentialing pathway, essential to the research design  was the co-creation of a context for collaborative exploration and reflection on shared teaching experience, positionalities, and commitments to social justice education. To innovate existing noticing interview methods by foregrounding affect, following c lassroom observations, Shivani leveraged affective evaluations (Levine, 2014) and improvised questions to explore the affective dimensions of participants’ previous in-the-moment responses.  Case 2 comes from an interview-based study with dance educators teaching full-time in K-12 schools in the United States. The researcher is also a dance educator, and the shared identity of dancer was an intentional design component. Participants completed three, 60-90 minute Choreographic Interviews via Zoom. To foreground creative processes, Interview 3 began with 10 minutes of improvisational time for both the dance educator and researcher to choreograph a short dance inspired by the two previous interviews and a “movement map” tracing the dance educators’ movements throughout a typical teaching day. These dances were then shared and taken up as both an embodied practice and a legible piece of data to refer to when reflecting on the embodied and affective experiences of the classroom. Case 3 comes from participatory design study (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) in which five improvisational jazz artists participated in 15 co-design meetings and performed three live concerts over the course of four years. Comparing across cases Theoretically, to explore the ways that participants noticed from a place of care, we drew on the tools of narrative what is said and how it is said to explore the values, beliefs, and attitudes that underpin participants’ ideological understandings of themselves and their practices (Lawrence-Lightfoot & Hoffmandavis 1997; Kim, 2016). To do so, we identified key narratives participants sha red within the research contexts either when reflecting on a particular salient moment or larger phenomena within their practice. We each identified a set of narratives that were representative of our larger data corpus, sharing both transcripts and audio/video clips with one another. Together, we watched and listened to the narrative to identify patterns that emerged across three data sets. We alternated between deductive and inductive approaches looking for both themes that emerged from the data and ways in which participants resisted deficit framings that were common to their fields of practice and instead engaged from a desire to collaborate and build and strengthen relationships, which we considered to be acts of care.

When we came together to explore the methodological impact of our work, we engaged in a new round of analysis through the conceptual lens of care. To explore the impact of our own contributions during research activities, we considered ways to conceptualize a unit of analysis for “care.” Where units of analysis are “bundles” or “containers” that hold both information about the originally collected data and researchers’ ideas and theories about what counts as knowledge (Marin 2020 p. 285), we sought to conceptualize care within our data as instances when researchers centered participants affective and embodied ways of knowing and doing to support collaborative sense-making within the research activities. Drawing on the notion of intersubjectivity or the process by which interlocutors co-create shared intellectual space in order to develop mutual understanding (Rogoff, 1998), we identified moments within our individual data corpus when participants and researchers collaborated 1783 to come tonew understandings that challenged deficit perspectives that are common to each context. After selecting representative clips, we engaged in video-based interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). In video-based interaction analysis, researchers examine human activities (talk, non verbal interaction, the use of artifacts, materials and technology) to identify routine practices and problems and resources for their solution. It often involves the viewing of audio-video recordings multiple times to identify patterns, shifts, and sequentially. Thus, we viewed our data clips multiple times and in different orders to identify themes and patterns that emerged across the three contexts, looking specifically at the impact of our contributions (as rese archers) on participants’ sense-making within the research context.

In designing research contexts to both explore and develop care-full noticing, we share two findings. The first is theoretical-we explore how our participants described the ways in which noticing in care-full ways involved wonder and curiosity. The second is methodological, in which we reflect on how designing research with care created opportunities for researchers to act as collaborators in noticing that supported participants to come tonew understandings about their professional practices. We consider the ways this care-full approach has implications for researcher noticing.

Curiosity as care-full noticing Across the three cases, we sought to understand what participants attend too, how they reason about observed phenomena, and how they subsequently decide to act. In doing so, a pattern in the ways in which participants described their affective experience and larger sociocultural lens when engaging with their professional work began to emerge. Participants across the three contexts described their approach to routine practices in their field as one of curiosity and wonder. Here, we offer data examples that i llustrate the ways in which curiosity and wonder guide participants noticing in equitable and relational ways. To support co-designers within case study 3 to be “in conversation” about designing a research activity around, Ananda invited story telling and story listening as a necessary and valid method of co-design. In doing so, they co-created a space in which to verbally share their collective and individual histories with improvisational music that would later be enacted physically in the moment of improvisational musiking. In one meeting, despite having been in relationships with one another for over twenty years, co-designers reflected on how they continue to learn about one another and how this is essential to their practice. M, a percussion player, reflects on his relationship with H, another percussion player in the group with whom he has collaborated with frequently over the years sharing, “Every time I come back to him, it's not the same thing. Even over thirty plus years, it's not the same thing. …there's never stasis. We're never fixed in time from the last time we were together.” This practice of approaching one another with wonder and curiosity echoes throughout the artists’ descriptions of their practice, positioning the work of improvisation in opposition to playing from scripted, sheet music as a process of searching for something together and of welcoming individual and collective transformation. The artists invite the “unknown” into their artistry as foundational to their creative practice, rather than something to be avoided or moved through quickly.

Case Study 1 also had moments in which participants attended to their work in care-full ways through curiosity and wonder. Shivani observed an interaction between a special education teacher, HL, and a student, S as she transitioned students to the special education office. During the interaction, S separated from the group and began walking towards the playground with his basketball. In response, Shivani observed HL pause to face S and hold her arm out in the direction of the classroom. S shrugged, smiled sheepishly and made his way back to the group.  Understanding that S is often interpreted in deficit ways, Shivani was interested in what noticings guided HL’s response. To center the affective dimensions of HL’s noticings, Shivani described the interaction and asked “what did it feel like to just wait?” In response, HL shared “I was really curious…like what’s going to happen next?” Rather than assuming disruptive intent from S and reprimanding him, HL embodied trust by waiting calmly. In noticing with curiosity and wonder, she created the opportunity for choice and ultimately, a transformation of behavior. Each researcher witnessed routine practices of their participants that made evident their approach to noticing and professionalism as one of curiosity and wonder. Collaborative noticing as a process of searching for goodness In methodologically operationalizing care as the instances within research activities when researchers and participants engaged in collaborative sense making, a pattern emerged within the sequence of events that led to participants developing new insights about their practices or the values that underpin them. Across the three contexts, researchers engaged in a practice of searching for goodness  (Lightfoot 1983) to narrate participants’ own practices back to them in ways that supported collective transform ation. First, in designing methods that 1784 foregrounded the affective and embodied ways of being and doing that were of importance to participants, the researchers invited participants to describe a moment or larger pattern in their practice. Then, researchers resisted deficit perspectives in participants’ descriptions and emphasized the caring dimensions of participants' practices by reinterpreting that practice back to participants. Through collaborative sense-making participants were able to develop new understandings about their practice. Here, we offer descriptions of the larger interaction between researcher and participant alongside data excerpts that highlight researchers’ contributions across two case studies. During another field visit, Shivani observed HL providing pull-out accommodations to support S to finish his math test, a task that can often carry on for days. During the session, S worked excitedly and diligently, responding to HL’s prompts both verbally and nonverbally, remaining  seated, and finishing  five questions. In fact, S wanted to continue working on the test, but the class period was over. S is not often described this way. In fact, just before  joining HL, another teacher had  written-up S for being defiant, walking around the classroom, and refusing to work. When Shivani asked HL how working with S felt that day, HL described feeling frustrated. She describes feeling caught in the “constant battle of intervention versus classwork” because she had to support S to finish a math test rather than the “systematic literacy intervention” that he needed. Hearing HL’s deficit perspectives of her practice, Shivani  shared how she interpreted the session. Shivani described how HL “took a test and made it a teachable moment” because it was “a moment where he felt proud of himself.” Through continued collaborative discussion about the interaction, HL comes to the new understanding about how years of feeling like she’s not doing enough has made it difficult to experience these affective and relational accomplishments as central to learning and development, and central to her job. Similarly, in Case Stud y 2, by foregrounding practices that were meaningful to participants, Lindsay designed a research context that allowed for collaborative noticing and sense-making of participants’ practices. During interview 3, Lindsay and dance teacher W both used the same “movement map” drawn by W, and translated W’s experiences and stories from the map into choreography to share in an embodied and collaborative noticing practice. This process centered an embodied artform, dance, as a particular practice of noticing within the designed context, and as a tool of searching for goodness (Lightfoot, 1983). Drawing on what she observed in W’s map and interpreted from previous interviews, Lindsay repeatedly represented (through choreographic strategies including repetition, gaze, shape, and space) the theater where W teaches dance classes as the metaphorical center of her experience on campus. After watching Lindsay’s choreography, W shared, “you made me think of something that I do, that I didn’t include [in the map] which is, I actually [do] think the theatre is kind of central [location]… my whole day revolves, going in and out and around it” (W, interview 3). The choreographic choices Lindsay made centered W’s agency as a dance teacher who is somewhat isolated teaching in the theatre alone. Rather than being isolated in a dark theatre, W’s teaching environment was framed as a central and positive space through the embodied analysis. The process of witnessing Lindsay’s choreographic piece, which was a tangible reflection of W’s movement map and interview responses, created a space for W to not only share more about her professional experiences, but to reflect in a positive way that allowed a new revelation to come to light. By intentionally attending to the centrality of W’s teaching space as important and special,  this embodied process was a validating, relational experience for the teacher. Using an embodied process to emphasize and explore moments of goodness within a teaching day was meaningful to W, and other participants. By approaching research with a desire to engage collaboratively and relationally, rather than with presumption or pre-determined processes, each of the researchers in these case studies created a space in which participants could engage more authentically with the research activities by using their professional practices  as special education teachers, dance educators, and improvisational musicians. By centering  the affective and embodied dimensions of their practices-- for example, using choreography as a tool to study dance educators -- these studies demonstrate the ways in which taking seriously the practices and skills of professionals can contribute meaningfully to the development of research designs. When researchers designed with care, research contexts became sites of collaborative sense-making. Noticing affective practices, reflecting on those moments, and researchers reinterpretations of those observations, led participants tonew understandings about their practice, and researchers, about the resear ch process. Together, these insights point to possibilities in the understanding and exploration of researchers’ noticing practices, which we argue is integral to the analysis and design of future research partnerships that contribute to meaningful and care-full learning and research.
