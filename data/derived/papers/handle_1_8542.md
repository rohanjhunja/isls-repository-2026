# Caste and Queerness in Hindu India: Examining How Human-More Than Human Relations Shape Learning Spaces

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In the aftermath of the Section 377 judgement that decriminalized homosexuality in India, the positioning of Hinduism as historically welcoming and accepting of queerness has swiftly pervaded the Indian landscape. While a useful shift in the public sphere, the intersectional axes of caste, gender, and sexuality within everyday learning spaces is still fraught. In this paper, I examine how the cultural practices of caste-privileged and straight Indian teachers were privileged and shaped learning spaces. Drawing on Indigenous, queer feminist and feminist new materialist theories, the intra-action analysis methods developed in this study trace how dominant communities’ ways of knowing become inscribed into the meaning that everyday objects in school spaces take on, making invisible, queer ways of knowing and being. This work offers an approach to examining precolonial histories that shape learning spaces in India towards fighting against the rise in Hindu nationalism.

Despite the Right to Education Act (Article 21-A) and state-wide reservation policies that require schools enroll students regardless of their caste and gender, intersectional forms of discrimination persist within schools in India. Briefly, rooted in the Hindu religion, the caste system divides human beings into four varnas– Brahmins, Kshatriya, Vaisya, and Sudras. These ‘Savarnas’ (with caste) are an inherited identity, distinguished in part by the level of purity their being is assumed to embody, with Brahmin cis-males considered the ‘most pure’ (Upadhyay, 2020). Those outside the caste system (e.g., the Da lit community) are considered ‘Avarna’ (without cas te) and therefore ‘impure’ and ‘untouchable’ (Upadhyay, 202 0). For centuries, Savarna communities have mobiliz ed Brahminical ideologies around purity to enact horrific violence against Dalit communities, while simultaneously working to institutionalize caste, gender, and sexuality-based discrimination into India’s sociopolitical, economic, and cultural spheres (Upadhyay, 2020).

Building on research that examines how Brahminical heteronormative ways of being are privileged and asserted as normative in India’s sociopolitical and  cultural spheres (Subramaniam, 2019), I examined h umanMTH relations that shaped learning spaces. Specific ally, I looked at how the cultural practices of Sav arna and straight teachers at New School (NS) were positioned as normative (Hand et. al., 2012) and traced how this shaped learning spaces in ways that further oppressed marg inalized communities. “Both caste and queerness are  about who we can and cannot touch, intimately, privately, and publicly” (Chatterjee, 2018). As such, I recognize these forms of discrimination as a material practice wher eby the MTH is deeply implicated in how Brahminical ideologies are (re)produced. I ask: How do human-MTH relations contribute to positionin g Brahminical and heterosexual ways of being as normative in school spaces? Through the analysis I highlight the need for reform in everyday material-discursive practices within In dian schools as one way to fight against the rise in caste and sexuality-based discrimination.

Caste and Queerness at New School: The site of this study NS was established eleven years ago as part of a township for families moving into the rapidly gentrifying rural area in South India, 2 hours from the closest city. At the time of this study, a large portion of the school identified as middle class families and Savarna castes, with n one of the teachers openly identifying as queer. Ba rfi and Princess were teachers at NS who taught high school  finance and psychology respectively. Barfi grew up  a in middle class Tamil Brahmin family and did not face significant barriers in accessing an education. Her life was shaped through ‘typical’ expectations off middle class Tamil Brahmin women – prioritizing a marriage arranged by her family and only then pursuing a career. In contrast, Princess belonged to a Christian and caste-oppressed community from the North-East of India. The product of a matriarchal culture where women were very powerful and respected, being unmarried at 28 years of age (and therefore outside the gendered, heterosexual and Savarna expectations of women) was not an issue for Princes s and her family. With Tamil and Brahmin ancestry, my positioning at NS was contextually dependent; I was  an outsider to the school community and the countr y. However, my family’s Brahmin lineage positioned me as an insider relative to knowledge of Tamil and Brahmin beliefs and practices. As a (then) unmarried 33-year-old who presented as female, Princess and I bonded through our desire to pursue a career over marriage. At the  same time, I understood many of Barfi’s cultural p ractices, having grown up within Brahmin communities in Tamil  Nadu and Canada. I also learned to navigate the complexities of being a queer and genderqueer perso n within my family and the broader Brahmin communit y, ICLS2022 Proceedings 1569 © ISLS “out of line” (Ahmed, 2006, p. 88) with the expecta tions of a cis-heterosexual Brahmin woman. At the t ime of the episodes analyzed, I was out as ‘lesbian’ to Princess and Barfi and as not out as genderqueer. Theorizing Human-More Than Human Relations Building on work that recognizes discrimination as a material, spatial and relational practice (Ahmed,  2006), I draw on theory that brings to the fore relations between (and within) humans and the MTH as the unit of analysis. Learning from Indigenous scholars (Bang & Marin, 2015), I build on practices rooted within India that decenter humans as an a priori individual unit and recognize the MTH as an active participant in interactions. Humans and the MTH world are not separated by an a priori static boundary. Instead, boundaries between entities are produced by enactments of power through material-discursive intra-actions. Here, I follow Barad’s (2007) use of  ‘intra’ versus ‘inter’ to recognize that humans and the MTH world do not ‘inter’-act across a predetermined boundary. Instead, boundaries between entities emerge through power charged boundary making enactments when matter ‘intra-acts’ with(in) the world (Barad, 2007). Furthermore, meaning-making emerges through intra-actions within a world of non-static human-MTH phenomena, shaped through boundary-making enactments which are informed by human-MTH relations. In this way, recognizing knowledge creation as the process of engaging in intra-actions illuminates the human-MTH relations that “shape the  contours of space” (Ahmed, 2006, p. 3), and the po wer charged boundary enactments that determine which human-MTH phenomena are allowed within proximity. As I illustrate through the data analysis, repeated powe r-charged boundary enactments shaped by human-MTH relations (re)produced Brahmin and heterosexual ways of being as normative in the space, what Ahmed (2 006) describes as the product of repetition within the vertical and horizontal normative axes. I examined human-MTH relations that took “shape as the ‘background’” (p. 88), whereby to deviate from the vertical and horizontal axes of “conventional genealogy” (p. 83) was to bring other human-MTH phenomena closer, “those that would not be allowed ‘near’ by straight [and Brahmin] ways of orientating” (p. 92) in the world (Ahmed, 2006). In the narrative that follows, I illustrate how Brahminical framings  of heterosexuality emerged through human-MTH relat ions and arranged human-MTH phenomena with the goal of p ositioning Savarna ways of being as normative by resisting deviant ‘acts’ (Ahmed, 2006) that attempted to bring queer human-MTH relations and phenomena closer. Documenting and Analyzing Human-Material Relations and Phenomena Over six months, I engaged with field observations (Childers, 2013) both inside and outside of classroom spaces, and one structured intra-view (for this study) with each participant (Kuntz & Presnall, 2012). When conducting the structured intra-views, I built on Bang (2020),  Marin’s (2020), and Kuntz & Presnall’s (2012) work  to recognize the material world as part of co-constructing meaning-making. Building on micro-analytic interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) methods, ‘intra-action’ analysis (Krishnamoorthy et. al., 2021) revealed the ways in which different human-MTH phenomena – or in tra-actants – intra-acted with each other. I paid c lose attention to audio recordings of moment-to-moment u nfolding intra-actions by examining how human-MTH boundaries emerged through material-discursive practices. Then, I traced the consequences those boundaries had for knowledge creation – particularly whose ways of  being were privileged within the ongoing activity.  In the analysis, I paid attention to footing (Goffman, 1981) – the alignment of intra-actants with each other – to examine the different speaker roles (i.e., ‘animator’, ‘aut hor’ and ‘principal’) that each intra-actant assume d, towards positioning certain ways of knowing as normative. E xamining production formats contributed to identify ing cultural frames emergent through the intra-action that allowed only particular types of human-MTH relations to shape the phenomena that emerged. The specific episode presented illustrates a ‘hot spot’ (Jordan & Henderson,

1995) in the data; a good example of how heterosexual Savarna ways of knowing were positioned as normative.

“I Just Wanted a Cool Nose Ring”: Caste and Queerness at Snack time This episode began when I entered the staff room du ring the morning snack break. Princess, Barfi, and a few other teachers were at their respective cubicles. I  walked past Princess’s cubicle and stood, leaning against a cubicle in the middle. Barfi was sitting in her cha ir by the wall and commented to me, “Look this is y our, this nosepin is your grandmom’s?” In this moment, the nosepin emerged in the intra-ac tion as a bounded material delineated as separate from my body and the surrounding matter through Bar fi’s utterance. I initially hesitated and explained  that the nosepin was owned by “M.S Subbalakshmi” (M.S.) whom I authored as a “Carnatic singer”, a “really old” and “dead” figure. Barfi and Princess’s quick raised pi tched overlapping utterances [“ha?”, “why?”, “you…h ow?] indicated surprise and shock at the nosepin-M.S. phenomenon that emerged through my utterance. Their reactions revealed a shift in alignment between the teachers and me, suggesting my utterance was a failure to provide the expected response that yes, the nosepin belonged to  my grandmother. The intra-action, which began as a ICLS2022 Proceedings 1570 © ISLS seemingly casual question to me transformed into an unexpected and surprising material-discursive event through this intra-actional break. Princess further probed, specifically asking how I/my body came to be in relation with the nosepin-M.S. phenomenon. In response to Princess, I built on my previous utterance to repair (Schegloff, 1997) the shift in footing caused by the intra-actional break and authored the nosepin-M.S. phenomenon as “fake” and a “back up”. Barfi didn't accept my efforts to position my relation with the nosepin as less significant through my authoring it as fake; which I authored to maintain the current footing amongst the other intra-actants. Instead, she continued to express her surprise with the nosepin-M.S. phenomenon. In a second attempt at repair, I t hen positioned myself as “not knowing the gravity” of t his event and authored a story in which it was sens ible that the teachers and I would not have a shared understanding about the importance of the nosepin-M.S. phenomenon. The story I authored involved characters from my bi ological family whose connection with the story character M.S. and practices of material inheritance explained my relation with the nosepin-M.S. pheno menon. In the story, my aunt character – who did not have children – inherited and was wearing my great grand-mother’s nosepin, and my siblings did not have their noses pierced so my aunt offered me the one she had receiv ed from M.S. In the story space-time, set in M.S.’s house, my aunt was drawn to M.S.’s nosepin and asked for i t. M.S. rejected my aunt’s bid for the nosepin entangled with her (M.S.’s) face but offered what M.S. authored as not the “real one”. The nosepin offered to my aunt was positioned as fake because M.S. wore it when her “real one (was) getting cleaned”. Here, I built on the repair initiated earlier to position ‘fakeness’ as determined by when the M.S. character was entangled with the nosepin she gave my aunt. While listening to the story, Barfi did not challen ge and therefore seemingly accepted the logic of inheritance and the history of human-MTH relationsh ips that allowed me to be relation with the nosepin-M.S. phenomenon, revealing a taken-as-shared recognition  of the straight vertical axis (Ahmed, 2006) that f rames heterosexual genealogical inheritance within this c ontext. Furthermore, Barfi did not challenge that t he aunt character in the story space-time might be at the M.S. character’s house and in a position to ask for an object of material significance (to the teachers in this cont ext) from her. This is significant because the soci ocultural and historic relations required to accept this story-specifically that the relationship between my aunt and M.S. might have existed and been intimate enough to allow my aunt to ask for and receive a nosepin reveals an acceptance of me (and my aunt) as someone with ancestral and current access to the largely Brahmin Carnatic music community. A few minutes later, I then offered a bid to nuance how I was in relation with the Brahmin community through the nosepin-M.S. phenomenon by offering, “um. But I wear it because…”. This attempt was not taken up by Barfi, who in overlapping Tamil utterances, continued to discuss the design of the nosepin with other teachers in the space. Then, a teacher who had just walked in paused when she heard the intra-action. She shifted her gaze, her eyes widened, and she reached over to my body a nd performed a movement in Tamil, referred to as ‘otthikardhu’. The act is often performed by touching the “eye or lip gently, after touching […] a revered or beautiful object” (Oxford University Press, 2009). This moment framed the MTH as sacred or that which was to be revered, further resisting my bids to shape my relation with the phenomenon. Once again, I attempted to refigure the nosepin-M.S. phenomenon as just “a really cool nose ring”, but instead Barfi authored me at a “different level of respect now”. That is, I was to be respected at a “different level” now that the sedimented framing  of the nosepin-M.S.-Rishi phenomenon was aligned with a Brahmin-Carnatic music sociocultural and historic meaning. Importantly through the intra-action, my multiple b ids to repair the intra-actional break initially and then contribute to meaning-making around my relation with the Brahmin community and the nosepi n-M.S-Rishi phenomenon were ignored by the other intra-actants when not aligned with(in) this framing. My higher level of respect was emergent through and contingent on my position as a genealogical descendent and member of a specific Brahmin and Carnatic music community, and my entanglement with an object symbolic of those relations. Heterosexuality, Brahminism and the Nosepin-M.S.-Rishi Phenomenon Key to this analysis is that as a queer and genderq ueer person, the ability to inherit valued material s and be a ratified member within the Brahmin community was also contingent on my gender and sexuality as invisible. Caste is inherited but only through the male lineage. As a queer, genderqueer, and (at the time) unmarried person, I was technically still considered a Brahmin with my memb ership in the community only accepted when hiding m y queerness. Through the episode, the vertical axis o f genealogical inheritance (i.e., inheriting from m y aunt and M.S.) produced the nosepin-M.S. phenomenon as a heterosexual object “personalized as […] my background or as that which allow(ed) me to” (Ahmed, 2006, p. 88)  be considered a human at ‘one different level of r espect now’. The sedimented framing of the nosepin-M.S.-Rishi phenomenon and the consequences of respect only held true when imposing Savarna heterosexuality as the background. As a queer and genderqueer person who is “out of line” (Ahmed, 2006, p. 92) with(in) the heterosexual Brahmin and Carnatic music community, my bids were evidence of my attempts to bring “other objects closer, those that would not be allowed ‘near’ by straight ways of orientating the body” (Ahmed, 2006, p. 92). In this episode, the heterosexual Savarna ‘background’ (Ahmed, 2006) ICLS2022 Proceedings 1571 © ISLS emerged through human-MTH relations that sedimented the nosepin-M.S.- Rishi phenomenon as a sacred material to be revered and me, as a human to be respected at ‘another level’, while silencing the queer ‘slanted’ frames. In the aftermath of the Section 377 judgement that decriminalized homosexuality in India, the positioning of Hinduism as historically welcoming and accepting of queerness has swiftly pervaded the Indian landscape (Upadhyay, 2020). At the same time, this acceptance exists alongside caveats that consider queerness as “a ‘psychological case’ and queer relations (as)  ‘not compatible with nature’” (Sethi, 2018, as cit ed in Upadhyay, 2020, p. 472). Therefore, while a useful shift in the public sphere, the ways in which caste, gender, and sexuality intra-act within everyday learning spaces is still fraught. In this episode, the nosepin drew Barfi’s attention the day I arrived at NS and did so once a gain when it entered the staff room the day of this episode. My Brahmin biological ancestry emerged through the nosepin’s intra-actions and shaped the permitted humanMTH phenomena brought into proximity in the staff r oom space. Drawing on Kondaiah et. al., this episod e illustrated that even though “no one talked about caste there […] everything gave (it) away” (2017, p. 75). In this case, my Brahmin biological ancestry emerged through the nosepin’s intra-actions and shaped the permitted human-MTH phenomena brought into proximity in the staff room space. Dalit feminists have long argued that the intersections of caste, gender, sexuality, and class cannot be ignored when working towards dismantling Hindu supremacy in India (Pawar & Moon, 2008). This paper builds on their work by illustrating that resisting the power of Hindu majoritarianism in education requires more nuanced analysis of how our learning spaces are shaped with/through human-MTH relations. I argue that resistin g the power of Hindu majoritarianism in education requires more nuanced analysis of how our learning spaces are shaped with/through human-MTH relations by challenging Brahminical heteronormative framings of humanMTH phenomena that privilege Brahminical ways of knowing.
