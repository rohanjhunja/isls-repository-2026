# Students' Perspectives on Artificial Intelligence in Biology Higher Education: Insights for Collaborative Curriculum Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We surveyed Norwegian post-secondary students in Biology to understand their perspectives on integrating tools powered by large language models (LLMs) into higher education. Students view tools such as ChatGPT positively and primarily as supplementary learning aids. However, they raised concerns about ethics, accuracy, and bias, and are particularly concerned about what constitutes cheating. Findings emphasize the need to develop guidelines ensuring these emergent tools support learning.

Emergent tools powered by large language models, su ch as OpenAI’s ChatGPT can produce a range of human-like outputs, presenting both an asset and a potent ial challenge in education (Kasneci et al., 2023; Y an et al., 2023). A major concern is the potential misuse of LLM-powered tools (which we will from hereon refer to a s LLMs, for simplicity’s sake) for cheating purposes (Cotton et al., 2023; Sullivan et al., 2023). However, there is limited insight into students’ perspectives on how to use these tools responsibly and constructively. Neglecting students’ input effectively reduces students’ role to a passive one (Bovill, Cook-Sather, & Felten, 2011). It is crucial to recognize that unproductive uses of tech nology may not stem from students’ malicious intent ions. Instead, it could merely be a reflection of student s navigating the complex landscape of academic and societal expectations and using available tools to cope with “the pressures, stresses, and struggles of participating in higher education” (Roe, 2022, p. 3). When school activities resonate with student s’ aspirations and priorities, they may leverage technologies in remarkably productive and innovative ways (Russel et al., 2014). To delve dee per into this, it is essential to foster dialogue between educators, students, and technology designers. Learning more about students’ experiences, needs, and concerns regardin g LLMs can lead to the co-creation of guidelines, best practices, and even novel educational tools that al ign with students’ perspectives. Bui lding on this premise, our study sampled a cohort of post-secondary students studying biology across Norway. Specifically, we asked: (RQ1) How familiar are students with emerging LLMs?; (RQ2) How are students using these tools?; and (RQ3) Wh at are students’ perspectives on the use of LLMs in higher education?

## Methods

The survey. We administered an online survey in October 2023, targeting biology-related programs at masters and bachelors levels across nine Norwegian institutions. We ultimately had 660 respondents. Questions rela ted to LLMs included an item that asked which of the listed tools (ChatGPT, Bing AI, Copilot, Open AI Playground, Bard AI) students were familiar with. Respondents w ere also asked “In which of the following ways have  you used these tools as a part of your education?” with several options including “write or test code for programming,” “solve mathematical or statistical problems,” and “improve text that I have written myself.” (We also included an “other” option and a space for elaboration). Anothe r Likert-scale question asked, “When you have used a  Large Language Model, to what extent have you done the following…?” with several options ranging from “questioned the accuracy of the results” to “had concerns about bias.” Finally, an open-ended question sought students’  views on how LLMs should be used in higher education, in a way that is fair and supports their learning. Data analysis. We used descriptive statistics to provide an over view of survey responses. We also qualitatively coded the open-ended responses related to RQ3 using an iterative c ut-and-sort technique to detect categories in two independent and randomly sampled subsets of the data. Then, these two sets of categories were curated to create an initial codebook, which was it self subjected to testing with a third randomly sel ected subset of the data. The initial codebook was m odified to accommodate these respons es, and then all student responses (n=401) were categorized.

## Findings and discussion

RQ1. We find that of 660 student respondents, 413 (63%) have used one or more of these LLMs, with ChatGPT being the most familiar, and 24 unique tools were reported in addition to the ones listed. Among these additional tools reported, the four most common were Grammarly, Phind, Elicit, and DALL·E. RQ2. Students say that they mostly use these tools to “find answers to question s,” (65%) followed by to “write or test code” (48%), “find answers to questions that are not related to [their] studies,” (44%), and “make a summary of a text.” (42%). While many students have questioned and evaluated the acc uracy and reliability of results, fewer have consid ered whether these tools are biased. RQ3. We grouped responses to the open-ended question about fairness and learning support into 10 categories, including, for example:  “Educational tool, but not for assessment” (“I bel ieve it's a useful tool for understanding and learning, but it shouldn't be allowed in exams and other assessment situations”); “must be used critically” (“Language models can be valuable in learning, but they need to be used crit ically. Always verify the information and be source-savvy”); “aid for assignments, not to replace per sonal work” (“It's great for enhancing understanding when working on semester projects, but it should never write the entire text for you"); 'complementary tool for other study resource s” (“I view them as any other resource, like referr ing to a book. They can explain complex theories simply and help with structure, but shouldn't replace the personal effort in assignments”); “a source of explanation and clarification” (“It's efficient for explaining complex problems and breaking down concepts. Good for answering simple questions and explaining in various ways”); “tool for errorchecking and debugging” (“Found it helpful for debugging code, but it may not always be reliable for text-based tasks”); “skeptical about use” (“They shouldn't be used for educational purposes as they often predict and can be misleading. It's best as a fun pastime”); “adapting education to incorporate LLMs” (“If educational systems adapt and embrace the existence of LLMs, they can be a va luable tool. We should be taught how to use them effectively”); “resource locator and basic guidance ” (“Ideal for finding resources and getting basic i deas, especially for students starting their research projects. Always double-check credibility.”) Our findings, drawing on both qualitative and quantitative data, indicate that Norwegian post-secondary students are optimistic about integrating LLMs into their educational tasks, findings that are aligned with previous research (e.g., Chan and Hu, 2023). We see that students primarily view LLMs as supplem ental tools that can support learning, a perspective that challenges con cerns that LLMs may be used primarily for academic dishonesty, and instead opens up discussions about how to thoughtfully integrate LLMs into the curricu lum in ways that enhance critical thinking, problem-solving skills, and understanding of complex concep ts. The diverse potential applications of LLMs indicate opportunities to meet varied learning needs. However, concerns about the ethical use, accuracy, and potential bias of LLMs must  also be addressed. These concerns highlight the ne ed to develop comprehensive frameworks within higher educ ation institutions that promote responsible use of such technologies, and pay special attention to clarifyi ng what constitutes academic dishonesty. Educators need to be equipped with knowledge of these tools and with strategies to incorporate them effectively into teaching practices. Furthermore, this study emphasizes the importance of student involvement in shaping the future role of LLMs in higher education. Fostering collaborative dialogues between students, educators, and technology designers is key to ensuring that LLMs support, rather than hinder, learning processes.
