# Combining Participatory Research With Responsible Research and Innovation to Develop Emerging Educational Technologies

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The learning sciences have strong established practices of participatory research that encourage projects to be more relevant to today’s educational issues. However, societal and personal considerations may also influence the relevance and impact of research. We propose using a Responsible Research and Innovation framework to build on participatory methods in the learning sciences and formalize a process for considering a project’s broader context. Through a case study on neuroadaptive technologies for teaching and learning, we demonstrate a methodology for Responsible Research and Innovation that can support participatory methods by encouraging reflexivity from researchers and anticipation of a project’s future use cases.

## Introduction

The learning sciences have strong traditions of com munity-engaged participatory research. These resear ch methods take the design of learning systems into re al-world settings, centering stakeholder voices dur ing the research process (DiSalvo & DiSalvo, 2014). Participatory methods range from contributory, where members of the public contribute data to a project designed by scientists, to collaborative projects with more public input into design, analysis, or research dissemination, to projects entirely co-created by scientists and community members (Bonney et al., 2009). Methods like Participatory D esign and Participatory Action Research tend toward s a cocreated model, bringing communities into research a s collaborators to define key issues and develop pl ans of action (Jacobs et al 2016). Other methods like co-d esign are more scientist-led but still engage deepl y with members of the public in developing and evaluating solutions to educational needs (Roschelle et al. 20 06). Participatory methods in the learning sciences often bring in students, teachers, and parents to create research that is more relevant and ideally provides benefits to the stakeholders involved in that research (Jacobs et al 2016). However, societal and personal considerations beyond participation are necessary for the development of more responsible and relevant research agendas. A recent article by Selwyn (2021) brought to questi on the future of educational technology amidst a global landscape of recent disasters, including the COVID-19 pandemic. Selwyn argues that, while the field has made signif icant progress to improve learning and teaching, it  has overlooked the potential of major disruptions and f ailed to anticipate the kinds of adaptations requir ed by something like a global pandemic. Unintended conseq uences may also arise outside of global disasters, for instance out of a dominant trend in human-computer interaction to assume that race does not affect technological interactions. As Rankin & Henderson (2021) demonstrate, the common practice of ignoring race has led to erasing certain racial groups from the discussion, tokenizing Black children in participatory research, and a suite of other consequences. Following this line of thinking, public participation alone is not enough to anticipate major shifts and consequences in education; participation must b e paired with an orientation to anticipate future s ocietal contexts and with reflexivity from researchers to challenge their own biases and assumptions. We propose that Responsible Research and Innovation  (RRI) can extend the tradition of communityengaged research in the learning sciences to consider additional societal and personal factors. In other words, RRI is novel in contributing a broader iterative framew ork for responsible societal development of educati onal technologies. RRI ties the social and scientific to gether by emphasizing the need for anticipation, inclusion, reflexivity, and responsiveness (defined later in this article) in research and innovation approaches (Stilgoe et al., 2013; Stilgoe & Guston, 2017). Like Participatory D esign and Participatory Action Research, RRI emphas izes researcher responsiveness to public input. However,  RRI takes participatory methods a step further by highlighting the importance of researchers consider ing their pre-existing assumptions (reflexivity) and anticipating future technological landscapes. RRI c an apply to future research landscapes globally or can pare down the scale to think about the future of a very specific technology (as we use it in this article). Responsible Research and Innovation Responsible Research and Innovation stems from the field of Science and Technology Studies, which acknowledges that science and culture are co-created and thus governance, innovation, and research must assess the socio-cultural systems that influence science and technology (van Atteveldt et al., 2019).  There are multiple definitions of RRI but one of the most resonant from Stilgoe et al. (2013) defines RRI as a “means of taking care ICLS2022 Proceedings 663 © ISLS of the future through collective stewardship of sci ence and innovation in the present.” RRI contains f our key components that call for (1) early  anticipation  of research consequences through (2) reflexivity (i.e., selfreflection and reassessment of pre-existing assumptions), (3) inclusion of the public in technological or research development, and (4) responsiveness of researchers and tech designers to components 1-3 (Stilgoe & Guston, 2017). One of the hallmarks of RRI is its reach to various sites of innovation; this means that RRI acknowledges that research projects alone are not charged with d emocratizing research and that every type of resear ch stakeholder, including governments and funders, mus t consider the broader landscape of their work. The  case study presented in this article decreases the scope  of RRI to demonstrate how the framework can functi on on a researcher and single-stakeholder level that could then be integrated into a more system-level view. In a review of RRI methodologies, Schuijiff & Dijkstra (2020) found that an RRI approach has been used most in the fields of nanotechnology and informatio n communication technologies, with a few examples f rom other fields like synthetic biology. Schuijiff & Di jkstra (2020) found that researchers who incorporat e RRI frameworks tend to focus on one of four methods in RRI: (1) Opening to stakeholders, (2) stimulating reflection from researchers and innovators, (3) anticipating ethical, legal, and socio-economic aspects, and (4) institutionallevel responsibility. These RRI methods also includ e sub-methodologies. Opening to the public in RRI m ay involve creating narratives with multiple stakehold ers, holding workshops, or other public engagement modes. Meanwhile, stimulating reflection can occur at different stages of research through facilitated workshops. This project attempts to incorporate each component  of Responsible Research and Innovation into the development of emerging educational technologies, specifically neuroadaptive learning tools. We primarily focus on methods to open up to stakeholders, encourage re flection, and anticipate context, excluding methods  to encourage institutionalizing responsibility. Our case study incorporates each element of RRI into interviews with teachers and a workshop with researchers of a neuroadaptive learning technology project (see Table 1). Neuroadaptive Learning Technologies In this paper, we extend the call for RRI to the de velopment of emerging educational technologies, specifically neuroadaptive learning tools. Developments in porta ble brain imaging and machine learning have led to an increased interest in developing neuroadaptive tech nology and brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) for education that could assess a learner’s cognitive state (e.g., frustrated, inattentive, etc.) and adapt in some way to that state (Wegemer et al. 2019; Liu et al., 2021). Our case study addressed an NSF-funded project that combines behavioral ICLS2022 Proceedings 664 © ISLS data with non-invasive brain sensors from functional Near Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS) to capture a learner’s cognitive state and adjust the learning experience appropriately in an Intelligent Tutoring System (ITS). Neurotechnology research currently lacks a breadth of participatory research methods and would benefit from both a learning sciences perspective on participation and an RRI framing to contextualize research. Beyond a lack of participatory methods, research on neuroadaptive learning technologies seems misaligned with a broader educational context, potentially driven by technological novelty rather than social or educational issues. However, a lack of attending to social or educational issues does not mean that neuroadaptive learning technologies will not come to fruition. In a critical review of developments in educational neurotechnology, Williamson (2019) brings up emerging concerns such as invasion of student pr ivacy and over-rewarding of specific cognitive skil ls. The review points out that studies of neuroimaging for education have led to inferences about quantifiable differences in brain function, for example differences resulting from socio-economic status, that could lead to tangible policy decisions. Even with some scientific skepticism about the accuracy of novel brain imaging devices, Williamson demonstrates that political support is emerging for  commercial educational neurotech. The policy and s ocial implications of such studies require that they be approached with attention to assumptions that are baked into the design and analysis of brain imaging, and with care for how findings are publicized. In applying Responsible Research and Innovation toneuroadaptive educational technology, we aim to show how RRI can help reorient an exploratory educational technology to appropriate use cases that don’t solely rely on technological novelty and consider potential downfalls. To the best of our knowledge, only one project to date has used an RRI framework in Mind, Brain, and Education research. van Atteveldt et al., (2019) conducted semi-structured interviews and focus groups with stakeholders to modify a neurofeedback intervention that aimed to improve learners’ agency. They conclude that soc ietal stakeholders are capable of deliberating on t echnical, social, and political constructs of innovation and that their viewpoints are necessary complements to researcher perspectives in the process of responsible research.

Building on the work of van Atteveldt et al., (2019) we are adding to evidence supporting the feasibility of RRI for emerging educational technologies, specifically brain-sensing learning tools. Actors in the educational neurotechnology space include researchers, educators, educational administrators, funders (private and  public), companies, and policymakers (from local to federal), however, for the sake of feasibility in the case study, this research narrows in on the application of RRI betwe en university researchers and teachers. We first ga thered teacher feedback on the fNIRS project then conducte d a workshop with the NSF project team to respond t o feedback (as described further in the case study). By extending a Responsible Research and Innovation Framework toneuroadaptive educational technology research, we aim to: 1: Demonstrate a methodology for, and the merits of, integrating a Responsible Research and Innovation framework and participatory methods into emerging educational technology research. 2: Synthesize educator views on emerging educationa l neurotechnology and researcher responses to educator feedback. This synthesis is meant to inform and refine both the specific neuroadaptive tool in question (fNIRS for ITS) and future projects that aim to develop BCIs for education.

## Case Study

We outline a case study that explored the feasibili ty of applying a Responsible Research and Innovatio n framework toneuroadaptive educational technology projects. We report on the project in question, then describe two initial pilot interviews, semi-structured interviews with teachers, and a workshop with the project team. Project and Project Team The NSF-funded educational neurotechnology project, initiated in 2018 with pilot research beginning 2-3 years earlier, aims to develop methods for using brain da ta in real-world educational environments by unders tanding changes in cognitive states during learning and bro adly improving the design of an ITS. The project it self is focused on developmental math learning in college settings and singles out specific cognitive processes to study through brain sensing and behavior. The project examines frontal lobe activity using fNIRS during use of the elearning system ASSISTments (Heffernan & Heffernan, 2014). The project team is composed of three co-PIs with expertise areas spanning fNIRS, human-computer inte raction, machine learning, ITS, learning sciences, and cognitive neuroscience. The team also includes 3-4 PhD students, several undergraduates, and 2 teachers. Beyond participating in the workshop, the project team des cribed here was not involved in designing, facilita ting, or analyzing the case study in this article. Friedman had been an undergraduate research assistant on the  project before beginning work on this case study, Kenner advised on all components of this case study, and Walker is a co-PI on the NSF project who advised on the framing and broader impacts of this case study after its completion. ## Methods

Pilot interviews were conducted prior to the beginn ing of the grant in early 2018 with two out of thre e co-PI’s from the neuroadaptive learning project (one of whom was Walker prior to her involvement in this case study). These interviews asked the researchers about their backgrounds, the projects they’re involved in, how they decide on collaborators in those projects, implementation or long-term goals for projects, and the funding landscape for educational and/or neurotechnology. Analysis of the pilot interviews then informed interviews with teachers.

## Results

The primary finding from pilot interviews that infl uenced teacher interviews was that the two co-PI’s were not clear on how they imagined the end use cases of the neuroadaptive technology project. When answering questions about end users, researchers instantly referenced u se of brain-sensing devices in formal learning envi ronments and then proceeded to question their assumption of formal learning applications. One researcher stated "we always talk about formal learning… [I] don't know why that's the case." On the other hand, both researchers talked about neurotechnology entering the market via gaming or s elf-directed learning. One researcher said it's poss ible that the technology could be used as a tool to teach students about technology without them ever actually using it. Teacher Interviews Teacher Recruitment and Demographics Teachers were recruited for interviews through various venues including an NSF-funded Research Experience for Teachers (RET) at a northeastern university, a univ ersity research center, and personal connections. T he RET program invites community college and/or high school teachers to spend three months working in a compu ting lab. Teachers received emails describing the interviews and asking if they knew others who may be interested in participating. Criteria for participation was simpl y that interviewees must be educators or teachers i n high school/community college settings, could teach any discipline, and be in any phase of their career. Teachers ranged widely in age (23-68) and number of  years working as a teacher (ranging from one student teacher to 31 years of teaching). All the people interviewed taught STEM + Computing courses (computer science, chemistry, physics, digital media arts, biology, environmental science, math, astronomy, and makerspace directing) to grades 7-12, and 6 out of 11 teachers taught in private schools. The high proportion of private schools represented in our sample of teachers is a limitation in our study for the inclusion aspect of responsible research and innovation, however the demographics do not affect the ability to demonstrate an RRI methodology. Teacher Recruitment and Demographics Interviews with teachers were conducted in person and virtually from 2018-2019. Interviews aimed to understand teachers’ experiences, gather feedback on the brain-sensing ITS, and fill in gaps that were identified in the pilot interviews. Questions were structured in five secti ons, two of which were tied to the anticipation and  inclusion components of Responsible Research and Innovation:

1. Teacher background: inquired about current job, age groups taught, previous positions, teaching interests,

and academic background.

2. Information on technology use in the classroom (tied to reflexivity in RRI): asked teachers to list the tools

they use in the classroom daily or occasionally and probed about comfort with classroom technologies

3. Speculation about future technology (tied to reflexivity and anticipation of future needs in RRI): teachers

speculated about their current and future needs in the classroom by describing the superpowers they would like to have to help them do their job. This speculation was modelled after a study conducted by Holstein et al. (2017) that aimed to explore teacher’s needs for real time analytics from Intelligent Tutors. In their study, the team used superpowers as a prob e to investigate challenges that teachers face in t he classroom. The question posed was “If you could have any superpowers you wanted, to help you do your job, what would they be?”. The intention was to elicit classroom needs without technological limitations.

4. Feedback on the neuroadaptive learning technology  (tied to inclusion of public opinion in RRI):

presented the neuroadaptive educational technology project to get feedback, asked about possible contexts of use (formal/informal learning, subject area), cognitive states of interest to teachers, and hesitations about using the technology.

5. School technology policies: Inquired about school policies that support or hinder implementation of new

educational technologies. This interview section is not addressed in this case study. ICLS2022 Proceedings 666 © ISLS Interviews were audio recorded and key quotes were later transcribed. Each interview was analyzed by a single researcher and commonalities were pulled o ut into a findings document to be presented to the research team. The focus of analyzing these interviews was t o efficiently translate feedback and integrate it w ith the neuroadaptive educational project, therefore rigorous coding methodology was less of a concern.

## Workshop Methods

In the second phase of the project, thematic findin gs from interviews were discussed in a workshop wit h the neuroadaptive learning team. There were 6 attendees at the workshop including the 3 co-PIs, 2 PhD students, and one masters student. Prior to the workshop, synthesized de-identified interview findings were sent to the project team. Throughout the workshop, researchers worked from a packet of questions to document their thoughts and shared the most important highlights on a whiteboard and with sticky notes. This workshop was conducted as part of a PI meeting at the start of the NSF grant period and included three phases:

1. Thinking reflexively about self and project:  Speculation about superpowers for teachers, person al and

team goals within the project, development of the project in the short and long term, discovering which parts of the projects are or are not amenable to feedback

2. Presentation and step through findings: Presentation of interview findings during which th e team was

asked to consider which feedback would apply to the  project, to what degree feedback could be incorporated, and how many years into the project the feedback would apply.

3. Finding actionable items:  A discussion about notable feedback, which feedbac k can feasibly be

incorporated into the project, and at what point in the project

## Workshop Results

In the reflexive thinking portion of the workshop, researchers aligned on individual goals and goals w ithin the timeline of the project. Individually, each researcher was motivated to partake in the project to learn more about the other disciplines (i.e., psychology, human-comp uter interaction, learning sciences). On a project level, the team agreed on three goals: The 1-year goal was to align cognitive constructs between controlled psychological tasks to the Intelligent Tutoring System ASSISTments; the 3-year goals were to collect brain data with the fNIRS device and to work on automatic classification of c ognitive states based on that data; the 5-10 year g oal was to begin prototyping learning systems to create meaningful learning interventions. One of the research te am’s key takeaways from a reflexivity perspective was the ne cessity of spending more time aligning goals betwee n each project team member and learning about each other’s disciplines. Aligning goals in the beginning of the workshop also helped to assess teacher feedback to understand where it could be incorporated in the timeline of the project. While stepping through the findings, resea rchers decided that the project’s most clear use case was as a homework assistant tool; the team also realized that supporting in-class collaboration was a major point of interest but was not necessarily suitable for this project. The team also landed on three ways that findings fr om the interviews could have an impact on the project and when in the project timeline these could have an impact:

1. Writing broader impacts sections – immediate implications for the project (0+ years)

2. Thinking critically about the cognitive and learning constructs being studied in this and future grants –

immediate (0+ years) and short-term (1-3 years) implications for the project

3. Measuring not only learning but also self-efficacy in learning – immediate (0+ years), short-term (1-3

While stepping through interview data, the project team became interested in reconsidering which cognitive states the project should assess and better mapping those states to goals in the current experiment. The project was initiated with an interest in assessing changes in attention, rule-learning, and proactive/reactive control. In hearing about the cognitive constructs that teachers were interested in, researchers had a rich discussion about how those constructs appear in psychology, HCI, and the learning sciences. For instance, the top cognitive state of interest for teachers (flow state) was discussed as potentially measurable through deep learning in the learning sciences and the ideas of proactive vs. reactive control in psychology. Proactive control, which is defined as more goal-driven behavior, is closely mapped with specific brain states and was discussed in the workshop as potentially related to more active learning. Other immediate implications for the project arose from teachers’ thoughts about ICLS2022 Proceedings 668 © ISLS brain-sensing as a tool to provide feedback to students on their own learning styles to help develop learning selfefficacy. In considering the possibility of a self-efficacy application, the research team concretely considered integrating self-report prompts into experiments wi th the fNIRS device to assess self-awareness in con junction with the cognitive state of interest. Integrating s elf-report would be a very concrete way to adjust t he project to address possible use cases early in the research of emerging technologies.

## Discussion and Conclusion

One of the strengths of our approach to RRI is that  each of the four dimensions were tied together acr oss the entirety of the case study. In their review of meth odological RRI papers, Schuijiff & Dijkstra (2020) concluded that most methods focus almost exclusively on one d imension of the framework. They speculate that focu sing equally on all of the dimensions could overburden the research process. However, the case study described in this article demonstrates the possibility of integrating each element in a single RRI process. The clearest benefits of using RRI in the neuroadap tive learning project were in the dimensions of reflexivity and anticipation. In the speculative portion of the interviews, asking teachers about which superpowers they would like instead of what technological support they need (as per the methods described by Holstein et al.,

2017) helped move beyond the concept of what is or is not technologically possible. Asking teachers about their

desired superpowers prior to getting feedback on the neurotechnology helped ensure that teachers, especially those who were skeptical of the tool, provided useful feedback. During the workshop, discussing teachers' needs in terms of superpowers also helped paint a fuller picture of what teachers struggle with in the classroom. Despite being useful in their own way, the speculat ive questions in the interviews did not directly le ad to anticipation in the workshop. Instead, anticipation of project use cases was enabled by exercises in reflexivity in the beginning of the workshop, which helped rese archers keep personal and project goals in mind whi le reflecting on teacher feedback. One of the teachers ’ top classroom goals involved building collaborati ve communities, which led the research team to realize  that facilitating collaboration was also a shared interest of theirs but was not a suitable goal for the grant’s scope and for the type of brain sensing. Instead, the team decided that the best framing for the neuroadaptive tool wa s as a homework assistant tool. This decision was i n direct response to teachers’ feedback on the neuroadaptive  tool, seeing it as most useful during solo work to  provide personalized feedback to students on their learning and feedback to teachers on their assignments. The anticipatory component of RRI was made more concrete by starting  with goal setting at 1, 3, and 5-10 years away and continuously revisiting that timeline to situate feedback in certain points of the project. Future work could involve analyzing policy and school contexts to weave that information into the reflexivity and anticipatory aspects of the case study. Our approach to reflexivity could also benefit from investigation into the pre-existing bi ases of the research team to draw attention to value systems at play in the design of emerging educational technologies. The responsiveness and inclusion dimensions of RRI were baked into the methodology of the project through consideration of teacher interviews during the workshop. The study demonstrated the feasibilit y and usefulness of extending participatory methods into the development of emerging technology not yet feasible for use in educational settings. However, the inclusion dimension would be strengthened by direct exchanges between researchers and teachers, as well as input from add itional stakeholders like parents and students. Fur thermore, beyond the workshop, there was minimal uptake of results into the project. The responsiveness dimension of RRI would have benefited from revisiting and more deepl y integrating feedback throughout the project lifec ycle. In this way, RRI might pull in participatory methods from the learning sciences, aiming for participation that is not just contributory as in our case study and instead co-creates a project with members of the public through methods like Participatory Action Research, Participatory Design, and Co-Design (Bonney et al., 2009; Roschell e et al., 2006; DiSalvo & DiSalvo, 2014; Jacobs et al 2016). This may help ensure that the inclusion component of RRI benefits teachers, students, and parents, by directly addressing their needs in the classroom. This research was done without value alignments wit hin the practices of Responsible Research and Innovation. One substantive contention with RRI is an underlying assumption that “good” research practices will lead to “good” outcomes. In fact, the same might be  said of participatory research methods (i.e., rese arch is inherently better if it is participatory). We also acknowledge that RRI does not necessarily account for secondary factors that make inclusion more equitable, such as  appropriate compensation and community agreements.  For our own future work and other practitioners looking to integrate RRI, Foley et al. (2016) suggest combining RRI with other aspirations such as sustainability, social justice, gender/racial equity, etc. Researchers in the learning sciences have already begun approaching participatory methods with specific value systems; Participatory Action Research has been imbued with ideas from feminist inquiry that call for an examination of power relations in the research process (Jacobs, 2016), while lenses of racial identity and Black feminist epistemology have been applied to participatory design methods for creating conversational agents with Black youth (Rankin & Henderson, 2021). Combining value-based participatory approaches with  anticipatory and reflexive practices from RRI may help ICLS2022 Proceedings 669 © ISLS formalize a process and definition for responsible research, ensuring that all stakeholders are on the  same page about what is responsible.

In this work, we propose expanding on typical parti cipatory methodologies in the learning sciences by contextualizing them in a responsible research and innovation framework and applying this framework to emerging educational technologies, specifically neu roadaptive learning tools. We conclude that partici patory methods and Responsible Research and Innovation complement each other in conducting relevant, responsi ble, and inclusive research. RRI contributes contextual,  anticipatory, and reflexive thinking to creating e ducational technology, especially tools that are still emergin g. Meanwhile, participatory methods in the learning  sciences, especially those that consider certain values, stre ngthen the responsiveness and inclusion dimensions of RRI. Connecting researcher and educator perspectives thr ough a lens of Responsible Research and Innovation can clarify immediate and long-term goals of a project, contextualize a project in a specific use case, and help define how emerging technologies may ultimately be relevant for education and not simply technologically novel.
