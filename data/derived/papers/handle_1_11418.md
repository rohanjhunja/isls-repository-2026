# Supporting Coherence from Students’ Perspective in Middle School Science Classrooms Using Students’ Experience Data

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
A key goal of science education is to give students a feeling for what it is to do science. This aim is supported when students perceive instruction to be coherent from their point of view. Teachers can facilitate coherence by building a classroom culture focused on collective knowledge building, supporting student agency in the direction of their own learning, and helping students to see the arc of their own learning. In this study, teachers participated in a virtual cycle of inquiry called the Student Experience Improvement Cycle (SEIC), in which they tested strategies for enhancing students’ perceptions of coherence. Over the course of the experience, students’ sense of coherence increased significantly. However, students’ growth was not the same for students of different backgrounds. The study shows the initial potential of teacher collaborative inquiry for enhancing students’ experience of science learning.

## Introduction and background

A key goal for science education today is to enable  students to build knowledge incrementally, using t heir prior understandings and building persuasive arguments with peers about science phenomena. Students are expected to exercise agency over knowledge bu ilding, working collaboratively with other students  to decide what ideas best explain the phenomena they are studying (Damşa et al., 2010; Stroupe, 2014; NRC, 2012). Students need to have a say both in the direction of learning—that is, in what questions are taken up to be investigated—but also in how to pursue those questions (Levine et al., 2020), building on their prio r knowledge and experiences (Miller & Krajcik, 2019). Teachers, for their part, are expected to be responsive to students’ ideas and questions, to support students’ going deeper with their emerging explanations of phenomena (Jabe r, Dini, & Hammer, 2022; Sikorski, 2015). In such classrooms, students are likely to experien ce learning as coherent from their own point of vie w, that is, where they can see the work, they do as taking up questions and designing solutions to problems they have identified (Reiser, Novak, & McGill, 2017). Such coherence can be supported by curriculum materials when they are organized around a few key science ideas (Krajcik, McNeill, & Re iser, 2008) and when they successfully anticipate many of the questions students are likely to generate when presented with a phenomenon (Reiser et al., 2021). They are also supported by teachers’ own efforts to support student sensemaking by supporting th eir agency over their learning  and by supporting and recognizing students’ own eff orts to make meaningful connections between different ideas encountered over the course of a unit (Sikorski & Hammer, 2017). Responding to calls to center students’ perspectives on coherence (Reis er, Novak, & McGill, 2017; Sikorski, 2015), we have been exploring an approach to helping teachers s upport coherence through testing teaching strategies and monitoring students’ perceptions of coherence in the classroom. Our approach involves a collaborative cycle of teacher inquiry called the Student Experience Improvement Cycle (SEIC). In the SEIC, educators come together to explore strategies for s upporting coherence from the student's point of view and investigate their effects. The strategies educators explore are how to support students’ incremental, collaborative sensemaking about phenomena and how to do so in a w ay that promotes equity of student experience. In t his study, a group of 18 middle-school teachers who volunteered to participate enga ged in the SEIC during spring

2021. Nearly all the teachers had constructed their own curricul um materials, and nearly all were teaching

remotely at the time of the study. The goal of our study was to test whether the strategies teachers u sed could increase students’ perceptions of coherence and do so equitably, that is, equ ally for students of different genders and races/ethnicities. We found that over the cours e of the study, students’ perceptions of the cohere nce of their learning increased significantly over time. In addition, students with initially lower perceptions of coherence grew more. Some student groups’ perceptions of coherence increased more: white students, Native Ha waiian/Pacific Islander students, and Asian students.

## Conceptual framework

One way to conceptualize coherence is to promote cohere nce from the student's point of view (Reiser, Novak, & McGill, 2017; Sikorski, 2015). In this conceptualization, the curriculum can play a role in supporting students in searching for consistent relationships among ideas, and its organization can facilitate such search when organized around student questions and creating the need to t hink more deeply about gaps and contradictions in m odels of phenomena (Reiser et al., 2021). But, as elaborated below, teachers also play several key roles in enhancing and attending to the diversity of students’ experiences of coherence, and these strategies were the focus of our teacher inquiry cycle. Teachers focused on implementing strategies from three overarching c ategories: 1) Classroom culture of science focused on seeking coherence: that aims to help individual students share, expand, or clarify their own thinking and to help students think with one another, one strategy relate s to using “Talk Moves” routines; 2) Supporting student agency in informing the direction of learning: it focuses to re-organize instruction around students’ questions related to phenomena bei ng studied, to support this in classroom one strategy relates to making heterogeneous grouping of students; and 3) Helping students see the arc of their learning: it focuses on to help students see how phenomena and problems they are studying could be relevant to their lives, one strategy related to it was asking students short writing tasks related to personal utility of science learned. These were some of the exemplar strategies implemented in the SEIC cycle. The current study This study builds from the potential of specific st rategies for promoting coherence, coupled with enga ging teachers in monitoring data on student experience, with explicit attention to equity. Specifically, ou r goal was testing a strategy of teacher collaborative inquiry, the SEIC, for promoting equitable perceptions of coherence in students’ science learning. In the SEIC, teachers learned about and chose to enact specific strategies for promoting coherence, while testing the effects of those strat egies using a brief survey (“exit ticket”) of stude nt experience. We addressed two primary questions: 1. How did students’ perceptions of coherence of instruction vary by lesson, student, and teacher? 2. Can a professional learning cycle support teachers in p romoting equity with respect to students’ perceptions of the coherence of science lessons? Description of the Intervention: The Student Experience Improvement Cycle The SEIC is based on a Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle (Bryk et al., 2015) that engages teachers in adapting res earchbased strategies for promoting coherence from stude nts’ perspectives (Penuel, Raza et al., 2024). As other PlanDo-Study-Act cycles do, the SEIC relies on tools called “pra ctical measures,” instruments seeking to measure progress toward specific improvement aims that are easy to use on repeated occasions in the context of  practice (Yeager et al., 2013). In a four-session virtual series, a cohort of participating teachers investigated research-based strategies for improving equity of participation in  science classrooms that elaborated the strategies presented in the conceptual framework. In the first session, we provided educators with a list of strategies grouped into different categories that aligned with key elements of our co nceptual framework. Educators read about the strate gies and annotate documents explaining how they could be int egrated within their adopted instructional materials. In this session, they also learned about a practical measur e we planned to use and were oriented to a web-based system that supports collecting and visualizing student ex perience data to reflect on patte rns (Raza et al., 2024). In the second session of the SEIC, teachers further explored strategies and chose a group to join to develop a plan to test a cluster of practices from the list provided in th eir classroom. They chose groups based on their int erest and the specific instruc tional materials they used. With members of their g roup, teachers completed a “team charter” template created by series facilitators, outlining their plans to test the strategy agreed upon by the ir group with their classes. In the third session, teachers r eviewed data from their own classrooms on student e xperience using the visualizations from the web-based system and discussed the data with their grou p using a structured protocol provided by facilitators. During this session,  they created plans for refining their strategies fo r further testing in the classroom. In the fourth and final session, teachers reviewed and discussed new data with their peers, looking at data for each strategy group. Teachers discussed  further refineme nts they wished to make to their strategies before summarizing and sharing what they learned fr om the experience with the group. Teachers also dis cussed the broader systemic context of their data collecti on and the limits of work at the classroom level th at does not also consider wider societal and school-level oppression as an ongoing challenge for equity.

## Participants and source of data

Participants were recruited in early 2021 for a spring professiona l learning cycle intended to focus on remote instruction in science, given the ongoing struggles of teachers in the pandemic to support student participation in science learning. We ended up with 26 teachers who participat ed. This study builds on the data from 18 teachers who completed all four sessions of the SEIC cycle. The teacher cohort participating in the series consisted of 15 females and 3 males, with the majority (10) being white. The source of data for the study was student experience data from “Student Electronic Exit Tickets” or SEETs. The data come from a short survey administered at the end of each lesson. The SEET was used both as a practical measure to inform educators about their own practice and as the primary research instrument in this study. V alidity evidence gathered as part of earlier studie s found that the SEET items were readily interpretable by studen ts in ways that corresponded to researchers’ intent ions and SEET responses correlated with performance on a thr ee-dimensional assessment of student learning (Penuel et al., 2018). When registering in the SEET system, students recei ve an anonymous identifier to log in. Exit tickets were administered at the conclusion of a lesson. The exit ticket, referred to within the project as the SEET, includes a set of items focused on the dimension of experien ce coherence, the three items of coherence that were used in the SEIC cycle are: 1) We work together to determine what ideas ar e most persuasive. 2) The teacher guides us to share our prior experiences or ideas about a phenomenon or topic to inform what we will donext. 3) Today we started class by reminding ourselves what we learned in the last class. Approach to analysis The primary outcome was growth with respect to students’ report ed coherence of the day’s lesson, measured as an average score across three coherence items. Growth over time was modeled using the temporal variable DAY, which represents the number of days since the first data point was collected for a given teacher and was recentered around a common baseline day for interpretation. Given the nested structure of the data-student observations (completion of a single exit ticket) within student s and students within teachers  – hierarchical linear modeling (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002) was applied. To explore whether student experience s became more equitable over time, we included gender and race/ethnicity as student-level predictors. Drawing from QuantCrit  scholars (Gillborn, Warmington, & Demack, 2018), we chose to de-center dominant groups in the choice of reference groups. Our analyses were guided by a commitment to promoting equity of participation rather than by a “neutral” stance toward the data. All models were fit using HLM 8.2 software with full maximum likelihood estimation.

## Results

How student experience varied by lesson, student, and classroom The final dataset contained 1,801 observations from 843 students across 45 classrooms. The average score of all coherence items was.77 (SD =.26). To address our first research question, we started with an unconditional means model to quantify the variance at each level. Approximately half of the variance in p erceived coherence (49%) was at the lesson level. That is to say, differences in s tudent experience of individual lessons – rather thandifferences among individuals or across classrooms – accounted for the biggest percentage of variation i n students’ classroom experiences. More specifically, 44 percent of the variance in reported experience was at the student level, but only 8 percent of the variance was due to the tea cher. The variation at the student and teacher levels was of particular interest to us, particularly as the l evels relate to students’ social identities (gender  and race/ethnicity) and teachers’ capacity to intervene in ways to improve the quality of student experiences. An important consideration for equity is whether the rates of change differ for individuals and classrooms that, at baseline, have low or high levels of perce ived coherence. The estimated correlation between t he baseline and rate of change was estimated to be -.385 at the individual level and -.662 at the classroom level. For students in the same classroom, those with a lower quality e xperience at the beginning had even better experien ces over time. The negative correlation seemed to bring stud ents together by boosting those at the bottom. Simi larly, classrooms that started lower may have grown at a faster rate. This pattern sugge sted both individuals and classrooms were becoming more “equitable” in that s tudents who perceived their classroom experience to be of lower quality narrowed the gap with those who perce ived their experience to be of higher quality. Furt her, classrooms became more equal across the sample: there was less variability across classrooms after participating in the SEIC in students’ average quality of experience. Modeling equity in growth in coherence To answer our second research question, we modeled growth by examining whether DAY  was a significant predictor of student experience. As shown in Model 1 (Table 1), on average, each one-day increase in DAY was associated with a.003-unit increase in  coherence (𝛽=.003, p <.001). Most students experienced  this gradual improvement, which translates to answering one more coherence item positively over the course of a year. We also examined how growth in coherence was related to gender, race/ethnicity, and both gender and race/ethnicity together  (see Table 1). In the growth model with gender included (Model 2), no significant differences were observed. In the growth model with race/ethnicity as predictors (Model 3), Latinx students (our reference group) exhibited a significantly higher q uality of baseline experience as measured by the SEET than that of white (𝛽 = -.124, p =.003) and Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander (𝛽 = -.161, p =.050) students. In terms of growth rates as compared to Latinx students, we found positive and significant growth rates for Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander (𝛽 =.009, p =.005) and Asian (𝛽 =.004, p =.023) and a borderline significant growth rate for white (𝛽=.003, p =.068) students. Conversely, American Indian/Alaska Native students exhibited a slower growth rate in the quality of their experience than Latinx stud ents (𝛽 = -.004, p =.063), though this was marginal significance. We caution that these results should be interpreted  carefully, given  the relatively large standard errors and small number of participants in these racial/ethnic groups. Furthermore, the full model, which included both gender and race/ethnicity (Model 4), yielded similar results with comparable significant levels, with no additional significant effects.

## Discussion

This study found that educators’ efforts to test strategies in their classroom that grounded in research for promoting coherence can support improvements to students’ experience of their science learning. The educators in the study tested all three sets of strategies for promoting c oherence—fostering a culture of collective knowledge-building, promoting student agency over the direction of thei r learning, and helping students see the arc of the ir own learning. These strategies have been established as  promising in a variety of studies of student sense making in science (e.g., Sikorski & Hammer, 2017), collaborative knowledge buil ding (e.g., Damşa et al., 2010), and experimental intervention studies (Damşa et al., 2010). Further, the results show that, over the period that teachers tried out these strategies, the quality of their students’ experience overall improved. What remains somewhat puzzling and unexplained is the fact that some student groups increased greater benefits than others. This study included white students (borderline significa nt), Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islanders, and Asian students. We do know from both large-scale and qualitative studies that students’ experience of science classrooms can and does vary by race and  ethnicity (Carlone, Haun-Frank, & Webb, 2011; Krumm et al., 2020). Further, past studies have shown that racial hierarchies in society intersect with classroom dynamics to shape student learning experiences (Shah, 2018). Thus, the mere fact that student patterns of experience and growth in their experience might vary by race is no t surprising. However, it is puzzling why strategie s intended to promote more collective knowledge building, situ ated within a cycle of inquiry aimed at fostering t he experience of racially minoritized students might y ield the pattern of results that we found. Future e thnographic and qualitative studies are needed to explore why this might be the case.

## Conclusion

Students in today’s science classrooms need to experience their learning as coherent from their point of view, not just the teacher’s. Such experiences give students a better sense of what it is to do science that is different from learning science as if i t were only a body of knowledge to be acquired. Tea chers play a critical role in fostering coherence from a student's point of view, regardless of the instructional materials they are using, and the findings of this study suggest the promise of collaborative inquiry for promoting coherence from the student point of view. Further, the study findings point to the possibilit y of inquiry focused on improving equity of student  experience for creating classrooms where, overall, students ’ experiences are of a similar quality with respect  to coherence. At the same time, inequities persist in ways that are not surprising and that are also d ifficult to diagnose. Taken together, the findings suggest that the goal of pro moting more meaningful engagement in science remain s an elusive one, but that teachers working together can make progress toward that goal.
