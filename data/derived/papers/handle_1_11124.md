# Narrative and Physical Contextualization in AR Learning Environments

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Augmented Reality (AR) offers unique opportunities to contextualize learning in authentic environments, connecting learning and its context as emphasized by situated and apprenticeship learning. We explored the impact of differently situated narratives and physical artifacts on AR-context perception, immersion, learning outcome, intrinsic motivation, and workload when learning a medical topic using HoloLens2. An experimental study with N = 40 participants was conducted in a 2´2 between-subjects design with narrative (education context vs. application context) and physical contextualization (presence vs. absence of a physical artifact) as factors. It revealed that the presence of a physical artifact significantly increased the perceived virtual-physical consistency within the AR experience. Application context narratives enhanced enjoyment and knowledge. However, the latter effect decreased when physical artifacts were introduced, potentially contributing to increased frustration. The contextualization of AR material thus influenced factors relevant for learners’ experience and learning processes. Future research should explore this connection more detailed.

## Introduction and background

The learning sciences have a long tradition of exploring the role of the learning context, highlighting the relevance of considering the social and physical environment in which learning takes place (Fischer et al., 2023). Research on learning with novel technologies can build on this tradition while also introducing new perspectives on the role of learning context. When looking at established perspectives, various theories suggest that the acquisition and retention of knowledge are strongly related to the particular situational context, distinguishing between the education context (mostly school) and the application context in which the knowledge is actually used  (e.g., situated learning and cognitive apprenticeship; Cakmakci et al., 2020). Education contexts such as classrooms are distinct from any other environment beyond education which is why learning environments should guarantee that learning is contextualized in real-world settings (Young, 1993). McLellan (1994) argues that the context in which knowledge is acquired should be either the actual application context, a realistic or virtual substitute of it. Through recent technological  developments, augmented reality (AR)  introduces new potentials for and perspectives on actual learning context and virtual substitutes. AR systems generate novel environments that combine virtual and physical information (Azuma, 1997), enabling education that is based in a physical context and enriched with virtual material.  B y embedding virtual elements in physical environments, AR can contextualize learning in authentic environments and bring authentic artifacts into the classroom  (Bower et al., 2014). Situated learning theory has been applied to AR in the past  (Garzón et al., 2020; Sommerauer & Müller, 2018), affirming that AR is suited to contextualize learning. The learners’ perception of this new form of context-based information in AR and its potential influence on the learning process are crucial from an educational human-centered perspective. In particular, the perceived level of consistency between virtual and physical information as  an integrated coherent environment plays an important role here and has been described as contextuality (Krüger et al., 2019). A closely associated concept is object-based context immersion, which is enabled by object recognition and establishes relationships between different virtual and physical objects (Kim, 2013). Providing learning content in an environment that combines virtual and physical elements raises novel  questions about the role of the learning context and how AR learning environments can be contextualized to address affective and cognitive processes in a way that facilitates learning.

There is not much research on the perception of AR learning contexts with focus on the integration of virt ual and physical information as a coherent context  so far. However, t wo factors that impact the immersion experience might help to investigate  AR learning environments  in terms of the perception of this new form of context. One is the use of a narrative  (Nilsson et al., 2016; Reid et al., 2005), such as a written story. The other factor is the use of physical art ifacts in the AR environment  (Reid, 2008). Georgiou and Kyza  (2021), who conducted a study of varying degrees of coupling of physical elements wit h a narrative in an AR game scenario, found that strong coupling of the physical elements with the narrative led to higher immersion and reasoning in a knowledge test. Since the narrative was used to convey the learning content in this study, we posit that both the narrative and the learning content should be strongly coupled to the physical artifacts. Blum et al. (2012) introduce ICLS 2024 Proceedings 450 © ISLS a magi c mirror that integrates physical and virtual elements. It projects computer tomography data onto the user’s body via a real-time camera view creating the illusion of looking into one’s own body. The first use of the magic mirror elicited positive feedback, with users particularly drawn to exploring and locating various organs within the body. The authors assume that AR could enhance communication and education between patients and doctors by directly visualizing surgical procedures on the patient and supporting explanations. Numerous studies have already demonstrated that AR is appropriate for vocational learning in, amongst others, the medical field (Chiang et al., 2022). For the current study we chose a medical learning topic that is situated in a  narrative education or application context in which the virtual learning materials are either coupled with  a person as a physical artifact or not. Both factors may interact, as for instance in a medical application context the focus may be on connecting the learning content to the patient, while in a university education context the focus may be more on the learning content itself. Thus, the integration of a person may affect learning in both narrated contexts differently.

## Research question and hypotheses

The main aim of this investigation is to examine how the perception of contextuality can be influenced in AR, with the goal to systematically determine the impact of di fferent narratives (describing an education context vs. describing an application context) and integrating a physical artifact into the AR environment for establishing perceived contextuality and to explore the resulting impact on the learning processes and outcomes. We formulated hypotheses concerning  the influence of both factors, narrative and physical contextualization, on perceived contextuality, immersion, and learning outcomes. With regard to the narrative contextualization, w e expected that a narrated application context lead s to higher perceived contextuality compared to an education context (H1a)  due to the alignment of content and context. Based on the assumptions that contexts in which knowledge is used are more authentic than pure education contexts, it is hypothesized that an application narrative will result in higher feelings of immersion than a n education narrative (H2a). It is also assumed that a narrated application context leads to higher learning outcomes compared to an education context (H3a) since the learning content is situated in a meaningful context, which facilitates learning processes. Regarding physical contextualization, we expected that the presence of a physical artifact leads to increased perceived contextuality compared to the absence of a physical artifact in AR (H1b), as virtual and physical elements are appropriately combined. Additionally, the presence of a physical artifact will provide a more immersive experience than its absence (H2b).  Due to the supportive impact of closely coupling the learning content with a physical object on learning, we hypothesize that the presence of a physical artifact in the AR environment will lead to higher learning outcomes compared to the absence of such an artifact (H3b). An exploratory investigation of the interaction between narrative and physical contextualization  on perceived contextuality (E1), immersion (E2) and learning outcomes (E3) is conducted as the existing literature does not present a distinct direction. In addition to the hypotheses and investigations, the impact of narrative and physical contextualization on intrinsic motivation (E4) and workload (E5) will be examined exploratively. We chose to survey motivation because it is often identified to be influenced through AR (Akçayır & Akçayır, 2017) and workload because this has proven to be the most frequently surveyed cognitive load construct in AR learning studies (Buchner et al., 2021). This makes it possible to compare different empirical AR studies.

## Method

A 2×2 factorial between-subjects design was applied. The first factor, narrative contextualization, determined the narrated context in which the learning setting was situated with two levels, education context (NE) and application context (NA). The second factor, physical contextualization, determined the absence (P−) or presence (P +) of a strongly coupled physical artifact. This resulted in four conditions (N E/P−, NE/P+, NA/−, NA/P+). The dependent variables were perceived contextuality, immersion, workload, learning outcomes,  and intrinsic motivation. To ensure that the participants in the two  conditions did not differ in terms of prior knowledge and domain-specific learning motivation before using the AR application, both variables were also assessed. A total of N = 41 participants volunteered their time for the study. One participant reported a low German language level and was excluded from the analyses because the learning materials were in German. The remaining N = 40 participants declared at least a proficient German language level (C1-C2), with the majority indicating to be native German speakers (95.0%). 24 participants identified as female, 15 as male and one individual did not indicate. The age ranged from 18 to 32 years with a mean of M  = 23.50 years (SD = 3.52). The sample consisted mainly of students (97.5%), with a large proportion enrolled in an interdisciplinary study program (92.1%) whose participation was compensated with course credits. There were n = 10 participants in each condition. Participants indic ated in a five-point response format (0 = never, 1 = rarely, 2 = occasionally, 3 = often, 4 = regularly) how frequently they had previously used or learned with  conventional mobile applications, AR applications on mobile devices or head-mounted displays (HMDs), and the related medium of VR applications ICLS 2024 Proceedings 451 © ISLS on HMDs. Th ey had used conventional  mobile applications almost regularly (M = 3.88, SD = 0.34) and occasionally for learning (M = 1.90, SD = 1.24), but mobile AR applications rather rarely (M = 0.58, SD = 0.68) and almost never for learning (M = 0.18, SD = 0.45). On HMDs, they had almost never used AR either in general (M = 0.30, SD = 0.46) or for learning (M = 0.15, SD = 0.36) and had rarely used VR in general  (M = 1.30, SD = 1.09) and almost never for learning (M = 0.43, SD = 0.71). Thus, participants were familiar with mobile applications that they occasionally used for learning and rarely for AR but had only little experience with AR or VR HMDs. The study was examined and approved by the ethics committee of the institution. Material During the study, participants used an AR learning application that ran on a Microsoft HoloLens2 and was developed with the Unity game engine using the Mixed Reality Toolkit (MRTK). In the application, users could see a virtual model of the digestive system and a visual slate that provided information about food processing. In 12 chapters displayed on the slate with a ‘next’ button for navigation, the participants were sequentially introduced to the processes of the digestive system and the involved anatomi cal components. The virtual model of the digestive system highlighted and labeled individual components based on the food processing stage. The information in the 12 chapters was derived from the animal nutrition chapter of a German biology reference book (Krüppel, 2016), and it was condensed and revised for the study’s requirements. The first factor manipulated was narrative contextualization in which participants were provided with a narrative at the beginning of the learning phase in which they were instructed to imagine learning the content either in an education  context (university course) or application context  (medical specialty practice). Both narrative variants are shown in Figure 1 (a and b). The second manipulated factor was physical contextualization, in which participants could see either a virtual model without reference to a physical artifact (absence of a physical artifact) or one that is strongly coupled to a physical artifact (presence of a physical artifact) during the learning phase. Both representations of physical contextualization are shown in Figure 1 (c and d). In t he following, the instruments and questionnaires used are presented and their internal consistency with McDonald’s Omega (ω) is indicated.  To account for potential differences between the four conditions in participants’ pre-study motivation for the topic, the Expectancy Value Questionnaire  (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000) was translated and adapted to the learning topic. The questionnaire measures  (1) ability beliefs (ω =.86),

(2) expectancy (ω =.63), and (3)  usefulness, importance, and interest (ω =.94) in a five-point response format

from 1 (low) to 5 (high).

Participants’ perception of the AR learning context as coherent and integrated was measured using a long version of the ARcis Questionnaire  (Krüger & Bodemer, 2022), which is answered i n a seven-point response format from 1 (low) to 7 (high) and includes subscales on perceived interactivity (13 items) and spatiality (11 items) in addition to perceived contextuality (12 items). Since this version of the questionnaire has not previously been used, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted. The CFA indicated poor fit measures for the complete-item version, χ2(591) = 1434.00, p <.001, CFI  = 0.34, TLI  = 0.29, SRMR  = 0.18, RMSEA  = 0.19, 90% CI [0.18, 0.20], according to recommended fit measures  (Bowen & Guo, 2011). To improve the model fit, we removed all items with factor loadings that did not reach significance and those below the critical threshold of Figure 1 Illustration of the Manipulation of Narrative (a & b) and Physical Contextualization (c & d) ICLS 2024 Proceedings 452 © ISLS 0.40.

In addition, the items with residuals that exhibited strong correlations with most other items were excluded within six iterations.  This resulted in five items remaining for perceived contextuality (ω =.89), six items for interactivity (ω =.81), and five items for spatiality (ω =.87), meeting the precise model fit requirements, χ2(101) = 112.00, p =.221, CFI = 0.96, TLI = 0.95, SRMR = 0.08, RMSEA = 0.05, 90% CI [0.00, 0.10]. The feeling of immersion was assessed using the Augmented Reality Immersion Scale  (ARI; Georgiou & Kyza, 2017) that employs a seven-point response format from 1 (low) to 7 (high) and considers total immersion (ω =.80) to be composed of  presence (4 items) and flow (3 items). Further, participants were asked to rate their ability to put themselves in the situation described in the narratives on a five-point scale (M = 3.23, SD = 1.00). Based on the learning material, a pre and a post knowledge test were developed and piloted with a small sample (N = 14) to measure the learners’ prior knowledge regarding the learning topic and learning outcomes. The pre-and posttest questions were different to avoid directing the learners’ attention to the posttest questions before the learning phase began.  The pretest was used to  check if the conditions we re unevenly distributed in terms of prior knowledge. It comprised ten multiple-choice (MC) questions assessing general preliminary knowledge on the digestive system. The posttest  consisted of 30 MC  questions asking about the functionality, appearance, consistency, and location of the respective components of the digestive system. It also included 11 multiple representation (MR) questions in which 2D images of the individual components of the digestive system were presented and participants were required to identify the correct name from a  drop-down menu. The MC questions had one correct answer option  and thre e distractors. Each correct response was scored one point. Participants achieved an average of 4.15 (SD = 1.59) out  of 10 points in the pre knowledge test  and 2 7.80 (SD = 6.09) out of 41 in the post knowledge test (MC: M = 20.30, SD = 4.37; MR: M = 7.55, SD = 2.26). We employed the German short scale of the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory  (KIM; Wilde et al., 2009) which measures intrinsic motivation in a five-point response format from 0 (low) to 4 (high) in terms of (1) interest and enjoyment (ω =.85), (2) perceived confidence (ω =.83), (3) perceived choice (ω =.82), and (4) pressure and tension (ω =.65). Workload during learning was measured using the Nasa Task Load Index (Nasa-TLX) by Hart

(2006) which consists of six single  items (1)  mental demand, (2)  physical demand, (3)  temporal demand,

(4) performance, (5) effort, and (6) frustration answered in a 21-point response format from 1 (low) to 21 (high).

## Procedure

First, the participants were made aware of the content, the purpose, and the procedure of the study. After signing the declaration of consent, the y conducted the HoloLens2 eye calibration and were randomly assigned to one of the four conditions. The study began with the pre  knowledge test, followed by the E xpectancy-Value Questionnaire. Participants then received information about the learning phase and read one of the two narratives (education context or application context). During the learning phase,  which was limited to 20 minutes, the participants could see the virtual model of the digestive system  and a digital board with  additional textual information. Here, the components of the digestive system were described successively, giving access to the next component through a button click. When accessing a new part of the digestive system, the virtual model also changed and  the corresponding areas were highlighted and label ed with component  names. Participants were asked to read  the texts carefully and  mentally connect the highlighted component names from the text with the virtual component models, with the goal of relating  the content of the text and the  visual appearance of the components. During this learning phase, either only the virtual model of the digestive system was shown (absence of a physical artifact), or the model was superimposed onto a physical person in the same place (presence of a physical artifact). After using the learning application, the participants returned the HoloLens2 and completed the questionnaires on cognitive load, workload, immersion, motivation and ARcis characteristics. This was followed by the knowledge test, where they first completed the MC questions and then the MR questions. At the end of the survey, participants provided information about their demographics and their previous experience with mobile, AR and VR (learning) applications. Finally, participants received a debriefing in which they were informed about the manipulation of the narrative as well as the presence of a physical  artifact, in the form of a person.

## Results

To verify that the participants in the conditions did not differ in terms of their prior knowledge or domain-specific motivation prior to the manipulation, equivalence tests for F-tests were computed along with one-way ANOVAs as a prerequisite for these (Campbell & Lakens, 2021). The smallest effect size of interest (SESOI)  serves as equivalence bounds and was set to η2 =.26, considering the achieved sample size. In terms of prior knowledge of the digestive system, there were no significant differences between conditions, F (3, 36) = 2.54, p  =.072. The equivalence test for F-tests showed that the conditions were also not significantly equal based on the bounds set (p =.105). Concerning knowledge beliefs, expectancy, and usefulness, importance, and interest, the conditions ICLS 2024 Proceedings 453 © ISLS did not s how any significant differences (p >.05). The equivalence test for F-tests showed that the conditions were significantly equal in their knowledge beliefs (p <.001) and in their expectancy (p =.041), but not in their usefulness, importance, and interest (p =.076).

Participants did not differ in putting themselves  into the narrative when it described the application (M = 3.20, SD = 0.77) or the education context  (M = 3.25, SD = 1.21), F(1, 36) = 0.03, p =.874, ω2 =.02, as determined by a two-way ANOVA. Additionally, there was no significant interaction between the context of the narrative and the presence of a physical artifact on this variable, F(1, 36) = 3.09, p =.088, ω2 =.05. However, descriptively it is noteworthy that in the application context  participants were better able to place themselves in the narrative when the  physical artifact was present  (M = 3.60, SD = 0.52) than when the physical artifact was absent (M = 2.80, SD = 0.79). In the education context, participants were better able to put themselves in the narrative when the physical artifact was absent (M = 3.40, SD = 1.17) than when it was present  (M = 3.10, SD = 1.29).

All hypotheses were tested with a two-way ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc tests, analyzing the main effect of narrative contextualization  on the respective variables for H1a – H3a, and the main effect of physical contextualization for H1b – H3b. Interaction effects of these two-way ANOVAs were analyzed for the exploratory assumptions E1 – E3. For E4 and E5, main and interaction effects were analyzed exploratorily. In terms of interest and enjoyment, participants in the N A conditions perceived the learning phase as considerably more interesting and enjoyable than those in the NE conditions, t(36) = 2.15, p =.039, d = 0.68. ## Discussion and conclusion

The aim of this study is to explore the impact of contextual elements as differently contextualized narratives and physical artifacts on perceived contextuality, immersion, learning outcomes, intrinsic motivation, and workload. Results indicate that learners perceived the combined learning context of physical and virtual information as more consistent, integrated, and coherent when the material was projected onto a person than when it was placed freely in the room. However, this perceived contextuality was not affected by the narratives through which learners were supposed to imagine a certain environment  without physically being there. These results are in accordance with the assumptions of contextuality, which focuses on the consistency of virtual and physical information (Krüger et al., 2019). While the physical object changed the virtual-physical consistency, the narrative did not. Regarding immersion experience, an interaction was found between the narrative learning context and presence of a physical artifact. Accordingly, learners experienced more immersion in the  narrative application context when the virtual content was projected onto a person than when it was placed freely in the room.  The opposite was found for the  narrative education context. A similar pattern emerged for the question of how well learners were able putting themselves in the narrative. Here, the expectations of the narrative context could differ in relation to the familiarity of a person as a reference object. The sample consisted predominantly of students who, from their study course, are unfamiliar with other people acting as a context for learning content. In contrast, they could probably imagine a different person in the context of a medical practice, since th is includes treating patients and thus the focus is on individuals, whereas in a university context the focus is more on the learning content. These potential explanations suggest that inconsistent relationships between the contextualization of AR learning environments may negatively impact learners’ immersion experiences. Participants experienced greater interest and  enjoyment and displayed higher proficiency in naming components of the digestive system  correctly when they were instructed to imagine an application context (medical practice) prior to the learning phase. This is accordance with the assumptions about situated learning described in the theoretical background, highlighting the importance of learning in application-near contexts (McLellan, 1994). However, the effect on correct naming of components depends on the physical contextualization. Only when no artifact was present, learning was increased by the imagined application context. Unexpectedly, learners reported more frustration when the physical artifact was present than when it was absent. These negative impacts of the physical artifact may be due to a tendency to distract from the learning content and ICLS 2024 Proceedings 455 © ISLS act as a s eductive detail rather than supporting the learning process  (Harp & Mayer, 1998). But this does not explain why individuals felt more frustrated with the presence of a physical artifact. Since the physical artifact was another person, it is probable that the social context was likewise impacted by the manipulation, even though the person did not socially interact with the participants during the learning phase. In this case, learners may have felt observed by the ‘physical artifact’ and consequently perceived the other person as a social stressor, leading to heightened anxiety and arousal and ultimately increased frustration levels (Kushnir, 1986). This may have been particularly the case in our study as this person was the experimenter. However, these are conjectures as to  the reasons for the negative effects of the physical artifact as used in this study design. Regarding the physical artifact, certain issues were identified. Initially, ensuring accurate placement of the virtual digestive system model on the socio-physical artifact was not consistently appropriate. In addition, it was observed that some participants did not move around the model, although some components could only be seen from other perspectives. To gain better insight into why participants did not move around the model and why they experienced different levels of interest and enjoyment and frustration in the conditions, qualitative data such as interviews should be collected in future studies. It is necessary to conduct further research to determine whether these negative influences of a physical artifact also occur when using a non-social object or the person serving as a reference object is more familiar, for instance a peer  in the classroom. Furthermore, future studies  should consider the interaction with the artifact, which is another potential that AR offers  (Krüger et al., 2019). In a setting similar to the current study, physical and social interaction with the person would be possible, which both offer the opportunity to support additional learning processes that were not the focus of the current study.  One possibility here would be to expand the learning activity to include the explain mode from the generative learning activity approach (Fiorella, 2023). This would allow dyads of peers to switch the roles of learner and reference object and explain the visualized content to each other to simulate sense-making and thus enhance learning. Overall, this study provides initial insights into the impact of contextuality in AR on learning a medical topic. It suggests that the use of a socio-physical artifact in an AR environment increase s the consistency and integration of virtual  and physical information, albeit at the cost of increased frustration. Moreover, a narrative application context is perceived as more interesting and enjoyable  than an education context  and can improve learning in the absence of a socio-physical artifact, in our case  especially regarding the integration of text and visual components of the learning material. Disentangling social and physical types of artifacts in research could shed further light on their suitability for different learning situations and help develop AR environments  that simultaneously facilitate abstract and applied understanding within the classroom.
