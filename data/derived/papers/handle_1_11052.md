# Designing Measurements for Learning Transfer of Computational Thinking in Game-Based Learning Environment

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study reports the design and evaluation of measurement instruments to assess the transfer of computational thinking (CT) skills from a game-based learning environment tonew contexts. A near transfer test and a far transfer test was designed by mapping game elements to an existing CT assessment. Although further refinement is needed to improve reliability and validity, the results provide initial evidence that tailored assessments can evaluate CT transfer beyond gameplay.

Learning beyond gameplay: The transfer perspective Despite the promises of accessible and active learn ing in digital game-based learning environments (DGBLE), researchers have challenged the idea of whether learning with games can achieve meaningful transfer (Jackson & McNamara, 2013). One of the challenges is that t he variability in contexts and levels also limits t he ability of learning to transfer beyond the scope of gameplay (Liu & Jeong, 2022). Without proper scaffolds and purposeful learning support, learners may focus more on playin g the game rather than building understanding and s kills. As a result, the "learning" that occurs in GBL may not transfer well  to other situations  (Nietfeld, 2020). However, effective learning design relies on valid and reliable measurement of the learning outcome (i.e., learning transfer). To this end, this study will  report the design efforts of the measurement instrument s to assess the transfer of computational thinking (CT) skills in a DGBLE.

Assessing computational thinking skills and the transfer Although past research has confirmed that CT can be  learned through gameplay, CT skills and their transfer still pose unique challenges because CT involves complex problem-solving processes (Rowe et al., 2021). In various proposed conceptual frameworks to assess CT skills (e.g., Shute et al., 2017), CT involves multiple cognitive or behavioral aspects. Furthermore, the contextual nat ure of CT performance creates barriers to transfer (Ye et al., 2022). Students may demonstrate competent CT skills wit hin a programming environment, yet struggle to appl y those same skills to math word problems.  To address these challenges, researchers emphasize using varied contexts and diverse assessment formats to evaluate CT transfer (Grover et al., 2015). One of the ways to provide multiple contexts is through designing external measures that represent various computing problems.

## Method

In the learning game design, students are presented  with various computing problems in the context of animal preservation. Students used various computing block s to help a penguin navigate through different terr ains (Liu & Jeong, 2022). Informed by the classical learning transfer theorie s (e.g., Royer et al., 2005), CTt (RománGonzález et al., 2017) is used as the benchmark for designin g the near and far transfer tests  for the game. In the original CTt, the instrument has a  Pacman look user interface (Figure 1 left panel). Therefore, the near transfer test is designed to map the game environment to the Pacman problems presented in CTt (Figure 1 right panel). Figure 1 An Example Item of CTt and Its Matched Item of the Near Transfer Test The far transfer test is designed with a brand-new context (i.e., serving fish and chips in a rest aurant). Figure 2 shows an example of one problem and its associated solution. All the contexts  were designed to align with the CTt and ensure  the underlying competencies are the same  (i.e., every item in CTt, near transfer test, and far transfer test has identical solutions). The only difference is how the problem is formulated. To validate the results of the transfer tests, we have collected data from 79 middle school students’ performance on these two tests. ## Preliminary results

The average score for the near transfer test was 9. 49 (SD = 2.76) out of 17. The Cronbach’s α was = 0.62 [0.50, 0.74]. Based on the item analysis, the first questi on was too easy (M >.85). Question 6 and 13 were negatively related to the total score suggesting that they wer e potentially problematic. However, dropping them i ncreased the reliability slightly (Δα= 0.01). It is foreseeable that the near transfer test does not have high reliability due to its isomorphism to the CTt pretest. The average score for the far transfer test was 8.63 (SD = 3.74) out of 17. The Cronbach’s α = 0.78 [0.70, 0.85], which was reasonable. The difficulty indices ranged from 0.28 (Question  9) to 0.71 (Question 4).  We further analyzed the convergent validity of the near transfer test and far transfer test by correlating with CTt (r =.43 for the near transfer test and.41 for the far transfer test).

## Discussion and future directions

This study takes an initial step toward developing assessments to evaluate learning transfer from digi tal gamebased learning environments. The near and far trans fer tests demonstrated moderate correlations with a n established CT assessment, the CTt. This provides some preliminary evidence that the instruments are measuring CT skills. Additional validation work is needed wit h multiple sources of data. For instance, c ognitive interviews and gameplay data would provide useful feedback to improve items and link the learning with transfer. Overall, the findings provide empirical evidence for trailing  instruments to DGBLE for evaluating transfer beyond the game itself. With further development, similar approaches could be applied to other DGBLE research.
