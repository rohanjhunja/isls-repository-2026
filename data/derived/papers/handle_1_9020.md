Introduction: The COVID-19 pandemic is a complex socio-scientific problem in that the virus (science) impacts communities in several different ways and requires people living in those communities to alter their behavior and lifestyles in order to address the problem. In this presentation we describe and present evidence for how storytelling can be a powerful approach that can be used for teaching and  learning complex socio-scientific problems. Teaching and Learning about complex socio-scientific problems such as COVID-19 and climate change requires educators and learners to use many non-traditional pedagogical to ols and approaches (Shepardson, et al 2012; Authors  2014; Lenzen, Day & Murray, 2002) where in individuals would: (a) teach and learn how to make decisions/take actions that are local, keeping in mind global impacts (Ostrom, et al. 1999); (b) address inequities caused by the problem (Schlosberg & Collins, 2014); (c) teach and learn i n ways that are grounded in local epistemologies (B ang & Medin, 2010); (d) teach and learn how to use data t hat is available to understand future scenarios (Re snick & Welinsky, 1998); (e) learn about and apply knowledge from both formal and informal learning environments (Bell et a., 2009) (f) teach and learn how to collaborate across local and global communities while addressing the power dynamics between and within those communities (Esmo nde & Booker, 2017). Storytelling is an approach th at can allow individuals to embed scientific content and data in their stories and also discuss how science problems can impact their communities. For centuries people from many different cultures and societies have tau ght principles of how to solve moral and ethical proble ms through storytelling (Wilson, 2008, Iseke 2013). Storytelling is a globally practiced approach to teaching and learning that is shared across all cultures and can be used an avenue that allows learners to leverage fun ds of knowledge (Moll et al.,1992) from their commu nities. Stories and storytelling provides a pathway for learners to make deep connections between funds of kno wledge and learned knowledge in order to see complex socio-scientific problems more comprehensively. Further, research and practice on storytelling pedagogy advances educational equity generally, as centering and affirming the lived experiences of diverse student populations challeng es both dominant educational narratives (Ladson-Bil lings, 2016; Solorzano and Yosso, 2016) and colorblind educational policies which have been found to maintain white supremacy (Bradbury, 2020; Gillborn, 2013, 2016).

Learning Environment Design The COVID-19 pandemic is a complex socio-scientific  problem. It is also a global challenge that impact s all everyday aspects of human life and has altered the social fabric of nations and local communities. This pandemic has illuminated inequities among and within communities with marginalized communities, in particular, having been impacted most acutely (Katamneni, 2020). The complexity of this problem requires nations to work together with local communities on issues of science, economics, socio-cultural norms and equity (Pirouz et al., 2020). Given the importance and complexities of thi s concern, we targeted COVID-19 to examine student understanding of the interconnectedness between the science (What is COVID-19?), the local context (How does COVID-19 impact community?) and equity (How does COVID-19 impact different members of the community?). We used the authoring of comic books as an approach  to engage the youth from three different nations (United States, India and Mexico) in understanding the thre e questions mentioned above. The youth (13-18 years  old) conceptualized stories about how the virus impacts their local communities. This presentation describes the design of a program to help students understand the intersections between science, community and equity. We also take this opportunity to describe how the students approached their work and presented their stories/comic books, which illustrated their understanding of how a complex science problem (COVID-19) impacted their community and different socio-cultural groups within their community. Given COVID-19 restrictions, we designed a virtual ICLS2022 Proceedings 2092 © ISLS program on Zoom that would take place three days a week, two hours each day over the course of three w eeks. The program was voluntary and one of the measures o f engagement was the extent to which students were punctual and actively engaged through the Zoom platform (video and chat) during the two-hour sessions. Program Activities We devised and utilized three main goals, each with  an associated question in student friendly termino logy, to frame our program design and learning content, such that students were constantly thinking about one or more of the following: Goal 1. Complex systems thinking: Ho w can I make sense of, and address, complex problem s? Goal 2. Community based science: How do scientific phenomena impact different communities, and in what actions can/do communities take in order to respond to scientific phenomena? Goal 3. Equity: How does inequity further complicate a complex problem, and what can be done to address inequity in order to better addr ess the complex problem? During week one we focused on getting to know each other in terms of identity, sharing and discussing perceptions of COVID-19, thinking about communities that are being impacted inequitably due  to COVID-19, and identifying student-generated topics for graphic novels. During week two we focused on t he elements of story-telling and graphic novel design,  which included creating storyboards, giving and re ceiving feedback on storyboards, learning about the various design features comic artists use to tell a story visually, and beginning to design comics using Pixton software or hand-drawing to create graphic novels. During week three we focused on providing students with feedback on storylines and graphics, and learning more about how graphic novels can impact sociopolitical agendas. And on th e final day of programming, students presented thei r completed graphic novels to invited guests, including parents, students’ school administrators, and faculty from SUNY at Buffalo. Students were organized into three teams, such that there were students from different countries in each team and discussion in the teams were led by a member of our research team.

## Analysis and Findings

Students presented various stories as a part of their graphic novels. The stories included how students thought about the intersections between science (what is COVID-19?), community (how does COVID-19 impact their community?) and equity (How does COVID-19 impact different members of the community?). For example, in a story created by student A (Figure 1), the student highlights dilemmas the scientific community is facing in India. In the hand drawn comic below, the student shows as a part of the story how since drug trials cost money and Indian medical scientists have less funding than western medical scientists, it is difficult for Indian scientists to try out new treatments for COVID-19. The student also shows the everyday life challenges faced by medical practitioners in India to treat COVID-19 patients. Developing comics in a learning environment not only fosters student engagement, but also offers an alternative method for assessing student comprehension of concepts and systems. Creating a comic offers students an avenue for applying systems thinking to a scientific concept in a creative and personally relevant way, as students can use graphic elements and a storyline of their own choosing to articulate learned themes and academic content. Comics additionally enable students to express and connect their identities to what they know, what they have learned, and what is important to them in artistic form.
