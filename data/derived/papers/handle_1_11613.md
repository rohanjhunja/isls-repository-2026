# Long and Winding Road to Coconstructing Knowledge in Design-Based Research Practice Partnerships

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Design-based research-practice partnerships (RPPs) bridge research and practice through shared design and knowledge co-construction. This paper examines two frameworks – design cycles and boundary crossing – while arguing they often understate inherent conflicts. Analysis of RPP data reveals how conflicts are minimized, sometimes marginalizing perspectives. Two additional frameworks – the narrative fiction act and the crossroad – are introduced as metaphors to understand discord's transformative potential in strengthening collaboration and promoting inclusive knowledge co-construction in design-based RPPs.

Objectives Research-practice partnerships (RPPs) are increasingly gaining traction within academic and educational systems (e.g., Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Farrell et al., 2021), aiming to foster ecosystems that bridge research,  practice, policy, and community. Within R PPs, design-based implementation research (DBIR; e.g., Fishman et al., 2013; Penuel & Potvin, 2021) holds particular promise for  cultivating collaborative spaces and enhancing the  co-construction of professional knowledge. Following r esearch that problematizes RPP ideals and highlight s issues of equity, power imbalance and role negotiation (e. g., Bevan et al., 2019; Marshal et al., 2021; Tanks leya & Estrada, 2022; Severance et al., 2014; Sexton et al., 2021), this paper critically examines two central frameworks commonly emphasized in DBIR: design cycles and boundary crossing. Design cycles encompass phases of shared learning o f concepts, joint design and planning, implementation and documentation of practices, and collective interpretation and refinement (e.g., Pen uel & Gallagher, 2017). Boundary crossing describes how p articipants navigate differing expectations and norms (e.g., Akkerman & Bakker, 2011; Coburn et al., 2013; Penue l et al., 2015). I argue that while design cycles p rovide structure and focus, and boundary crossing encourag es productive practices, both frameworks u nderemphasize the conflictual nature inherent in partnerships. I discuss reasons for this gap and propose additional  metaphors that might better facilitate recognition and resolution of the discords intrinsic to RPPs.

## Theoretical perspectives

Researchers describe RPPs as deliberate, sustained relationships between researchers and practitioners addressing systemic problems of practice (e.g., Farrell et al., 2021). While partnership models vary, they usuall y share five principles: long-term colla borations; focus on educational improvement; engage ment with research; diverse expertise; and shifted power relations (e.g., Cobur n et al., 2013; Coburn & Penuel, 2016).  Within RPPs, designbased implementation research aims to create spaces  for joint work  in which participants negotiate and build innovative designs (Penuel et al., 2011; Fishman et  al., 2013). DBIR includes four core principles: i.  focus on persistent problems of practice from multiple stakeholders' perspectives ii. commitment to iterative, collaborative design processes iii. concern with developing theory related to classroom learning and implementation iv. concern with capacity building for sustaining systemic change. The second principle, iterative design processes, relates to a key concept in RPP literature: boundary crossing. I expand on both in the following. Iterative design cycles provide structure for compl ex collaborations between diverse partners (e.g., Penuel et al., 2011; Fishman et al., 2013). Drawing  from DBR traditions, these cycles allow participan ts tonegotiate and work toward tangible outcomes (Brown,  1992), creating opportunities for participants to express their individual knowledge and expertise. In design-based RPPs, design cycles function as a litmus test  for the partnership itself (Zala-Mezö & Datnow, 2024). Acknowledging RPP complexity, Penuel and colleagues  (2015) conceptualize RPPs as "joint work at boundaries" (see also Akkerman & Bakker, 2011; Coburn et al., 2013; Farrell et al., 2022). This requires researchers and practi tioners to co-construct goals and navigate traditional domain boundaries. Successful boundary crossing is enabled by i ndividuals committed to bridging cultural differenc es, power imbalances, and knowledge disparities.

## Methods

To illustrate complexities in design-based RPPs, I examine a partnership co-designing dialogic instructional units for language arts in Israeli elementary classrooms (Barak & Lefstein, 2022; Barak, 2024). The design team included eight language arts teachers, three instructional coaches, two senior coaches, and four researchers. Over one academic year, nine design cycles were completed in 20 team sessions (totaling 70 hours), each involving six joint activities emphasizing boundary work.  Researchers introduced dialogic pedagogy principles  developed by leading scholars (e.g., Alexander, 2020; Michaels &  O'Connor, 2015). While the design cycle allowed te achers to express their own concepts, some interpretations conflicted with researchers' frameworks. Despite discussions about these conflicts, tensions remained largely unresolved. I analyzed team session data using Horn and Garner's

(2022) definition of teachers' concepts as short na rratives describing teaching practices with associa ted contexts

and consequences. Analysis involved identifying epi sodes where teachers articulated concepts of dialog ic pedagogy, transcribing these episodes, developing c odes to categorize expressions, and identifying dis tinct concepts of dialogic pedagogy.

## Findings

I identified seven distinct teacher concepts  of dialogic pedagogy: (1) Getting students to talk; (2) Encouraging students to talk among themselves; (3) Developing s peaking skills; (4) Cultivating polite discussion n orms; (5) Students arguing their points of view; (6) Creating  interpersonal encounters; and (7) Encouragin g critical engagement. Concepts 1 –4 significantly conflicted with researchers' framew orks, while concepts 5 –7 aligned more closely.

Analysis of the design cycles reveals a pattern of conceptual conflicts throughout the year-long partnership. The earliest sessions (1-2) in September-October showed three conflicting concepts during th e first cycle, arising across all four key activities.  This conflict intensified during sessions 3-6 (October-November), when the second and third design cycles exhibited t he highest number of conceptual conflicts (4), dist ributed across all activities. As the partnership progressed to sessions 7-10 (December-January), the fourth and fifth cycles showed fewer conflicts (2), appearing in three acti vities. This declining trend continued in sessions 11-17 (February-May), where the sixth through ninth cycles maintained two conflicting concepts across three activities. By the final sessions 18-20 (May-June), devoted to reflection and refinement, only one conceptual conflict remained. This suggests a gradual reduction in conceptual tensions over time, possibly indicating either increased alignment between teachers and researchers or the g radual marginalization of divergent teacher perspec tives as the designed artifacts took their final form.  While the collaborative process allowed participant s to express conflicting views, the team generally bypassed pote ntial tensions. Only in retrospect did the conflict ual nature become apparent. Despite frequent boundary-crossing practices, these did not fa cilitate real-time recognition of gaps between participants. The team successfully pr oduced nine instructional units, however – importantly – teachers' conflicting concepts were largely absent from these artifacts.

## Discussion and scholarly significance

Design-based RPPs require participants to reconcile differ ing norms, expectations, and interests. While desig n cycles and boundary-crossing metaphors are valuable, they may present a n overly optimistic view of collaboration. The findings suggest that co-constructing knowledge can falter when perspectives are marginalized. As others have highlighted (e.g., Bevan et al., 2019; Tanksleya & Estrada, 2022; Severance et al., 2014), the RPP field would benefit from understanding the deep div ides between practition ers and researchers.  For RPPs to succeed, it's crucial to address inherent conflicts  between different perspectives. I propose two conceptual frameworks to support this: (1) viewing knowledge co-construction as a narrative fiction act and (2) conceptualizing resolution of differences as occurring at crossroads. Syd Field's (1979) "narrative fiction act" metaphor  illuminates conflict resolution in RPPs. Field's paradigm frames narrative as co-creation between storyteller and audience, where te nsion drives story development. The narrative fiction act hinges on es tablishing conflict, building toward climactic tensio n, and reaching resolution —mirroring how RPPs must navigate disagreements to a chieve breakthroughs. Just as storytellers use conflict to deepen character development, RPPs can leverage divergent perspectives to strengthen collaborative understanding and innovative solution s. The complementary "crossroad" metaphor (Turner, 196 9) captures the junction where diverse perspectives meet. As a liminal space, the crossroad offers resolution potential through conscious negotiation. Here, participants m ust confront differences, reflect on shared goals, and make deliberate decisions about moving forward.

Unlike passive conflict resolution, these metaphors emphasize intentional, collective effort to chart paths aligned with partnership goals and community needs. Both fr ameworks view conflict resolution as transformative  and necessary in research-practice partnerships, requiring active engagement rather than  avoidance or minimization of tensions.
