# Future Selves in STEM: An Epistemic Network Analysis of Past and Present STEM Minority Program Participants

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
STEM minority participation programs have been widely implemented in higher education institutions to diversify and expand the U.S. STEM workforce. Research highlights the potential of targeted exploration of STEM roles and careers, and of intentional reflection on the self in relation to STEM (identity exploration). This work examines how engagement in a federal-funded STEM minority participation program shaped these processes in current students and alumni. Epistemic Network Analysis visualized connections between inductive identity themes in interviews, highlighting differences between interviewees at different stages of their careers and identity work: students discussed action-taking as they explored less-certain possible future STEM roles, while alumni integrated more diverse and holistic identity facets when conceptualizing their futures. Results offer insights into the identity-related impacts of STEM minority participation programs and longer-term trajectories of minoritized students working toward STEM majors and careers.

Identity and STEM participation Shortages in the science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) workforce have been a persistent issue in the U.S., exacerbated by a representation gap in  terms of minority participation (Camilli & Hira, 2 019). Programming is needed to encourage URM youth to declare STEM majors and work towards degree completion. To address this, STEM minority initiatives that foster  facets of identity exploration have been highlight ed as a tool to broaden participation in STEM education. Research o n STEM minority participation emphasizes identity w ork as supportive of student self-efficacy (Syed et al., 2 019), self-authorship (Morton & Parsons, 2018), and motivation to persist in STEM (Hernandez et al., 2013). Reflection on future possible selves in STEM, or the roles and characteristics an individual hopes or expects to one day embody (M arkus & Nurius, 1986) has been shown to play a key role in student’s STEM learning, academic development, and career certainty (Syed et al., 2019; Verdin et al., 2018). Identity exploration (Flum & Kaplan, 2006) is groun ded in the idea that self-reflection in one moment is meaningfully built upon prior experiences and conceptualizations of self. Learners craft future possible selves as an awareness of what they might, would like, or fear o f becoming based on an understanding of current and  past roles (Markus & Nurius, 1986). Despite affirmations of the value of identity-focused interventions on institutional outcomes (Crawford et al., 2018), identity exploration research has found mixed results. While large-scale studies on the impact of university STEM programming connect current scie nce identity (feeling part of the scientific commun ity) to beneficial outcomes such as goal-setting and academic achievement, others suggest having a current science identity and values may not be predictive of STEM career suc cess beyond four years post-graduation (Estrada et al., 2018). This disconnection could be representative of resea rch that emphasizes reflection on the “current self ” as a science community member, but not on a future possible self in STEM. Existing longitudinal studies of URM STEM identity exploration also tend to be survey-based (Habig et al., 2020), which may not elucidate how URM participants engage in identity exploration at different stages. This work adds to knowledge about long-term outcomes of higher education STEM minority programming through an examination of  how participants at different stages of their STEM trajectories engage in identity exploration around future possible selves. The research question asks, “What patterns of identity exploration emerged when students and alumni discussed their future possible selves in STEM?”

## Research methods

This work is a part of an NSF-funded project through the Louis Stokes Alliance for Minority Participation (LSAMP), which supports underrepresented students earning de grees in STEM. We examined individual interview dat a with eleven current and ten former LSAMP participants. T he sample represents four LSAMP participating insti tutions, ICLS2022 Proceedings 1385 © ISLS various STEM disciplines, and different stages within the STEM pipeline. Semi-structured interviews were conducted via zoom where participants discussed identity exploration processes as they related to their future possible selves as they participated in LSAMP programming. A priori themes/codes were selected using an inductive thematic analysis; however, codes were broadly framed based on the theoretical positioning of identity exploration and future possible selves research discussed above (See Table 1).

## Codes Related to LSAMP Participants’ Future Selves in STEM

Code Definition

1. Career Change Expected change in STEM major or career

2. Current Interest Describing liking or enjoyment of a STEM topic

3. Current Valuing How a topic, domain, etc. is personally valuable

4. Future Career Field Expected field or discipline in which participant may work

## 5. Future Career Role Expected role or position which participant may hold

6. Future Skills Used Expected skills to have and/or use in future

7. Less Certain Future Lack of certainty or specificity in discussions of the future

8. More Certain Future Expressing certainty and/or specificity in future career, role, field, or skills

9. Role Models Describing individuals who inspire future major or career choices

10. Self-definitions Descriptions of current identity labels and features; self-concept

11. Self-efficacy Expressing confidence in one’s ability to achieve desired results or goals

12. Taking Actions Describing actions or steps taken to achieve desired results or goals

Once identified, codes were deductively applied to the interview data, with each sentence coded for occurrence (1) or non-occurrence (0). Interrater ag reement was reached using social moderation (Herren kohl & Cornelius, 2013). To understand differences in the ways that current LSAMP students and alumni concept ualized future possible selves, we applied epistemic networ k analysis (ENA) (Shaffer, 2017), a quantitative et hnographic technique used to model the relationships between c odes in discourse. ENA generates network visualizations of the co-occurrence of codes within  a moving stanza window, which means that code connect ions are determined and measured when they co-occur in the same sentence or a few sentences from each other (in this case 3 sentences). To generate the epistemic networks, units of analysis were set as the alumni and student groups, subset by each participant. Conversations were segmented by each interviewee and any breaks (i.e., off-topic discussion) in their interview. Nonparametric Mann-Whitney U tests were conducted t o test whether differences between student and alum ni discourse were statistically significant. Network findings are supplemented with qualitative findings.

## Results and discussion

The epistemic network model had strong goodness of fit across the first (0.97 Pearson, 0.97 Spearman) and second dimensions (0.99 Pearson, 0.99 Spearman). ## Conclusion and implications

Results highlight the distinct discourse patterns a nd conceptual connections that emerged as current a nd former LSAMP participants discussed their future identities in STEM. While students were more likely to describe the current actions they were taking in relation to their exploration of more diverse and nebulous STEM interests, roles, and topics, alumni reflections revealed more holistic discussio ns of self that diverged into unique career and con text-specific trajectories. As alumni move further along their increasingly unique STEM trajectories post-graduation, whole-group claims may start to obscure their socio-culturally distinct yet contextually valid patterns of growth and self-reflection they enact over time. This work contributes to research on STEM identity and minority participation by highlighting patterns of connection-making enacted by past and c urrent participation and suggesting how and when in dividual trajectories might start to diverge post-graduation. Future work may extend this exploratory applicati on of ENA to larger student and alumni datasets and secondary sources to understand identity exploration with greater nuance.
