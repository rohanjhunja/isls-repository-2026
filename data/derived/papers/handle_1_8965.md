# Designing to Support Teacher Reflection on Science Classroom Discourse: Insights From Implementation

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper informs designs for teacher learning and reflection that advance equitable discourse practices in secondary science classrooms. We share design insights from the initial iteration of a design-based research project, ClassInSight, that supports teachers’ facilitation of dialogic-discourse practices in science classrooms through guided reflection on classroom discourse visualizations. We analyze transcripts of reflection sessions of the participants and ask: How did teacher-researcher dyads interact with the initial design prototypes of classroom discourse data visualization and reflection practices? How were reflections on data visualizations constrained by design? We found that teachers and researchers did not share an understanding of classroom discourse moves and that teacher reflections did not connect explicitly to issues of equity in the classroom. The findings show the need to design for shared meaning-making between teachers and researchers on classroom discourse categories and make visible the ways they conceptualize equity in science classroom discourse practices.

Design-Based Research to Improve Science Classroom Discourse Practices Several studies have highlighted a systematic pattern of classroom dialogue that predominantly serve c hildren from minoritized background, that is, a culture of low expectations, whereby the quality and intellective rigor of classroom talk requires low cognitive demand of engagement from students (McNeill & Pimentell, 2010; Kelly, 2008). Research has shown that when teachers lead students in discu ssions that externalize their reasoning and thinkin g, students benefit in terms of steep increases in learning (Resnick et al., 2015; Clarke et al., 2016; Alexander, 2018). Our work takes the view that equity (and inequity) can take shape in micro-level interactions in-classroom dialogue, and teachers play a critical role in constructing an intellective space that provides robust opportunities to learn science. It is for these reasons that we sought to design te acher professional development to support teachers in reflecting on how opportunities to learn are structured in classroom discussions that teachers facilitate, and planning on how to utilize their pedagogical tools to support students’ sense-making in science. We view teacher learning as a key opportunity space to make rich discourse in science classrooms more accessible. We conjecture that well-designed classroom discourse data representations and reflec tion practices can help teachers critically examine  and improve their own classroom discourse practices, and have been designing an online application, ClassInSight, that visualizes science classroom discourse data through multiple representations, (Gomoll et al., 2021), as well as reflection practices for teachers and researchers to engage in shared meaning-making, goal setting, and pedagogical refinement with the discourse data (Schön, 1983).

Two threads of existing research frame our designs.  First, we draw from research on dialogic science c lassroom discourse that has been shown to improve learning (Clarke et al., 2015). These discourse practices pro mote shared reasoning, and include types of talk, or what we here on call discourse categories, by students and teachers that surface rationales for claims, build on existing claims mad e by others, and questioning that connects content knowledge to prior student knowledge (Michaels et al., 2010). We are designing classroom discourse data visualizations to represent student and teacher speech types that are consequential to science learning and equity. Second, following the literature on teacher reflection and learning (Schön, 1983), w e conjecture that teachers improve their pedagogy w hen they engage in reflection-on-action that question, rumin ate on, and reason with their classroom practices (Clarke et al., 2020). We situate such teacher learning in their practice contexts, technological resources, and interactions with others, including the researchers (Korthagen, 2010). While our larger equity goal i s to make discourse-rich science classrooms more ac cessible to students from minoritized backgrounds, here, we mak e theoretical contributions to the processes and designs for teacher learning toward these educational outcomes.

Using a design-based research methodology, we seek to contribute to theories and practices that reflec t our core learning goals and conjectures by observing the use of designs that embody these conjectures “in the wild” (Lee et al., 2022). This paper seeks to refine theories and desi gns that support teacher reflection on classroom di scourse data. More specifically, we ask: How did teacher-researcher dyads interact with the initial design prototypes of classroom discourse data visualization and reflection practic es? How were reflections on data visualizations con strained by design?

## Study Design and Background

The present analysis is situated within a larger design-based research project between three universities and two school districts in southern California which serve lingui stically and ethnically diverse student bodies. Cla ssInSight shows visualizations constructed from discourse categories informed by the literature, such as  Teacher and Student Close Ended Statements and Questions, Teacher and Student Open Ended Statements and Questions, Teacher Lecture, Turn Taking Facilitation,  Metacognitive Modelling Questions, and Student Explanation. Science lessons are recorded on a monthly basis, transcribed, with talk turns by te achers and students coded as one of the discourse c ategories. Researchers then shared visualizations of this data with the teachers, before entering into a one hour reflection session with them.

## Participants and Data Sources

The participants of this study were 12 middle and h igh school science teachers. For this analysis, we analyzed data from 12 reflection sessions conducted on video conf erencing software (audio recording transcripts and screencasts) from the first round of reflection sessions that immediately followed the research team sharing the first prototype of the classroom data visualizations.

Analysis In order to understand how teacher-researcher dyads were interacting with the designs for teacher learning and related constraints, we analyzed the interaction between te achers and researchers with the initial data visual ization and reflection protocol. Transcripts and videos from initial reflection sessions were analyzed to examine how teachers and researchers sensemaking of the data and articulate pedagogical actions. Using the constant comparative  method (Glaser and Strauss, 2017), we surfaced descriptive themes that paid attention to the constraints the teacher-researcher dyads experienced in accomplishing these steps to inform improvements in our designs and theoretical conjectures.

## Findings

Developing Shared Understandings of Talk Category Definitions In many reflection sessions, teacher-researcher dyads undertook a process of developing shared understandings of the discourse categories that were core to the data vis ualizations. Initially, the teachers' interpretations of the discourse categories often did not align with the theoretical  definitions, which the researchers based their own  design and analyses on. Two processes emerged in the way that teachers worked with the researchers to construct m eaning for the different talk categories: developing a concept ual understanding of the talk categories, and conne cting them to prior experiences.

First, teachers sought to develop conceptual unders tandings of the different discourse categories that  were represented in the data visualizations. As a conseq uence, teachers reported challenges in determining what kind of talk would fall into what particular talk type. In such instances, they turned to the researchers to e xplain the researcher’s conceptualization of these discourse categories. (Excerpt 1, [Evan]) Here, Evan asks for a clarification of not only the  definition of the discourse categories. They additionally convey a lack of shared understanding about the differences between two of the discourse categories in the visualization. Second, in making sense of the discourse categories, teachers related the different categories to thei r own practices. We saw participants connect discourse ca tegories like “open ended questions” to terminology  that their schools used, in this case, Inquiry-Based questions. Tom, a high school teacher, shares how some discourse categories are similar to the ones he uses in school Instead of open ended questions…[we use] inquiry based questions or inquiry modeling... So we use terms like that and we get instructions. So in that sense it could be helpful in terms of helping a teacher move towards a, a, the definition that the school uses. (Excerpt 2. Beyond developing an understanding of discourse categories by identifying instances in which they embody these talk moves, the teacher suggested that using categories like “inquiry-based  or inquiry modeling” might be helpful in scaffolding teacher understanding as it relates to the definition that schools generally use. We see the distance between the teachers’ meaning of the discourse category and the researchers’ meaning as a constraint of the design for teacher sense-making.

## Tethering Discussion Goals to Science Learning Outcomes and Equity

The initial reflection sessions typically led to te acher goal setting on classroom discourse that was unconnected to concerns of equity in science learning. Teachers set goals that were specifically related to characteristics of classroom discourse. However, these goals were not explicitly  tied to broader visions for equity in science lear ning. When prompted to set goals for upcoming classes, the most common response by teachers was to state that they would like to increase student talk and reduce how much they t alk. Even when teachers used specific discourse cat egories like “open ended” or “meta-cognitive modeling” to formulate their goals, it was challenging for the researcher to support teachers in tethering their goals to equity or spec ific student learning outcomes or equity. The follo wing interaction between a researcher and Tom, a high school chemistry teacher, shows how a broad prompt by the researcher asking them to articulate a goal moving forward led to a response by Tom that included a specific goal for classroom discourse but was not tethered to particular science learning outcomes, or equity goals: Researcher: goals you might set or other things that you might want to keep in mind as we're continuin g to refine the design?

Tom: Well, the same thing I said last time[earlier in the session]. I mean… having students participate in the discussion is a goal. So having more student partic ipation… I tend to think that if the number [of stu dents talk]  goes up, that's awesome. (Excerpt 3 [Tom]) In Excerpt 3, we see a teacher respond to the researcher's questions about goal setting. Along with the researcher, they formulate a goal to have more students participate in discussion. Then they provide the rationale for why they think that having more participation should be the goal b y tapping into their belief of why it was good. The  consequent designed interactions between the researcher and th e teacher failed to create space to build on and co nnect the mentioned goals to student science learning outcomes and equity.

## Discussion and Implications

Shared Meaning Making of Talk Categories During reflection sessions, reflection dyads sought to build a shared understanding of the discourse categories. There was a dissonance between the discourse category codes as the research team understood them primarily through the literature (Clarke et al., 2015) and the ways teachers understood the language of the discourse categories, which were primarily informed by their practice. This constrai nt speaks to the challenge of leveraging theory and  practice in a collaborative Design-Based Research where researchers work with practitioners in the field. ICLS2022 Proceedings 1639 © ISLS In designing for representations of classroom data and reflection practices, we suggest that there is a need to pay further attention to teacher voices and build o n their experiential knowledge while planning the p rocesses of creating and naming these categories. This would allow teachers to play a more active role in the collaborative process of design-based research by bringing in their rich experiences; additionally, it might also allow teachers to leverage their knowledge better to conceptualize their practices to address the project’s goals. For the field, it reaffirms the need to be intentional and attentive to create space for dialogue between the theoretical knowledge of researchers and the practical and experiential knowledge of participants. As a research team, we revised the constructions of the codes to build upon the teachers’ experiential knowledge in future iterations. Scaffolding Thinking about Equity and Learning Outcomes ClassInSight aims to work with teachers to reflect, identify, plan, and implement equitable discourse practices for their science classrooms. Hence, it is important that teachers make explicit connections between their classroom discourse and students' science learning outcomes for all the ir students in order to work towards equity. We see  through the findings that teachers are making goals based on increasing participation in their classrooms. However, the researcherteacher dyads are unable to extend teacher goals to explicitly connect to student science learning outcomes and equity. This is especially important as, as a research team, we are ultimately engaging teachers in a process of developing practice-based theory about equitable discussion in  their science classrooms. In response, as a design  team, we included further systematic probes into our reflect ion protocol to work with teachers to explicitly co nnect their discussion goals with student learning outcomes in science classrooms. Additionally, scaffolding teachers’ reflection and goal setting with a mind towards equity must en sure that teachers have the opportunities to make s ense of how their actions and decisions for talk in classrooms are linked to creating equitable spaces for all students to learn.
