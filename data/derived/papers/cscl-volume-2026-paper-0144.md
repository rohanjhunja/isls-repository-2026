# Novel Figurative Language to Identify Promising Ideas in Knowledge Building Scientific Discourse

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research explored grade 4 students ’ novel figurative language to identify promising ideas in knowledge building community discourse. A mixed-method approach— semantic analysis using Word2Vec and qualitative coding—was adopted for the analysis. Results showed figurative language use involved conceptual reframing across science and everyday life domains and embodied perspective-taking. Our next step is to incorporate similarity to resource material and investigate how ideas containing figurative language supports the community discourse. Figurative language and creativity in scientific discourse Figurative language, such as metaphors, play a crucial role in understanding and communicating science concepts (Cameron, 2002), through conceptual mapping of a familiar source domain onto a more complex, abstract target domain (Lakoff & Johnson, 1980). C reating associative connections between remote concepts has been characterized as a capacity for creativ e thinking (Mednick, 1962). C omputational and neuroimaging studies confirmed a strong correlation between semantic distance in associative word pairs and creativity (Beaty & Johnson, 2021; Liu et al., 2020). Further, a recent study reported that semantic distance between mapped concepts aligns with creativity ratings in metaphors (Yang et al., 2025). Existing evidence is largely derived from elicited data; s emantic distance may serve as a quantifiable indicator of creative metaphor and ideation in naturally occurring discourse. Studies report low levels of metaphor production in the writing of elementary school students (Gardner et al., 1975; Thomas et al., 2025), particularly novel metaphors (Pollio & Pollio, 1974). Novelty (e.g., originality, uniqueness) and appropriateness (e.g., usefulness, value) are two qualities of creativity repeatedly discussed in literature (e.g., Runco & Jaeger, 2012). In knowledge building communities, theory development requires drawing on one’s experience to map the familiar with complex, abstract, theoretical, and distant concepts and create new explanations and accounts. From a socio-cognitive perspective, students are dealing with difficult-to-understand concepts in a community context, trying to communicate to peers. In such contexts, students this age and younger have demonstrated the capacity to exceed curriculum expectations, engaging in metadiscourse and demonstrating significant vocabulary development (Khanlari et al., 2019; Resendes et al., 2015). These advances require sociocultural creativity (Glăveanu, 2012): novel conceptual and linguistic affordances are created through social judgement and acceptance (Amabile, 1988) of their value and usefulness to the group. This process is inherently cyclical, as new language develops through divergent alternatives and convergence through adaptation to community norms (Piccardo, 2017). Student discourse is captured in  Knowledge Forum technology built to support Knowledge Building. Theory-building epistemic markers (e.g., “my theory,” “a better theory,” “new information,” “this theory cannot explain,” “I need to understand,” “putting our knowledge together”) may foster novel figurative language despite its rarity in grade 4. W e conducted the current pilot data analysis to explore  the potential of figurative language serving as means to identify  promising ideas, asking the following research question: C an novel figurative language serve as means to identify promising ideas with epistemic value to the community in knowledge building community discourse?

The data source was 132 Knowledge Forum archived notes on light and optics, produced by 23 grade 4 students over three months.  We adopted a mixed-methods approach for data analysis: semantic analysis and qualitative coding. First, a  semantic analysis was conducted using Word2Vec, a neural network –based natural language processing technique that represents words as continuous vectors in a high-dimensional space (Mikolov et al., 2013). This enabled the calculation of cosine similarity between all noun combinations within each sentence unit in notes with theory-building epistemic markers to measure their semantic relatedness on nouns, with values ranging from 0 (semantically close) to 1 (semantically distant). Pairs containing overly general words (e.g., thing, stuff), c ompound nouns, noun phrases, and pairs without syntactic  relation were excluded. In addition, we excluded sentences that appeared to be repetition of figurative language used in source material. Since resource 752 material was not available, we could not check semantic relatedness to it, so this procedure may not fully rule out its influence. This limitation  will be addressed in future research. Procedures resulted in a total of 320 pairs included for qualitative analysis. Next, the first author, with extensive experience in English writing and Knowledge Building, examined the pairs and originating sentence units  to identify students’ figurative language use and open code contributions for use of figurative devices, as shown in Table 1. In this study, c reative ideas were defined as “unique contributions that create epistemic value to the community beyond scientific content.” Table 1 shows five figurative expressions (simile, personification) identified in the discourse, which distributed across the semantic distance scores ranging from 0.22 to 0.61. There were seven additional instances of figurative language that were eliminated due to our effort to exclude repetition of figurative language used in source material. All instances suggest promising input to community discussion. Similes involved conceptual reframing— mapping science concepts onto the everyday life domain. Personification reflected students’ perspective-taking through embodiment.

This pilot analysis explored grade 4 students ’ novel figurative language use to identify promising ideas in knowledge building communit y discourse. In the current dataset, novel figurative language use, particularly metaphor production, was rare in written scientific  discourse of this age group. Some instances of figurative language were excluded from the current analysis because they may have been derived from source materia l. However, the fact that students reused figurative expressions from source material suggests that the borrowed figurative framing supported their discourse.

Ideas co-occurring with figurative language were distinct from questions and explanations seen in standard discourse with literal language use.  T he results suggest that s tudents’ figurative language use may support them to create a conceptual link across different domains. Similes were used to create authentic, real-life connections across science and everyday concepts, diversifying students’ ideas. P ersonifications may have enabled the students to take an anthropomorphic approach to objectifying, simulating, and discussing complex, abstract concepts.

The methodological implication is that s emantic distance between noun pairs may serve as an indicator of conceptual distance students map through similes and metaphors. However, the  limitation of Word2Vec in handling polysemous words (e.g., light) will need to be addressed in future research. The current findings provide only preliminary results of a pilot investigation. As our next step, we plan to expand our analysis to other community datasets, incorporate  similarity to resource material, and investigate how ideas that co-occur with figurative language support community discourse.
