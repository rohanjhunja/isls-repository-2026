# Examining How Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI) Professional Development Influences Struggling Students’ Mathematical Problem Solving

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
When students solve complex mathematical problems by themselves, many will experience and encounter difficulties, struggles, and tensions. Existing research provides little guidance on how teachers might productively respond to students’ various struggles. Using the video data from the second year of Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI, a professional development program) and comparison classrooms, this study examines the effects of CGI on struggling students’ problem solving and investigates the mediation effects of enacted instructional practices on students’ problem solving. Struggling students in the CGI classroom had higher problem-solving scores than their counterparts in the comparison classrooms. The mediation analyses showed that the two instructional practices (for student agency support and for extending student thinking) had a positive and significant impact on struggling students’ problem solving. While the other two instructional practices showed a negative and nonsignificant impact.

## Introduction

Primary grade students often make errors and get mired in struggles during problem-solving processes. Struggle in problem-solving has not always been seen positively. Some early researchers saw struggle as a sign that students had failed the mathematical tasks, and they perceived struggle as something that needed to be overcome and to be avoided (Brown & VanLehn, 1982; Radatz, 1980).  Hie bert and Grouws (2007) argued that struggle does no t mean that students are undergoing needless frustration or extreme levels of challenges caused by nonsensical or overly difficult problems; instead, they argued that certain kinds of struggle indicate that students are expending intellectual efforts to make sense of mathematics and to figure out something that is not immediately overt. Their interpretation of struggle reconfirmed the importan ce of struggle in mathematical problem solving; tha t is, the process of struggle is essential for constructing deep mathematical understanding. Moreover, students engaged in productive struggle with meaningful mathematics are  experiencing math as more than procedures and corr ect answers (SanGiovanni et al., 2020).

The significance of struggle has been recognized since the time of Dewey, who dedicated much attention to the importance of struggle. Deep subject knowled ge is “the fruit of the undertakings that transform  a problematic situation into a resolved one” (Dewey, 1926, p. 242-243). In developing deep knowledge, students inevitably encounter “some perplexity, confusion, or doubt” (Dewey, 1910, p.12). Struggle provides instructional opportunities through which students candelve more deeply into the mathematical structure of problems and the relationships among multiple mathematical ideas and  concepts (Baker et al., 2020; NCTM, 2014). Though the importance of struggle in problem solving has been investigated, the specifics of how teachers might m ore effectively respond to students’ struggles and support students’ productive mathematical learning remain less well understood. Even less is known about the kinds of professional development programs that could increase teachers’ awareness of their students’ struggles and help tea chers capitalize on those struggles in their mathem atical teaching.

This study is embedded in the CGI setting, a profes sional development (PD) program based on the research of students’ mathematical thinking. Teachers who have taken the CGI PD use their students’ thinking to inform and to guide their instruction (Carpenter et  al., 1989; Carpenter et al., 1996). The purposes o f this study are to look at the influence of CGI on struggling s tudents’ problem solving and to investigate paths a nd mechanisms for how interventions on teacher PD would promote students’ engagement in problem-solving. By focusing on teachers’ enacted instructional practic es in classrooms, this study addresses the importan t and complex link between teacher learning from PD and students’ learning outcomes. The guiding research questions were the following three:

1. What are the effects of the CGI on struggling students’ problem-solving? To what extent are CGI’s

effects on struggling students’ problem-solving moderated by students’ gender, grade, and struggle levels? ICLS2022 Proceedings 599 © ISLS

2. What mediation effects of CGI on struggling students’ problem solving through teachers’ interactional

time and enacted instructional practices emerge after controlling students’ gender, grade, and struggling levels?

3. How did the teacher-student interactions look li ke in the CGI classrooms, when teachers often used

the enacted instructional practices but did not result well in students’ problem solving?

## Data sources and participants

The teacher and student samples for the current study were extracted from a large random controlled trial (RCT) study that explored how CGI influenced teachers’ be liefs and knowledge and further impacted students’ mathematical learning (Schoen et al., 2017; 2020). Twenty-two elementary schools from two Florida scho ol districts were randomly selected as the participating schools. Eleven schools were randomly assigned to receive CGI PD, and 11 schools provided their usual PD and served as the comparison schools. Over two years, volunteer teachers in the CGI schools participated in extensive CGI PD workshops. In the first year, 157 teachers from the CGI schools received a total of eight days of CGI w orkshops; and 171 teachers from the comparison scho ols followed the district’s PD plans. In the second-year stage, 156 teachers were assigned to the CGI group and 172 teachers to the comparison group. This study targeted the year-2 sample in order to investigate teachers’ support for struggling students who may have been more sophisticated in implementing CGI. All 47 teachers in the year-2 sample were videotape d for one lesson based on teacher consent and students’ parental permission. First-grade teachers accounted for 57.45%, and second-grade teachers ac counted for 42.55%. All the teachers were females. White teachers across each condition accounted for 91.67% in the CGI condition vs. 91.30% in the comparison condition.

## Data analysis

The use of video data has become widespread practice within education research studying teachers’ behaviors or social interaction in classrooms (Knoblauch et al.,  2006). In this study, video constituted the main d ata corpus; hence, the preliminary analysis step was to analyze classroom videos. Identifying mathematical struggle A mathematical struggle episode was coded when stud ents showed difficulties, confusion, or uncertainty while they endeavored to solve a problem. Multiple signs and sources were used for identifying a mathematical struggle episode. A clear indicator is students’ spoken words, such as “I do not know what to do”, “It is so hard”, “I do not know how to do the problem”, or “I need help”. Othe r sources included students’ written work, gestures, facial expressions, body movements, long pauses, and modal particles. The coding of struggle types followed the three categorizations from Warshauer (2014): Struggle to start, struggle to carry a process, and struggle to explai n and sense-make. His fourth type of struggle (expr ession of misconceptions and errors) was not considered since it could occur at any of three previous stages, and sometimes, the misconceptions are the reasons for students’ st ruggles. Thus, the categorizations of student strug gles came from one of the following: (1) confused (consciously or unconsciously) about what the task is asking and did not know how to begin and initiate the task, which was coded as struggle to start (level 2), (2) encountered difficulty or impasse in carrying out a process, using an algorithm to do the computation, or transferring information among representations, which was coded as struggle to carry out a process (level 3), (3) unable to and/or ha d difficulty in justifying their work, explaining their solving processes, giving reasons for their choice of strategies or solutions, which is coded as struggle to express and explain (level 4). Level 1 of struggle is more than one type. The assigning of levels to struggle types is based on the closeness to the success of problem solving. Developing codes of instructional practices The development of a coding manual for teacher instructional practices for struggling students was a combination of deductive and inductive approaches (Guest et al., 2011). First, a prior coding scheme of teachers’ instructional practices for mathematics struggles was developed b ased on a wide range of perspectives in the literat ure concerning teachers’ pedagogical behaviors. Meanwhile, a grounded-theory method was adopted to identif y all the other potential instructional practices that we re not found in the literature. This inductive-dedu ctive cycle allowed for building a more systematic and complete framework of teacher instructional practices for struggling students. In the end, four groups of instructional practices were identified (see Table 1). To establi sh inter-rater reliability of video coding, at least 17% of lessons were separately coded by three coders who have a background in and knowledge of CGI. The post-discussion interrater reliability for coding struggle types is 97%, and 90% for the coding of the instructional practices.

Scoring of students’ problem-solving The instrument for scoring students’ problem-solvin g performance was based on Gojak’s (2013) rubric. F or adapting this rubric to assess students’ problem-so lving performance in a CGI learning environment, tw onew dimensions—representation and explanation/communica tion, were added. Those two aspects were also highlighted in other problem-solving rubrics, for i nstance, Lopez’s (2008) holistic scales. The dimens ions for scoring struggling students’ problem solving include: Task understanding assesses whether students show a complete understanding of the task and can use and translate all the important information in the task. Use of strategies  assesses whether students can generate an efficien t and appropriate strategy by themselves and whether the using strategies would lead to a complete solution. Mathematics accuracy assesses whether students execute the computations  correctly and reach an accurate solution/answer. When computation was not required, the scoring focused exclusively on whethe r the student got an accurate solution/answer.

Representations assess whether students use models, drawings, tool s, and/or symbols to represent the task, strategies, and/or solving processes.

Explanation and communication assess whether students can clearly and coherently use mathematics language and/or notations to explain their solving strategies, thinking process, and/or answers. Inter-rater reliability for problem-solving scoring was checked. Seventeen percent of lessons were scored by three raters for each dimension. There was substantial agreement in all the scoring dimensions; Krippendorff’s alpha value was greater than 0.75 across all dimensions (Krippendorff, 2004).

## Analysis methods

Overall, this study takes a sequential explanatory mixed-methods approach (Creswell et al., 2003), wit h initial quantitative methods to examine the effects of CGI on students’ problem solving, then followed by an explanatory qualitative method to further explain the previous quantitative results. To answer RQ1, a series of linear regression models were run on students’ problem-solving scores as the outcome after adjusting their grade, gender, and st ruggling levels. To address RQ2, a two-way mediated  path analysis was conducted by using interactional time and the four types of instructional practices as th e potential mediators. Interactional time is transformed from the length of the struggle episode, which is the amount of time ICLS2022 Proceedings 601 © ISLS that teachers interact with the struggling students to help their problem solving. Path ax was to investigate the CGI effects on the mediators, and path b x was to investigate the direct effect of mediators on struggling students’ problem-solving after adjusting for students’ gende r, grade, and struggle levels. The specific indirec t effect of CGI on struggling students’ performance through int eractional time was a 1 x b 1, whereas the specific indirect effect through four instructional practices was a2 x b2, a3 x b3, a4 x b4, a5 x b5, respectively. In RQ3, a macro and in-depth interactional analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) was used to explore the teacher-student int eraction during students’ problem-solving struggles within the CGI classrooms. Interactions involving struggling students with low problem-solving scores but receiving a lot  of the selected instructional practices were chose n for this analysis. The conversations between students and teachers, as well as their actions, gestures, body movement, and eye gaze, were transcribed. Talk turns, especially in the whole class setting, were coded to examine the teachers’ attention distributions among the initial struggling students and their peers.

With one level increase in students’ struggle, their problem-solving scores would increase by 0.63 points. There are no grade differences (grade 1 vs. grade 2) or gender differences (female vs. male) in students’ problem-solving performance. These findings support the conclusion that providing teachers with CGI-based PD is an important lever for improving students’ mathematical performance when they encounter problems that elicit struggles. The path by which these effects are felt to be through increased instructional time and implementation of some instructional practices (i.e., supporting students’ agency develo pment and extending student thinking), Some instruc tional practices, such as supportive strategies that direc tly resolve students’ struggles and encouraging the ir learning through others, did not support students’ problem solving. An in-depth analysis of teacher-student interaction was used to investigate what actually happened between teachers and students and to better interpret this result. ## Discussion

The debate regarding which instructional approaches  (student-centered vs. teacher-led) are more effect ive for students’ mathematical learning continues to be intense among educators and researchers. This study, u sing the data from CGI PD, indirectly sheds some light on that debate since it investigated teachers’ instruction of students who were engaged in mathematics struggles within a problem solving and student-centered learning environment. ICLS2022 Proceedings 604 © ISLS It is important to note that not all students from this study are struggling students in the sense tha t the term is generally understood in the literature. Some students in this study could have been high performing, but they were engaged in mathematics struggles involved in very specific problems that they found difficult. Although this study did not compare the two types o f instructional approaches, results showed that the instructional practices that were more student-centered learning, such as allowing students to use str ategies that they preferred, encouraging students’ independent thinking, and directing students to use multiple strategies, etc. were positively related to students’ high performance in their problem solving. On the other hand, the instructional practices that were more on the side of teacher-exp licit instruction, including suggesting alternative  strategies, giving hints/cues, and suggesting representations a nd tools, did not support students’ performance as they struggled with problems.

The findings discussed above have important implica tions for both the design of CGI professional development and classroom instruction. First, the f indings of this study offer exciting news as well a s some insightful suggestions for improving the CGI program. Results from this study suggested that participation in the CGI program did benefit engaging students in struggles. Those benefits include increased interactional time and teachers’ use of practices that help students successfully solve the mathematical tasks, even though some of the practices are not the specific focus of the CGI wor kshops. This might suggest that CGI focusing on bui lding a conceptual model of students’ mathematics thinking has the potential for teachers to develop equitable practices in real classrooms. This indication was also confir med by Moscardini’s (2009; 2010; 2014) year attempt s to transplant the CGI program to the Scottish context, especially for students with special needs (i.e., students with learning disabilities and low-performing students).  His observation studies demonstrated that special-needs learners have the capacity to make sense of mathematics and show engagement in classroom learning activities if the teachers adopt a CGI-approach instruction. Seco nd, the results from this study help teachers recog nize that struggle is a valuable part of learning with unders tanding. Students’ various struggles are not someth ing that should be avoided; instead, they offer teachers ins tructional chances to engage students in productive mathematical learning (i.e., collective discussions of unresolved problems, devising alternative problem-solving strategies).
