# Exploring STEM Teachers’ AI Readiness, Attitudes, and Uses of Generative AI across Teaching Subjects

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the integration of AI among Taiwanese STEM teachers, revealing a shift from utilitarian tool use toward co-creative partnership. While efficiency-driven tasks currently predominate, innovative applications are emerging in interdisciplinary contexts. Disciplinary norms significantly influence adoption: mathematics teachers prioritize precision, while science and interdisciplinary teachers leverage AI for creative integration. The findings suggest that professional development could provide discipline-specific support to empower teachers as collaborative partners in AI-integrated classrooms.

Teachers in schools are widely adopting artificial intelligence (AI) technologies to improve the quality and effectiveness of their work. They have been used in teaching to help teachers analyze student performance and provide immediate support (Esiyok et al., 2024). Recently, generative AI has been shown to aid teachers in lesson preparation, for example, generating relevant teaching materials (Kim et al., 2022). It is necessary to understand teachers' AI readiness and their experiences incorporating A I technologies into their teaching practices (Miao & Cukurova, 2024).  Previous studies suggest that teachers with higher AI readiness may be more capable of deploying AI technologies to support their teaching (Luckin et al., 2022). However, Popenici and Kerr (2017) noted that teachers need to rethink AI and teachers' roles i n schools. Teachers should have a comprehensive understanding of AI and know when, where, and how to apply it, along with AI-generated data, to optimize their teaching practices (Nazaretsky et al., 2022). Previous studies have used quantitative questionnaires to investigate teachers' readiness and behavioral intentions regarding AI use (e.g., Ramazanoglu & Akın, 2024; Sanusi et al., 2024). However, while large-scale quantitative studies can provide an overview of teachers' AI readiness, they fail to fully account for the actual use and various considerations that teachers across disciplines face when using AI to prepare for teaching tasks, such as the knowledge-creation and critical-thinking processes involved i n adopting AI-generated knowledge. Therefore, this study aims to use semi-structured interviews to understand teachers' AI readiness, attitudes, and actual use of AI technologies. The research questions proposed in this study are: (1) What were the teachers' AI readiness, attitudes, and uses when using generative AI? (2) How do teachers' AI readiness, attitudes, and uses differ regarding teaching subjects?

## Method

This study us ed purposive and convenience sampling methods. We recruited STEM teachers with experience teaching in northern Taiwan for interviews in the summer of 2025. Participant selection criteria included: (1) having at least one semester of K-12 teaching experience; and (2) having experience using generative AI in school administration or teaching work. Ten  participants from K-12 public schools in northern Taiwan participated in this study. These included 3 mathematics teachers, 3 natural science teachers, 1 information technology teacher, and 3 interdisciplinary STEM teachers. They had taught for at least one semester in elementary (n = 5) and secondary (n = 5) education. The researchers adopted semi-structured interviews and open-ended questions. Participants completed an informed consent form and reviewed  the interview questions in advance. Interview questions included teachers' background (e.g., age, years of teaching experience, university background, grade levels, and teaching subject), teachers' understanding of AI concepts, attitudes (e.g., How do you feel about the development of AI tools?), responsible AI use (e.g., How do you use AI tools to search for or generate knowledge?), concern about AI's development (e.g., What role do you think AI tools will play in future school education?). The interview was recorded and transcribed with the participant's consent. The qualitative data collected from these interviews were analyzed using an open coding approach. The data were categorized into six  themes: (1) T eachers' practical uses of AI: Theme 1-1 Instructional support, 1-2 Student learning, 1-3 Teachers' uses, 1-4 Limited use. (2) Teachers' understanding of AI: Theme 2-1 Structure information, 2-2 Generate diverse ideas, 2-3 Provide immediate feedback. (3) Teachers' perspectives towards AI: Theme 3-1 Tool, 3-2 Partner, 3-3 Context and experience dependence. (4) Teachers' emotional responses: Theme 4-1 Positive, 4-2 Negative, 4-3 Co-existing. (5) Risks of AI: Theme 5-1 Weaken students' cognition, 5-2 Content quality issue, 5-3 Behavioral problems. (6) Response to the uses of AI: Theme 6-1 Instructional adjustments, 6-2 Teacher professional judgment, 6-3 Information literacy. 626

## Primary findings

Teachers' attitudes toward AI: Between “tool” and “co-creative partner” Interviews suggest that teachers are cautious yet optimistic. They recognize AI' s usefulness in handling routine tasks but are hesitant to rely on it entirely. Some repeatedly stressed that “AI is just a tool” (Theme 3-1), primarily for administrative support (Theme 1-4), item generation, and information retrieval (Theme 1-1). Others, however, described AI as a potential partner, engaging with it directly and viewing it as a teaching assistant or a source of inspiration (Theme 3-2). This positioning is not fixed but dynamic, shaped by contextual resources and teachers ' own orientations (Theme 3-3). In well-resourced settings with strong institutional support, teachers with an exploratory mindset were more inclined to treat AI as a co-creative partner (Theme 3-2); conversely, in resourceconstrained contexts or where professional authority was emphasized, AI was used mainly in a utilitarian capacity (Theme 3-1). Overall, the “tool –partner” distinction operates less as a rigid dichotomy and more as a fluid continuum shaped by context (Theme 3-3).

Efficiency-driven AI use, with emerging innovation in interdisciplinary STEM field Teachers' most frequent uses of AI remain efficiency-driven, such as drafting administrative documents (Theme 1-4), preparing test items, or compiling background information for science teaching (Theme 1-1). These practices reduce teachers' workload and allow more time for instructional guidance. At the same time, some teachers have begun to explore AI for more innovative applications (Theme 3-2). In science classes, AI was used to gather and compare disaster-related data to design inquiry-b ased tasks (Theme 1-2) that combined geography and engineering perspectives, such as analyzing resilient building designs across regions. In technology courses, AI supported students in programming or prototyping (Theme 1-2), helping them refine initial ideas into tangible project designs (Theme 2-1). At science fairs and in interdisciplinary projects, AI was employed to contextualize data (Themes 2-3) and generate visualizations (Theme 2-2), and even to create music or narrative scenarios for presentations (Theme 1-2), thereby enriching students’ multimodal communication. These practices show that AI in STEM education is gradually moving beyond a “time-saver” (Theme 3-1) toward a resource for cross-disciplinary integration and innovative learning (Theme 2-2). Divergent patterns across STEM disciplines Disciplinary cultures also shaped how teachers adopted and evaluated AI. Math and science teachers emphasized accuracy, noting that AI's high error rates in advanced problems (Theme 5-2) confined its role to generating items and diagrams rather than functioning as a dependable problem solver. By contrast, science teachers regarded AI as a valuable tool for integrating information (Theme 2-1) and expanding ideas  (Theme 2-2), particularly in the design of science fair projects and inquiry-based activities (Theme 1-2). Teachers engaged in interdisciplinary projects went further, using AI to create multimodal materials (Theme 2-2) that enhanced student motivation and contextual engagement (Theme 4-1). Taken together, these contrasts highlight how disciplinary orientations influenced AI adoption: mathematics prioritized verification and precision (Theme 6-3), while natural sciences and interdisciplinary teaching placed greater value on integration and creativity (Theme 2-2). This study reveals that generative AI adoption among Taiwanese STEM teachers is a dynamic process shaped by the interplay of individual attitudes, pedagogical practices, and disciplinary cultures. Teachers ' perceptions fluctuate between viewing AI as a utilitarian tool and a co-creative partner, a transition largely dictated by environmental resources and specific instructional needs. While current applications primarily focus on efficiency —such as administrative automation and material development —an evolutionary shift toward innovation is emerging. This is particularly evident in interdisciplinary contexts, where AI facilitates inquiry-based design, project prototyping, and multimodal content creation, evolving from a time-saving utility into a catalyst for deep learning. Crucially, disciplinary norms significantly influence adoption. Mathematics teacher s, prioritizing precision, remain cautious due to potential errors. On the other hand, those in natural sciences and interdisciplinary fields leverage AI's capacity to integrate information and spark creativity, leading to more flexible instructional strategies. To optimize AI's educational potential, professional development must abandon one-size-fits-all models.
