# An Exploration of Spatial Typologies for Active Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Active learning classrooms are continuing to gain popularity worldwide, requiring educators to make effective use of these spatial environments. However, limited systematic frameworks exist to guide the alignment between instructional strategies and spatial configurations, leaving educators uncertain about how to configure and use space for pedagogical goals. This study introduces a typological approach based on Space Coupling Levels (SCL) combined with pattern visualization. Drawing on active learning strategies documented in educational practice, we assessed 50 strategies using the Space Coupling Levels approach, identifying 10 high-dependency cases. We selected five representative strategies for detailed pattern visualization, extracting and visualizing their characteristic spatial geometries and associated interaction patterns through conceptual pattern diagrams. This work establishes an alignment matrix of "activity types × spatial prerequisites" as a comparative baseline, offering researchers a replicable analytical tool while providing educators and space designers with visual design resources.

In higher education, there is growing investment in Active Learning Classrooms (ALCs), which are designed specifically to support active learning approaches and maximize their benefits (Talbert & Mor-Avi, 2019). Unlike traditional lecture-style classrooms,  ALCs feature moveable furniture and writeable surfaces, often within technology rich environments (Brooks, 201 1). Research demonstrates that ALCs enhance student engagement and perceptions of the learning environment (Peng et al., 2022). Yet despite this momentum, limited research addresses how educators actually design curriculum for these environments (Bingen et al., 202 4). Existing frameworks provide conceptual foundations, such as the Pedagogy Space Technology model (Radcliffe et al.,

2008) and spatial pedagogy research (Lim et al., 2012), which highlight the interplay between space and

pedagogy. However, these frameworks fall short in offering actionable design patterns that specify which spatial factors support which active learning strategies. This study addresses a central question through a typological lens: How can we systematically map active learning strategies onto a typology of spatial configurations? This paper introduces a spatial typology approach that bridges conceptual frameworks and operational practice. We propose the Space Coupling Levels (SCL) as an analytical approach to evaluate the spatial dependencies of active learning strategies. Applying this approach to 50 strategies documented in educational practice, we identified 10 exhibiting high spatial coupling and selected five representative cases for detailed pattern visualization. Through visual pattern representations capturing essential spat ial geometries, this work offers researchers an analytical tool and aims to provide practitioners with an adaptable design base. The paper proceeds as follows: First, a literature review outlines active learning strategies and spatial pedagogical frameworks. Next, the rationale for a typological approach and the SCL analytical method are explained. Five high-coupling strategy examples are then presented, followed by a discussion of contributions, limitations, and future directions.

## Literature review

Active learning encompasses a wide variety of instructional strategies  that vary significantly in operational specificity (Michael, 2006; Doolittle et al., 2023). Some strategies, like gallery walks, provide relatively detailed procedures, while others, such as problem-based learning or inquiry-based approaches, remain more conceptual, with limited specificity about spatial configurations or pedagogical operations. Although many educators express strong interest in active learning, there is little guidance for their design of such strategies for specific contexts, particularly when spatial configurations play a critical role in enactment. Without such guidance, teachers are challenged in implementing strategies like flipped classroom or use collaborative learning. When active learning occurs in physical classrooms, spatial factors introduce additional implementation challenges. The Pedagogy Space Technology (PST) framework  was introduced to highlight the reciprocal interplay between instruction, physical environment, and technological tools (Radcliffe et al., 2008). Yet PST 1947 functions as a meta-framework and evaluation tool rather than offering prescriptive guidance for concrete spatial configurations such as seating arrangements or functional zones. Spatial pedagogy research identifies categories of classroom space use (autho ritative, personal, supervisory, interactional) and their relationships to instruction (Lim et al., 2012), but these accounts often hinge on specific room layouts and have not been systematized into transferable configuration families. Recent  research on  i ndoor positioning has quantified teacher and student proximity and movement patterns, linking them to outcomes such as group cohesion (Yan et al., 2022).  Yet such work lacks any catalogue of spatial arrangements.  Hence, a n exploration of spatial typology that systematically classifies strategies by their spatial dependencies and translates them into actionable design patterns could offer a practical pathway for practitioners.

Aligning pedagogy and spatial configurations: Rationale Typologies are one means of  organizing complex phenomena by identifying dimensions along which cases can be systematically compared. In architecture and design methods, Colquhoun (2009) defined building types as socio-spatial organizational schemata sedimented over time, in forms that have proven apt for human activity. He argues that we should acknowledge and debate the influence of types in order to pursue a rational practice that is neither strict determinism nor pure intuition.  Complementing this, Dobson (1996) argues that  the purpose of a typology is not to seek a single correct definition but to present a panoramic set of constitutive dimensions within which conceptual discussion must proceed. By making components and dimensions explicit, typologies can accommodate plurality while preserving comparability across contexts. In educational research, scholars have developed typologies to simplify complex phenomena and provide analytical frameworks, for instance examining pedagogical forms (Bloom, 1956) and educational programs (Rix & Twining, 2007). These studies treat typology as a research tool to compare practices, reveal similarities and differences, and inform curriculum design and educational development. Typologies are typically conceived as exploratory “working frameworks” that foster understanding and practical improvement rather than fixed or definitive classifications. The space-pedagogy relationship is neither deterministic nor one-to-one. As Sundstrom et al. (2025) demonstrate, named methods do not map uniformly to classroom enactment; the same pedagogical strategy can manifest through varied spatial arrangements depending on context. This variability underscores the need for a classification approach that emphasizes family resemblances over rigid categorizations, which is well suited for a typology.

Analytical components: Space Coupling Levels (SCL) Before developing spatial pedagogical patterns, we first acknowledge that pedagogical strategies differ in how much they depend on spatial configurations. Spatial configurations are not equally important to all pedagogical strategies. Some activities can be performed with nearly any arrangement, while others require specific geometry, circulation pathways, or display surfaces. The variability of the activities necessitates the development of a preliminary classification tool to identify those that merit more detailed spatial-patterning analysis. Thus, we introduce the notion of Space Coupling in reference to the strength of association between a pedagogy and some concomitant spatial configuration.  We created a rubric for this conceptual idea, called the Space Coupling Levels (SCL), building on three research strands. Drawing on Smith's (2017) work on classroom geometry, active learning research on furniture flexibility and circulation (Byers, 2021; Peng et al., 2022), and interaction mapping studies (Lim et al., 2012), we derived five dimensions: (1) Geometric dependency— disrupting axes, symmetry, or focal points; (2) Circulation requirements —rotating among stations or accessing distributed zones; (3) Vertical surface/display needs —whiteboards, projection, posting areas; (4) Density and accessibility—adequate area, aisles, sightlines; (5) Positioning and orientation—teacher location, grouping proximity, directional attention.  We scored each dimension according to the following rubric: 0 (not relevant), 1 (helpful), or 2 (essential), yielding an overall Space Coupling Level score of 0–10. Activities are then classified as SCL-3 (high spatial dependency, score ≥ 7), SCL-2 (moderate, score = 4–6), or SCL-1 (low, score ≤ 3) The classification assesses implementations, not just activity names, as  the same strategy could span multiple SCLs depending on design choices. In practice, resource and time constraints may lead educators to enact the same strategy at lower coupling levels. For instance, Jigsaw can be implemented with high coupling (dedicated expert and jigsaw zones  with writable vertical surfaces for collaborative thinking) or with moderate coupling (tabletop discussion with in-place role rotation when vertical surfaces are unavailable). Higher SCL levels indicate spatial constraints, not pedagogical superiority. SC L-1 activities, precisely because of their spatial flexibility, serve as versatile building blocks embeddable within diverse configurations. Our subsequent pattern development focuses preliminarily on SCL-2 and SCL-3 activities, where spatial alignment most impacts enactment. Spatial pedagogical patterns as a narrative form 1948 While the SCL identifies which pedagogical forms are most dependent on specific spatial configurations, spatial patterns offer a visual medium for communicating these configurations. Following the tradition of design pattern in architecture and learning sciences (Alexander, 1977; Goodyear & Retalis, 2010), we conceive Spatial Pedagogical patterns as a diagrammatic visualization that encodes: (1) the activity's pedagogical goal, (2) typical sequence, (3) spatial geometry (axes, symmetry, focal points),  (4) teacher positioning and movement. These compact visual representations  make spatial-interaction couplings inspectable and adoptable, enabling practitioners to recognize and adapt configurations to their contexts. We analyzed active learning strategies documented in current educational practice, reviewing practitioner-oriented resources from leading institutions (Harvard ABLConnect, n.d.; Harvard Project Zero, n.d.; SALTISE, n.d.; UC Berkeley, GSI Teaching & Resource Center, n.d.; Notre Dame Learning, n.d.; University of Waterloo, Centre for Teaching Excellence, n.d.), identifying 50 strategies that met three criteria: (1) operational clarity with explicit procedural steps; (2) spatial enactment in physical classrooms; (3) documented current practice. Space Coupling Levels (SCL) assessment Each strategy was assessed using the SCL rubric through a structured protocol. The lead researcher conducted assessments with iterative refinement to ensure consistent criteria application. Final classifications reflect consolidated judgment across multiple review passes to provide a proof-of-concept for the SCL approach. Future work will include formal inter-rater reliability testing with multiple independent coders to establish psychometric properties of the rubric.

From the 50 strategies assessed, 10 (20%) exhibited high spatial coupling (SCL-3), 15 (30%) showed moderate coupling (SCL-2), and 25 (50%) demonstrated low coupling (SCL-1), confirming substantial variation in spatial dependency across active learning strategies. Spatial typologies serve as conceptual scaffolds rather than prescriptive blueprints, providing explicit spatial dependencies, allowing informed adaptations, and fostering dialogue between pedagogical intent and architectural affordances. Typological approaches offer practical advantages: teachers can project these patterns onto  their own actual classroom spaces and adapt them according to local features, physical constraints, and curricular content. The flexibility of this approach honors foundational spatial principles while maintaining contextual adaptation, and its standardized visual language facilitates a holistic understanding of interaction dynamics. In spite of this, typological abstractions have inherent limitations. Some high spat ial coupling strategies exhibit scenario-dependent variability that is resistant to standardization, as evidenced by the exclusion of Role Playing and Simulations. In this sense, pattern-based templates can be distinguished from parametric design frameworks. The exploratory nature of SCL classifications, without formal inter-rater reliability testing, and the need for empirical validation of pattern usability by educators are limitations.
