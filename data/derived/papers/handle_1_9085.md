# Historians’ Criteria for Appraising Historiography: An Interview Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Developing and applying epistemic ideals or criteria is a core component of epistemic practices. In history education, epistemic criteria have been included in approaches to teaching history as well as in theoretical frameworks for investigating the learning and understanding of the domain. One justification for these frameworks is in the epistemic practices of expert historians; however, empirical evidence about historians’ epistemic criteria is scarce. Thus, we set out to explore epistemic criteria historians use to determine quality in their own and others’ work through an analysis of interviews with 26 Finnish historians. We identified over 80 criteria that we placed in categories such as internal characteristics of works; connections to past and present knowledge and practices; connections to future knowledge and practices; proxies; connections to non-epistemic use; and emotions. These findings open up possibilities for better developing criteria in history education and considering their connections to those of experts.

## Introduction

Epistemic criteria are a central component of epist emic practices: They are ideals or standards for de termining when and why inquiries have reached their goals, or when and why some epistemic products might be preferred over others (Chinn & Rinehart, 2016). The use and need for epistemic criteria is well recognized within philosophy and practice of historiography (Jordanova, 2019; Kainulainen et al., 2019; Kuukkanen, 2015). In history education too, epistemic criteria are relevant for many approaches to learning and teaching history. These inclu de, for instance, modeling and supporting historical readin g skills, employing historical evidence, classroom argumentation, and broadly methods that involve stu dents in evaluating and interpreting products of historiography (Fogo, 2014; Herrenkohl & Cornelius, 2013). Epistemic criteria also play a crucial role in the study of learners’ understanding of the nature of history  or their epistemological beliefs (VanSledright & M aggioni, 2016). In recent decades much of history education has indeed centered on simulating the practices of historiography and has emphasized the educational aim of “the development of an understanding of how historical knowledge is constructed and evaluated” (Voet & De Wever, 2017, p. 312). We propose that the part of history education that  involves appraising products of historiography should build more strongly upon an evidence-based understanding of the ideals or criteria that domain experts themselves use in such tasks; however, research on this topic is still scarce. As one exception, Gottlieb and Wineburg (2012) studied religious historians’ use of epistemic crit eria in evaluating historical claims presented in c urated sets of short document extracts, and they identified criteria applied by the historians such as trustworthiness of evidence and intertextual references. They also found that h istorians applied different epistemic criteria depe nding on whether the documents related to their religious commitments or not. We explored this topic by conducting interviews in  which historians at length presented and discussed their own research as well as some research by others. We used the AIR model of epistemic cognition (Chinn & Rinehart, 2016) as one framework guiding our methods and analysis. According to this model, epistemic cognition refers to people’s ways of knowing. The model analyzes epistemic cognition into three components—epistemic Aims and value, epistemic Ideals, and Reliable epistemic processes. Epistemic aims refer to the goals people set for knowing and understanding. We have documented a  range of epistemic aims relevant for expert histor ians; these include critiquing existing ideas, gaining un derstanding, popularizing research, and developing their own epistemic competences (Kainulainen, et al., 2022). Epistemic ideals are the criteria or standards that  people use to appraise whether they have achieved their aims and to evaluate epistemic products. In science, epistemic ideals for good explanations can include fit with evidence, fruitfulness, and simplicity. Chinn and Sandoval (2018) proposed possible differences between scientists and historians in the ideals that are commonly used; in this paper, we explore what historians’ epistemic ideals are. F inally, reliable epistemic processes are the indivi dual or community strategies, activities, or methods that people use to achieve their aims. (1)

## Research Question

In this paper, we analyze the ideals that historians describe as important to their epistemic practice. We ask: What ideals (criteria or standards) do historians use for appraising quality in historiography? ICLS2022 Proceedings 989 © ISLS

## Participants

The participants were 26 volunteer Finnish historia ns, who were recruited by contacting various histor y departments and writing an invitation to a national  online forum for humanities scholars. The historia ns represented a variety of different faculties and traditions of historical research. All had earned a PhD degree, but their career lengths varied from post-doc researchers to emeriti professors. All of the participants were somehow affiliated with at least one Finnish university. In  order to secure the confidentiality of the partici pants, we only refer to the participants as “Historian [nr]” and exclude their universities, degrees, and details of research topics in all of the excerpts below.

## Procedure

The first author conducted semi-structured intervie ws with the historians in Finnish language. The len gth of interviews varied from less than one hour to almost three hours, averaging 90 minutes. The full interview scheme included questions related to all components of the AIR model as well as other topics, and the historians discussed their research in detail and at length. In terms of  identifying historians’ epistemic ideals, key ques tions in the interview included: “What is your best work, and wh y?”; “What about someone else’s best work, and why? ”; “When reviewing papers, books, or presentations, which criteria do you usually use to evaluate such work?”; and “What are your common critiques regarding methods i n such review situations?” The full interview schem e is available from the authors.

## Data Analysis

The transcribed interviews were analyzed by means o f a qualitative content analysis (Schreier, 2012) t hat proceeded through various iterative and collaborative stages. In the beginning, codes were developed extensively. Later on, when it became apparent that some of these codes did not prove useful in characterizing the data, they were either removed or merged with another similar enough code. Hence, in a way, the coding scheme from the earlier stage of research can be considered a self-made analytic tool, which we then used, tested, and  further developed during the analysis. The final coding scheme is available from the authors.

## Results

Our analysis of historians’ epistemic criteria identified over 80 different criteria (or codes), divided into to eight categories. Below, due to the short length of this paper, we limit our discussion to six of the categories (2), focusing on those criteria that (a) were mentioned by several historians, and (b) we consider especially relevant for history education and its practices.

We begin with the category titled “internal charac teristics of historiography”—i.e. criteria that ref er mainly to features of the products (e.g., articles,  books, conference presentations) themselves. All h istorians mentioned ideals assigned to this category, including 26 different ideals. The most frequently mentioned ones can be roughly grouped into ideals for evaluating the o utput’s literary or rhetorical quality and ideals for evaluating its methodological quality. These groups have clear implications for history education: Both should be accounted for. Regarding the first group, historians spoke of internal coherence (whether the work fits together); quality of argumentation; readability; communicativeness; and, as in this excerpt, style of text and presentation: [A] fluent writing style that moves one’s own argum entation forward is important. So even though the topic might be interesting as such, if it is written in a dull and bland way, it is not a good article. (Historian 9) The methodological quality of the products of historiography could be assessed based on the perspectival aspects of the work (whether there is a perspective; whether it is brought forth; and whether it is interesting or important); scope and quality of sources; amount of theory, analysis and narration; and transparency of processes (whether and how the frameworks and methods of the study are  presented). As an example of the last, one histori an (13) criticized some work for not “explicating their met hods” and considered the experience of reading such  work irritating and “an unfair game, because you are not telling what you do, and why.” The second category—also one that all historians r eferred to—was “connections to past and present knowledge and practices.” This category reflects Kuhn’s (1977) famous notion of the ‘essential tension’ between tradition and invention in scholarship. In this category, some of the mentioned ideals—namely contextualization; timeliness; and positioning the work in relation to ongoing discussions—are already incorporated in some history ICLS2022 Proceedings 990 © ISLS education practices; however, they might not be explicitly discussed as ideals of the domain. Other ideals, however, require considerable domain expertise. These includ e originality, exceptionality and innovativeness; or methodological originality, the last being exemplified below: It combined the kind of things that had not been done before; read sources in a whole new way. And by studying a small detail he was able to say s omething about a very wide thing, by generalizing something that nobody had noticed before. (Historian 16) Third, historians also highlighted “connections to future knowledge and practices.” This implies that historiography should not just build on or challeng e existing understandings, but also be fruitful in opening up vistas for new work. This appears something that the tasks and practices typical to history education do not always allow. The specific codes in this category include ideals such as connections to future theory and knowledge; connections to future methods and practice; ability to promote future research; or interdisciplinary value. Exemplifying the last, one historian (15) considered their work valuable to the research field because “it not only contributes to historical research, but it could al so have use for researchers in other fields.” In ed ucational applications, it is possible that students could co me to appreciate the value of ideas that open up ne w questions for inquiry.

Fourth, when assessing historiography, the intervi ewed historians also referred to “proxies” in determining quality in their own and others work. T his category refers to externalizing or outsourcing  (part of) the criteria to others. The category can be further  divided into expert and lay proxies. Thus, in educ ational applications, students could also learn the value of expert and lay audience evaluations as central to how historical work is appraised. Expert proxies include ideals such as the very act and process of experts publishing the work; more generally, reliance on expert commentary and evaluation;  and experts’ acceptance or engagement in or with the work. The last is exemplified below (in a statement that also resonates with the previous category): Well, the criteria comes through that discussion that is presumably formed after the publication of this book, and it has already formed in many workshops and conferences. Through that, I can then see how well it works. (Historian 22) Some historians also referred to lay visibility (the extent and nature of public visibility); or, as exemplified below in a statement related to engaging local communities with one’s studies, lay excitement and interest: I don’t use any criteria [to determine whether aims  have been reached]. I don’t, like, sit and think with some criteria how they [the aims] are fu lfilled. […] It is not rational to think of criteria, even though perhaps I should. But one see s it through the echo given by teaching, whether students are excited. Once or twice a week I go to talk to different [groups and organizations], so in a way that is the criteria: t he interest the research raises in others than colleagues. (Historian 6) Fifth, historians also referred historiography’s “ connections to non-epistemic uses,” in terms of whether the work had societal impact. What differentiates this from lay or expert proxies is influence on societal changes and political debates (and not only interest in or acceptance of the work). For example, one historian (20) wrote a history of a profession, and mentioned as an import ant feedback from contemporary practitioners of the  same profession how they “considered it as an empowering  book […] that meant a lot to their professional se lfperception.” It is possible to envision historical inquiries among students in which students consider community applicability of ideas as one of the criteria for evaluating them (see, Goldenberg, 2019). Finally, historians referred to their “emotions and  affects” that works of historiography raise. These include happiness, fun, and satisfaction; and many historians mentioned excitement and interest  related to historiographic output. While many referred to thei r own interest upon engaging with others’ work, som e also referred to the excitement of other historians producing and presenting the work: Also, if it seems that a person is somehow themself not at all excited about it; If someone comes to a conference and does a terribly mechanical pape r, then you cannot pick up the important things from there, even though there may be some. (Historian 12) Incorporating such ideals into historical inquiries in educational settings could enrich these inquiries with the epistemic emotions that help animate professional historical work. ## Discussion

In sum, the interviewed group of Finnish historians  working in the contemporary academia mentioned ten s of different epistemic ideals they use as criteria for  evaluating historiography. These ideals range from  the characteristics of the (literary) output to its significance both scientifically and societally, and to historians’ own affective responses to the work in question. Our ob servations have at least two general implications f or the development of history education. First, we should explicate not just learning goals or teaching practices, but also the epistemic ideals of the domain related to them,  since they apparently play a role in how experts i n the field conduct their work. Second, the sheer number of epi stemic ideals identified in this study makes one he sitate to claim that we are even now grasping any full scope of them. It is, then, no wonder that teachers are n ot all that familiar with the range of epistemic criteria in hi story (e.g., Koutsianou & Emvalotis, 2021). However, it still seems apparent that experts apply a wide range of i deals; therefore, why should educators expect stude nts to operate with only a few? And even if only a few, which few? To be sure, not all epistemic ideals we identified are as useful in designing learning and teaching as oth ers, but, at the minimum, awareness of the scope of  expert epistemic ideals in history seems necessary, if educators aim to bring history learning closer to what the practice of historiography entails.
