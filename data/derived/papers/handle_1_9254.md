**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study combines discussion data and natural language processing (NLP) approaches to examine if the language students produce in online discussions is an indication of their engagement with the content and their peers in a socio-digital platform. We coded 2805 posts from the platform for an undergraduate information sciences and technology course. The findings show what NLP indices are indicative of engagement level of students.

## Introduction

As online learning becomes more prevalent, educators must rethink the design of content and interactions between peers, instructors, and resources. Specifically, interaction among students has been shown as an important factor that leads to shared construction of knowledge and meaning making (Paloff & Pratt, 200 1). However, students are usually only able to successfully interact with guidance, and instructors face challenges in large online courses to provide quick and accurate feedback on student interaction. To give feedback to students in online collaborative peer activities, instructors need support to translate students’ engagement behaviors and assess their pedagogical approaches and tool use for adaptation (Chi, 2021) within their local contexts of use. As a result of the need to identify means of assessing and providing support for student engagement online, a research agenda focusing on automatic assessment of student engagement has emerged. Interactive-Constructive-Active-Passive (ICAP) framework (Chi, 2009) has  been adapted for the automatic assessment of students’ level of cognitive engagement with learning materials and their peers, as it is suitable for quick assessment of student engagement. ICAP classifies lea rning activities into a taxonomy of interactive (I), constructive (C), active (A), and passive (P), and proposes that learning increases as students become more engaged with the content and progress from passive to interactive. Machine learning methods have been used in other research to develop an automatic analysis of online discussions to classify cognitive engagement using the ICAP framework. For example, in our work (Sharma et al., 2022), we trained a Long-ShortTerm-Memory (LSTM) classifier, which reached an 83% accuracy rate and a substantial level of agreement with human raters (Cohen’s kappa = 0.717).

In this paper, we combine NLP approaches and online discussion data to examine  if the language that students produce in online discussions is an indication of their cognitive engagement in online peer activities. We investigated the potential for NLP indices related to text length and lexical sophis tication in conjunction with online discussion data to predict the cognitive behaviors expressed in a post. Understanding what NLP indices are indicative of students’ cognitive engagement level can help improve the accuracy of the trained LSTM classifier.

## Method

We downloaded approximately 4,650 discussion posts from the Slack group for an undergradua te information sciences course. Based on initial qualitative discourse analyses, we created an adapted version of the ICAP framework. Since we could not observe passive activities, we eliminated that category from our coding scheme. We created two categories within the active category to account for (1) general course related talk including assignments, requirements and schedules, which we classified as A1, and (2) talk related to the content of the course, which we classified as A2. We removed the off-topic posts and ke pt 2805 in total. Three human raters manually coded 2805 discussion posts. Coders then discussed all discrepant coding decisions, resolved conflicts, and adjudicated codes for the 2805 posts.

We used TAALES (Kyle & Crossley, 2015) to assess the lexical sophis tication of the 2805 posts. The indices that yielded non-normal distributions were removed to meet statistical assumptions. In addition, 398 posts were removed to cope with outliers in the data. Finally, 74 indices for lexic al frequency, lexical range, psycholinguistic word information, age of acquisition, contextual distinctiveness, word neighbor information, and multi-word units were retained for MANOVA that was conducted to examine what lexical features significantly varied between engagement groups (i.e., A1, A2, C, and I). In the analysis, NLP indi ces calculated by TAALES were used as the dependent variables, and the cognitive engagement category assigned to the posts was used as the independent variable.

## Results

Engagement groups had a significant effect on NLP indices, F(219, 7011) =  6, p <.001. However, separate univariate ANOVAs on outcome variables revealed non-significant effects on 11 NLP indices. Thus, 63 indices retained for the analysis had significantly varied means between engagement groups. These indices were used in the subsequent discriminant function analysis (DFA), which revealed three di scriminant functions. The first explained 87.04% of the variance, the second explained 9.44% of the variance, and the last explained 3.52% of the variance. Plots of the DFA classification showed good separation of A1 and A2 categories along discriminant function one (LD1), whereas the separation of engagement categories along other discriminant functions was not good. ## Conclusion

Previous studies have implemented machine learning methods to develop new mode ls that can be used for the automatic classification of students’ level of cognitive engagement in online disc ussions. This study combines discussion data with NLP approaches to gain insights into what factors impact the classification of students’ posts/messages in online discussions into four engagement categories of the ICAP framework. We investigated whether the lexical sophistication indices predict the level of students’ cognitive engagement.  The results suggest that a linear combination of lexical sophistication indices can be used to e nhance the separation of A1 and A2 categories.

In the next phase of this study, we plan to examine whether other linguistics  features, such as text structure, text cohesion, and syntactic complexity, are indication of the level of cognitive engagement students demonstrate.
