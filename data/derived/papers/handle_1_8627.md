# A Growing Problem: Student-Worker Retention at an Urban Farm

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We investigate student-workers’ motivation at an urban farm situated on a Historically Black College’s campus. This is one phase of research embedded within the beginning stages of a larger research practitioner partnership (RPP). This paper aims to address: How do student-workers stay motivated to continue their work placements at an HBCU’s urban farm in the Southwest US? The basis of our analyses are interviews, observation field notes, and artifacts collected over the course of this research. We examined the data to identify recurring themes which led to student-worker turnover and discuss opportunities for improvement of student-workers’ learning motivation. As a whole, we seek to support key partners, as the ICLS 2022 calls for, by investigating innovative learning environments.

## Introduction

Consistent with the “investigating innovative pedag ogies, technologies, and learning environments” the me for ICLS 2022, researching innovative learning environm ents plays an important role to diversify education al experiences. To achieve these new educational exper iences, we must look at new systems for marginalize d communities and individuals to access systems of power.  We report on a budding research practitioner partnership (RPP) between the researchers and an HBCU’s urban farm, to examine and address student workers’ motivation to learn at an urban farm. The purpose of this plac e-based research alliance was formed to investigate  and understand the overall driving question for this RP P: How can we strengthen the connections between le arning and student engagement in perceived community space s? The research questions were co-designed with stakeholders using Penuel and Gallagher’s (2017) RPP framework of finding questions of mutual concern.  The overall study aims to answer the following research  questions: How do student-workers stay motivated t o learn in their work placements at an HBCU’s urban farm? W hat are organizational strategies that are used by the university and urban farm to allow for continued co llaborations between student-workers and community partners? What does the process of learning look like for student-workers at an urban farm, and how do the expertnovice roles come into play? This paper will focus on question one.

## Theoretical Framework and Methodology

We found using an RPP framework beneficial to look at the initial stages of this project, with goals to become a long-term and sustainable RPP. There is a wealth of  literature on informal and interest-based spaces (Rogoff et al., 2016) from amateur and hobbyist astronomers (Azevedo, 2013), middle school field trips to science museums (Whitesell, 2016), and adult social events at children’s museums (Harris et al., 2016). However, there is a dearth of research within the learning sciences surrounding food-based spaces. This research is also informed by studies related to students and adults’ motivation to learn (Coker, 2003;) This budding partnership is comprised of the researchers, the college’s urban farm, the community, and a local National Football League (NFL) franchise. The researchers include a professor and two doctoral students in STEM education. The college is a HBCU work-study college in an urban setting. In 2010, said HBCU converted one of its sport fields into a four-acre urban farm which began to and continues to grow seasonal vegetables. The community members involved with the partnership are  local benefactors of the farm – representatives of  local food banks and community centers. The final partner, a local NFL franchise, works with the farm to pur chase locally grown produce to supply their restaurant, b oth at their training facility and their stadium. T he research problem and question were jointly identified by the key stakeholders, who took a participatory design approach by listening, specifying problems of practice, mapp ing systems, and identifying shared values (Penuel & Gallagher, 2017).

The urban farm is situated within an informal learning and interest-based participation setting (Azevedo, 2013; Rogoff et al., 2016).  This participatory res earch study was conducted at a historically Black w ork-study college’s urban farm in the Southwest U.S. The participants of this portion of the study include three staff members of the college, three current student-workers, and two former student-workers. The researchers worked at the urban farm site to build trust and establish a rapp ort with the partners. All research activity and ma terials were supported by the stakeholders. Data collection incl uded participating on the urban farm, observations, microethnographic field notes, interviews, and artifacts. Rigorous data was collected until saturation was met. ## Findings

In analyzing the qualitative data related to studen t-workers' motivation and retention, it was apparen t that there were both student-level and organization-level chal lenges and successes. Student-worker interviews ide ntified reasons for leaving their positions and staying in their positions. First, student-workers discussed complications that prompted their peers to leave. The time and am ount of work involved at the urban farm was much mo re strenuous and demanding compared to other on-campus  positions for the same financial incentives, which prompted students to take advantage of the less str essful job sites. Additionally, there was always a shortage of student-workers to help on the farm, leaving the remaining students and staff responsible for the ongoing demands of a working urban farm.

Second, students proposed reasons for staying on the urban farm, despite the implications offered in the above section. There were two primary ideas that we re raised in the first round of coding: interest-ba sed motivations and gaining new skills. Student-workers  mentioned their desire to expand their experiences  by learning new transferable skills related to farming  and gardening. Also, student-workers disclosed how  they gained necessary 21st century skills that will prep are them for the workforce. One student-worker, Jan e, said, “[the farm] taught me patience and trusting the process,” by: Working on the farm and gardening, we take the process of planting a seed, you have to trust the process of watering it daily, giving it what it needs to be fed, the nutrients, so it can become a sprout and flower or fruit or vegetable or whatever it is you’re trying to grow. It’s not going to happen right then and there on the same day. It's going to take time.

These skills were deemed important to this student worker to stay in their placement at the farm so th ey could continue to grow as learners. This unique learning environment, which is situated within a formal context provides a complex layer of the site.

## Scholarly Significance

The initial findings from this study parallel the learning motivation in similar studies. For example, Coker (2003) examines Black females adult learners’ motivation. She finds that black female adult learners continue  their educational journey for self-development, family development, and community development. Similar to Coker’s

(2003) study, the current student workers continue their work placements to further enhance their self-development by becoming more marketable in the workforce and inspiring confidence. Additionally, the studentworkers have found a sense of purpose working on th e farm by positively impacting their community, which is echoed in Coker’s (2003) research. This study will start the process of studying agriculture and food systems using research and theoretical tools from a learning sciences perspective. The initial findings from this study can continue to advance and contribute to the RPP liter ature about student-worker’s motivation to learn wi thin an urban farm context.
