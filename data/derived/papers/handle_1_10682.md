# Empowering Teachers’ Competencies: A Task-Centered Teacher Development to Integrating Robotics in STEM Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Robotics can promote Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics education as well as 21st-century skills such as creativity and computational thinking. Nevertheless, robotics is not a mandatory part of the curriculum in many countries. One possible solution could be to integrate robotics into existing subjects such as science. Integrating robotics may be challenging because teachers lack relevant pedagogical and technological competencies, and their self-efficacy is correspondingly low. In this study, we examined how a task-centered teacher development program for integrating robotics in science education can be used to foster competencies science teachers need to integrate robotics activities into their teaching. We found that the task-centered instructional strategy is an approach that can potentially foster teachers’ competencies to integrate robotics activities into science education.

## Introduction

Robotics in the classroom creates a learning environment where students can interact with the environm ent and work with real-world problems (Papert, 1990). Robotics can also foster scientific understanding and contribute to developing practices identified in the Next Generation Science Standards  (NGSS), providing students with the necessary technological skills while supporting the ir scientific understanding (NRC, 2012). Studies sh ow that learning robotics activities impacts students’ science-related skills, such as integrating  quantitative aspects into physical problem-solving processes (Addido et al., 2023). In addition to positively impacting students’ scien ce studies, robotics programs have the potential to improve technological literacy, increase students’ self-efficacy in using technology, improve their attitudes toward technology, and enhance 21st-century skills (Usart et al., 2019). The multidisciplinary nature of robotics provides a  unique and original learning environment that rein forces students’ interest in the content they are exposed to, as well as opportunities for students to explore, express ideas, and think constructively (Addido et al., 2023).

Nevertheless, in many countries, robotics is not a mandatory part of the curriculum (Usart et al., 201 9), and often, there is not enough time in the existing curriculum  to integrate robotics. One possible solution could be integrating robotics into subjects such as scien ce (Saad & Verner, 2019). According to the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS Lead States, 2013), science teachers need to develop engineering practices, such as building robots, programming the robot, and using the sensors of the robots to record data. However, integrating robotics into other domains can be challenging due to teachers’ lack of technol ogical competencies and, consequently, their low self-efficacy (You et al., 2021). Compared to learning that focuses on one discipline, integrating robotics activities in science educatio n requires synthesizing different disciplines. Teac hers need to know not only how to operate robots but also how to integrate the m into effective teaching. Teachers need to be confident in the use of technology, know the content they want to teach, and the pedagogical strategies needed to teach the content with the technology.

Therefore, this study aims to examine how a task-centered teacher professional development  (PD) for integrating robotics into science education may foster teachers’ TPACK and 21st-century skills. We decided to utilize the task-centered instructional strategy (Merrill, 2007), which was developed to foster complex learning and combines direct instruction with the completion of real-world tasks. This Task-Centered Instructional Strategy is a content-centered modification of traditional instructional design in which the contents  to be learned are specified first. This strategy was found to promote confidence and l earning through cognitive-affective positive feedback loops, especially for novice learners (Rosenberg-Kima et al., 2022).

## Participants

Sixteen (6 males and 10 females) middle school science teachers participated in a robotics (PD) and were observed for the competencies they demonstrated. Eleven of the teachers (~69%) had no prior experience using or teaching robotics. We also interviewed five teachers about the PD’s contribution to 21st-century skills development. Research tools Observations During the PD, we observed the teachers’ development of robotics models (building and programming with EV3 LEGO Mindstorms software), experiences with the mod els, analysis of results, and conclusions. Also, w e followed the teachers’ design of science lesson plans with robotics activities. More specifically, we observed the Technological, Pedagogical, and Content Knowledge (TPACK), as well as 21st-century skills (Saad et al., 2023) that were practiced during training.

Semi-Structured Interviews After the end of the PD, we interviewed five participants about their opinions on the program’s contribution to 21st-century skills.

The task-centered professional development program A 30-hour PD in robotics was designed to promote competencies for integrating robotics activities in to science education and to enhance the 21st-century skills of science teachers (see Figure 1). The program’s design followed the task-centered instructional s trategy (Merrill, 2007). Three tasks were designed, all involving technolo gical, pedagogical, and scientific knowledge (Mishra & Koe hler, 2006), with increasing difficulty and decreasing support levels.

## Results

The current study is the second phase of a study conducted to identify the competencies required for the integration of robotics activities in science education (Saad et al., 2023). The purpose of the present study is to examine how a task-centered teacher development program for integratin g robotics in science education can be used to fost er competencies science teachers need to integrate rob otics activities into their teaching. We designed and implemented a task-centered PD for sixteen teachers. We tracked competencies durin g the program, such as the basic ability to troubleshoot robot operation, alon g with open-ended observations by the study researchers and post-program interviews with five teachers. The data obtained from the observations were recorded and analyzed by the observer during the sessions while following  the process of solving tasks, applying strategies,  and identifying teachers’ strengths and successes. We conducted a thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006)  of the interview transcripts and the documenting observation. Throughout the analysis of the data, there was a repeated examination of the definitions of the categories. Two researchers reached an agreement over the emergent themes. In each session, we examined which competencies were practiced during the session (see Table 1). All five teachers who were interviewed indicated that the task-centered PD in robotics contributed to the development of skills and in particular 21st-century skills to integrate robotics activities int o their teaching, “I recommend every teacher to take this training as it  helps to develop essential skills such as computat ional thinking”. Three additional skills that had not appeared in the validation phase emerged from the interviews and/or observations: (1) Computational thinking-All five teachers noted that  as the program progressed, solving the robot activities was mainly characterized by automa tion, algorithmic, and logical thinking, in contras t to solving the activities at the beginning of the program, whi ch was mainly by trial and error. (2) Systematically thinking-Three teachers indicated that the design of the PD included the application of the principles of the task-centered instructional strategy when the activities were mor e complex and required the ability to observe robot  models composed of many elements and to develop programming strategies. Although the tasks became more challenging during the program, the teachers were more independent of the program supervisor. ## Discussion and conclusions

The present study gives a new perspective on the po tential of task-centered teachers development for integrating robotics into science education and provides insights into teachers’ competencies. The first task focused primarily on teachers ’ practical experience of using robots in the context  of science subjects. This task promoted the development of technological knowledge, such as the basic ability to build and program an educational robot and the basic ability to troubleshoot robot operation. In addition, teachers developed collaboration, team work, communication, and problem-solving skills, which are considered part of 21st-century skills. The second task promoted the development of more competencies than the first task; in addition to technological knowle dge, teachers acquired technological-pedagogical knowledge (e.g., the ability to teach s tudents how to build a robot), technological-scientific knowledge (e.g., the ability to define an applied problem in robotics and solve it based on mathematical and scientific methods) and technologi cal-pedagogical-scientific knowledge (e.g., the ability to enrich an explanation of a scientific concept to deepen its understanding and illustrate it using a robot). Similarly, teachers encouraged greater development of 21st-century skills such as creativity, critical thinkin g skills, self-regulation skills, and the ability to make decision s. The third and final task, considered more complex  than the first and second tasks, promoted most of the competencies promoted in the first and second tasks, as well as more teacher competencies needed to integrate robotics a ctivities, such as the ability  to guide students to a robotics project, which were not a part of the previous tasks.

In addition, the results of this study showed that the interviewed teachers mentioned three additional competencies that were not included in the list of the teachers’ competencies for applying robotics in  science education proposed in the previous study (Saad et al., 2023). These competencies are computational thinking, systematic thinking, and differential instruction.

## Limitations and future directions

The present study was conducted with a limited number of participants; only 16 teachers participated in the study; hence, generalization is limited. Furthermore, we did not follow up on teachers’ implementation of robotics activities in science classes. Further research is needed to explore their actual implementation. In addition, next, we want to validate the list of competencies from (Saad et al., 2023), including the three additional competencies suggested by the teachers and diagnosed through the observations during the task-centered program.
