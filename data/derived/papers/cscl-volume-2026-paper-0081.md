# Are We There Yet? The Roles and Development of GenAI-Based Tools Supporting Collaborative Learning: A Scoping Review

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Tools based on generative AI (genAI), i nteracting with learners in a human-like manner, such as conversational agents and ChatGPT, offer potential to develop adaptive support for collaborative learning. Following PRISMA guidelines, after screening results of searching three databases, our scoping review identified 239 relevant records reporting about genAI tools in CSCL including 138 empirical articles this paper focuses on. They were analyzed in depth regarding genAI tool’s maturity level of implementation in collaborative learning, and the role genAI takes within. Results show studies at different levels of maturity in implementation of the genAI tool ranging from designing the tool or investigating its usability to studies focusing on human learning outcomes. GenAI takes multiple roles simultaneously, mostly providing epistemic and metacognitive scaffolding to individuals or groups. Our results indicate substantial development in the growing number of empirical articles, while pointing out gaps in specific support type provision, especially for school learners.

While c ollaborative learning is a widely used pedagogical approach, learners do not necessarily engage in productive learning activities autonomously. Thus, there  is a long-lasting research tradition developing instructional approaches or scaffolding, often implemented with digital technologies, to facilitate the collaborative learning processes (Wise & Schwarz, 2017). In this vein, researchers in the field of CSCL have been working on the development and investigation of adaptive support for collaborative learning with digital tools (Hmelo-Silver & Jeong, 2021) such as intelligent tutoring systems for peer feedback (Walker et al., 2014). Such adaptive systems can be based on automated discourse analysis, performing analysis and adjusting support in real time (Bae et al., 2019; Rosé et al., 2008). Since digital tools based on generative AI (genAI) and large language models (LLMs) became widely available, new opportunities have emerged to develop adaptive support for benef icial CSCL processes (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023, Yan et al., 2024). By reviewing research on the use of genAI in CSCL, our scoping review provide s an overview about existing research and gaps, regarding genAI-based collaborative learning support.

## Theoretical background

The developments of integrating genAI into learning environments enable new forms of supporting collaborative learning. For instance, genAI-based tools can take on a range of roles  substituting support that had so far been mostly offered by humans, such as tutors or peers, or digital tools based on more straightforward algorithms (Yan et al., 2024). In CSCL learning environment s different types of support can be  distinguished. Epistemic support provides content information or guides learners to build understanding about a subject (Weinberger et al., 2005). Social support structures and guides learners through social activities (Vogel et al., 2017). Metacognitive support can provide guidance for planning, monitoring, reflection, or conversation on individual and group levels (Järvelä et al., 2023).  Support can also be geared toward socio-emotional or motivational aspects emerging in group exchange or it can be used to help teachers for classroom orchestration of CSCL (Bae et al., 2019). For example, argumentation scripts (Noroozi et al., 2013) and group awareness tools (Schnaubert & Bodemer, 2022) combine social and metacognitive support in collaborative learning, opening the field to socially shared regulation (Järvelä et al., 2023). Collaborative learning often consists of alternating individual and collaborative learning phases and may lead to  differences to whom the  support is provided. Using metacognitive support  as an example, tools supporting collaborative learning  can target self-regulated learning  (SRL), co-regulation (CoRL) or socially shared regulation of learning (SSRL) (Järvelä et al., 2016). What stands out is that genAI may provide these types of timely support both separately and simultaneously, highlighting its potential to support learners and teachers across diverse tasks and contexts. This support can have diverse functions such as a peer, a tutor, or a co-creator, engaging dynamically with learners rather than serving as a static tool (Yan et al., 2024). 504 Since the release of the highly capable genAI-based conversational agent  ChatGPT 3.5 in November 2022, many publications have appeared regarding the use of genAI in CSCL. Due to its novelty, it is not surprising that the level of development of these tools in research is as diverse as their  research objectives are. Researchers started using the new tools with different objectives: testing the tool’s abilities, observing learners’ use cases and perceptions, or investigating the tools’ influence on learning processes. H ence, the maturity level of genAI tool implementation within learning settings ranges on a spectrum from development to pilot to full implementation into CSCL. To provide an overview of what has been published and how genAI has been used, we c onducted a scoping review guided by three research questions  (RQs). With regard to  the whole scope  of published papers: (RQ1) Which type of papers have been published about the use of genAI in CSCL since November 2022? With a particular focus on the empirical articles: (RQ2a) What aspects of CSCL are supported by and (RQ2b) who are the support recipients of genAI tools used in the identified papers about using genAI in CSCL ? (RQ3) What are the maturity levels of genAI tool’s implementation into CSCL? Following the PRISMA-ScR guidelines, we conducted several steps to identify studies for inclusion in the scoping review. We searched three databases, Scopus, Web of Science, and ERIC, to find relevant articles. The search was restricted to papers published after the release of ChatGPT in 2022. A consistent set of keywords and phrases, adapted to each database’s query input rules, was used in all searches: “(“learning” OR “knowledge building” OR “knowledge co-construction” OR “knowledge acquisition”) AND (“large language model ” OR “generative AI ” OR “ChatGPT” OR “conversational agent” OR “chatbot”) AND (“collaborat*” OR “cooperat*” OR “CSCL”)” To broaden coverage, we included various alternative keywords for “learning”  to retrieve papers that might not include the term learning yet still address education and learning. The search  yielded 1,132 potential articles published between 2022 and September 5, 2024, when the search was initiated. Removing duplicates reduced the dataset to 856 articles. In total, three rounds of coding were performed. The first round focused on applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria to determine relevance, first on the basis of abstracts, then based on the entire paper. In the subsequent coding rounds, inclusion and exclusion criteria were further applied based on  additional information. The three inclusion criteria comprised whether the articles (1) pertain to human learning or research in an educational context, (2) include genAI or LLMs, and (3) involve human learner collaboration. The five exclusion criteria comprised (1) languages other than English, (2) restricted retrieval, (3) published as a list of papers or as a whole book, (4) published before November 2022, and

(5) focusing on interdisciplinary researcher collaboration, or technical systems collaborating with each other. Two

coders trained until they reached acceptable inter-rater reliability (κ > 0.75) for each coding category, using 20% of the sample. Each coder subsequently reviewed half of the  residual articles independently, resulting  in 239 relevant articles identified for the scoping review. For the second round of coding, we developed and both coders applied a coding schem e to the 239 included articles  categorizing the publications into reviews, empirical, conceptual or discussion papers as well as editorials (κ =.662) (RQ1), solving discrepancies through discussion. The third round served to answer RQ2 and RQ3, focusing on empirical articles only (n = 13 8). We developed another coding scheme to categorize the empirical papers into 3 dimensions. Two coders double coded the whole sample for each dimension with acceptable inter-rater reliability (κ ⌀ =.771, ICC⌀ =.828). Discrepancies in coding were re solved through discussion and mutual agreement. The aspects of CSCL support (RQ2a) and support recipient (RQ2 b) w ere operationalized by dimensions ‘ type of support ’, ‘support recipient ’ and ‘educational level of participants’. More than one type  of support could be coded  simultaneously. The type of support provided by genAI for collaborative learning consisted of: epistemic/conceptual, social/collaboration, metacognitive/regulation, socio-emotional/motivational, and orchestration support category. The coding rules are presented in Table 1. The support recipients were categorized into individual, group, both, unspecified and other (e.g., for orchestration). For example, in think-pair-share studies we searched in methods and procedure for which parts the learners were allowed to use the tool. If it was provided for individual preparation, we categorized it as individual support; if it was provided support during sharing, we categorized it as group support; if it was provided throughout the learning process for individual brainstorming as well as sharing, we categorized it as  ‘both’. Beyond that, we coded the educational level of the support recipients distinguishing between university students, other adult learners, school learners, kindergarten and pre-school. The tool’s level of maturity in implementation into the learning setting (RQ3) was operationalized with an inductively built hierarchical categorization. Mostly dependent on the reviewed study’s research focus (e.g., tool performance focus, usability, learning outcomes), the tools’ level of maturity in implementation differed. Research focusing on tool performance was mostly found in 505 design process and coded as ‘in-development’, whereas research that focused on usability and usage perceptions of learners that experimented with genAI-based tools was categorized as ‘pilot.’ Studies concerned with human learning outcomes, that integrated AI tool s in genuine collaborative learning scenarios, received the highest category with ‘implemented’. Furthermore, descriptive sample details about the used AI’s name were coded. Overall sample (n = 239) Starting with  descriptive information  about the article sample, most of the relevant articles (n = 2 39) were published in 2024 (64%). Empirical papers comprise about 58 percent of the entire sample, followed by discussion (20%), conceptual (11%), review articles (10%), and editorials (1%). Empirical articles (n = 138) We first present descriptive details about this sample of empirical articles (n = 138). The research methods applied in these studies are  quantitative only (32%), qualitative only (22%), using both quantitative and qualitative models were the dominating genAI-based tools used in the studies, comprising about 49 percent. GenAI is being used to support a  variety of CSCL aspects.  T he greatest proportion of support type provided in collaborative learning is epistemic/conceptual, followed by metacognitive support. The genAI-based tools were mostly provided to individuals while learning collaboratively (36%), followed by supporting the whole group (26%), or individuals and groups  simultaneously (17%). In most studies the participants were university students (59%) or other adult learners (15%), followed by school learners (9%), and none of the studies reported about participants from kindergarten or pre-school. Around 14 percent included participants of multiple educational levels and 3 percent did not provide information on educational levels of their participants. Figure 1 shows how support recipients are distributed within the support type categories. We note that more than one support type could be provided.

A few examples of the actual implementation may  reveal what lies behind the categorization. In one example, support on an individual level is provided by ChatGPT 3.5 as rewriting tool to improve lab report writing: after students finished writing their drafts individually, they had to use the genAI tool for a co-writing process, what increased knowledge of academic writing norms and audience aw areness (Kim et al., 2024). Another study on human-AI collaboration patterns found differences in performance, depending on the actual use and (metacognitive) support of genAI-assistance in academic writing (Nguyen et al., 2024). Supporting students on an individual level in sharing their own thoughts (Liu et al., 2015) and scaffolding transactive group exchange (Noroozi et al., 2013), de Araujo and colleagues (2024) similarly combined individual support with group level support in their collaborative conversational agent’s design to assess its impact on student dialogue. One article that was coded for all  support types, investigated how to design educational chatbots for collaborative learning. Presenting itself as team member and tutor, the designed Chatbot guides each student group through a learning journey by scaffolding all of our support type categories (Burkhard et al., 2022). Figure 1 506 Distribution of Empirical Articles (n = 138) by Support Type and Support Recipient With regard to the genAI tool’s level of maturity in implementation into CSCL, around 23 percent of the tools used in the empirical studies were still ‘ in-development’, focusing on tool performance and improvement for further development. More articles were in ‘pilot’ (33%), as they mostly provided a genAI-based tool to participants without focusing on specific learning outcomes, but for testing perceptions of usage. Remarkably, in 44 percent of empirical articles the genAI tool was ‘implemented’ into the CSCL setting with a focus on human learning outcomes, collaborative learning processes or learning performance improvement. In total, 56 percent of the tools used in the empirical articles have  not yet moved beyond pilot  implementation, showing the potential for studies to be conducted and published in the future that have genAI-based tools fully implemented. The analysis showed that genAI-based tools have mostly been used for epistemic support in collaborative learning, for instance, to clarify questions, explain concepts, or to generate content (e.g., in form of images, Trizedes et al., 2024). This application does not yet differ far from older CSCL approaches (Vogel et al., 2017). Al though some studies still focus quite heavily on one type, all support types were presented within our findings. Our results show numerous studies that take advantage of the new applications, specifically integrating and exploring the diverse and simultaneous use of support by genAI. Although research about  adaptive collaboration support has been ongoing for some time now (Bae et al., 2019; Rosé et al., 2008, Walker et al., 2014), only the latest developments have made adaptive scaffolding in real time possible  (e.g., Tai & Chen, 2024). The support provision differs in direction to individuals to prepare for collaborative work, to the whole group, or both. Despite the  collaborative learning context, AI support is more often directed toward individuals than groups, and more often toward adults than K-12 learners, representing important limitations in current research. The relatively small number of articles reporting metacognitive support at group level i ndicates a research gap, leaving great opportunities for further research, for example on genAI-based co-and shared-regulation support, to enhance the theoretical and practical understanding of regulation in collaborative learning (Järvelä et al., 2023). Despite the field’s recent emergence, the scoping review revealed a large number of empirical articles, suggesting rapid development and progress in research.

Although the number of studies having genAI tools implemented  into CSCL environments is already surprisingly large given the short time since ChatGPT  3.5 was released, the number of articles with genAI tools ‘in-development’ leaves ample opportunity for further research and levels of development beyond the piloting stage in the future as more research is conducted. The results of this scoping review open the door to deeper analyses of the learning outcomes these AI-supported interventions produce, which will be crucial for understanding their real impact in collaborative learning contexts.
