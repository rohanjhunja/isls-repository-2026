# Designing the Next Generation of Computational Modeling and Learning with AI

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract

As generative AI rapidly transforms how humans interact with computation, it challenges the foundations of many educational technologies designed to teach coding, modeling, and computational thinking. Systems that were once built around writing or arranging code—whether block-based or text-based—now coexist with tools that generate programs through natural language. This workshop invites the learning sciences and design research community to collectively explore how educational technologies that include coding must be re-envisioned in light of these shifts. What will it mean to “code,” “debug,” or “model” when AI becomes a co-author? How might our existing environments—like MoDa, a block-based modeling platform, or text-based NetLogo simulations—need to be rethought for an era where learners reason through prompts, outputs, and model interpretation rather than syntax and structure? Our goal is to identify key design principles, challenges, and opportunities to guide the next generation of AI-powered computational learning environments. Theoretical background and significance Integrating computational thinking (CT) into science education equips students with powerful tools to explore scientific phenomena, foster deeper conceptual understanding, and engage in authentic scientific practices. The interdisciplinary potential of this work empowers students and practitioners to engage meaningfully with emerging local and global challenges (Barr et al., 2011; Weintrop et al., 2016). Existing literature identifies several core CT practices: abstraction, generalization, decomposition, algorithmic thinking, and debugging (Caeli & Yadav, 2020). Computational modeling, including agent-based modeling (ABM), has long been recognized as a valuable tool that can simultaneously support students' science learning and CT (e.g., Wilkerson et al., 2015; Musaeus et al., 2023; Musaeus et al., 2022). Recently, however, developments in Artificial Intelligence (AI) have introduced new possibilities for how learners and educators may interact with computational models (Chen et al., 2023). In the future, it may no longer be necessary to write code to create scientific computer models, putting learners in the role of collaborators with generative AI. For decades, coding has served as both a tool for learning and a language for thinking (Papert, 1980; diSessa, 2000). Block-based and text-based environments embody constructionist ideals, empowering learners to express, test, and refine ideas through computation. Yet today, this paradigm is shifting. AI’s ability to generate executable code from natural language redefines what it means to “speak computation.” As we transition from programming languages to prompting, foundational concepts—such as loops, conditions, and agents—risk becoming invisible. At the same time, new epistemic practices emerge: prompt engineering, iterative interpretation of AI outputs, and ethical reasoning about algorithmic agency. This timely workshop for the field of learning sciences demands that we rethink not only curricula, but also what fundamental concepts in programming that we still need to teach and how to design the tools themselves; how they mediate learning, distribute agency, and scaffold understanding when code is no longer the learner’s sole artifact of construction. Organizing team Line Have Musaeus leads research at the Center for Computational Thinking & Design, focusing on computing education, computational modeling, and STEM learning. Her work explores how integrating computational thinking into school subjects supports both student learning and teacher development. She co-designed CoTinker and has extensive experience organizing workshops on computing and AI in education. Tamar Fuhrmann leads research at the Transformative Learning Technologies Lab at Teachers College, Columbia University. She is a founding leader of the MoDa project, focusing on computational modeling in science education. Her work integrates STEM, AI, and constructionist approaches, and she regularly leads professional development and international collaborations. Paulo Blikstein is an Associate Professor at Columbia University and director of the Transformative Learning Technologies Lab. His research focuses on using technology to t ransform learning in STEM and AI. He founded the FabLearn program and has received major awards, including the NSF CAREER and AERA Early Career Award. Ole Sejer Iversen is a professor at Aarhus University and director of the Center for Computational Thinking & Design. His research focuses on computational empowerment, helping young people become active creators of technology. He works at the intersection of participatory design, computing education, and policy, leading large-scale international projects. Workshop goals and intended audience This workshop is designed for researchers, designers, and educators in the learning sciences, computing education, and educational technology who are exploring the future of computational learning in the age of AI. It will particularly interest those developing or studying coding-based environments—such as block-based, text-based, or modeling tools—and those investigating how generative AI transforms students’ epistemic, creative, and design practices. Participants with backgrounds in design-based research, computational modeling, or AI in education will find opportunities to share perspectives, contribute to collaborative redesign activities, and help shape a research agenda for rethinking the role of coding and modeling in AI-mediated learning environments. Outline of planned activities and facility requirements The four-hour workshop (plus one hour for lunch and coffee) will begin with an opening and framing session (20 minutes) introducing key tensions in AI-integrated coding environments. This will be followed by case study provocations (40 minutes), during which participants engage with two computational modeling environments: MoDa, a block-based agent-based modeling tool, and CoTinker, a collaborative modeling environment. Participants will explore and design models while considering how these tools might evolve as learners use AI to generate, modify, and explain models. In the collaborative design challenge (60 minutes), small groups will reimagine an educational technology that includes coding, redesigning it to integrate AI while preserving learners’ epistemic and creative engagement. This will lead into a group synthesis and discussion (40 minutes), during which participants share insights, identify emerging principles, and surface tensions, producing a poster as the group outcome. The workshop will conclude with a closing reflection (20 minutes), focusing on articulating open research questions and opportunities for future collaboration. Participation requirements Participation is open to researchers, designers, educators, and graduate students interested in computational modeling, coding education, or AI-supported learning environments. No specific technical expertise or prior experience with educational learning environments is required. We would like to support a dynamic, collaborative environment in which multiple perspectives on the redesign of coding-based educational technologies can emerge. Relationship to similar events conducted in the past Over the past several years, ISLS conferences have hosted numerous workshops and symposia exploring computational modeling, data science education, and AI-supported learning, for example, Kafai et al., 2025; Wagh et al., 2024; Fuhrmann et al., 2022; Fuhrmann et al., 2024; and McElhaney et al., 2020. These events advanced theoretical and design frameworks for integrating computational practices into science and engineering education. However, few have directly addressed the implications of generative AI for redesigning the educational technologies that include coding, particularly how block-based and text-based modeling environments must evolve when learners can construct programs through natural language. This workshop builds on that lineage but reframes the central question: not how to teach coding, but how coding itself—and the tools built around it—should be reimagined for an AI-mediated future. By convening researchers working at the intersection of computational modeling, design-based research, and AI in education, the session extends ongoing ISLS dialogues toward a new design agenda: understanding how AI reshapes the epistemic and pedagogical foundations of computational learning tools. Expected outcome and outputs The workshop will strengthen a community of practice focused on designing tools that preserve human sense-making and agency alongside AI assistance. It will generate open-ended questions that help rethink computational educational technologies in an AI-mediated context, and build a shared vocabulary around emerging hybrid practices where coding, prompting, and modeling intersect. Rather thandefining what the future of coding should be, the session aims to open a dialogue on how our tools—and our field—must evolve to meet it.
