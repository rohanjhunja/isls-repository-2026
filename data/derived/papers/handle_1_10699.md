# Immersive Virtual Field Trips to Enhance Inquiry-Based Learning on Climate Change

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates the use of immersive virtual reality in climate change education as part of an inquiry-based learning environment designed for middle school students. A pre-existing 360-degree documentary about melting glaciers and a newly designed interactive sea level rise experience were used as field trips in the exploration phase of the 5E inquiry model. We employed a learning progression based on sea level rise to identify learning outcomes and assessment strategies. Students' test scores significantly improved after the learning experience. This finding was further supported by students’ responses written in a formative assessment tool. The results demonstrate the successful integration of immersive technologies into learning environments related to climate change education and highlight its effectiveness.

## Introduction

Sea level rise, one of the severe consequences of c limate change, has far-reaching impacts on coastal areas, including erosion, storm surges, and saltwater intrusion. Addressing climate change through education with learner-centered, inquiry-based, and hands-on learning opportunities is essential to combat climate change (Monroe et al., 2019). However, learning about climate change is challenging given that it h as invisible causes and distant consequences (Busch et al., 2019). Most of the human population today lives in urbanized cities, where their daily lives are not affected by climate extre mes that much, which leads to people being  psychologically distant from climate change and thereby less willing to take climate-friendly actions and decisions (Markowitz & Beilenson, 2021).

Using immersive virtual reality (IVR) is a promising technology-enhanced embodied learning approach to make the consequences of climate change more real, close, current, and relevant for learners (Fauville et al., 2020). More specifically, immersive virtual field trips (iVFTs) offer simulated real-life experiences by providing presence, immersion, embodiment, and interaction (Klippel et al., 2020). Embodied learning suggests that bodily experiences influence how people learn by connecting new experiences with past ones (Nathan, 2021). Firsthand embodied experiences provided by iVFTs make it possible to g et connected with environmental problems more closely as body and sensory experiences are part of the learning processes. Therefore, iVFTs can play an important role in climate change education. Previous studies showed that IVR can improve knowledge gain in environmental topics (e.g., Markowitz et al., 2018; Plechatá et al., 2022). Despite IVR's potential, only a limited number of studies  (Petersen et al., 2020; Makransky & Mayer,

2022) have examined the use of iVFTs embedded into an inquiry-based learning (IBL) environment on climate

change for middle school students.  Considering the positive effects of immersive learning experiences  in these studies and joining the call for  research on the integration of IVR into real educational settings through learn ing activities (Radianti et al., 2020), this study examined middle school students' understanding of the impacts of sea level rise after participating in an iVFTs-integrated IBL experience (iVFTs-IBL) about sea level rise. In this study, we utilized the 5E inquiry-based model  and a  particular learning progression  (LP) for considering the pedagogical aspects of the IVR-integrated learning environment. We structured the learning experience in five phases of the 5E learning cycle: engagement, exploration, explanation, elaboration, and evaluation (Bybee et al., 2006). Furthermore, while designing the iVFTs-IBL environment, we employed the LP on sea level rise proposed by Breslyn et al. (2016)  in identifying the learning outcomes and determini ng assessment strategies. Our research question was “How d oes sixth-grade students’ level of understanding of sea level rise change after participating in iVFTs-IBL experience about sea level rise?”.

## Context and participants

The study was conducted as an after-school workshop in the virtual reality lab oratory of a boarding school, accepting students from across Turkey. The school is funded by donations for students whos e parents passed away. We purposefully selected this school as it already has an active virtual reality laboratory, and its` students are from a broad socio-economic distribution. The participants were 38 sixth-grade students (19 females) who were selected from volunteer students. The criteria  for selection were (a) being already familiar with  IVR technology and (b) not taking any prior courses related to environmental education or climate change. Instruments We used the Impacts of Sea Level Rise Assessment In strument (ISLRAI) to assess students’ understanding of sea level rise before and after the iVFTs-IBL experience. ISLRAI is an adapted version of the assessment instrument developed by Breslyn et al.

(2016) and contains five multiple-choice questions, each

accompanied by an explanation prompt. We scored students’ responses by a rubric developed based on the levels and the described students’ thinking, in the utilized LP. To indicate internal consistency, the instrument was given to 1 54 middle school students in another school with similar char acteristics to the participants (Cronbach’s 끫뷸 = 0.79).

We traced the process of learning with the formative assessment KLEW (know, learn, evidence, wonder)  chart is a graphic organizer to activate past knowledge, creat e learning goals, and arrange new information  (see Figure 1). With the KLEW chart, students can link their evidence from the investigation with their previous knowledge and claims.

Analysis Before addressing the research questions, we checke d for assumption of normal sampling distribution. Then, the paired samples t-test was performed to check learning gains after participating in iVFTs-IBL experience. The effect size for the paired samples t-test was calculated as Cohen’s d and reported. Data collected through the KLEW chart was analyzed with content analysis to support the quantitative findings and to examine the development of students' understanding of sea level rise in detail. Main emerging concepts in written responses of students were determined. For the “know” and “learn” parts, the frequencies and percentages of these concepts were calculated and tabulated to represent their level of understanding.

## Results

The paired samples t-test revealed that participants’ understanding of s ea level rise has significantly improved after participating in the iVFTs-IBL experience, t(38)=-7.68, p <.001. The mean ISLRAI score increased from M = 7.82 (SD = 3.85) to M = 22.63 (SD = 3.52) with a very strong effect size (r = -1.25) as measured by Cohen’s d. In qualitative findings, five main concepts were generated in the analysis of the students’ responses to the “what I know” part of the KLEW chart (Table 2). 28 out of 38  students initially related sea level rise with melting glaciers. For example, one student wrote “Glaciers are melting, and the melting glaciers flow into the sea, and so the sea level is rising ”. 15 students stated a relationship between temperature, ice melting and  water. One said that “As the temperature rises, the glaciers cannot stand  the heat and they melt. ” Another one noted that “Since ice is made up of water when the ice melts, it turns into water, raising the sea level.” Some students related melting ice to climate change. For example, they stated things like “Sea level has risen due to global warming”, “I know that climate change causes the melting of glaciers.”. ## Discussion

This study investigated the use of immersive virtual reality in an inquiry-based learning environment. The results showed that students who participated in a two-hour-long iVFTs-IBL experience significantly improved their understanding of sea level rise. Despite the short implementation time, the iVFTs-IBL experience was effective in improving students' understanding, as described in the LP. The cognitive gains shown in this study are parallel to previous research that uses immersive technology for climate change learning (e.g., Markowitz et al., 2018). We designed the iVFTs-IBL experience based on a LP; therefore, the current study also supports the claim that LPs can be used to develop instructional approaches, materials, and assessment practices (Sztajn et al., 2012). Also, the results are consistent with research on investigating short-term LPs and learning trajectories, such as what happens over a course or an instructional unit (Steedle & Shavelson, 2009). The main limitations of the study are the short duration of the learning experience and the lack of delayed test due to the fact that the study  was embedded in a one time after school workshop. The m ain aim of climate change education is helping people to adopt pro-environmental behaviors which is expected to happen over a long time. However, this study only shows short term lea rning gains related to sea level rise. Still,  this research highlights the value of IVR-supported inquiry-based learning and the use of LPs for framing these experiences.
