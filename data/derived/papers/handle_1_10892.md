# Student Engagement as Microcultural: Empirical and Conceptual Arguments from a Teacher Residency Program

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Much is known about individual-to school-levels of student engagement, but more work is needed for local-to global-contexts. We worked with a 15-month residency program in the Northeast US, conducting two focus groups that sought to better understand Residents’, Mentors’, Administrators’, and students’ conceptions and perceptions of student engagement. Our qualitative phenomenological study evidences what we call microcultural engagement, a framing that juxtaposes smaller-and larger-scale levels of learning and development.

## Introduction: Motivation and research questions

Student engagement has broad appeal within and between many fields associated with the learning sciences, both on its own merits and as connected with theories of  motivation, flow, and self-directed learning (Reschly & Christenson, 2022). Some scholars have critiqued th e construct as being too broad, individualistic, or  malleable, creating disagreements across roles (e.g., practitioner, res earcher, evaluator), specializations (e.g., instruc tion, psychology, management), and life stages (e.g., pK-12, 13-20, career, informal), ultimately leading to calls for more unifying models of student engagement (Galindo et al., 2022; Jackson, 2022). We partnered with pre-service teacher Residents in a 15-month residency program.  In this study w e sought to answer the research questions,

1.  How do Residents in a teacher residency program conceive of student engagement?

2.  How do Residents, Mentors, Administrators, and Students perceive student engagement?

3.  What, if any, connections can be made between Resid ency Program design and its influence on

conceptions and perceptions of student engagement?

## Methods

We worked with  the “Aes University Residency Program” (AURP; all p rogram, school, and participant names are pseudonyms) in a Rust Belt city of the Northeast US. All 21 Residents of Cohort 4 were invited to participate in focus groups, six accepted the invitation, and five completed one of the two focus groups. Though this amounts to convenience sampling, it is also consistent with  representative sampling (Creswell, 2013), given that the participant demographics are similar to overall Resident demographics. Our research design is a qualitative phenomenological study, seeking to deeply understand the essences of Residents’ lived experiences during the AURP (Creswell, 2013). The full interview protocol is available upon request. A total of 90 minutes of audio were transcribed. The two authors proofread transcriptions. We performed two rounds of coding, with analytical memoing between the rounds (Saldaña, 2009). The first round included deductive codes per a six-dimensional framework for student engagement  (Jackson, 2022) expanded from a four-dimensional framework by Fredricks and colleagues (2016). Namely, we took the omnibus fourth dimension of social engagement and divided it into social-affective, social-behavioral, and social-cognitive dimensions. Our second round consisted of pattern and axial cod ing (Saldaña, 2009). Finally, we shared a draft for Residents’ and program staff members’ feedback.

## Results

Residents’ conceptions and perceptions, as related to program design: “Oh, sh*t! There’s a better way to do this.” Coursework, incl uding seminars, provided space for Residents to expand th eir philosophical and pedagogical awareness, specifically suited to the local  context of a city in the Rust Belt of the Northeast US. Cat herine summarized such expansion when she shared that during her coursework she realized, “Oh, sh*t! There’s a better way to do this [teaching].” Kristina echoed these s entiments, saying that compared to her own K-12 education, the AURP revealed that there could be a “much more enjoyable teaching experience”. In terms of engagem ent, the more academic dimensions of behavioral and cognitive engagement w ere enhanced with more humanistic dimensions of affective/emotional and social engagement. Mentors’ changes in perceptions and practices: “Against the grain… a lot” In general, Mentors led more direct-instruction and teacher-centered activities, relative to Residents’ conceptions and enactments of student engagement. And though so me Mentors appeared not to have changed, other Mentors had slight or substantial changes in their percepti ons of student engagement. Kristina noted that Resi dents were often going “against the grain” in terms of their M entors’ approaches, a notion echoed by Catherine’s metaphor of “crashing with the older generation”.  Contrastingly, Jayda’s Mentor had openness to socia l and affective/emotional engagement, which enabled the Mentor to connect more deeply with her students, despite them having vastly different racial and ethnic backgrounds. Administrators’ perceptions of instructional approaches and student engagement: “Get with our program”, “not pushing”, “all about it”, or “join in clubs” In the sample of five participants, there was wide variation of regressive, neutral, progressive, and extra-curricular approaches to engagement. Respectively, some Administrators had perceptions of student en gagement as quiet and compliant, expecting that Residents and Mentors  would “get with [the Administrators’] program”; a second approach of Administrators trusted teachers’ own perceptions, or perhaps did not care, by “not pushing” their own visions; a third approach was “all about it”, meaning student-centered and project-based learning; and a fourth approach perceived engagement as what happened outside of school hours, such as students “[joining] in clubs”.

## Discussion

This study reported on conceptions of student engagement from pre-service teachers in a residency program, along with perceptions of Residents, their Mentors, and A dministrators. Overall, we found more affective/emotional and social orientations towards engagement from the  Residents, relative to the more behavioral and cognitive approaches of Mentors and Administrators. Nonethele ss, there was evidence of slight or substantial shi fts for some Mentors, along with much natural variation in the perspectives of Administrators. On a conceptual level, we believe the evidence supp orts a framing of student engagement that honors both micro-and macro-level dynamics (Galindo et al., 2022), which we call microcultural engagement. The more individual and psychological connotations of the pr efix “micro-“ are juxtaposed with the more collective and social connotations of “culture”, in a way that ref lects a dialectical approach to levels of analysis that are often isolated in research on student engagement (Reschly & Christenson, 2022). In terms of practice, a microcultural stance on stu dent engagement would compel practitioners, researchers, and evaluators to co-define with learners what “counts” as engagement. I n the case of residency programs, Residents – many of whom are much closer to their students than  are Mentors or Administrators – could use their microcultural expertise to “mentor up”, or guide others who might have Mentor/ Administrator status, yet who still have much to learn about/with the youth in their classrooms. Though this work is still in progress and of a rela tively small scale, we believe that it merits furth er analysis, especially of artifacts generated directly from Mentors and Administrators. We believe this wo rk could help move pK-12 student engagement from its current status as construct or meta-construct, and towards a theory of microcultural engagement that would prove useful for youth and those supporting holistic learning.
