# The Power of Gestures: Video Club to Support Teacher Awareness of Gestures as a Form of Multimodal Science Instruction

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teacher candidates attend to many responsibilities and interactions during lesson enactment. Video club (Barnhart & van Es, 2020; Jilk 2016) can narrow reflection to focus on specific aspects of teaching. This study explores the power of video club to develop awareness of multimodal resources, such as gestures. We analyze data from a science methods course, finding that video club makes visible teachers candidates’ subconscious gestures and can position gestures as resources for inclusive science teaching.

## Objectives and significance

Gestures offer powerful representations of science that promote understanding (Herrera & Riggs, 2013; Johnson & Cotterman, 2015; Williams, 2020). Yet, the complexity of teaching can prevent teacher candidates (TC) from noticing the subconscious gestures that they use to support learning and the gestures that students use to express understanding (Johnson & Cotterman, 2015). To this end, we ask: How can video analysis help s cience TCs see their gestures and students’ gestures as powerful learning tools that support student sensemaking? This paper illustrates  the power of video as a reflective tool for TCs to notice and le verage gestures, focusing on how video club (Barnha rt & van Es, 2020; Jilk, 2016) can be used to draw attention to gestures as ways to support science learning by: (1) creating safe spaces for students, (2) directing attention, and (3) representing science concepts.

## Method

Data for this study comes from a middle childhood science methods course at a large university in the midwestern US (20 students total). As part of the course, TCs par ticipated in video clubs in which they selected short video clips of their teaching (3-4 minutes) from their field placement to watch and discuss in small groups (five TCs and a facilitator from the research team). We use pseudonyms that were chosen by TCs to represent themselves. In this paper, we focus on one video club in which a TC, Luke, shares a video clip. The facilitator (Blue) noticed the significance of gestures in the clip and focused the discussion on the role of gesture. The video was watched one time through without interruption. During the second playback, the video  was repeatedly paused for discussion (Johnson & Cotterman, 2015). At the conclusion of video club a brief survey and semi-structured interview captured TCs’ key “take-aways” from the experience. Interaction analysis and inductive coding were used to explore how video club supported teachers in noticing how gestures can be used to support science learning (Charmaz, 2006; Jordan & Henderson, 1995).

## Findings and discussions

Our analytic focus on gesture emerged from a video club segment where Luke used her hands to talk about tectonic plate movement. She described these gestures as subconscious: “I also talk with my hands, that’s just happening, it’s subconscious.” We show how video club helped Luke and her peers focus on these subconscious gestures as resources for learning that they could leverage intentionally in their future teaching.  We propose that video club allowed TCs time and space to slow down and see gestures that they might not have noticed in the moment as they were teaching. Here, we focus on how viewing and discussing the vi deo helped TCs attend to gestures to support learni ng by: (1) creating a safe space for students, (2) directing attention and (3) conveying scientific information. First, TCs attended to gesture as a means to create a safe and inviting space for students. Abbey commented “Your body language was really positive. Of just lik e ‘come on up.’ I feel like that's kind of underest imated in teachers today, like creating that safe space for kids.” Blue built on this comment by asking how body language might affect class dynamics. Elton replied, “I notice what Luke’s doing. She's spending more time facing outward, towards them, her arms out giving a very open, welcoming body language.” Additionally, Elton interpreted the body language of students as providing valuable feedback to Luke regarding student engagement: “While the students, instead of having their back to her and looking behind their s houlder, they're actually turned towards her. He paused the video to note tha t Luke’s body language helped direct students to a pictorial representation of plate boundaries, saying: “I thin k that sort of positioning (Luke turned towards the  screen) gives students a cue to kind of also put their attention on that graphic.” Third, Blue explicitly connected gesture to science concepts. She pointed out that Luke brought her hands together to illustrate a convergent plate boundary, then moved them apart to demonstrate a divergent plate boundar y, while at the same time verbally emphasizing the prefixes of each word to bridge hand motion tonew science voca bulary (Williams, 2020). In this way, Blue offered TCs a lens for seeing gestures as visual representations of science content to support student understanding. Prior to video club Luke identified her use of hand  gestures as being “subconscious.” Video club allowed Luke and the other teachers in their group to atten d to gestures as resources for learning and to cons ider intentional uses of gestures in their future teaching. The impact of gestures was evident through its emergence as an analytic focus during the last two video clubs. It also surfaced during interviews where three of the five TCs referenced gestures as memorable. Lindy stated, “Body language was something I recognized a lot in others' videos and my own. ” Xavier described how he embodied Luke’s movement in his fi eld placement, “I am more of, like, a lower energy kind of presence in the classroom. So, like that next week,  like I tried to step it up a little bit. You know,  I had a little extra coffee and moved around a lot more.” Xavier’s effor t to enact movement in his own classroom is evidenc e of the impact of video club on his teaching.

## Conclusions and implications

Our data demonstrate how video club can help TCs se e the power in gestures, elevating them from subcon scious motions to resources that can be intentionally used to support learning about science concepts. Video club allows TCs time to pause and reflect on this singular aspect o f teaching, even though they may have been attendin g to other elements as they were teaching. Our findings suggest that video clubs could be used to support noticing and reflection about gestures in this and other contexts (science education courses, professional development), which could support effective and inclusive science teaching.
