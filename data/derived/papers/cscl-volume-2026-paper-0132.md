# Principle-Driven Scaffolding: Implementing and Evaluating LLM-Generated Micro-Scripts in an Informal Learning Community

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
How can AI scaffold collaboration in voluntary online communities without over-scripting participation? This poster presents Principle-Driven Micro-Script Generation (PDMSG), an LLM-based approach that translates activity objectives, local constraints, learning principles, and recent community activity logs into optional weekly suggestions for each member. In a seven-week deployment of PDMSG in BookClub, text volume per Presence increased without a corresponding rise in post-count density, and participants rated the suggestions as readable and minimally coercive.

Informal online communities need support that encourages collaborative inquiry without turning participation into compliance. In CSCL, collaboration scripts have long been a major approach to providing such guidance by structuring roles, sequences, and procedures for interaction (Kollar et al., 2006; Fischer et al., 2013). At the same time, too much scripting may undermine agency, especially in voluntary and asynchronous settings where goals, topics, and participation shift over time (Dillenbourg, 2002; Johnson & Majewska, 2022).  Recent work on generative AI, shared control, and AI-supported regulation suggests that this issue remains salient as AI enters collaborative learning design (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023; Hernández-Leo et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025). To implement this idea, a framework called Principle-Driven Micro-Script Generation (PDMSG) has been proposed (Kondo, 2025). PDMSG integrates four elements into a single prompt for an LLM to generate context-aware micro-scripts for each member: (1) activity objectives, (2) local constraints, (3) learning principles, and (4) recent activity logs.

The initial PDMSG proposal tested a script-generation method grounded in the 12 Knowledge Building principles. It showed that the generated scripts could be context-relevant, actionable, and theory-aligned, but it did not evaluate whether the method functi oned in a live setting.  This poster reports a field deployment in BookClub and focuses on the feasibility, acceptability, and behavioral tendencies associated with its introduction. Principle-Driven Micro-Script Generation Figure 1 presents the PDMSG pipeline. The shared inputs were the activity objectives, local constraints, learning principles, and recent community activity logs. These inputs were  combined to generate, for each member, the collaboration script itself and an explanation of the rationale for its generation. In this deployment, the learning-principles slot was instantiated with the 12 Knowledge Building  (KB) principles. The unit of generation was the individual member. Because BookClub is a voluntary and emergent community, the prompt did not assign stable roles such as questioner or summarizer, as fixed role allocation risked over-scripting participation. Still, the generated suggestions could emphas ize moves analogous to questioning, synthesizing, or linking ideas, depending on recent logs and the shared community context. ## Method

We employed a single-group pre-post comparison design, establishing a seven-week intervention period within the active BookClub. BookClub is a small-scale, long-running informal reading community that uses a threaded improvement of ideas rather than predefined performance goals, making it a useful setting for KB-grounded scaffolding (Kondo, 2025; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 1994;  Scardamalia, 2002). Six members (excluding the facilitator) participated.

During the intervention, every Monday morning, we generated a unique collaboration script and its rationale for each member and sent it by email, explicitly stating that members were free to ignore the suggestion. The quantitative log analysis focused on the asynchronous discussion board. W e defined Presence as the weekly sum of daily visits to the discussion board, capped at two per day, and analyzed posts and characters per unit of Presence ("presence density") across the baseline and intervention periods. Following the intervention, we administered a survey with 5-point Likert items and open-ended questions; open-ended responses were reviewed descriptively. This study does not claim causal effects; it examines  feasibility, acceptability, and associated behavioral tendencies. The Likert items were designed to probe script acceptability, feasibility under busyness, and perceived changes in the KB-consistent activities that the intervention was intended to support. The main pattern in the log data was not in post-count density, but in the volume of content per unit of Presence. New-text density increased from 39.8 to 44.6 characters per Presence (RR = 1.119), and reply-text density increased from 71.1 to 100.8 (RR = 1.418), while post-count density remained stable or slightly lower. At the individual level, the text volume of new  posts increased for all six participants, and the text volume of replies increased for five of the six members.

Survey responses were broadly consistent with this pattern. R eadability was high (4.7/5), while specificity (3.8/5) and feasibility under busyness (3.7/5) were moderate to high; perceived coerciveness was low (2.0/5). Members also reported increases, particularly in following up on existing threads and in summarizing or organizing ideas. Contextual fit was rated moderately (3.0/5), and one open-ended response noted that "[The system] treated me like an expert in an area I had only mentioned briefly, which made me feel pressured and unable to write anything." This study examined whether PDMSG was acceptable to members and associated with KB-oriented participation patterns in BookClub. The clearest shift was not in post-count density but in text volume per unit of Presence. Taken together with participants’ self-reports, this pattern may reflect more elaborated contribution practices, especially following up on existing threads and summarizing or organizing ideas.  The low perceived coerciveness ratings provide no clear indication of perceived coercion in this setting. However, this should not be taken as a full measure of epistemic agency.

At the same time, this small-scale, seven-week study of six participants lacked a comparison condition, so the results indicate association  rather than causation; specifically, we cannot separate PDMSG  effects from those of regular weekly suggestion emails. While text volume per Presence may be a useful proxy here, finer-grained analysis remains future work. Open-ended responses suggest two design improvements: weighting recent logs more heavily and linking  to the posts underlying each script. Embedding this support in a KB-oriented environment such as Knowledge Forum may make each suggestion’s basis  more visible, though whether that would change the pattern remains an open question.

This seven-week study provides initial indications of the feasibility and  acceptability of the PDMSG framework in this setting. In an implementation that incorporated the 12 Knowledge Building principles, system logs revealed a consistent increase in text volume per Presence, and survey responses correspondingly suggested increases in activities such as following up on, summarizing, and organizing existing ideas. Taken together, these findings suggest that PDMSG may help link collaboration scripts with KB-oriented activity and support the translation of theory into situated practice.

For the poster session, we invite discussion on (1) how much pipeline transparency is needed to make PDMSG reusable; (2) what more direct and fine-grained measures of epistemic depth and agency should complement presence density and perceived coerciveness; and (3) how this kind of support might be embedded in KB-oriented platforms.
