# Social and Emotional Learning (SEL) in Elementary Engineering Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Although social and emotional learning (SEL) has become more commonplace in schools, currently available frameworks (e.g., CASEL) do not detail how SEL can be used in the elementary engineering context. Using secondary analysis of transcript excerpts from the literature, this project explores which specific SEL competencies are beneficial for elementary students engaged in engineering group work and attempts to demonstrate how SEL has a place in engineering education.

## Introduction

Engineering design is a social endeavor (Pawley, 20 19) and when introduced into the classroom, it chal lenges students not only academically, but socially and emotionally as well. At the elementary school level in particular, engineering design curricula are almost always enac ted with the expectation that students work in team s. This approach puts students in positions where they must  deal with not only their own emotions, but also with the emotions of their peers. Situations of uncertainty in engineering can lead to tension and frustration (Jordan & McDaniel, 2014), stirring up negative feelings that can impact how students view themselves within engineering. To counter these possibilities, the movement for so cial and emotional learning (SEL) can be benefici al to elementary students. Specifically, we hypothesize t hat two main SEL competencies, Relationship Skills and Social Awareness, can help students navigate engine ering design challenges by providing them the neces sary skills to work in teams. This project  uses a secondary analysis (Hinds et al., 1997) appr oach to explore the question: How do elementary students use specific SEL competencies when engaging in engineering group work? Social and Emotional Learning (SEL) SEL encompasses the process through which individua ls come to recognize and handle emotions, establish  and work toward personal goals, develop positive relati onships with others, and exercise responsibility (J agers et al., 2019).  Within the United States, a popular resource for SEL curriculum is the CASEL framework, which includes five main areas of competency: (1) self-awareness, (2) self-management, (3) social awareness, (4) relationship skills, and (5) responsible decision-making (CASEL, n.d.). Each main competency has several sub-competencies. This project focuses on the Social Awareness and Re lationship Skills competencies and their importance  to elementary school engineering.  Social Awareness (S A) involves students being able to take the perspec tive of others and demonstrate empathy and compassion. Rela tionship Skills (RS) address the skills students ne ed to effectively communicate, actively listen, and cooperate with others in order to develop healthy relationships.

## Methods and findings

Following a secondary analysis approach (Hinds et a l., 1997), in this qualitative study, we constructe d a data set consisting of transcript excerpts from the pre-college engineering education research literature. In order to be included in the data set, transcripts needed to showcase upper elementary students (grades 4-6) and include enough dialogue and context to provide a clear understanding of what students were doing during their engineering design challenges. The final data set, consisting of 9 tra nscript excerpts from 5 different publications, provide d insight on the kinds of SEL (sub)competencies students demo nstrated when participating in engineering design challenges with their peers. We coded the moments o f dialogue within the transcripts for evide nce of five sub-competencies of Social Awareness (SA) and Relations hip Skills (RS): recognizing strength in others  (SA), showing concern for the feelings of others (SA), recognizing situational demands and opportunities  (SA), resolving conflicts constructively (RS), and communicating effectively (RS). We then used constant comparative analysis across the coded moments to generate engin eering-specific definitions of these SEL sub-competencies. In the section below, we share the results for two of these sub-competencies. Social awareness (SA): “Higher Level than Us” Social Awareness (SA) consists of perspective-taking, empathizing and feeling compassion for othe rs, and understanding social norms to promote personal and collective well-being (Jagers et al., 2019).  In one excerpt in our data set, a group of fifth-grade girls were attempting to construct a claw car using  building instructions from online. One of the girls, Shamitra, had difficulty with the physical structures and ended up relying o n her teammates to better understand the problem: … she’s like at a higher level than us and sometime s we don’t understand because she has all these fancy words that we don’t know yet; so then w e’re like “Huh? What does that mean?” like, I wasn’t sure. You know, I actually started t o figure out … like fancy words about the robot, but [I would say to Adriana], “I’m not reall y sure about what you’re saying and what it means or how it’s helping us right now,” but then she’s like, “Well, this word means this,” and I’m like, “Oh, okay” (Interview, 6/1, as cited in Jordan & McDaniel, 2014, p. 518) In this excerpt, Shamitra demonstrates the SA sub-competency of recognizing strength in others  by acknowledging that Adriana is at a “higher level” t han her and taking advantage of that strength to be tter understand the content material. It is important to  point out that because of the positive way Adriana  reacts to Shamitra’s need for help, Shamit ra feels compelled to keep seeking out Adriana’s ex pertise. This excerpt shows how recognizing strength in others candevelop trust and cooperation within teams. Relationship skills (RS): “Argument with nobody” Relationship Skills (RS) consists of clear communic ation, active listening, cooperation, and negotiati ng conflict in order to establish and maintain meaningful relationships (Jagers et al., 2019). In another excerpt, a student, Jata, explains why she proposed combining everyone’s ideas together: Because, sometimes, I can get in arguments. Because  you don’t want Ms. Humphrey thinking you’re in an argument with nobody. You get in an ar gument then it turns out to be a fight then you get suspended, and you get a whoopin’ at home, you now? (Wright et al., 2018, p. 294) This moment was coded as evidence of the RS sub-competency resolving constructs constructively. In this specific case, Jata’s decision to combine ever yone’s ideas together was her strategy to avoid con flict. For Jata, in this situation, resolving a conflict “constructively” means not getting into an argument at all.

## Discussion and conclusions

While our analysis treated each transcript excerpt as exemplifying a single sub-competency, the reality is that multiple sub-competencies can be demonstrated at the same time. For instance, “Argument with Nobody” was showcased as an example of relationship skills, but it also can be seen as demonstrating self-awareness. One main finding from this work is that conflict resolution is not straightforward in elementary engineering groups. What it looks like to resolve conflicts  “constructively” differs across groups of students, and it is also influenced by students’ attunement to the broader set of classroo m behavioral expectations. For elementary students,  one key strategy appears to be avoiding conflict in the fir st place. This study reveals how adept students are at predicting consequences of emotional display  during classroom engineering activities, a theme that appears in both social awareness and relationship skills examples. Students seem to have a dual goal of solving the engineering problem at hand while also preventing new social problems from emerging in the classroom.
