# Youth Designers’ Insights on Information Literacy Learning Tools

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines youth designers’ insights on the Publication Facts Label (PFL), a tool designed to enhance the evaluation of research articles. Using a Youth Participatory Design Research approach, high school students redesigned the PFL to better meet local needs. Students emphasized features like summative evaluations of trustworthiness (e.g., approval stamps, percentage scores) and simplified access to critical information. We discuss implications for empowering communities to evaluate information while fostering critical literacy skills.

## Background

Today, American public audiences frequently encount er misinformation online, primarily through user-created content platforms like social media and blogs, on i ssues like health and climate (e.g., Chen, 2024; Su arez-Lledo & Avarez-Galvez, 2021). For the past few years, the authors have been working on the challenge of misinformation with a non-profit organization focused on supporting public engagement with research. They have recently turned to youth and their communities to p rovide new insights for interve ntion design, with their social media expertise in mind.

The Publication Facts Label (PFL) is an information tool that has shown early success in supporting high schoolers and science journalists in evaluating the  trustworthiness of information in research journal s. This tool, designed to look and function like the Nutrition Facts Label (but instea d provides information about essential publication criteria), is affixed to the top of aca demic journal articles and has shown to help suppor t journalists’ and high schoolers’ evaluation of the quality of information (Willinsky & Pimentel, 2024). Early adoption of the tool has already begun in several South American jo urnals and is now expanding more widely to European journals. We have begun work to center American you th and their local communities in guiding the inter vention toward public audience use.

## Research methodology

Our study employs Youth Participatory Design Research (YPDR), a form of Youth Participatory Action Research, which centers youth as design researchers (Southert on, in press). Through our research, we asked: How do students design to support their communities in und erstanding the trustworthiness of research articles ? What features of an intervention do they design? What is their rationale?

The study took place in a small city outside of Phi ladelphia, in a charter high school AP research cla ss. There, 15 students followed the YPDR approach over three class days. The youth used an abbreviated des ign process–Empathize, Imagine, and Create –to redesign the PFL to better suit their communitie s’ needs. First, students “empathized” with their communities’ by co llecting data on information literacy from 15 peers  and 15 “community members” (which included friends and family members of their choice at home) using pre-designed data collection tools. Students then collectively a nalyzed their data, established common themes, and imagined multiple ways to incorporate their resulting insights into a PFL re-design. Finally, the students created prototypes of new and improved labels.

We analyzed students’ final design documents, their  presentations of those designs, and focus groups where students further clarified the reasoning behind their designs, including purpose and audience. The research team also took field notes. We conducted a content analysis of these data sources using multiple round s of open coding. The team coded student artifacts and explanations for 1) design features, 2) targeted audience for feature, and 3) rationale of feature. The research team then conducted a thematic analysis of each area to identify broader patterns in students’ designs and rationales.

## Findings

Based on our analyses, we identified themes in stud ents’ designs related to their features, target aud iences, and rationale.

Design features All of the groups designed labels with new, promine nt “summative” evaluations of trustworthiness. Thes e summative evaluations were based on a cumulation of  features that researchers had previously provided via the initial Publication Facts Label. Additionally, all groups retained some of these disaggregated, educative evaluation features of the PFL, including indicators of peer r eview, journal selectivity, research funding, and c onflicts of interest. All groups kept aspects of the PFL’s lear ning tools and added new educative features like si mplified language in information pop-ups, as well as hyperlinks to more information. There are several ways that students designed “summ ative” evaluations of trustworthiness. One group designed a stamp of approval at the top of a journal article, which students compared to FDA approval at multiple points (in design sessions, presentations,  and focus groups). Students shared that the approva l stamp is directly connected–both locally through a link but also informationall y–to the considerations of trustworthiness determined by academics by “adding up” the PFL’s features to a composite score that determines approval. Other groups designed a similar composite trustworthiness  score, with one including a percent trustworthines s in their design. A member of the third group shared that they designed a visual summation of trustworthiness that operated similarly to a traffic light, giving readers the “r ed, yellow, or green” light to move forward and rea d. In addition to the “FDA” label, the first group used a similar sliding scale of trustworthiness. In this last case, the sliding scale included an information button on the score dot that helps readers see why the article is scored a certain way. Audience Students shared in presentations and focus groups t hat they made these and other design decisions for users like themselves, their parents, and the general public. Students found the information on the original PFL to be useful in light of their recent project on Literature Reviews but felt it was difficult to interpret quickly, resulting in a lack of efficiency that they felt was necessary for the PFL to gain public use. Students viewed their parents/communities as less savvy and up-to-date on how to effectively use the label as is to make decisions about the trustworthiness of information. One described their parent as not valuing the information (e.g., funding source) in the same way that they had come to. Therefore, s tudent designers wanted to support th e reader in interpreting the label more effectively with their design decisi ons. Their reasoning consistently showed that they did so to increase the use of scientific research in decision-making by increasing the quickness and efficiency of the label’s use. People are trying to choose what to read in the sea of information and misinformation –students indicated– they will need a tool that helps them make quick, c omparative decisions so they can move on to consumi ng the information from better sources.

Rationale for designs Additional themes from students’ presentations and focus groups supported our understanding that stude nts and their communities are well aware of the proliferati on and impact of misinformation in their and others ’ lives. People hope to experience a lessening of misinformation’s impact, and students are eag er to be a part of creating that change themselves. While one group wanted “mor e informed” academics to contribute information tha t led to summative evaluations, others wanted the public to be involved in co-contributing information that led to summative evaluations. The latter, for example, inc luded a community “edit” button on their label, whi ch supported public users in contributing Wikipedia-like corrections to the label, which they described  as holding the label-makers accountable. Finally, despite the proliferat ion of misinformation, student designers feel that interventions like ours and theirs may still suppor t the public’s information literacy but call on res earchers and designers to come together to respect and support t he public’s wants and needs for speed and efficienc y when making information-based decisions.

## Discussion

Student designers’ calls for summative evaluations of trustworthiness pushed our research team to cons ider not just the educative power of the PFL but its potenti al as a “mediating” tool as well. In this study, students noted that public audiences may increasingly find themsel ves looking at academic journal articles and in nee d of tools with features to support their evaluation of credib ility. The student designers in this study advocate d for features that align with the public’s need for speed and eff iciency when determining whether or not to read the  article. This invites a further question: Should science communication tools acquiesce to the public’s desire for speed and efficiency? A dual approach, that of supporting information evaluation and teaching information literacy, may be one path forward. Incorporating dual-purpose designs that offer evaluation guidance whil e facilitating learning through contrasting cases to address misconceptions (Pomerance et al., 2016) could strengthen public literacy and combat misinformation in meaningful, sustainable ways.
