# It’s Not Working!: Critical Problems in Emergent Infrastructures of Classroom Physical Computing

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Moments of breakdown in any infrastructure make the often-invisible components visible (Star, 1999). In this paper, we explore how the breakdown of one physical computing system used in middle and high school classrooms (the DaSH) made determinant the sociotechnical infrastructure undergirding it. Using ethnography and situational analysis mapping, we make meaning of two major sources of the issue that became barriers to the system operating in teachers’ classrooms: shifting organizational goals & the cloud-based component of the programming environment. Uncovering these sunk components of the computing technologies infrastructure offers insight into what to consider when researching and implementing work with physical computing in K-12 and out of school environments including what large-scale relationships it relies on and how they shape students’ relations to computing.

## Introduction

Whispers of widespread breakdown In December of 2021, a classroom of high school Phy sics students worked in pairs to program and build their physical computing system  (PCS), called the Data Sensor Hub (DaSH, Figure 1), to gather environmental data. Running into issues, students raised their hands to  ask their teacher, Karla, for assistance. Two stud ents in the back of the room who had programmed and wired the sound sensor and checked their programming several times kept running into a strange output from the PCS. The micro:bit screen read “927:(“. The pair aske d Karla for suggestions. Karla quickly exclaimed that the output was an error code and looked up the code online exclaiming “oh man we are trying to use a V2  [version 1] feature on a V1  [version 2] board”. Shortly after, several other students experienced the same error code. As the “927” error spread around the room, Karla briefly huddled with Jessie (author 2) who reached out to Colin (Author 1) via message and then phone call. The period ended without any students getting a working DaSH. Over the next few days, Karla remained calm with her students as they utilized a hastily put together work around  that did not fully work. She apologized to the students and expressed frustration, in solidarity with theirs, that the se nsors were not working as planned. Ultimately, Karl a ended the five-lesson unit early.

Figure 1 DaSH (left), and the Corresponding MakeCode Program (right) Made by Another Teacher Months before Karla and her students encountered th e DaSH’s large-scale breakdown, the COVID-19 pandemic sparked a breakdown of the global supply chain (Panwar et al., 2022). Goods manufacturing was halted around the world and shipping was slowed drasticall y, due to lockdown  policies to stop the spread of the virus amongst people. Similarly, the blockage in the Suez Canal by a ship run aground in March 2021 halted 369 ships, delaying over $9 billion worth of trade a day (Russon, 2021). The impacts of these global infrastr ucture shifts were felt for months all over North America, where Karla and her students were living. This breakdown of the supply chain limited access to large amounts of items from household products to microchip technology, making real the material, relational, and ecological aspec ts of supply chains that are often invisible to peo ple buying a variety of products. This included  drastically slowing down our (the research team)  timeline for getting the components for the DaSH ready for teachers like Karla new to the project. In the summer of 2021 we were unable to obtain any micro:bits due to the supply chain is sues brought about by both the COVID-19 pandemic and the Suez Canal blockage. Eventually, we were able to fi nd a supplier which only had the newest version of the micro:bits (V2) which had not been tested with DaSH yet; our shipme nt arrived after the summer professional development sessions with a new cohort of teachers and just before they were planning to use the DaSH in their classrooms. Moments of breakdown in any infrastructure make  the often-invisible infrastructures visible (Star,

1999) at the scale of global supply chains or a sec ondary science classroom. In this paper, we explore how the

breakdown of the DaSH in Karla’s room made determin ant the sociotechnical infrastructure resulting in the DaSH’s operation.

The DaSH  (a sensor-based PCS), and its accompanying curriculum, were the result of a Research Practice Partnership (RPP) charged with integrating  physical computing systems into secondary classroo ms in accessible and scalable ways. It’s design, completed in partnership with a  small electronics company (called EC here) utilized an existing physical computing technology designed for youth, the BBC micro:bit, with an extension attached for connecting DaSH-specific sensors for ease and accessibility  (gator:bit). It was also designed to use the micro:bit browser-based programming environment (MakeCode) with either a block-based language or textbased language. The sensors’ programming libraries that students’ programs drew on were engineered by EC and officially incorporated into the MakeCode website by Microsoft. Eventually we uncovered the source of the issue: the BBC Micro:bit was upgraded to a new version (micro:bit V2) which was not compatible with the EC-produced sensors, even though the V2 was expected to be backwards compatible. Therefore, wherever teachers tried to use the DaSH – and the curricula co-designed with partnering teachers – with a micro:bit V2 as part of their build, it would not work. In this widescale breakdown in the DaSH’s operation the relations between the EC, our research team, and Microsoft (owner of the Micro:bit) came to matter. Physical computing as educational technologies Physical computing, tangible systems of microcontrollers and sensors that can take in information about that world to control physical outputs such as strings of lights and sound, were originally too cumbersome and expensive for widespread classroom adoption. However, over the last decade the rise of user-friendly microcontrollers, such as the BBC micro:bit, that can be controlled by block-based programming opened the world of physical computing to youth with little previous computer science or electronics experience (Sentence et al., 2017; Austin et al., 2020). This has created space for applications in environmental data collection (e.g., Hardy et al., 2020), e-textiles (e.g., Jayathirtha et al., 2020), and more. These to ols support students to go beyond traditional introductory programming experiences to create meaningful physic al artifacts that they, and others, can interact with. In essence, they can take the program off the screen and into the real world. Unique challenges arise when implementing physical computing activities in the classroom because errors can happen in both the hardware, software, a nd the space in between (Desportes & DiSalvo, 2019). When integrating physical computing into secondary classroom, teachers often have little to no experience with how to handle these errors or support students to handle t hese errors (Nixon et al., 202 3). Navigating the errors that students encounter as they program and assemble phy sical computing  remains a chal lenge (Jayathirtha et al, 2020). Debugging, or dealing with these errors, is a key skill for students to develop as part of learning computing practices as well as more general critical thinking practices (DeLiema et al., 2020). Educational technologies, or technologies for learning, have be en a focus since the inception of the Learning sciences over 30 years ago.  They continue to be important objects to envision a lternative conceptualizations of learning that our world needs (Cerratto Pargman, 2020). Yet, many studies of the technology themselves are under theorized (Hew et al., 2019). To give credence to educational technologies as art ifacts of learning theories and the social and political cont exts they were designed within (Watters, 202 1), scholars have articulated the need to take a “relational lens” that “problematizes learning technologies’’’ purposes and the kinds of futures they are producing (Cerrato Pargman, 2020). Ames (2019), describing the charisma of laptop designed for learning,  reminds us that such technologies are often commiserate with the capitalistic enterprises of technology companies. Therefore, the case of this paper contributes to a larger question of h ow physical computing technologies in classrooms are contributing to particular visions of computing inf rastructures that background certain issues of power for students.

Infrastructuring the learning sciences Star and Ruhlder (1996) argue that researchers shou ld ask “when is infrastructure” rather than “what” to better articulate the shifting, relational, and emerging a spects that come to matter at different points in t ime and space. Scholars in the learning sciences have recently tak en up this charge to depict ingenious, and critical, relational practices at various organizational scales. For example, Penuel (2019) describes infrastructuring as the intentional activities to impact the components, relations, and routines of schools, key to RPPs in education. Greenberg et al.

(2020) depict the critical, justice-oriented, work necessary during the COVID-19 pandemic for a particular

community as infrastructuring to “open up new possi bilities for recognizing the ingenuity of communities” (p. 519). Further, Hladik et al. (2023) showed how muse um facilitators’ in-the-moment design work was part of the relational practices making the infrastructure of exhibits real for visitors. Our analysis, just like the sociotechnical infrastructure it uncovered, came to matter in the time just after the breakdown in Karla’s classroom as we  scrambled as a research team to make the DaSH workable in partner teachers’ classrooms. This paper is guided by the question: What components of infrastructures became important during the large-scale breakdown of the DaSH’s ability to work in classrooms and the work to fix it?

## Conceptual framework: Sociotechnical infrastructures

While Star (1999) describes infrastructures as boring and sometimes “singularly unexciting” (p. 377), in the case of this paper the “embedded” and “transparent” components came to be important dramatically. We draw on Star and colleagues ’ (Star, 1999; Star & Ruhleder, 1996) conceptualizati on that infrastructures are “fundamentally relational” and become real through the social practices and relati onships of organizations (Hladik et al., 2023). Therefore, both the material components (i.e., the DaSH’s microcontroller and sensors, curriculum, the classroom materials, the school network systems) and the social relationships at different scales (i.e., teachers’ support of students, research teams’ engagement with teachers,  hierarchies of schooling) are all part of the soci otechnical infrastructures made important in the DaSH’s operation. In other words, “one person’s infrastructure is another’s topic, or difficulty” (Star, 1999, p. 380) where the infrastructure is defined by one’s orientation to it. Winner (2001) argues that artifacts “have politics” to draw attention “ to the momentum of large-scale sociotechnical systems, to the response of modern societies to certain technological imperatives, and to the ways human ends are powerfully transformed as they are adapted to technical means” (p. 21).  As an example, Winner examines how Robert Moses’ design and production of highways (large-scale artifact) engineered how people move through New York City. Winner (2001) further articulates how the physical creation of Moses’ design  – which did not allow for buses on specific highways – embedded systematic racial inequities into the infrastructure of the highway itself as well as Moses’ personal racist ideologies. Benjamin (2019) calls similar design phenomena in digital technologies “coded inequities” that often obscure and perpetuate systemic racism through “the New Jim Code.”  Thus, we use the term sociotechnical to emphasize t he expansive and positional nature of infrastructures, particularly for educational technologies that are seemingly small-scale artifacts which often draw on large-scale sociotechnical systems. Learning from Wise et al.’s (2020) call for “subversive learning analytics” that articulate issues of power and oppression hidd en by common-sense assumptions, we conceptualize the powered relations of educational technologies as artifacts of shifting infrastructures. We conceptualize learning technologies like the DaSH  as artifacts that have sociotechnical infrastructures that include the shifting social and material relations existing within each learning space as well as larger technological structures. Learning technologies are infrastructured, continuously, to take use of the term as a verb a little further than Penuel (2019). For other learning sciences researchers, the artifacts being infrastructured are often particular structures for learning environmen t design and implementation such as  classrooms (e.g., Penuel, 2019), or a more just community (e.g., Greenberg et al., 2020) or a museum exhibit (Hladik et al., 2023). In our case, it is the DaSH that is being infrastructured. We use a conceptualization of sociotechnical infrastru ctures as shifting relational networks that undergird particular artifacts (social or material). Our unit of analysis is th e infrastructural components, or “modular increments” (Starr, 1999), that surface in our retrospective ethnographic analysis. In anal ysis, we first organized these into four overlapping categories: 1) organizations and people (i.e., the school district, teachers & students, engineers at partner organizations), 2) technologies (micro:bits, environmental sensors, cloud-based storage, student computers), 3) materials & environments (classroom spaces, STEM Education), and 4) discourse and policy (i.e., pushes for STEM education, imagin ed teachers in design processes). Following the consequentiality of these components, and most importantly their relationships,  shines light on the kinds of infrastructures that are often invisible, or sunk into backgrounds  (Star & Ruhleder, 1996), that designing technologies for classrooms relies on. Generally, it offers insight into what to consider when doing sim ilar kinds of research and implementation work with physical computing.

## Participants and settings

Karla was a high school Physics teacher participating in the expansion of our RPP into a new state and district to integrate curriculum and sensor technology into middle and high school classrooms. Prior to implementing, Karla had participated in professional learning experiences to gain experience with the curriculum and DaSH technology (Nixon et al., 2023). The opening vignette occurred during her first and last time integrating the sensor technology and using the curriculum in her classroom. For us, the research team, the vignette was the particular moment that started the long process of finding a way to ensure that the DaSH technology using a micro:bit V2– integral our research goals and partnership with teachers – could be used in classrooms. This work is part of a larger RPP including researchers from two universities, teachers and administrators from two districts (one large and urban; one medium-sized and rural) districts and a small electronics company (EC) that would design custom-built data analysis tools for students to use in the classroom. The goal of the RPP was to design a usable and adaptable curriculum co-designed by  teachers that used custom-built physical computing tools. Further, the RPP aimed at providing middle s chool students with equitable exposure to computational thinking by developing curriculum and  professional learning experiences that support tea chers to integrate computational thinking with  mainstream science and integrated STEM courses (Nixon et al., 2023; Gendreau Chakarov  et al., 2021). RPPs, such as the focus of this study, often involv e numerous players with varying roles and organizations with their own goals and infrastructures making RPPs a complex environment to study.

## Analytic methods

This paper is an ethnography of the sociotechnical infrastructures involved in the DaSH’s  operations. An ethnography of systems, according to Star (1999), i mplicitly involves the study of infrastructure, a t ask that is difficult due to the large quantity and types of da ta. However, Star (1999) suggests that researchers can conduct ethnographic research of infrastructures by studying the design,  understanding the paradoxes, identifying both narratives that are dominant and marginal (or invisible) and surfacing invisible work. Similar to Hladik et al. (2023), our unit of analysis is the components that emerge in situations of infrastructure breakdowns. However, rather than focus on making meaning from a catalog of small interactional breakdowns, we focus on the scale of breakdown for the whole community of teachers able to use the DaS H. In other words, we focus our meaning making on the com ponents that became real because of the breakdown t hat occurred in Karla’s class as an imagination of what  would happen in all classrooms wher e the DaSH uses a new version of the microcontroller. To do so, we traced  the narratives, invisible work, design, and parado xes that became important in understanding the sources of the breakdown and developing possible fixes. Our ethnographic process involved four stages. To g ather all of the data sources from the five years we started by documenting the process our team took to  solve the problem from 2021-2023. Doing so required us to also document what occurred in the early years of the RPP (from 2017-2021) to understand the breakdown itself. Stage 1: Three researchers were chosen to write the  memos because the first researcher  (Alex) was the lead designer of the project during the initial stages of the RPP (from 2017-2020); the second (Jessie) was present in the classroom with Karla on the day of the break down and years following (from 2021-2023); and the third (Michael) was the graduate student on the project starting in 2021 who completed the majority of debugging and rewriting of underlying code to remedy that the new  microcontroller could draw on to work with the sen sors correctly. The three researchers pulled from the following data sources to write chronological, analytic memos:

• meeting notes with the larger research and meetings with EC (from 2017-2023)

• email correspondence with EC (from 2021-2023) and among research members (2021-2023)

• initial design and grant proposal documents

• field notes and video of classroom implementation in Karla’s class (December 2021)

Stage 2: Each member of the research team (all auth ors) individually analyzed the memos using deductive coding (Miles et al., 2014). Codes were f ocused on the “sociotechnical dimensions” and devel oped from the practice of doing a technological implosion (see Dumit, 2014) by analyzing the material, technological, social, ecological, labor and policy dimensions that were made real with the DaSH not working. Stage 3: To triangulate, we met as a full research team to compare our coding and analysis to seek interrater reliability. Where we found disagreement, we discussed our coding and analysis and if we did not  come to agreement, we did not include the analysis in the findings. Stage 4: From the main dimensions brought forth thr ough the analysis, we created, and iterated on,  a situational map (Clarke  et al., 2015) highlighting actors  (people, organizations), relationships, and  artifacts (material, technological, social, and political) involved in the infrastruc ture. Our situational map depict ed the actors and artifacts as well as the relational, and  positioned nature of the infrastructure that becam e determinant in working to fix the “V2 issue” and make the DaSH work in all participating teachers’ classrooms. As described above, we organized this map across four overlapping categories: 1) organizations & people, 2) technologies, 3) materials & environments, 4) discourse and policies.

In this section, we share our analysis resulting fr om cycles of mapping, discussions, and analytic memoing. Analyzing these data together, we focused on investigating how infrastructures came t o matter during the breakdown in Karla's class and the resulting scramb le to get the systems working again. We sought to m ake more visible the networks, relationships, and conne ctions often taken for granted in working education al technologies and computing systems like the DaSH. Finally, we sought to make meaning of the relationships, by highlighting and considering further the actors and artifacts that had more connections (i.e., participating teachers) and less (i.e., cloud data storage, Microsoft).

When it all went wrong in Karla’s class, we (Colin & Jessie) immediately became concerned with how we were going to make the DaSH work in her classroo m and future classrooms  of other teachers. Before the breakdown, we had introduced the DaSH and curriculu m that previous teachers in the project had co-designed with university researchers. Starting as a fledglin g professional learning community within the large r RPP, the five teachers from the rural school district were eager to see what their students could do with this technology. After the breakdown, we started to contact our colleagues across the two universities and partners at EC to identify and rectify the problem. Early in this problem formulation state, we identified as a team that the issue was mostly because the base-code the browser-based programming environment was drawing from had changed its structure. This meant the sensor’s programming did not align with the new version of the microcontroller (the micro:bit V2). This lack of alignment was both at the production scale of our project (using the sensor kit designed for it) and the global scale of micro:bit, owned and operated by Microsoft. Therefore, trying to get n ew programming uploaded to the digital spaces that students would be drawing on in the programming environment for this project was going to take a redesign for t he sensor software and a new approval/upload from M icrosoft. In realizing this during discussions with EC, the infrastructures at play in making the technol ogy work in classrooms shifted, making the networked systems of the programming env ironment had material consequences. This new unintentional infrastructuring meant we needed to investigate fur ther the networks and relationships at play to make the DaSH work in classrooms, which also became the genesis of the analysis in this paper. Through situational mapping, we traced the infrastructures happening at the moments of breakdown and eventual problem formulation. Below, we share from the material, relational, and networked highlights from our situational mapping that became particularly clear during and after the  breakdown that were not mattering in the design or implementation of the PCS previously.  We focus on two major themes of these components th at emerged in analysis: 1) tensions in organizational goals and 2) the digital infrastructures that were what Starr (1999) called the installed base of relational infrastructures.

Organizational goals A common practice of RPPs is regularly discussing a nd reflecting on a sense of shared goals to drive t he partnership forward (Penuel, 2019). Navigating the breakdown, problem formulation, and eventual fix, helped us also uncover shifting goals across the various orga nizations involved in making the PCS work in classr ooms, including those of organizations that were not part of our formulation of the RPP  like Microsoft but imminently important for moving forward with a fix. Reckoning with what was not represe nted well in our situational map helped us realize the misalign ed goals that may be breaking or fraying relationships  that were represented. The tensions between goals – within and outside of the RPP – became obstacles to fixing the problem (see Table 1). Table 1 Organizational Goals at the Time of Breakdown Organization  Goals University Research Team  Get the specific sensor-based PCS, and it’s codesigned curriculum, working for more teachers Two Partnering School Districts and Teachers Have innovative lessons for their middle school students that integrate computing into science and STEM classrooms Electronics company (EC)  Support the sales of the PCS components Microsoft  To further develop the microcontrollers’ (micro:bit) ease of use through an updated version The goals of the research team and the partnering s chool districts remained somewhat stable. Yet, the transient nature of EC’s workforce and the context of supply chain disruptio n shifted how the project of getting sensor-based PCSs into classrooms was championed at EC and surfaced the need to  maintain sales during financially hard times  more of a key goal. This reality, rooted in the capitalistic nature of the educatio nal technology landscape, meant that figuring out how to make units already s old workable  was harder to prioritize as a company. Locating this shift in goals, unearthed the shifting labor dimensions of the infrastructure. With the focus on sales, the research team was directed to send their email requests and questions to a salesperson rather than an engineer. Because the salesperson was not a computer scientist, they could not directly answer inquiries from our team. This resulted in week-long email chains as the salesperson was acting as a lia ison between the engineers and the research team.

Digital infrastructures that communicate with physical hardware We, as a research team, were able to eventually loc ate the source of the breakdown as an updated digit al infrastructure for the programming environment that  changed both the libraries the PCS drew on while s tudents programmed but also the structures of the libraries  themselves; this was a major shift in what was previous a “sunk” infrastructure or installed base. The new version of the micro:bit (DaSH’s microcontroller) drew on a different library structure when students used the block-based programming environment on their browser. Therefore, the specific blocks students used to pro gram actions using the sensors did not work with th e updated microcontroller. Locating this issue and possible workarounds involv ed the often-invisible labor of graduate students in relation to online communities (i.e., GitHub). First, unclear about whose job it was to find and make the fix,  the majo rity of the debugging and programming to create new Ma keCode coding bases fell on two graduate students who happened to have expertise in  programming. While debugging and seeking out their  own solution, the two graduate students relied heavily on GitHub to see if others had discussed a similar problem, to ask questions of others who use the same technologies, and to locate example code or fixes that we could provide to our teachers. The distributed, unnamed, and unpaid labor of GitHub proved to be extremely valuable. The ease of use – or “low floor” (Papert, 1980) – that was incorporated into the design of our partic ular PCS relied on cloud-computing capability where students and teachers would not need to install any new software on t heir local machines  (Gendreau Chakarov et al., 2021). Instead,  classroom users could use cloud-based computers like Chromebooks to run the programming environment right  in a browser with the PCS attached via usb port. Further, this offered a “high ceiling” fo r use where different kinds of sensors and lights c ould be connected and programmed with the microcontroller a nd, even the programming environment offered opportunities to also program PCSs text-based languages (Python and JavaScript). Yet, in sc aling the physical computing activities a nd technologies with a new group of teachers the distributed nature of t he programming environment meant that we, as intermediary actors in the educational technology infrastructure, had no access to changing what students accessed from the website. Therefore we –research team members, teachers, and students themselves – were unable to fix the problem at its source. To continue the metaphor, the low floor was no longer walkable or workable and the high ceiling crashed down on us. Further, aiming to fix the problem made explicit how these cloud-based technologies operate on physical infrastructure bases. Making the MakeCode programming environment accessible from anywhere with an internet connection requires accessible data stored on massive networks of servers that sit in cooled ware houses around the world (Monserrate, 2022), which produce increas ing ecological costs. It also requires layers of source code that are seemingly invisible to users (i.e., teachers, students, and researchers), a digital infrastructural base. Discussion: Consider the relations to computing and data offered to students Much like Hladik et al.’s (2023) description of mus eum facilitators’ design work as infrastructure eme rging in breakdowns, the collective work required to fix our  breakdown was endemic to the larger infrastructure s that make up the DaSH’s operation. Yet, instead of focusing on our own work infrastructuring relations, organizations, and materials – both before and after the breakdown – this paper focuses on what the breakdown can inform  us about sociotechnical infrastructures of educational  technologies; facilitated further by the larger sociotechnical breakdowns of manufacturing and shipping infrastruc tures from the COVID-19 pandemic and the Suez Canal blockage, our case of breakdown  has helped us consider what hidden assumptions were unsettled. Building on Cerratto Pargman’s (2020) call to problematize learning technologies’ purpose and the future they are shaping for students, the critical components of infrastructure we identify in this paper  depict the kinds of relations to computing, digital data, and their own environments the DaSH offers to students. Changing organizational goals and shifting programm ing infrastructures were key contributors to DaSH’s inability to work in Karla’s classroom. They are al so products of the larger social contexts like the push for K-12 students to learn computing and company’s capitalistic needs while supplying hardware and software engineering. In other words, the policy-driven pushes for students to learn computing and t he financial system that rewards sales of new things and not upkeep of current products were both bases that the DaSH was built on, and it’s co-designed curriculum relies on. For students using a working DaSH, these components  were obscured by the ease of use (i.e., programming the microcont roller with a USB connection and a browser-based environment) which extends the normalization of computers – and instant access – in their lives to include physical computing. Thus, we urge critically considering the relationships and politics (Winner, 2001) of artifacts like the DASH and the ways they are shaping students’ lives.

The competing goals of organizations that house and run the data systems and digital infrastructures we rely on as “bases” for learning technologies matter.  And it makes us consider: how are we engaging a critical stance about th e relations to data and to computing with the teacher s and students  who use computing technologies and their connected curricula?  This work has implored us to build learning practices th at support research teams, teachers, and students in critically considering the often-implicit assumptions about what makes a learning technology work. In our case this would require considering assumpti ons like 1) access to the data in the cloud is effortless and has little material  or ecological impact or 2) the push to learn computing is not laden with a particular  political vision to shape the  future (see Philip & Sengupta, 2020  & Benjamin, 2019). This implores a move away from viewing youth as consumers of technology towards “youth as pragmatic philosophers of technology” (Vakil & McKinney de Royston, 2022) whose sensemaking is rooted in what technologies are for and what they could be, and should be, for. This change requires engaging with – and uncovering – how students imagine and reason the powered infrastructures of technologies as they learn computing.

## Conclusions and implications

In conclusion, w e wonder what other powered relations were not surf aced but remain key to the ongoing operations of physical computing systems in K-12 spaces. We see our ethnography of our own problem as a cautionary tale for other learning sciences researc hers who aim to work with youth, and educators, usi ng digital tools. From a relational stance on the sociotechnical nature of infrastructure, it is imperative that we – as learning scientists – consider all the invisible material, social and political components that make up the infrastructures of our project, technologies, and pedagogies involved. It is imperative,  not just because technologies may fail like ours, but because they have politics and are shaping particular orientations to the world for students. In the context of physical computing tools, both research teams and classrooms should engage in critical interrogations of what computing infrastructures these tools draw on and w here those are located including things like the ec ological impacts of massive data storage, and the ways racial inequities are coded into our digital s ystems (Benjamin, 2019). Considering what different organizations come to ma tter in the ways digital tools and connected learni ng designs are constructed is critical to understanding better what researchers, educators, and learners are developing together; what they are infrastructuring.
