# Examining and Scaffolding Collective Idea Improvement in Knowledge Building Using Analytics and Meta-Discourse

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Knowledge Building (KB) engages students in meta-discourse to improve collective ideas. Multiple studies have examined the roles of learning analytical tools in supporting meta-discourse, while this study focused on the supportive roles of the KF-embedded tools. We designed a learning environment that engages meta-discourse among students with the assistance of word cloud and idea-building tools, both of which are embedded in KF. A total of 22 Singapore secondary school students participated in a six-weeks long study to learn photosynthesis in a KB learning environment. We adopted social network, inferential statistical, and semantic analyses. The results show that students’ learning gain is correlated with the number of posts in KF. In addition, the Learning Analytics-augmented meta-discourse supported students’ KF participation and collective idea improvement. Qualitative analysis showed how students engaged in meta-discourse sessions. The methodological and practical contributions were discussed.

## Introduction

Knowledge Building (KB), and its technological platform, knowledge forum (KF) promotes students’ epistemic agency (Scardamalia, 2002). Researchers do not predetermine ideas or inquiry pa ths, rather, the ideas emerge as students engage in inquiry practices. Such a process is facilitated by meta-discourse which is defined as discourse about discourse (Zhang et al., 2018). In a KB-learning community, discourse is cons idered an object of inquiry upon which students co-reflect (van Aalst, 2009). The entire community regular ly monitors and reviews its collective understanding, identifies remaining questions, and co-constructs inquiry structu res to identify inquiry topics, participatory structures, and inquiry activities (i.e., reflective struc turation; Zhang et al., 2018). While progressive meta-discourse is essential for creative knowledge work, students in KF require visualizations to perceive and understand meta-level discourse information (Chen & Zhang, 2016). Lear ning analytics (LA) have been applied in KB classrooms to show idea development trajectory, participation norms, and discussion contents among students (e.g., Zhang et al., 2018) in tandem with the interest in CSCL collaborative analytics. Our ongoing work is based on the paradigm of using LA tools as an object of inquiry to engage students in knowledge creation discourse (e.g., Yang et al., 2016), limited studies have examined the supportive r ole of KF-embedded learning analytics in conducting meta-discourse thus, improving collective ideas. We designed a KB learning environment as part of a larger design study; in which students engaged in collective inquiry into photosynthesis, enriched with KF learning analytics of how they had written on KF, and engaged in meta-discours e to reflect on their accomplishment and to plan for future inquiry. Our previous studies demonstrate  the effectiveness of such a learning environment in supporting domain understanding, collective idea improvement, and knowledge-building inquiry activities among students (Yu et al., 2022). However, the argument was based on descriptive analysis, and we did not focus on tracing the idea development trajectory of students through m icro lens. Here, we used the network analysis approach to expand our analysis and examined the following researc h questions: (1) to what extent did LA-augmented meta-discourse support students’ development of conceptual understanding of photosynthesis? (2) how did students’ collective community knowledge advance in KF throughout the four phases? (3) how did students engage in collective idea development process?

## Instructional contexts & pedagogical design

The goal of this study was to promote collective ideas improvement about photosynthe sis among eighth-grade students in a Singaporean secondary school through a six-week KB inquiry course. Students participated in LA augmented meta-discourse sessions throughout the course. The instructional design was divided into four phases. In phase one, students worked individually. They participated in the Padlet platform and created their own questions, which they transferred to KF and discussed criteria of good resear ch questions with their teacher and peers (week 1-2; see Figure 1.a). In phase two, students, working in pairs, ge nerated rise-above ideas (i.e., idea CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 164 synthesis view) through LA-augmented meta-discourse and engaged in KF discourse further (week 3-4; see Figure 1.c). At this stage, students co-reflected upon visualization exported from the word cloud. In phase three, students, working in same pairs as in phase two, created and annotated group concept maps, which summarized main ideas in KF and engaged in community-level meta-discourse to share the concept maps (week 4-5; see Figure 1.b). In phase four, students, working in pairs, manipulated the LA tools (e.g., word cloud a nd ideas building tool), extended ideas by engaging in group-level meta-discourse, engaged with reflective as sessment, wrote portfolio notes individually using scaffolds that encouraged students to summarize ideas that had been discussed and identify future inquiry directions (week 5-6; see Figure 1.d). Students were tested before and after the intervention with the same open-ended question (“Write down as much as you know about photosynthesis”) to ensure consistency. In addition, students w rote 280 notes during the four phases of intervention, which included 96, 58, 32, and 70 notes in phase one, two, three, and four, respectively. Students wrote 23 portfolio notes and 12 reflective notes. To answer the first research question, we conducted the following analyses. First, we performed a preliminary round of Social Network Analysis (SNA) with UCINET 6. We compute d individual students’ normalized in-degree and out-degree centrality which indica ted the extent to which students’ notes were responded by peers and they responded to peers respectively. Second, we conducted conte nt analysis. For knowledge assessment, students’ answers were coded based on a scheme  adapted from the literature examining middle school students’ systems thinking (e.g., Hmelo-Silver et al., 2007). Three themes were identified to characterize students’ trajectory of understanding: 1) describing the components involved in the process of photosynthesis, 2) explaining the processes but not mentioning the mechanisms of photosynthesis, and 3) offering explanations that integrated processes and mechanisms. For portfolio notes, we adopted Lei & Chan’s (2018) scheme on characterizing students’ epistemic engagement in portfolio notes into nine levels from simply reiterating to constructing meta-theory. Third, we performed a set of correla tional analysis to examine the contributing relationships of students’ participation in KF discussion and portfolio notes writing on students’ postassessment scores. To answer the second research question, we performed another round of SNA. We parsed students’ KF notes into four phases following the implementation sequence of LA in class. W e measured the development of CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 165 networks’ cohesiveness with density (the proportion of all possible ties that are present), arc reciprocity (the extent to which ties are bi-directional) and number of ties. We used KBDeX (i.e., a knowledge innovation discourse analytical tool developed by Oshima et al. (2012) to investigate how community knowledge advances with similar techniques as SNA) to examine how students’ collective ideas improved across the four stages. Before conducting the analysis, keywords were assigned following the curriculum guideline. We used network average degree centrality (ADC) and centralization to measure the extent to which th e network connectivity progressed across the four phases. The increase of ADC suggests the keywords become more  connected whereas the decrease of centralization indicates the increased connectivity is caused by more keywords  being connected rather than the intense connections among certain few keywords. Betweenness centrality (BC) m easures “the number of node pairs for which the shortest path between them passes through a selected node” (Oshima, et al., 2012, P. 911). In this study, we used average betweenness centrality to measure the change of mediating roles of keywords. In addition, based on degree centrality (DC) computed from KBDeX, we ranked the keywords and parsed them into two categories: keywords with DC gradually declining and those with DC gradually incr easing across the four phases. We conducted repeated measures ANOVA with DC as dependent variable and phases as within-subjects variable to examine the extent to which the DC of keywords evolved across the four phases. To answer the third research question, we conducted qualitative analysis. We trac ed how students engaged with LA-augmented meta-discourse to collectively develop the idea across the four phases. We adopted a phase-by-phase approach to construct a trajectory of students’ collective ideas and inquiry practices developed across the four phases.

The analysis showed that in the early stage, especially when no LA tools w ere involved, students freely participated in KF and engaged in a knowledge-sharing activity. Students engaged in the  LA-augmented metadiscourse from the second to the fourth phase. They summarized major them es from their peer discussions, identified how their existing discussion resolved major issues and indicated eme rging questions worthy of additional discussions. Students identified new inquiry topics throughout such a process, which catalyzed further idea development in KF. Specifically, in the second phase, students compared group discussions with the community ideas which was presented by the word cloud and identified inquiry di rections by integrating new CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 167 words with their existing discussions. Meanwhile, different views were cr eated. This is considered the first idea rise-above process. In the third phase, students rise above the group’s ideas to the concept maps which link different key notions of photosynthesis and reveal ideas development process. They engaged in community-level meta-discourse by sharing the concept maps. This encouraged students to ask more questions and continue improving community ideas. In the final phase, students were granted more agenc y; they manipulated the LA tools, analyzed results, and identified inquiry directions. Students’ engagement in meta-discourse across the four phases constitutes a trajectory of students’ continuously rising above ideas and agency enactment.

## Phase I: Students freely participate in KF discussions

In the first phase, students demonstrated intuitive understanding of the key question pos ed in the video: “why dumping algae into the ocean help to dissolve CO 2”. They posted diverse ideas to construct initial explanations, asked facts-seeking questions, and exchanged information. They were seeding id eas in KF. For example, S16 asked, “if phytoplankton has any negative effects on marine creatures”. S10 ans wered that “When too many nutrients are available, phytoplankton may grow out of control and form harmful algal blooms. These blooms can produce extremely toxic compounds that have harmful effects on fish, shellfish, ma mmals, birds, and even people”. In this exce rpt, S16 asked one broad question focusing on the beneficial or detrimental effects of phytoplankton. His peers described the phenomena caused by the harmful algae bloom. Although these two students engaged in a conversation, their discussion focused on shallow feature s of phytoplankton. In addition, they did not construct knowledge by linking different sources of information and elaborating on the relationships. They engaged in sharing information.

Phase II: Students engaged in word cloud-augmented meta-discourse Although students shared an intuitive understanding and new information about photosynthesis in phase one, they deepened their KF discussions in phase two. They engaged in group-level meta-discours e which was facilitated by word cloud visualizations. Students summarized major research topics of the community, reviewed how their peers approached these questions and identified research gaps and emerging inquiry directions. In this phase, the word cloud demonstrated the extent to which community ideas were being unfolded, which ideas were discussed more thoroughly, yet others were focused less. This dynamic encourages students to explore the ideas that were not paid attention to by the community, and make connections between different ideas. New inquiry directions emerged from this process. Students investigated these new ideas that were identified from the word cloud session and continued posting new information on KF. This inspires more students to join th e conversation and extend ideas. For example, S16 and S 10 identified interesting keywords from the word cloud (“we found light and photosynthesis on the word cloud”), summarized questions that their peers had asked (“We found our peers asked: ‘how does artificial light affect photosynthesis?’”), reviewed their  peers’ responses to this question: “artificial light can be used to grow plants, but sunlight is still best for plants” and ident ified their interesting topics: “we want to find out more about artificial light, benefits, disadvantages and possible hazards”. ## Discussion

This study examines an iterative process of idea improvement in knowledge building and how it is supported by learning analytics and meta-discourse strategies. Besides, we explore th e possibility of granting more epistemic agency to students by allowing them to manipulate learning analytics tools, supporte d with scaffolds and reflections on KF. Students demonstrated an improved conceptual understanding of photosynthe sis. The correlation analysis also showed that the conceptual comprehension of students grow s as they publish and edit CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 170 notes in KF. Additionally, the participation of students in KB inquiry activities continuously increased across the four phases. Our phase-by-phase analysis showed that students generated more novel ideas, participated in more conversations, and actively responded to peer notes from phases two to one and four to three than from phases three to two. This suggests the effectiveness of using word cloud visualization a nd encouraging students to manipulate learning analytics in supporting their KB activities. One explanation for the slow increase in 3rd phase could be that students put more effort into participating in community meta-dis course and wrote fewer notes in KF as a result. The phase-by-phase analysis of discourse keywords usin g KBDeX showed high-level keywords (i.e., the ones with increasing DC) continuously emerging from KB discussio ns as students were engaging with LA-augmented meta-discourse. The changing pattern of average DC showed mor e keywords became connected across the four phases. The continuous decline of centralization from phase  one to three suggested the connectedness of keywords became more equally distributed among words in later phas e. A slight increase of centralization in phase four suggested after the portfolio notes activities, stu dents’ KF discussions became more focused on certain promising ideas. The dynamics of average BC reveals ric her information than the other two metrics. Students showed similar BC in the first two phases. This suggested  though students were exposed to word cloud and reflected upon it by engaging in group-level meta-discourse, similar number of words were acting as mediators among words. This indicated students’ expanded the scope of th eir discussions by including more words but had similar level of connections. However, students’ ideas became more connected in the third phases, which were shown as more words became mediators. In the last phase, BC  reached the lowest point, which suggested students used less amounts of mediators if they wanted to connect two  words. This indicates their discussions became more focused. Our qualitative analysis showed that LA tool s play an important role in students’ ideas development process. According to other studies, LA tools demonstrated meta-level participation data to students, encouraging their idea development activities (Chen & Zhang, 2016). L A visuals act as objects of inquiry for students to undertake knowledge creation discourse (van Aalst, 2009). Thi s study extends this paradigm by further enacting the agency of students and allowing students to mani pulate the tools. Connecting with the quantitative results, granting students with epistemic agency promotes  students’ participation and collective ideas improvement most among other phases.

This study aims to provide practical and methodological contributions Practically, this study shed lights on how to design a KB learning environment with LA tools and meta-discourse. With the assistance of collective meta-discourse in advance, instructional designers could provide students agency to  manipulate the tools. This study also provides a feasible methodological analysis by integrating multiple networ k analysis approaches to examine how student participation, inquiry practices, and ideas developed across  multiple phases. In future studies, we will conduct a longer implementation using a more micro-level analysis approach to examine the roles of different types of LA tools.
