# Timing Matters? An Exploratory Study of Immersive Virtual Reality Integration in Middle School Career and Technical Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Career exploration in middle school is critical, yet Career and Technical Education (CTE) continues to face persistent challenges. While immersive virtual reality (IVR) has demonstrated value for engagement and skill learning, little research has examined its pedagogical integration in CTE, particularly the timing of its use. This quasi-experimental study (N = 42) compared three instructional conditions: IVR as introduction, IVR as review, and traditional CTE instruction (control). Results from baseline –endline surveys and weekly reflections over six weeks suggested that students in the control group experienced declines across all attitudinal measures, whereas IVR conditions helped sustain engagement and career awareness. Qualitative findings further indicated that the timing of IVR use may influence how students engage with and interpret CTE learning. Overall, this exploratory study provides preliminary evidence that IVR can serve as a pedagogical complement to CTE instruction, while the timing of its use, as another pedagogical design factor, warrants further investigation.

Career exploration during the middle and early high school years is important. Research shows that career exploration significantly enhances students’ academic motivation, self-efficacy, and career readiness, with exploratory expectations predicting long-t erm academic achievement and adaptability (Oliveira et al., 2017; Ali et al., 2017; Paixão & Gamboa, 2017; Chen et al., 2021).  Within this context, Career and Technical Education (CTE) has played a central role in supporting students’ career exploration in the United States. Federal policy has positioned CTE as central to linking education with labor market needs, promoting equity, and aligning secondary and postsecondary pathways (NCES, 2024). CTE participation has been linked to higher graduation rates, stronger workforce earnings, and smoother school-to-work transitions (Dougherty, 2023; Stone, 2017). Yet, CTE implementation faces persistent challenges in staffing, course participation, and pedagogy guidance. In 2020–21, 31% of public schools reported difficulty filling CTE teaching positions, up from 20% in 2011–12 (NCES, 2024). Participation is also uneven: fewer than 10% of students took courses in manufacturing, construction, or transportation, while over 20% enrolled in IT, business, or health care, suggesting a mismatch with labor market needs (NCES, 2024). While some states have developed CTE frameworks, their reach remains regional, and national reports monitor outcomes without prescribing pedagogy. These persistent challenges highlight the need for innovative pedagogical strategies that can supplement lim ited teacher capacity and better scaffold students’ career and self-exploration. One promising approach is immersive virtual reality (IVR), which has shown potential to realize “hands-on experience, mastery by doing, [and] learning in… an applied venue” (California Department of Education, 2007, p. 4)” in CTE (Catterson, 2013; Carroll et al., 2019).

## Literature review

IVR environments have demonstrated learning affordances, including feedback and reflection mechanisms that enhance student engagement, cognitive performance, and real-world task proficiency, and  foster higher-order skills such as problem-solving and critical thinking (Huang et al., 2024). Training novices in technical fields is often costly, hazardous, or infeasible, making IVR particularly advantageous in scenarios that are Dangerous, Impossible, Counterproductive, or Expensive (Bailenson, 2018). The Cognitive Affective Model of Immersive Learning (CAMIL; Petersen et al., 2022) theorizes that immersive environments generate presence and agency that activate cognitive-affective processes shaping engagement, self-efficacy, and motivation (Bandura, 1997; Deci & Ryan, 2000). However, IVR research has identified a persistent novelty effect in IVR research may inflate engagement gains, reflecting technological unfamiliarity rather than instructional properties (Grassini & Laumann, 2020), underscoring the importance of pedagogical design. Beyond these affordances, Boel et al. (2025) showed that the timing of IVR integration matters: sequencing classroom activities before IVR led to better learning outcomes in performance, motivation, and self-efficacy. Yet the pedagogical timing and integration of IVR within CTE remain underexplored.

To address this gap, we aimed to evaluate the impact of using IVR as a CTE intervention on grade 6-8 students during their career discovery CTE classes. S pecifically, we investigated the impact on the self efficacy, 1402 motivation, teacher influence, understanding self, career exploration, and career decision of different CTE pedagogical strategies and the timing of the IVR intervention. The study addressed two research questions: ● RQ1: To what extent does integrating IVR into CTE classes, compared to traditional classroom teaching, influence students’ attitudes in terms of self-efficacy, motivation, teacher influence, understanding of self, career exploration, and career decision?

● RQ2: How does the timing of IVR use, either as an introductory tool or as a review tool, shape students’ attitudes and learning experiences in CTE classes?

We adopted a quasi-experimental, between-subjects design with three instructional conditions. Group A experienced IVR as an introductory tool, followed by discussion and regular class instruction. Group B participated first in discussion and traditional class, followed by IVR as a review tool. Group C served as the control group and engaged only in discussion and regular class without exposure to IVR. The study was conducted in two public schools in Colorado, with two CTE teachers and their classes recruited. The program spanned six weeks and covered six career topics: car mechanics, cooling, construction safety, production line, plumbing, and logistics. The CTE content was provided by Inspirit Learning Inc. IVR was delivered via headset in approximately 15-minute sessions, during which students completed career-specific procedural tasks. Of 82 students invited, 75 returned signed consent forms from both students and guardians. By the end of the program, 42 students completed both baseline and endline surveys. 72 students submitted one or more weekly reflection forms. Figure 1 The Flow Diagram of the Procedure.

Prior to implementation, teachers and administrators received training sessions to ensure fidelity, and students completed VR pre-training before the program began. On the first and last day of the program, students completed baseline and endline surveys consisting of 25 items designed to measure self-efficacy, motivation, teacher influence, understanding of self, career exploration, and career decision-making. The baseline and endline instrument was a synthesized version of selected items from established measures, including the Science Motivation Questionnaire II (Glynn, 2011), STEM Self-Efficacy Scale (Rittmayer & Beier, 2008), Student Interest in Technology and Science (Romine et al., 2014), Test of Science Related Attitudes (Fraser, 1981), and the Student Career Construction Inventory (Savickas et al., 2018). The 25-item survey demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.94). In addition to baseline and endline surveys, students completed weekly reflection forms that demonstrated strong inte rnal consistency (Cronbach’s α =.92; subscales: engagement α =.90, awareness α =.80, self-efficacy α =.59) in which they rated their engagement, career awareness, and self-efficacy related to the weekly career topic on a 1–5 scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). Negatively worded items were reverse-coded; construct scores were computed as means of constituent items. Each form also included two open-ended prompts: “What did you find most interesting about this career? (1– 2 sentences)” and “What else would you like to know about this career? (1 –2 sentences).” All groups engaged in weekly class classrooms. For baseline–endline surveys (matched N = 42; A = 16, B = 12, C = 14), we conducted descriptive statistics and paired-samples t-tests at both construct and item levels to assess pre –post changes across all students, and examined within-group descriptives to describe patterns across groups. For weekly  reflection forms (n = 72 students; 20 in Group A, 28 in Group B, and 24 in Group C;  208 total reflections), each student’s mean score across the six topics was calculated for each construct, and one-way between-subjects ANOVAs with Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) tests compared group differences at both construct and item levels. Qualitative data from open-ended reflection responses were coded using thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006) through a two-step process: initial coding by one researcher and independent review and cross-checking by a second researcher, with discrepancies resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Regarding baseline and endline surveys, paired-samples t-tests across all students (Groups A, B, and C combined, N=42) revealed a statistically significant decline in understanding self (Baseline M = 4.01, SD = 0.60; Endline M = 3.73, SD = 0.95), t(41) = 2.345, p =.024*, d = 0.362. No other constructs demonstrated significant changes. At the item level, four items showed statistically significant declines: two in motivation (“I strive to perform better than my peers,” “I am likely to share what I learned”) and two in self-understanding (“I know what values are important,” “I have a good understanding of my interests”; see Table 1). At the group level, no within-group differences reached significance, but descriptive results revealed distinct trajectories (Table 2). Group A remained essentially stable, Group B showed a modest gain in self-efficacy but declines in other  constructs, and Group C exhibited consistent declines across all measures.

Item-level analyses showed several significant group effects (Table 4), with the control group consistently scoring lowest. Qualitative reflections offered further insight into how three groups differed in their experiences and interpretations of the activities. Those in the IVR groups described the experiences as “realistic,” “interactive,” and “hands-on,” aligning with their higher engagement scores. One wrote, “I found being able to change the tires and move things around,” and another noted, “The IVR lets you pick things up.” Group A emphasized realism and technical understanding, “realistic and fun to learn about”, whereas Group B highlighted procedural mastery and curiosity, “The most interesting part was changing the oil.” In contrast, control-group comments were abstract (e.g., “how everything works like rims”), reflecting lower engagement and awareness. This exploratory study investigated how the timing of immersive virtual reality (IVR) use influences student experiences in middle school CTE classes, addressing the limited pedagogical research in this area. Building on evidence that CTE participation fosters motivation, self-efficacy, and career readiness (Oliveira et al., 2017; Stone, 2017), we evaluated IVR’s influence on six attributes central to career exploration: self-efficacy, motivation, teacher influence, understanding of self, career exploration, and career decision-making. For RQ1, results showed that while traditional CTE teaching instruction (Group C) was associated with declines across these attributes in surveys and consistently scored lowest across all items in the reflection forms, IVR conditions (Group A and B) appeared to h elp maintain or slightly improve motivation, teacher influence, and career exploration. Weekly reflections further showed that both IVR groups outperformed the control group on engagement and career awareness (both p <.001). These patterns are consistent with CAMIL (Petersen et al., 2022), which suggests that immersive presence activates cognitive-affective processes supporting motivation and engagement. The control group's declines may reflect insufficient support for competence and autonomy needs in conventional instruction (Deci & Ryan, 2000). For RQ2, no statistically significant differences emerged between introductory and review conditions. Descriptive patterns and qualitative reflections suggest that introductory IVR (Group A) tended to emphasize realism and technical understanding, providing an engaging entry point tonew career topics, whereas review IVR (Group B) experiences highlighted procedural mastery and curiosity as students revisited and deepened prior learning. These preliminary patterns point to timing of IVR use as a possible pedagogica l consideration for future CTE design research. This aligns with Boel et al. (2025), who reported sequencing learning before IVR enhanced performance, motivation and self-efficacy, suggesting that the timing of IVR integration may shape learning outcomes. Given the novelty effect common in IVR research (Grassini & Laumann, 2020), future work with larger samples, teacher interviews, and longitudinal designs is needed to isolate timing-specific effects and further examine how IVR integration shapes career learning over time. 1405 Ali, S.
