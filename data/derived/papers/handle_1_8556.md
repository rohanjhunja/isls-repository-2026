# Centring BlackJoy in Design for Learning and Research

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This theoretical paper presentation will articulate BlackJoy as a framework for designing research-to-practice spaces that centre Black liberation and flourishing for authentically equitable learning engagements and to counter ongoing deficit narratives and corresponding oppressions that Black and other racialized/minoritized learners experience in schools and other learning environments. The BlackJoy heuristic has four key tenets: Black Excellence, Black Inventiveness, Black Kinship and Black Aesthetics. Data and vignettes from prior research projects will be described of examples of BlackJoy in education and used as a point of departure discussions about the design of research and learning environments that cultivate BlackJoy and expanded ways to think about evidences of learning.

Black joy is healing, resistance and regeneration.” -Kleaver Cruz The global pandemic allowed for a collective witnessing of the ongoing and violent inequities experienced by Black and other racialized people especially in postcolonial and settler colonial contexts.  For example, Black bodies in the United States disproportionately experienced the grave effects of COVID-19 than others (Poteat et al. 2020). This is compounded by the higher likelihood of Black people working in essential services (Lancet 2020). The circumstances of George Floyd’s murder that ignited a global resurgence of the #BlackLivesMatter movement brings to the surface Sylvia Wynter’s (1994) essay No Humans Involved that she wrote to her colleagues after the Los Angeles murder of Rodney King where she noted the acronym used by the judicial system, NHI, meaning “no humans involved,”  referred to “any case involving a breach of the rights of young Black males who belong to the jobless category of the inner-city ghettos and noted that “systems of classification direct our thinking and order our behaviors”. This classifactory schema, Wynter argues, is based on the colonial logics of race science that emerged from Western Modern Science and contributes to the denial of humanity to Black people.

In education this presents in schooling where Bla ck students are framed with deficit lens and often positioned to be less capable than their non-Black counterparts, especially in science (Mutegi, 2012). Language such as “at risk” and, in certain contexts, “urban students” positions Black and Brown students as lacking in intellect, discipline, stable homes, etc. as impediments to success in the classroom, “the Black is constructed as always already Problem—as non-Human, inherently uneducable, or at very least, unworthy of education.” (Dumas 2016, p. x). And yet in these spaces of pain, violence and oppression Black people still manage to seek joy, “…Black people’s everyday commitment to locating joy in our lives often pierces through the troubled moments like the light shining in my room…[it] center[s] on our capacity to overcome despite all that might try to harm us (Cruz, 2017) In North America and other antiblack settler-colon ial and postcolonial contexts, Black pain and Black joy exist in a dialectic where Black joy is resistance, resilience and illumination in the darkness of the violence and pain. The lived experiences of Black people, including in learning contexts, like schooling, exist in what in what Sadiya Hartman calls the “afterlife of slavery,” which entails “skewed life chances, limited access to health and education, premature death, incarceration, and impoverishment” (Hartman, 2008). It is within these circumstances that Blackness is constructed which is the “cultural material, and political signifiers associated with the lived conditions of African and African-descended communities; [it is] a series of strategic and spiritual identifications that subsume directed, desired, and ascribed connections between endarkended bodies in the afterlife of slavery” (Morton, Gee and Woodson, 2020).

Articulating Black Joy This theoretical paper presentation will articulate BlackJoy as a framework for designing research-to-practice spaces that centre Black liberation and flourishing for authentically equitable learning engagements. Authentically equitable means a science education that does not simply remove conceptual statues that are ICLS2022 Proceedings 1625 © ISLS symbols of the embedded coloniality, antiblackness and oppression, but rather a science education that disrupts and transforms the very foundation on which these oppressive ideals are based (Author, 2020). In authentically equitable contexts, teachers and learners become critically cognizant of the power structures that organize their learning and lived experiences and they work collectively to dismantle while co-creating new structures that afford the agency of all learners. These spaces are centered around cultivating Black joy, and allows all stakeholders to liberate the mind, various senses and emotions in order to move outside of a given reality (Davies, 2013).

BlackJoy, as a framework, could be first thought of as freedom space of imagining alternatives to given realities, “More than a method to endure, however, black joy allows us the space to stretch our imaginations beyond what we previously thought possible and allows us to theorize a world in which white supremacy does not dictate our everyday lives” (Johnson 2015, p. 180);   envisaging life outside of the specter of the white gaze. BlackJoy as being able to use the radical tool of Black imagination and the centering of community and love (Cruz, 2017); freedom, imagination, community and love are central constructs of BlackJoy. It is a counternarrative to the poverty, crime, and overall despair as often depicted in media and an assertion of humanity through the experience of a range of emotions—joy, laughter, happiness and imagination—that were denied to Black people during enslavement through present times; it is resistance in a society shaped by oppression, racism, and violence” (Lu and Steele 2019). Thus, in places that were not designed to support Black existence, Black people have used innovation, creativity and love for each other to generate joy. Language is used to create and shape learning spaces, therefore this framework explores ways to extend BlackJoy into research to practice spaces.  The framework of BlackJoy emerges from the authors’ various research projects were Black learners and those who teach them were central stakeholders as well as Black identity and learner focused research and theoretical works some of which are cited in this proposal.

Black Identity and Black Joy Several Black researchers have put forward identity strategies, the moves that Black students do in order to ensure their success in STEM spaces that dominated by white, neoliberal culture and are hostile to Black students. These STEM spaces reflect larger society and are therefore also hostile to Black bodies. Black students who aim to succeed are compelled to find and cultivate Black joy. For example, Terrell Morton, Destiny Gee and Ashely Woodson (2020) named Black X Consciousness as a strategy that Black students use to understand and reconcile their identities in relation to STEM.  It is an active, metacognitive process students used to navigate structures that were often unwelcoming. They noted that Black X Consciousness is not emancipatory, but rather it is an adaptive strategy that is “influenced but not organized by inequity and marginality”; Black students made sense of their Blackness in STEM spaces through the performance of signifiers of Blackness that included collectivism, language and love. Sharing spaces with other Black students contributed to their “comfort, connection and even salvation” even in the midst of hostile environments. Similarly, in a different study Terrell Morton and Eileen Parsons (2018) found that Black women navigated STEM spaces through authoring their own identities while affirming their diverse and intersectional Blackness,, “Being a Black woman in STEM both equates to and transcends the oppression and struggle they endure as a result of social constraints on their identity; their identity as Black women being socially regulated yet self‐determined to mean power, strength, and success that manifest through their positive and negative encounters (p. 1383).” In both examples, identity/Blackness is a strategy, a process that Black people use to sustain themselves and each other in challenging and unfriendly spaces. Cultivating Black Joy in Science Education Miles and Roby (2022) advanced a Black liberatory science education that centres joy and liberation for Black learners, with practices of exploration, questioning, speaking and reminiscing in ways that allow for racial freedom. Building on Ridgeway and Roby, this presentation describes a heuristic for the design of learning environments that afford these and other liberatory and transformative practices for Black learners. This presentation will elaborate on these tenets with vignettes and interpretations from previous research projects with Black learners and educators and use these as a point of departure for a discussion about creating research-to-practice spaces that foster all four tenets as once and exploring other facets of BlackJoy that could be centred in teaching, learning and learning research. The BlackJoy Heuristic ICLS2022 Proceedings 1626 © ISLS The BlackJoy Heuristic is meant to frame the design of research to practice and learning environments that afford liberation and transformative opportunities for Black learners.  It includes four tenets that are highlighted in Figure 1 and briefly outlined below.

Figure 1 Black Excellence-this tenet emphasizes positive associations with race and learning.  This entails celebrating the achievement and success of Black learners and creating learning environments and activities that allow them to build positive identities and associations with learning. Black Inventiveness-focuses on leveraging the innate creativity, curiosity, resourcefulness, and innovation of Black learners.  This means fostering open-ended explorations of phenomena and allowing Black students to guide the direction of observations and inquiry.

Black Kinship-centers collectivism, values kinships and integrates Black linguistic practices as central to relationships, learning and Black identity.  This tenet emphasizes the interconnectedness of language and culture and fostering the role that these play in building trust, solidarity, safety and risk-taking in learning environments.

Black Aesthetics—recognizes things that Black learners find beautiful and pleasing and the ways that Black lived experiences intersect with artistic and creative practices.  For example, music for focus, specially fabrics and hairstyles for significant events (i.e. graduations) and dance as a way of expressing community. Black Excellence, Black Inventiveness, Black Kinship and Black Aesthetics allow us to assert a Black liberatory education and research paradigm that centers Black learning, Black humanity and BlackJoy. This is central to building race affirming frameworks that work towards elevating Black learners. All students come to learning contexts with values, orientations, and practices that are reflective of their culture and when these are supported, students engage more holistically and successfully in learning (Medin and Bang, 2014).
