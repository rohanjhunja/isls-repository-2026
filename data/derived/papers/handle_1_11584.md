# “I was debating and now I’m on board”: Supporting Shifts in Teacher Uptake of Computational Modeling in Science Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
There is a need to examine how to support teacher uptake of computational modeling in science classrooms. This poster presents code-the-familiar modeling to foster teacher uptake of computational modeling using code. Drawing on a case study of two high school teachers from a research-practice partnership, we examine data from professional development and interviews to illustrate how teachers shift in their uptake, and share two lessons learned: Supporting bite-sized changes and leaning into teachers’ conceptual expertise.

## Introduction and theoretical background

Integrating computational modeling into K-12 science classrooms has gained traction over the last decade (e.g., Guo et al, 2024; Weintrop et al., 2017) because it can support science learning while engaging in comp utational thinking (CT) practices (e.g., Arastoopour Irgens et al 2020; Dickes et al., 2020; Hutchins et al., 2020; Peel et al., 2021; Wagh et al, 2017). However, integrating science and computing can be challenging for teachers who often see computing and science as inherently incongruent  with ea ch other (Bain et al., 2020). A recent review of research on teacher learning has pointed to a lack of research on how to support teachers’ disciplinary knowledge, particularly around such integration instruction (Yoon et al., in press).

In this paper, we present lessons learned from a Re search-Practice Partnership (RPP) (Coburn et al.,

2013) that aimed to support teachers to integrate c omputational modeling into their high school scienc e

classrooms. We share how a simple model building a ctivity that we call a code-the-familiar activity led two initially reluctant high school teachers to shift i n their uptake of modeling with code for science le arning. Our research questions are: How did the code-the-familiar modeling activity impact teachers’ uptake of computational modeling into their classroom? What lessons did we learn from teacher uptake? ## Methods

This RPP addresses a large urbandistrict’s vision to integrate computational thinking (CT) practices into high school science classrooms to rectify the district’s  concern that enrollment in Computer Science electi ves did not represent its gender, racial, and socio-economic makeup. We instantiate CT practices using the agent-based modeling (ABM) paradigm: A scientific phenomenon is  modeled through its underlying entities and the ru les they follow to examine how the system changes over time (e.g., Wilensky & Reisman, 2006). There is abundant evidence that ABM supports science learning and eng agement in CT practices (e.g., Arastoopour Irgens e t al., 2020; Dickes et al., 2020; Wagh et al., 2017). The RPP has developed 6 ABM units for Biology, Chemistr y, and Physics, and is providing teachers with professional development (PD) opportunities to implement these units. A Code-the-familiar modeling activity: Catching Flies To develop teachers’ comfort with modeling in StarL ogo Nova (Wendel, 2011), the ABM platform being use d, our PD includes simple model building activities that we call code-the-familiar activities. These activities tap into a familiar idea and invite teachers to represent that idea using code. One example is Catching Flies (Zhuang et al.,

2013) in which teachers program a model to represent a simple and familiar mechanism using code-frogs eating

flies. Working in groups of 2-3, teachers conceptually model the idea by identifying the nouns and verbs of thei r model (e.g., the constituent entities and what they  do), and so on. Teachers then translate these idea s into code, and build model extensions such as adding monitors to track the number of flies caught.

## Data collection and analysis

We identified two focal teachers with different levels of experience-one as an AP Computer Science teacher and the other with no prior background with programming. Both showed salient shifts in their uptake of the  practice of integrating computational modeling into their sc ience classrooms. Our data comes from Summer PD  in  2023 and includes teachers’ models and exit ticket responses, PD observation notes, observation notes from a class that involved students programming a model, and teacher interviews and reflections after teaching the target lessons.

Using thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2012), we started with two teachers who showed a salient shift and traced back their development through the proje ct to develop a narrative around their shift. This involved starting with their interviews and identi fying moments in their PDs, co-design work and developed artifacts to trace shifts in their approach. We also used observation notes from their teaching for triangulation.

## Findings: Shift from initial reluctance to uptake of CM

Our analysis revealed shifts in both teachers’ uptake of the idea of bringing programming through computational modeling into their classrooms.

The first case is that of Ms L, a Chemistry teacher  without any programming background. Her initial reluctance stemmed from being “nervous that the model will overtake the Chemistry content for students.” In PD, she joined a group of teachers to model catching fl ies, and noted how the model provided a conceptual structure to think about chemical reactions. The teachers acknowledged the “simplicity of using the [catching flies] model for kinetics”, and loved that students could do exp eriments to “see variabi lity but also the trends”. The teachers considered design tradeoffs of using catching flies to teach reaction rates and ultimately decided that “frogs” and “flies” would be better because it was “not as schooly”. Ms. L developed and taught a lesson sequence consisting of two lessons: Students building the “catching flies” model and then modifying the code to represent and test the relationship between temperature, concentration, surface area and reaction rates. In her interview, Ms. L shared that many students s uccessfully built and modified the model to explore temperature and concentration, and how it affects the rate of a reaction though she also noticed a lack of alignment between coding and chemistry for students, and had ideas for how to better connect the two. However, s he saw the lesson as a “win” and shared that though she ha d been “a lot more skeptical” earlier, she now thou ght, “I probably will use this next year, and like it'll be effective… it feels manageable and useful.” The second case is that of Mr C, a Physics teacher who also taught AP Computer Science. Mr. C shared that he was “reluctant to complicate teaching some of the physics by also modeling” because he thought it would “make things more difficult”. Mr. C joined the same PD session as Ms. L to work on the catchin g flies activity. Our observation notes indicated that he spent time trying, among other things, “ trying to make the fr og bigger” as it ate. In the physics co-design session, Mr. C decided to modify the C ollisions unit by starting the unit with Catching Flies to highlight to students the idea that you can model something happening in response to a collision in StarLogo Nova. When Mr. C taught the lesson, he experimented with it by having student groups present their model extensions. Two groups succeeded in doing wha t Mr. C was unable to do-frogs growing when they eat a fly. Perhaps this success led Mr. C to admit that t he experience of trying it out in his classroom led  him to want to do “more work like that in future iterations” and he would be “interested in helping design that!” This activity became a precursor to the Collisions unit in Mr. C’s class.

## Lessons Learned and discussion

Drawing on our analysis of the shifts in teacher up take reported here, we note two takeaways: Leaning into teachers’ expertise, and supporting teachers to make (even) bite-sized changes if that is what they are comfortable with. In both cases, the teachers’ conceptual expertise-Ms L’s in Chemistry and Mr C’s in Physics and Computer Science-and their pedagogical expertise led them to see the value of this activity. Both teachers made a relatively simple change. Ms L used the catching flies activit y as is, and simply added a lesson for students to extend the model to represent and explore three factors affecting reaction rates. Mr C simply inserted the activity into a unit he had co-designed with the physics team. Even though the changes was simple, both teachers witnessed the value of CM on students’ learning experience. Ms L saw how it helped students draw on the analogy to reason about an otherwise abstract idea (Wendel et al., in press) w hich led her to share, “I was debating and now I’m on board.” Mr C saw that students could do this work in ways that w ere meaningful and conducive to their physics learn ing and engagement in class.

Our work contributes to the literature on computati onal modeling in a few ways. While existing work has proposed supporting teachers to “look inside” existing computational tools and activities as a way to examine the power of making underlying computational layers  explicit (Kelter et al., 2021) or presenting model ing as mathematizing phenomena (Sengupta et al., 2018), ou r work points to the power of simple code-the-familiar modeling activities as an entry point for teachers, including ones who have expertise with coding. This relates to, and leads to a second contribution-pointing to the importance of taking an asset-based approach (e.g., Smith et al., 1994) in our work with teachers to leverage existing domain expertise, resources and/or pedagogical practices as a way to bring still relatively new practices and tools into their classrooms.
