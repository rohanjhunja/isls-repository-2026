# When Students Disagree About What Counts as Science: Examining Epistemic Disagreements in High School Science Classrooms

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Unprecedented exposure to public debates about the reliability of science have led scholars to recommend engaging students in classroom dialogue about scientific epistemology. However, an underexamined aspect of these recommendations is how to navigate the epistemic disagreements that arise during such discussions. Epistemic disagreements refer to differences in what students judge as scientifically reliable. Such differences often stem from conflicting views about what science is for and how it should inform public action. This study examines the nature of epistemic disagreements that emerged as 20 high school students engaged with a curriculum that was designed to surface conflicting aspects of scientific epistemology. Analysis of classroom dialogue show how that these disagreements extended to the role of science in public decision-making. Findings highlight utility and limitations of existing frameworks to navigate these disagreements productively in classrooms.

There are growing curricular recommendations to encourage dialogue about scientific epistemology in classrooms (Chinn et al., 2020; Covitt & Anderson, 2021;  Duncan et al., 2018). This involves recommendations to anchor classroom inquiry in issues that mimic the messiness of real-world scientific inquiry (Chinn et al., 2021), to use simulations that model the complexity of investigating a novel virus (Author et al., 2023; Covitt & Anderson,

2021) and to invite students to engage in meta-epistemic reasoning about how scientific knowledge is produced,

evaluated, and justified (Chinn et al., 2020; Duncan et al., 2018). These instructional recommendations respond to broader concerns about preparing an informed public to respond to unvetted discourse  in the media  about scientific reliability. However, Chinn et al (2020) anticipate that inviting discussions about scientific epistemology in classrooms is likely to result in a new instructional challenge, one around navigating epistemic disagreements. Epistemic disagreements refer to disagreements about what one chooses to believe as accurate or true (Chinn et al., 2020). In classroom inquiry, epistemic disagreements  can range from basic disagreements (e.g., interpreting a data trend differently), to deep disagreements (e.g., conflicting judgments about the standards of reasoning used to justify scientific knowledge). Epistemic disagreements are likely to arise when students engage in inquiry practices and encounter competing claims (Chinn et al., 2020; 2021). For example, in the curriculum we examine in this study students design and conduct experiments using data from an agent-based simulation, to determine which mitigation strategy (e.g., masking, distancing, vaccination or lockdown) most effectively contains an epidemic that closely resembles COVID-19. Students are then challenged to present and justify their claims for critique from  their peers. In these exchanges, disagreements about the reliability of scientific claims arise. Some disagreements around errors such as whether a group miscalculated average infection rates in a 50-day trial, can be resolved by clarifying reasoning or fixing the calculation error. However other disagreements on critique about fairness of using infection rate over death rate as the primary dependent variable or mandating vaccination to the public, can be more difficult to address or resolve. These epistemic tensions remain understudied in classroom settings (Chinn et al., 2020; Duncan et al., 2018). Understanding how epistemic disagreements unfold is essential for designing instruction that prepares students to navigate both the scientific and civic dimensions of science as debated in public discourse (Covitt & Anderson, 2022; Osborne & Allchin, 2024). This study examines epistemic disagreements that emerged in a high school science classroom as students engaged with a curriculum that designed using to elicit conflicting dialogue on aspects of scientific epistemology that makes science reliable, (Yoon et al., 2023; Chinn et al., 2023). In the curriculum, students test five mitigation strategies (e.g., masking, virtual school mandate, vaccination, and lockdown) to contain a novel epidemic  that closely resembles COVID-19 outbreak. In the final two lessons, students compare claims and evidence among peer groups that used different experimental designs, to arrive at a consensus on which mitigation factor  is most effective in containing the spread. Students then present their recommendation to a fictional town council. In this 1817 study, we examine the implementation of the unit in a high school science classroom. We use interactional analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to examine student talk  among 20 students to identify  moments of disagreements that emerged during the consensus-building lessons. We use the research question: How do students engage in and reason through epistemic disagreements about scientific evidence and its use? Theoretical considerations Defining basic and deep epistemic disagreements Chinn et al (2020) provide a useful way to conceptualize epistemic disagreements  about scientific epistemology. They suggest breaking down disagreements  about scientific epistemology  to the discipline’s epistemic aims, ideals, and processes (i.e., AIR framework). For example, s cientific epistemic a ims are to generate models and explanations that are accurate representations of the world. Scientific epistemic i deals include standards of reasoning used to determine if the aim has been met, like, ensuring claims are backed by empirically sound evidence and are informed by pre-existing theories. Scientific e pistemic process are the methods scientists used to achieve the a im. This includes systematic observation of natural world, which are considered more reliable over nonsystematic observation or anecdotal reports. Using AIR framework, Chinn et al. distinguish between two kinds of epistemic disagreements that can emerge when students discuss about scientific epistemology: basic and deep. Basic disagreements are about the scientific content. For example, students candisagree on whether a group misread a graph or miscalculated an averag e. In such a disagreement s students are in alignment about scientific epistemic aims, ideals, and reliable processes  (e.g., generate accurate representations of the world using systematically examined evidence). The disagreement is about  a group’s misalignment with shared standards of epistemic reasoning (e.g., incorrect reporting). Such basic disagreements can be resolved through by examining the data or by collecting additional evidence. Deep epistemic disagreements, in contrast, are not about scientific content, they are about standards of reasoning used to establish the scientific content. For example, when groups disagree on using infection or death rate as a primary independent variable, there is agreement on  scientific epistemic aim (e.g., generate accurate explanations), however, there is a difference in the epistemic ideals and/or processes (e.g., what constitutes representative or fair  evidence). Such disagreements are not unique to classrooms; they also occur in scientific communities, where they can support the refinement of knowledge through justification and critique. However, in classroom contexts, deep disagreements may also involve tensions between disciplinary and personal ways of knowing (González-Howard et al., 2924; Krist et al., 2019) particularly when scientific reasoning intersects with decisions about public action.  Importantly, while the AIR framework captures disagreements grounded in scientific epistemic reasoning, it does not fully account for disagreements that incorporate civic or value-based considerations (e.g., differing views on whether scientists should prescribe or inform public decisions). This limitation is relevant for interpreting the findings of this study. Data sources and analysis This study took place in a ninth-grade biology class at a private school in the northeastern United States, where 20 students worked in groups of four over eight 60-minute lessons to design experiments, use simulations to test mitigation strategies (e.g., masking, vaccination, virtual schooling), and develop data-based recommendations. The unit was taught by Elizabeth, who co-designed it with the research team, and marked students’ first engagement with scientific epistemology. We analyzed approximately five hours of video from the final lessons, selected for their extended epistemic disagreements as students presented claims and engaged in whole-class critique. Our analysis centers on a three-hour focal episode in which three groups presented claims about a single mitigation strategy while one group combined two, prompting debate about the reliability and scientific validity of their claim. With the teacher encouraging critique, students challenged both the group’s evidence and broader questions about whether scientists should prescribe action or simply present findings. Using interactional analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), we examined disagreement during consensus-building through five stages: identifying episodes of disagreement, categorizing them as basic (clarifying content) or deep (questioning how or why claims were constructed), coding epistemic aims, ideals, and processes using the AIR framework (Chinn et al., 2020), validating coding with an external expert, and conducting constant comparative analysis to develop themes. This process yielded two key themes of deep epistemic disagreement: what counts as reliable scientific evidence and the role of scientists in society, revealing a shift in students’ reasoning from interpreting data to debating scientific responsibility.

This exchange illustrates a deep epistemic disagreement centered on conflicting epistemic aims—specifically, the purpose of scientific communication. Caleb frames the aim of science as guiding or persuading the public toward a specific action (“If they opt out, vaccination won’t work”) suggesting that scientific authority entails a responsibility to advocate for compliance. In contrast, the presenting group frames their aim as informing the public with accurate, evidence-based options, maintaining a stance of neutrality and respect for public agency. These contrasting aims— persuasion vs. transparent reporting—reflect fundamentally different views about the role of scientists in society and what science is supposed to achieve.

In this study, we report on two themes of deep disagreements we observed in our data: Disagreements about what counts as reliable scientific evidence and disagreements about the role of scientists. We argue below, how students used sophisticated meta-epistemic reasoning, that went beyond descriptive narrations of what made their scientific claims reliable.

Disagreements about what counts as reliable scientific evidence and role of scientists as subject-matter experts These disagreements centered on how students determined what made  their scientific claims reliable. Students agreed on the scientific epistemic a im, which is to get to the most accurate explanation of how to contain the epidemic. However, they differed in the epistemic  ideals and/or reliable process, they believed best fulfilled the aim of generating accurate explanations. For example, In  one exchange, a group presented their claim that vaccines are the most effective mitigation strategy  using infection rate as the main measure of effectiveness. A peer challenged this choice, questioning whether infection r ate was sufficient to justify the claim that vaccines were the most effective mitigation strategy.

Sasha:  Our group found that vaccines had the lowest infection rate by Day 80, so we concluded that vaccination is the most effective way to stop the spread. Liam:  But infection rate isn’t the only thing. What about the death rate? If people still die, even if fewer get infected, how is that better?

Sasha:  Well, if fewer people get infected, fewer people die, that’s just math. Liam:  Not necessarily. You could have a small number infected but still have more deaths if it’s more severe.

Teacher:  So, you’re saying you disagree on which data best shows effectiveness? Liam:  Yeah. We used the death rate because that’s what really matters. Sasha:  But your experimental design is different than ours. We ran ours for 80 days. It’s not fair to compare it to 100 days, then it’s a different experiment. The above exchange is an example of  meta-epistemic discourse on evaluating ways of knowing (Chinn et al., 2022). Liam’s critique reflects an epistemic ai m of capturing the full scope of the epidemic’s impact —he questions whether infection data alone can provide an accurate explanation without considering deaths as an outcome. Sasha, in contrast, demonstrates a different epistemic i deal of reliability—she emphasizes consistency in data collection and fairness in comparison across experiments, explaining that their group focused on infection rate because reducing infections would, in turn, reduce deaths. When Liam continues to challenge this reasoning, 1819 Sasha defends her group’s methodological choice, noting that comparing findings from experiments using different outcome variables would not be fair. This moment illustrates how students used reasoning to defend the aims and i deals guiding their claims. However, this  discussion did not progress toward integrating both approaches. Neither group discussed how the two experiments (e.g., one that looked at the death rate and the other that looked at infection rate) could be used together to better understand which mitigation factor most effectively contained the epidemic. This suggests that while students were reasoning at a sophisticated meta-epistemic level— evaluating and defending what counts as reliable evidence —their discourse remained bounded within their own epistemic commitments rather than moving toward synthesis. Disagreements about role of scientists as subject-matter experts centered on whether scientists should prescribe or merely inform public action. For example, one group suggested using a combination of mask mandate and virtual school, along with vaccination. A peer, however, questioned why the group had not simply concluded that vaccines were the best option.

Paris:  Why didn’t you just say vaccines?

Linda:  We noticed it’s effective in complete recovery only when 100 days are met… Other mitigation strategies, when combined, also helped. So why wouldn’t we report that? That’s what scientists do.

Paris:  That’s not fair, people are not scientists. You will confuse them with options. Caleb:  Well, aren’t you taking your jobs as scientists lightly by not telling them what to do? Linda:  No, we’re not. We did controlled trials, and we know these mitigation factors work. We just think it’s for the public to decide.

Caleb:  As the town government, we’re trying to get people to do the mitigation factor that works, but you’re not forcing it—you’re just suggesting.

In this exchange, students were debating scientific responsibility, drawing on different epistemic aims for what it means to “act scientifically.” Paris and Caleb’s critiques, “people are not scientists, you’ll confuse them,” and “aren’t you taking your jobs lightly ”, reflect an epistemic aim of  persuasion: that scientists, because they understand the data, should guide public behavior toward the most effective course of action. In contrast, Linda’s defense, “why wouldn’t we report that, that’s what scienti sts do”, reflects an epistemic aim of reporting: that scientists should present evidence transparently and allow others to decide. This study makes two contributions to research on epistemic reasoning in science classrooms. First, our findings extend the use of the AIR framework  (Chinn et al., 2020) by showing how it can be used to trace differences in students’ epistemic aims, ideals, and processes during classroom disagreement. Consistent with prior work on epistemic aims in classroom discourse (Krist et al. 2019), students engaged in sophisticated meta-epistemic reasoning to justify their claims. However, our findings also highlight  a limitation of the framework: while AIR captures disciplinary epistemic differences, it does not fully account for disagreements that emerge when scientific reasoning intersects with civic considerations, such as differing views on whether scientists should inform or direct public action. Second, our findings suggest that supporting epistemic dialogue requires more than creating opportunities for disagreement. While students articulated and defended competing epistemic positions, their reasoning remained bounded within their own perspectives, with limited movement toward reconciliation. This points to the need for greater attention to how epistemic disagreements are mediated in classroom interaction. Recent work on classroom discourse emphasizes the role of language and participation in shaping how scientific meaning is negotiated (González-Howard et al. 2024). Building on this perspective, we argue that future research should examine how teachers can support students in not only articulating epistemic diff erences, but also in coordinating and integrating them toward shared understanding.
