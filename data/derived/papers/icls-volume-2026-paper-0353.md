# Reimagining Learning through Arts and Making: Creative Identity Work in Partnership

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This hybrid symposium brings together learning sciences research exploring intergenerational identity development in relation to the arts and making as a means of deepening partnership. We look across continents through projects that center art and making as identity work, particularly for people navigating historic oppression, to better understand the ways these creative acts can provide pathways of resistance and reimagination. We follow the conference theme to reimagine the ways that learning scientists interpret “impactful learning experiences” (Fishman et al., 2013) in partnerships that emphasize notions of joy and creation as meaningful forms of reimagining learning in our present global context. Each paper reimagines how intergenerational participants inscribe themselves into the world, positioning acts of identity building as a deeply rooted engagement in possibility. Through this session, we strive to build conversations across these nine projects and in collaboration with attendees toward more nuanced, critical, and culturally grounded notions of partnership. Overview This proposed hybrid symposium brings into conversation learning sciences research exploring how people develop identities across lifespans in relation to the arts —broadly conceived as making and tinkering, creating, writing, composing, and creatively engaging with others toward more transformative futures. Drawing from traditions of design-based research (e.g., Brown, 1992), participatory design research (e.g., Bang & Vossoughi, 2016), testimonio and pláticas (e.g., Delgado-Bernal, 2012), and decolonizing and indigenous methodologies (e.g., Bang, 2020; Patel, 2016), we examine the ways that partnerships resonate and grow alongside the identities of participants when grounded in notions of joy, play, creativity, and belonging (Mirra & Garcia, 2024). In this symposium, we collectively define learning as ontological, a process of becoming someone new (e.g., Lee, 2016), 2340 and view learning and associated identities as expansive, developing along pathways in relation to our histories, communities, partnerships, and collective ecologies (e.g., Bang, 2020; Gutiérrez et al., 2019; Nasir et al., 2020). We see identity as an ever-changing part of human experience, both related to and disruptive of structures of power that have continuously led to oppression and genocide based on race, ethnicity, culture, religion, language, gender, socioeconomic status, ability, and the growing number of traits deemed “illegal” by those in power in the U.S. and other nations. We follow theorists whose work has pushed boundaries of what it means to embody artistry, particularly for historically marginalized peoples whose collective efforts have alwa ys been the catalyst for movements toward more equitable futures (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Our aim with this collective conversation is to look across our projects, all of which center art and making as part of learning trajectories through which people build identities (Nasir et al., 2020), particularly for people navigating historic oppression, to better understand the ways creative acts can provide pathways of resistance, reimagination, and transformation in relationship with and to one another, our environments, and the more-than-human beings whose ecologies we share. This session will center on the questions: 1) How can projects focused on identity-building within creative contexts help to reimagine the ways partnerships operate toward more joyful, just, and transformative ends? 2) How can conversations across projects move the learning sciences to more deeply attend to the creativity essential for reinvention of systems and structures? In this symposium, we discuss papers in three 18-minute rounds, ending with collaborative discussion with Dr. McKinney de Royston, audience, and participants, to push back against restrictive notions of identification, instead taking up dialogical approaches to identity (e.g., Akkerman & Meijer, 2011). Across these papers, scholars examine the ways that making and the arts can extend toward more transformative notions of what it means to be and become a certain kind of person (Enyedy et al., 2014). We bring into conversation projects drawing from narrative (e.g., Delgado-Bernal, 2012), proleptic (Lee, 2016), and liberatory notions of identity development across generations (Nasir et al., 2020), and engage in conversations to push these silos toward more holistic and transformative ends. Framing identity building as an artistic process, rather than a solely scientific one, we reimagine how young people and their elders inscribe themselves into the world. We position every act of identification as a potential artistic act of defiance, imagination, and creation toward more transformative futures. In considering the call for this year’s conference, we use these pieces to speak together about the ways that partnership and design can act as levers to engage in joyful resistance and creative reimagining of structures of power (Hayes & Kaba, 2023). The scholars in this symposium draw from sociocultural, sociocritical, and constructionist theories of learning, as well as notions of criticality, embodiment, temporality, and design to reconsider the ways that our work can build knowledge and community collectively. Although the papers feature a variety of methods, all share perspectives that data can and should be representative of the multiple and complex ways learners navigate the world (Bridges, 2020). This complexity is part of our session structure, where we offer both traditional and creative opportunities for participants across modalities to engage and build knowledge collaboratively and in partnership. Despite differences in epistemology and analysis, each project suggests new ways of understanding identity development as a deeply cultural and artistic process of reimagining both oneself and the world. While all papers engage in examining arts and identity development through design, partnership, and collaboration, the projects differ in stages of development and are situated across varying contexts, nations, and continents with an array of participants. Papers 1, 3, 5, and 6 examine the ways university students engage across settings to build identities artistically alongside diverse communities of learners. P apers 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, and 8 take up notions of intergenerational identification and learning to examine the ways that making and the arts can support attunement tonew conceptions of self as activist and part of a “political-ethical becoming” (Vossoughi et al., 2021). Paper 9 acts as an overarching commentary across the papers, examining making and tinkering and proposing new ways of framing acts of artistry as supportive of learners’ critical consciousness (Freire, 1970). Each of the papers focuses on a diverse community of learners, where participants with historically marginalized identifications as well as other actors draw upon powerful identity resources (Nasir and Cooks, 2009) to support the reimagining of self and the world as a joyful and creative act, deeply rooted in cultural pathways of learning (Nasir et al., 2020). Together, these papers offer rich groundwork for conversations in the learning sciences, pushing on notions of partnership and learning as identity-making, while interrogating disparate yet deeply interconnected sociopolitical contexts. Paper 1: Identity work in a community and art based engaged university program Sanne Akkerman, Jeroen Vermeulen 2341 This paper reports on identity work taking place in a transdisciplinary community-engaged master’s track at Utrecht University. In this year-long program, students are invited into the everyday life of an urban public park, where they meet, talk with, and collaborate alongside local citizens representing a wide range of interest-based communities, including skaters, graffiti artists, rappers, youth care workers, and caretakers of trees, grass, and water. Through the track, students are asked to move beyond predefined disciplinary orientations and questions. They are challenged to explore the stories and practices of these communities, while inviting and bringing diverse community members and groups together to exchange perspectives and potentially build new relations around central communal themes. In this paper, we examine what it means for students to engage in what in this track is intended as a form of ontological synchronization with what happens and matters to local communities at a specific site and what it means for community members to become involved in prescenery science (Akkerman, Bakker, & Penuel, 2021). Our focus is on identity work that emerges in the process of synchronization and community engagement. Following Akkerman and Meijer (2011), we understand identity work from a dialogical perspective, where people come to explore and dynamically understand themselves through relations and collaborations with others. In the context of this track, we analyze how students and community members from various subcultures develop new understandings by relating to others with less familiar backgrounds and perspectives. We consider motivation letters, reports of interest, week ly meetings during the year and the communal creation of a light-art story and public event over time. Findings show the ways in which students and community members of the park take outside and inside perspectives of others but also develop new shared perspectives in the art works being produced functioning as an effective boundary object (Star, 1989) to materialize and structure the open, and at times discomfortable, educational process of mutual engagement. These findings illustrate sustained community joy through collected, family-made artifacts. Paper 3: Identity work in a community and art based engaged university program Teyona James-Harris, Shirin Vossoughi, Lulu Madise This paper examines educator learning and mutual becoming in a longstanding STEAM and storytelling program serving Black, Latinx/e and Asian/South Asian middle schoolers. We ask: What conditions support learning across an intergenerational collective of ed ucators of color? How do educators describe the identity shifts and forms of mutual becoming they experience over time? Here mutual becoming refers to moments when student and educator learning are dynamically interrelated and mutually generative. Research has identified key conditions that support teachers’ professional learning and efficacy (e.g., communities of practice, guidance around equitable pedagogies, instructional materials designed for teacher learning [Patton & Parker, 2017; Wei, et. al., 2010]). Yet there is a need to better understand the paradigmatic identity shifts and forms of political-ethical becoming that can emerge for educators in settings that reimagine disciplinary domains and social relations, and practice expansive forms of arts and political education. Here we draw on Vossoughi et al.’s (2021) definition of political-ethical becoming as “not only reorganizing our thoughts but reshaping our relations,” and Rogoff’s (2023) fractals as attuning to mutually constituting processes at smaller and larger scales over time. Utilizing interpretive ethnography (Erickson, 1985) and participatory design research (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) we report on a data set documenting student and educator learning within a 6-week summer STEAM/Storytelling program over eight years through audio-video recordings, field notes, student and educator interviews, and documentation of co-design processes. We share findings developed through qualitative coding and memoing of pre-and post-program educator interviews, analysis of one focal educators’ t rajectory across years, and field notes that capture mutual becoming through educators’ reflections on key interactions with students. Our analytic approach involves working closely with an intergenerational group of educators of color (ages 13-62) who blend the role of teachers, artists, and researchers. Our findings show how participants describe both structural and relational conditions of educator learning as consequential to the identity shifts they experience. Structural conditions include intergenerational apprenticeship, daily debriefs and educator reflections, opportunities to witness and try out others’ pedagogical moves, and fieldnote writing. Relational conditions include intentional community building and care among educators, encouragement to show up as their full selves, and a culture of playful communion that aligned with the forms of artistic creation supported among students. Patterns across field notes and educators’ pre-and post-interviews reveal the ways educator learning and political-ethical becoming involved embodying the artistic ways of being supported with students (e.g., taking our imaginations seriously, embracing the process of creation and learning, engaging in various forms of language work and conceptual revision) in ways that connected with educators’ own complex histories with schooling, learning, and life. One educator, for example, discussed the ways she was taught to avoid mistakes within a rigid schooling context, and how she needed to learn to soften this tendency within herself as she supported our middle school students to embrace process. Another example of language revision shows how the kinds of thinki ng we were supporting our students to engage around rethinking inherited terms was taken up by novice educators. One undergraduate educator referred to Red Winged Black Birds during an outdoor walk as “territorial” and then later revised his language to “protective,” sharing with students the “why” of his revision. Our paper also includes a deeper look at one teacher’s trajectory over time and the ways she took up storywork. Pedagogical storytelling was a routine practice within this setting and involved careful decision making on the part of educators around language and nourishing forms of political education and storytelling for middle school students. This teacher's journey reveals how guided feedback and revision of her story through intergenerational apprenticeship played a key role in expanding not only what she offered youth story listeners, but how she thought about and walked with her own story over time. We also found educators practiced these ways of being in relationships and contexts outside the learning environment, with family and friends. This included discussing the ways they noticed their language changing in conversations with others, and how they are enacting new forms of careful noticing and attunement to others and the world outside the educational settings. We found that all educator interviews narrated this type of “rippling out” of learning as a key indicator of the identity shifts supported by transformative projects. Such findings can support intentional design of the conditions that cultivate transformative educator learning, and ways of conceptualizing educator learning as a deep process of identity work and political-ethical 2343 becoming. Further, mutual becoming both troubles student-teacher binaries common within oppressive educational structures (Freire, 1970) and helps theorize identity shifts in ways that carefully attune to the centrality of relationality and creativity in human learning. This paper offers a speculative framework for understanding three practices (tinkering, gambiarra, and bricolage) as distinct epistemologies of making. By juxtaposing these traditions, the paper advances a comparative epistemology 2348 bridging perspectives in maker education research to explore how knowledge is constructed through diverse relationships among people, materials, and places (Nasir & Cooks, 2009). Tinkering rooted in constructionism (Harel & Papert, 1991) and characterized by playful iteration, feedback-rich environments, and open-ended exploration (Resnick & Rosenbaum, 2013; Bevan et al., 2015). Knowledge in tinkering emerges through iterative prototyping, a  process of learning by manipulating materials, observing behaviors, and refining ideas through cycles of experimentation. While often practiced in well-resourced spaces, tinkering can also be deeply social, echoing the “four Ps” of creative learning: Projects, Passion, Peers, and Play (Resnick, 2017). Blikstein (2008, 2018) extends this lineage toward Freire’s pedagogy of empowerment, positioning making as a form of critical engagement and emancipation. Through this lens, tinkering is not merely playful but emancipatory: a way of knowing that cultivates curiosity, agency, and self-directed discovery.

Gambiarra, by contrast, emerges from Brazilian material culture -- a makeshift improvisation and repair practice that transforms scarcity into creative agency. Knowledge here is constructed through embodied adaptation and social improvisation, where making becomes both a technical and political act. Gambiarra represents a form of empoderamento, a practice of empowerment resonating with Freire’s (1970) call for praxis: reflection and action upon the world in order to transform it (Blikstein, 2013; Domingues, 2016). It draws attention to inequities in infrastructure, epistemic authority, and access to tools, revealing how creativity can flourish in contexts of limitation. Rather than translating gambiarra into Western design studios, I argue for recognizing it as a valid epistemology of making, aligning with Papanek’s (1971) vision of socially responsible design, where necessity, ethics, and imagination converge. Gambiarra challenges educators to value the knowledge embedded in constraint, repair, and improvisation, rather than idealizing abundance. Bricolage, the act of “making do with what is at hand” (Lévi -Strauss, 1962), offers an epistemological counterpoint to rationalist notions of engineering. Knowledge in bricolage emerges through recombinatory interpretation: assembling existing materials and meaning s to address new situations. Papert (1980) reframed bricolage as a mode of computational thinking that privileges intuition and connection over formal planning. Ackermann (1999, 2013) later expanded the idea, describing children and digital creators as “digital bricoleurs” who learn by appropriating, remixing, and personalizing tools and media. In this transition, bricolage evolved from a solitary metaphor of problem-solving tonetworked epistemology, reflecting how knowledge circulates through shared digital and social environments. While gambiarra responds to scarcity and power, bricolage reconfigures abundance, working not from lack, but from the multiplicity of what is already there. Viewed together, these epistemologies compose a constellation of ways of knowing. Tinkering privileges learning through iterative experimentation; gambiarra constructs understanding through collective adaptation and critical reflection; bricolage generates insight through recombination and reinterpretation. Each is simultaneously material, aesthetic, and relational. Making, in this sense, becomes an artistic inquiry, a conversation with materials through which learners come to know themselves and the world.  Framing these practices as epistemologies also highlights their role in culturally responsive education. Learners engage more deeply when making practices resonate with their lived experiences and are validated as legitimate ways of knowing. In contexts where certain creative forms are undervalued or marginalized, recognizing these practices as epistemic frameworks affirms cultural familiarity while expanding what counts as knowledge and creativity. Building on this synthesis, I propose three speculative research directions: 1) Theoretical: What new insights emerge when we understand tinkering, gambiarra, and bricolage as distinct epistemologies of making rather than universal creative behaviors? 2) Design and Empirical: How might learning environments be designed to incorporate cultural logics of these practices, valuing uncertainty, constraint, and recombination as core to knowledge construction? 3) Practice and Facilitation: How can educators and facilitators recognize, support, and sustain these culturally situated ways of knowing in diverse learning contexts ? By addressing these questions, we move toward a more pluralistic and equitable understanding of making. One that resists the universalizing tendencies of the Maker Movement while retaining its spirit of curiosity and joy. Reframing tinkering, gambiarra, and bricolage as epistemologies invites us to design learning partnerships that honor creativity not as a singular method but as a constellation of situated, relational, and embodied ways of knowing and becoming.

During each presentation, papers will be grouped with a mix of online and in-person participants to ensure dialogue across modalities. Additionally, paper authors will be partnered so that each paper is guaranteed a generative and productive audience (see Table 2).
