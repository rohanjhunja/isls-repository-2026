# Adapting the Use-Decode-Modify-Create Framework for Teacher Professional Learning about STEM-based Computational Activities

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To effectively facilitate integrated STEM and computing instruction, K-12 teachers must support students’ engagement in computational practices. We describe the design of teacher professional learning activities based on the Use-Modify-Create instructional framework and examine how they prepared teachers to enact computational modeling activities. In interviews, teachers indicated that exploring simulations and evaluating code were helpful scaffolds for learning to construct computational models. Teachers also identified the need for additional supports for code interpretation and evaluation.

## Introduction and study context

To prepare K –12 students for the increasing reliance of science,  technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) disciplines on computing, integrated STEM and computing (STEM+C) instruction enables students t o apply computing practices within a STEM contex t (e.g., Cabrera et al., 2023; Li et al., 2020; Wei ntrop et al., 2016). Science teachers may not be prepared to faci litate STEM+C instruction, as science teacher educa tion programs do not typically focus on cross-disciplinary pedagogy (Czerniak & Johnson, 2014), and science teachers may not have formal training in computing or programming (Ketelhut et al., 2020; Killen et al., 2023). To address this gap, teacher professional learning (PL) experi ences and supporting resources that promote discipl inary integration are essential for high-quality enactment of STEM+C instruction (Rich et al. 2019). This paper reports on the design and implementation  of a five-day PL experience to prepare teachers to enact computational modeling activities in the Water Runoff Challenge (WRC). The WRC is a 10-hour curriculum unit that integrates computational modeling with Earth science and engineering for 5th and 6th grade students (Chiu et al., 2021; McElhaney et al., 2020). In the  WRC, students (1) investigate and develop a scient ific, conceptual model of water runoff, (2) construct a c omputational model (CM) using a  block-based modeling environment, then (3) use a simulation based on the CM to design and test an engineering solution (a configuration of surface materials that minimizes water runoff for a school under heavy rainfall). This study is part of a larger project that investi gates the impacts of three types of computational modeling activities on student learning and teacher s’ instructional practices. In the current study, w e developed two sets of instructional activities as a lternatives to constructing the CM. The Interpret and Evaluate a CM (IE) activities provided students with multiple versions  of the CM code, only some of which were correct. S tudents interpreted and evaluated each piece of code, selecting only those that will result in a working model. The Explore and Evaluate a Simulation (EE) activities provided students with buggy or incomplete versions of the simulation, but the students did not see the underlying code. Students tested simulations and examined the results to evaluate whether simulations made correct runoff predictions  or needed changes. Figure 1 illustrates the comput ing environments students use for each of the three instructional conditions. Design of professional learning activities to support computational modeling To prepare teachers to enact the WRC in their class rooms, we drew from the Use-Modify-Create (UMC) framework (Lee et al. 2011), a three-stage engagement sequence for novice learners to de velop confidence and competency in computing. Recent implementations of UMC, especially in science modeling contexts, in clude a “Decode” step prior to the “Modify” stage where lea rners link the underlying mechanism in coding and t he scientific process it represents, enabling learners  to explore and better understand the mod el code (Lee, 2024). This Use-Decode-Modify-Create sequence thus provides a helpful framework for engaging teachers with all three types of instructional activities as part of PL for the WRC. In the "Use" phase of the PL sequence, teachers learned about the EE activities, were abstracted from programming, and only interacted with the given simulation (Figure 1c). In the "Decode & Modify” phase (aligned with t he IE activities), teachers were then asked to inte rpret and select segments of code that would be part of a working CM of water runoff (Figure 1b); they then assembled the appropriate code segments and conducted simulation tests to confirm their code interpretations. Finall y, in the "Create" phase (aligned with the Construct activiti es), teachers constr ucted the CM in the block-based programming environment (Figure 1a) and then tested  their own CM until they achieved a desired model t hat correctly predicted water runoff.

We designed a five-day activity sequence for the PL workshop for the teacher participants. Day 1 focused on Earth Science, Day 2 on Engineering Design, Day 3 on the EE activities, Day 4 on the IE activities,  and Day 5 on the Construct activities. Although students experience the WRC as Earth Science, followed by computational modeling, and finally engineering design, we chose to facilitate the engineering activities prior to t he CM activities. We believed this sequence would improve the coherence of the PL experience for teachers by enabling them to see the end goal of the CM activities (the final engineering simulation), before working with the runoff simulation and its underlying code. Each day, teachers alternately engaged with the unit activities from the student perspective (e.g., Lowell, 2024), then assumed the perspective of teachers to anticipate student assets and challenges, identify student supports, review lesson plans, customize materials, and plan enactment. Teacher feedback and next steps We facilitated the PL workshop with six teachers in  a large urban school district and conducted interv iews with each teacher: one shortly after the PL workshop and  one following their unit enactment. Our analysis o f the interviews illustrates that the PL w orkshop activities appeared to benefit teachers. Ta ble 1 identifies two key themes and shows examples from teachers’ interview responses. Future work will investigate how teacher s’ enactment of the CM activities relates to student outcomes such as engagement and STEM learning.
