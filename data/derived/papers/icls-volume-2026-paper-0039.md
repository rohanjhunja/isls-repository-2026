# “Explain photosynthesis to a Swiftie:” How Students Remix and Subvert “Answer Engine” AI Chatbots into a Dialogic Tool

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As GenAI tools enter classrooms, their interactional role in supporting students is not well understood. This study examines 53 chat transcripts from two sixth-grade photosynthesis classes to investigate how students engage with a general-purpose chatbot. Rather than treating the bot as a one-shot “answer engine”, students actively shaped the exchange, prompting changes in voice, audience, and genre to explore the same core ideas in multiple ways. We examine three focal cases and trace how exchanges either works, wobbles, or breaks, as students calibrate explanations and responses to refusals or rigidly literal replies. These interactions show how style and audience design can support revisiting core mechanisms, not just adding play. Our analysis highlights the work students do to sustain dialogue, and the implications for designing more inclusive and dialogic AI systems.

Research in the learning sciences has long shown that understanding is rarely linear or orderly but instead emerges through dialogic and iterative talk rather than a one-shot question-and-answer (Michaels & O’Connor, 2012; O’Connor & Michaels, 1996). Students also routinely use play, humor, and popular culture to reframe school knowledge, moves often dismissed as off-task yet shown to support participation and sense-making (Moje et al., 2004; Roth et al., 2011). From this perspective, how an explanation is voiced, for whom, and with what stance is not secondary, but part of how understanding is built. Yet much of educational technology, and increasingly Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI), is organized around a different interactional model. Classroom technologies are routinely cast in metrics of speed, accuracy, and efficiency, and this script is sustained by a discourse that naturalizes efficiency as a learning goal (Blikstein & Blikstein, 2021; Selwyn, 2022). GenAI inherits an “answer engine” script: one-shot queries, immediate output, and minimal back-and-forth, promising personalization at scale via information retrieval, task automation, or tutoring (Holmes et al., 2019; Luckin et al., 2016). This script narrows what counts as legitimate participation by privileging clean questions in a neutral register and concise exchanges, while treating other voices (e.g., playful, vernacular, performative) as noise or misuse (Delpit, 1995; Lippi-Green, 2012). In practice, this becomes visible when policy refusals, capability limits, or literal responses misread students’ intent or treat playful framings as off-task rather than as resources for sensemaking (Liao et al., 2018). This can position some students’ stylistic and cultural resources as needing to be filtered out, with uneven consequences depending on whose registers the system recognizes. What we still lack are accounts of how students actually engage with GenAI in classrooms. Much current work on GenAI emphasizes outcomes (e.g., writing quality, exam scores, problem-solving), often assuming a single mode of engagement: students ask, AI answers (e.g., Kim et al., 2025). That leaves underexamined interactional work students do when they do not treat the chatbot as a one-shot answer machine. In our data, middle school students prompted a chatbot about photosynthesis to shift voice and genre (“for a five-year-old,” “like a Swiftie,” “now in cat language”) and used improvisational cues (“shorter,” “sillier”) to steer revisions. These exchanges make visible not just what the bot says, but how students try to shape the terms of the interaction. We offer a process-focused account of student-AI interaction in a lesson on photosynthesis. Using chat logs, we analyze how students prompt revoicing, use improvisational cues, and navigate refusals, tracing when play works, wobbles, or breaks. Through vignettes and cross-case analysis, we argue that these interactions blur boundaries between content and performance, utility and play, and position GenAI as an epistemic object whose refusals and literalness shape the interaction. In doing so, we extend existing theories of dialogic learning by showing how students engage in revoicing by proxy, prompting the system to shift voice, genre, or persona to better fit a particular audience. These moves position style not as decorative but as a resource for sense-making and play as a method for calibrating explanations. Taken together, the paper offers an interactional account of how students iteratively refine explanations, a lens for tracing when playful framing becomes fragile, and implications for designing more dialogically responsive AI tools. 300 From “answer engine” to dialogic partner Most educational technologies have historically been framed around optimization and delivery; tools that streamline instruction, automate feedback, and enhance efficiency (Cuban, 2001; Selwyn, 2022; Williamson, 2017). This framing extends to recent narrati ves around GenAI, emphasizing frictionless accuracy, on-demand information, task automation, and personalized tutoring at scale (Holmes et al., 2019; Luckin et al., 2016). While general-purpose chatbots are capable of extended conversation, they are often positioned, through design and use, as “answer engines”, tools that prioritize direct responses to one-shot queries with minimal back-and-forth. Interfaces, user expectations, and educational norms often reinforce this pattern, nudging students toward transactional Q&A formats (Cazden, 2001). In tightly packed classrooms governed by assessment alignment and instructional coverage, this can yield long, undifferentiated responses and a flattened sense of interaction, limiting opportunities for exploration or play. This reduces opportunities for the kinds of interactional work through which understanding is often built in classroom discourse.

Dialogic pedagogy and instructional conversations Dialogic pedagogy treats classroom talk as a primary medium for building and revising ideas in interaction, emphasizing dialogue that supports reasoning and cumulative development of ideas (Alexander, 2008; Mercer, 2000). Learning is visible in discourse moves that keep ideas open and make reasoning accountable, and in how meanings are shaped across turns (Lemke, 1990; Michaels & O’Connor, 2012; O’Connor & Michaels, 1996). In classrooms, instructional conversations are typically organized around an asymmetr y where a more knowledgeable participant elicits, evaluates, and presses for elaboration in ways that make reasoning public and accountable (Cazden, 2001). GenAI chatbots generate explanations on demand, but they do not reliably sustain the uptake, respons iveness, and pressing that characterize dialogic teaching. In our data, the interaction is a student-led instructional conversation where students use the bot as a partner to produce drafts and treat the dialogue as a place to refine how an explanation is voiced, for whom, and with what stance. The bot functions less as a tutor and more as a draft-and-response partner that students recruit for exploratory explanation-building (e.g., testing versions of an idea, calibrating voice and audience, repairing misa lignment with the system, which is too literal or refuses). In what follows, we treat “dialogic” moments as turns where students request revoicing, prompt an analogy or a metaphor, shift the audience, use improvisational cues (e.g., “shorter,” “simpler,” “ more like X”), or repair breakdowns after refusals. We focus on those exchanges because they show dialogic negotiation, and we do not consider short, one-shot factual queries to be dialogic, as they provide little evidence of negotiation in the chat logs.

Play, register, and negotiated interactions Human Computer Interaction (HCI) research offers a counter-script to the “answer engine” framing, one in which users treat AI agents as negotiated partners whose contributions can be steered, revised, or revoiced across interaction (Luger & Sellen, 2016; Porcheron et al., 2018). From this perspective, chatbots are sites of interactional work, where learners may try on voices, test boundaries, or prompt revisions to get correct answers and to shape how an explanation is voiced and received. This steering work intersects with classroom norms that often privilege formal academic registers. Moves like jokes, slang, genre shifts, or meme-speak are read as off-task because they deviate from expected tone, assessment alignment, or behavior “appropriate” norms. Draw ing on productive disciplinary engagement (Engle & Conant, 2002) and work treating these moves as a resource for sense-making (Brown, 2006; Brown & Ryoo, 2008), we treat students' genre shifts and revoicing attempts as legitimate disciplinary practice rath er than “ornamental.” From a classroom discourse perspective, revoicing (restating a contribution in a way that invites elaboration) helps show how meaning is shaped across turns (Lemke, 1990). Here, students often elicit revoicing by proxy, prompting the system to restyle explanations for a particular audience or stance.

Alongside revoicing, prior research has highlighted the roles of humor, play, and affect. Students rarely respond in formal registers; they joke, use slang, riff on pop culture, role-play, and invent roles (Vossoughi & Bevan, 2014). Such moves are increasingly seen as productive. Humor can focus attention and anchor ideas in science talk (Roth et al., 2011), and “third spaces” link academic content to youth culture, offering students personal relevance and agency (Moje et al., 2004). Work on co-creative AI shows similar dynamics. Users steer voice and genre through short prompts and rapid revisions (Lee et al., 2022; Roemmele et al., 2021). In home settings, studies with children have documented playful testing and anthropomorphism, and negotiated refusals (Beneteau et al., 2020; Druga et al., 2017).

## Context and setting

This study took place in a public urban middle school and was embedded in a larger design-based research project examining how computational modeling can be integrated across multiple units. The photosynthesis unit, including the chatbot activity analyzed here, was co-designed by the research team and the classroom teacher. The activity took place during the first part of the unit alongside hands-on and computational modeling activities on photosynthesis. The in-class chat activity lasted 20 minutes with 53  consenting sixth-grade students (11-12 years old) working independently on computers across two classes taught by the same teacher. Students were invited to “ask the bot about photosynthesis,” and then continued the conversation using their own questions, stylistic requests, and modifications. The teacher introduced the task and circulated, but did not script prompts or intervene in student-bot exchanges; no teacher turns appear in the logs. The chatbot was new to the teacher’s photosynthesis instruction, and students had no prior exposure to GenAI in the classroom, although we did not assess out-of-school use. The tool used was a standard version of GPT-4 accessed through a shared account. Students saw no visible system prompts, and the assistant responded to both on-topic and playful prompts. Data corpus The activity produced 53 chat transcripts. We analyze only the GenAI chat logs, not students’ experiments or computational models, and we did not collect individual demographic data. Each transcript included time-stamped turns between a student and the chatbot. Some students continued their conversations later the same day outside class using the same interface, and those turns are included. In one focal case (Jordan), two sessions are analyzed as a single continuous transcript. For close analysis, we selec ted five interactionally rich transcripts that showed sustained steering, repair, and genre or audience shifts (13-58 turns). From these five cases, we developed three vignettes based on narrative coherence and their representation of three distinct trajectories. We retained the full corpus to contextualize broader patterns and to assess how widely particular moves appeared. To check for selection bias, we scanned the remaining shorter transcripts for our core codes (Table 1). These moves also appeared in s horter chats, but with fewer iterations, suggesting that the focal cases offered a thicker view of processes present more widely. All quotes used are reproduced verbatim from the logs, and each student was assigned a non-identifying ID. We refer to them by pseudonyms. Combining interaction analysis with reflexive thematic coding (Braun & Clarke, 2006; Jordan & Henderson, 1995), we analyzed the corpus in four stages. First, for descriptive turn-level coding, we coded each student and bot turn using a set of five codes developed inductively through repeated review of the logs (see Table 1), allowing multiple codes per turn. Second, we grouped adjacent turns into short episodes organized around a single explanatory thread, with attention to uptake, refusals, and repair. Thi rd, we labeled higher-level arc trajectories (see “Arcs and vignettes” section below) based on how episodes accumulated over time within a transcript. Fourth, we compared across focal cases and the broader corpus to identify recurring sequences, repair strategies, and outcomes. Our logic was hybrid: codes and organizing phenomena emerged from the logs, while the arc lens and our attention to uptake/revoicing/repair were informed by dialogic pedagogy and classroom discourse traditions. From codes to arcs to vignettes The codes in Table 1 emerged across many conversations. However, what shaped the outcome of an interaction was the sequence, pattern, and timing across turns. Thus, we moved from individual turns to arcs to better capture the interactional patterns over time. Turn-level codes better show what a student attempts and what the bot answers at a given moment, whereas arcs trace how meaning unfolds across turns, including students’ improvisation, bot uptake or refusal, and the consequences for the continuity of a  thread. A single transcript could contain multiple arcs.

We labeled arcs as works, wobbles, or breaks. A works arc involves two or more audience/genre shifts on the same idea, plus an explicit continuation cue that leads to another rewrite. A wobbles  arc involves a policy refusal, capability limit, or literal reply, followed within two turns by a repair attempt that keeps the same explanatory thread going (e.g., reframe, softened re-request, or pivot that stays on the same concept). What makes a wobble recoverable is that the student has an alternative resource (another genre, a rephrase cue, a different audience), so when one avenue closes, the thread can continue through another. A breaks arc involves a policy refusal, capability limit, or literal reply, followed within the next two turns by abandoning the thread (e.g., exit, silence, or a hard topic change that ends the prior explanatory target). What makes a break unrecoverable is not the number of refusals but the boundary negotiation that ends, and the absence of a content anchor to return to. When there is no concept to iterate on, there is nonext move. To better account for transitions within and across arcs, we also identified a set of micro-moves observed across the data: revoice, cue, repair, uptake, and stance. These were not treated as separate codes but instantiations of the broader categories. For example, a “boundary negotiation” could manifest as a refusal that ends the thread or as a test the bot accommodates. Finally, to preserve dialogic texture, we tra nsformed the arcs into analytic vignettes that trace shifts in genre, voice, and stance over time (Riessman, 2008; Van Maanen, 1988), prioritizing interactional richness and narrative coherence (Barron & Engle, 2007; Vossoughi & Bevan, 2014). Methodological rationale We cannot see the faces at the “little kitchen,” nor know whether “slayyy” came with a grin or an eye roll; nor do we infer changes in conceptual understanding, motivation, or emotions behind the “:(”. However, we focus on how each prompt, reply, and response unfolds to see how students improvise and how the AI responds. By tracing those back-and-forth, we can see not just whether an exchange worked or failed, but how students and AI built meaning together.

The three focal vignettes illustrate the three arc types: one in which play sustained the thread (works), one in which stacked refusals or literal responses challenged continuity without ending it (wobbles), and one in which the thread 303 dissolved (breaks). These cases are representative, not exhaustive. Across the dataset, the same arcs appear in many shorter interactions, though with fewer iterations. Across these cases, we identified three higher-order phenomena that organize the findings. Student-led revoicing refers to moments when students prompt the system to restate an idea for a new audience, voice, or genre. Improvisational cueing refers to short directives that recalibrate the exchange without restarting it. Epistemic wobble  refers to moments of misfit (e.g., refusals, capability limits, literal replies) that make the continuity of explanation-building uncertain. Together, these phenomena show how students initiate, steer, and sometimes lose explanatory threads in interaction with GenAI. Cross-case patterns Student-led revoicing In dialogic pedagogy, revoicing is a teacher move that restates or reformulates student thinking in ways that make it available for public elaboration (O’Connor & Michaels, 1996). Across our focal cases, however, students themselves initiated and directed the restating by prompting the AI to recast a single idea for new audiences and genres (“for a five-year-old,” “like a Swiftie,” “in cat language,” “as Sabrina Carpenter”). When the AI produced recognizable restyles, threads typically met the criteria for works arcs, of two or more successive shifts anchored to the same idea, followed by explicit continuation and visible rewrites (e.g., Avery and Riley). By contrast, threads that failed to get to at least one satisfactory restyle tended to pivot or end within a few turns (e.g., Jordan). Across cases, responsive restyling was associated with persistence around the same conceptual anchor (usually photosynthesis), whereas early misfit led to quick redirection or exit. Students appropriate and delegate the restating function to make an explanation land differently for a particular audience. Improvisational cueing as glue The interactions were also held together by short, turn-by-turn directives (“shorter,” “more silly”). Such moves resemble other-initiated repair in that they adjust the trajectory of an exchange without restarting from scratch (Schegloff et al., 1977). Across the focal cases, improvisational cues functioned as glue between turns. They are short directives that solicit immediate adjustments to tone, length, or format, as repair or steering moves. Two patterns were common. First, several threads began with lo ng, undifferentiated definitions, after which students narrowed the exchange through cues that requested chunking, simplification, or a more recognizable audience frame. When the AI took up those cues early, the conversation continued (e.g., Avery and Riley). Second, when the AI responded too literally (e.g., answering “to a cat” in English), students tightened the cue as a repair move, extending the play, or pivoted if the miss continued. Cues that received partial/no uptake often triggered a quick pivot, and repeated misses ended the thread, sometimes with key-mash, profanity, or silence. Wobbles around boundaries A third cross-case pattern involved moments when meaning-making became unstable because the system treated a student's move as illegitimate, out of scope, or not quite interpretable. We refer to these as epistemic wobbles because they reveal the structural conditions under which explanation-building can continue or stall. In this sense, wobble is also a sort of test of what the system recognizes as acceptable register, voice, or task. Whether a thread continues depends partly on the student’s ability to reframe or redirect the exchange, echoing broader dialogic accounts in which productive engagement depends on whether learners can sustain a shored problem space (Engle & Conant, 2002). Across the corpus, we observed three recurring forms of boundary negotiations. First, capability limits (e.g., image requests) and policy refusals (e.g., “I can’t provide copyrighted lyrics”) often prompt escalation or redirection (e.g., Riley’s threads and Jordan’s image request). Second, classroom norms and limits (e.g., profanity or chaos prompts, “brainrot,” “cringe”) strain the register and are more likely to end a thread unless the AI offers a restyle that partially matches the students’ framing. Third, social and relational play (such as anthropomorphism or identity testing), even when partially accommodated, often drained momentum once the 306 game ended. In Jordan’s case, the break is not caused by refusal alone, but by the fact that the interaction became a self-closing game with no content anchor to return to. We offer an account of how middle schoolers converse with a general-purpose chatbot in ways that move beyond an “answer engine” script. Rather than simply asking for answers, students repeatedly try to make the system speak differently, to restate ideas fo r new audiences, to recalibrate tone and format across turns, and to continue through moments of refusals, literalness, or misfit. Across the cases, explanation building is not a one-shot exchange. It is an interactional process in which students test, steer, and revise in real time. The findings extend dialogic revoicing by showing that students can recruit a chatbot to perform discourse moves that are typically associated with teachers or peers. In classroom discourse, revoicing is typically a teacher move, here it is student-led. Learners delegate the restatement to the system while retaining direction and evaluation. This revoicing by proxy matters because it reframes style as a first-order resource for sense-making and relocates familiar classroom discourse moves into student-AI interactions. A second contribution concerns the fine-grained mechanics of how these exchanges hold together. Students used short, often playful directives (“shorter,” “like…,” “now…”) together with stance tokens (“wow,” “slayyyy…yyyy,” “bruh”) as quick feedback, repair, and steering. What becomes visible is a fast-paced, rhythmic calibration toward a version of the idea that lands with a particular audience. At the same time, the chatbot is an interaction partner, albeit unfinished, sometimes inconsistent, and shaped by  refusals, literal responses, and cultural misfires. These moments are not just breakdowns. They expose the boundaries of what the system can, cannot, or will not do. Importantly, not all breaks follow from refusal. In Jordan’s case, the thread ends after a successful exchange because the game itself closed the interaction. Without a content anchor, there is no generative next move.

These interactional patterns are not neutral. Equity enters through the register. Our data do not allow us to document whose registers were refused or flattened, but they show that the system’s recognition was uneven. restyled effectively, while prompts rooted in subcultural slang (“brainrot,” “cringe”) were more often refused, misunderstood, or met with generic replies (see Vignette 2). If some ways of speaking require more interactional work to be taken up, the burden of “making it fit” ends up unevenly distributed. Research on language and identity in science education has long shown that students whose everyday registers overlap least with school-valued academic norms often bear a disproportionate burden in making themselves legible, and that this burden shapes participation and belonging over time (Brown, 2006; Moje et al., 2004). A chatbot that fluently handles mainstream pop registers while flatte ning subcultural and vernacular expression risks reproducing the same asymmetry at the level of design. Designing for equity means not only offering stylistic flexibility, but also auditing which registers the system recognizes and building in mechanisms to surface and repair those gaps. Seen this way, our cases suggest three priorities for learning-oriented chatlogs: support revoicing with uptake, enable calibration, and make breakdowns repairable. Sustained threads depended on recognizable restyles that students could iteratively refine; students used improvisational cues as rapid feedback and repair to maintain momentum; and refusals or literal responses without usable alternatives often ended threads, while repair kept explanation-building alive. However, our claims remain bounded by the data and the context of this study. We describe interactional patterns in one setting, using a general-purpose chatbot that wasn’t pedagogically tuned. We analyze chat logs only, not students’ experiments or computational models, and do not assess out-of-school GenAI use. We also did not collect individual demographic data, so equity implications are framed through participation and language. Finally, we treat the findings as interpretive and do not infer learning gains beyond what is visible in the inter actions. Future work should (1) examine how these interactional patterns vary across students’ linguistic repertoire and participation histories, and whether some registers are systematically easier to make legible to the system; (2) link arc types to conc eptual explanations and learning trajectories; (3) replicate across teachers, subjects, classroom norms and chatbot designs including pedagogically tuned systems; and (4) use mixed-method work, incorporate classroom videos, fieldnotes and student interview s to clarify students’ intentions and how these dialogues connect to classroom activities. More broadly, these cases suggest that students didn’t treat a chatbot as a fixed “answer engine”, but as something to steer, test, and sometimes abandon. The risk of “answer engine” design is that it trains students to become efficient askers and efficient acceptors. Classrooms do not need another machine that delivers monologues at speed. What these interactions make visible is different: students shape a voice for an audience, repair misfits, and know when to walk away. This is a form of “self-regulation in the wild.” Treating those moves as legitimate practices keeps agency with the learners.
