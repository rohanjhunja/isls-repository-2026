# “We are all Learning Here!”: Negotiations of Power within an Intergenerational Participatory Design Research Project

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, we examine the ways power and privilege were negotiated within the context of a participatory design research project focused on data science as a means to pursuing environmental and social justice through community-engaged projects. Using critical ethnography and interaction analysis methodologies, we explore relational dynamics among the project team – which included high school youth, science educators, graduate students, and university faculty – as they negotiated meanings and action related to local research projects. We draw conceptually on Holland and Lave (2009) and Gutiérrez et al (2019) to understand participants as historical actors and their negotiations of self and possible futures in the context of our program. We consider social positioning and authoring of who has knowledge, who has authority, and who is learning in interactional dynamics among the project team over a two-day kickoff meeting. We highlight the ways youth engage productive resistance to youth-adult hierarchies.

## Introduction

Over the past few decades, the Learning Sciences ha s explored more equitable forms of research design that trouble the historical “researcher-researched” paradigm (e.g., Bang et al., 2016; Brown, 1992; Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016). From this work has emerged the need for explicit consideration of the ways power and privilege unfold in design-based research (Vakil et al., 2016) and how designs  themselves can level power dynamics (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016) t o amplify the expertise and diverse axiological posit ionings (Bang et al., 2016) project members bring to design studies. More, however, can be unde rstood about how power dynamics are negotiated amon g project team members that include both youth and adults as participants who are learning and working in parallel toward issues of educational and social justice.

In this article, we build on this scholarship by ex ploring how such power negotiations unfolded in the context of a participatory design research project (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) – called EMPWER (Engagement in Mentorship and Practice with Emerging Researchers) – which included adults and youth as co-learners in a program focused on data science and STEM research as tools to making sense of and pursuing local issues of environmental and social justice. In our analysis, we view participants as historical actors (Gutiérrez et al., 2019) engaging with sociopolitical issues of inequity and  in local contentious practice (Holland & Lave, 200 9) as they work to be recognized as particular types of people  in the context of our program and their school liv es. We consider the ways social positioning and authoring of who has knowledge, who has authority, and who is learning in interactional dynamics among the project team ov er a two-day kickoff meeting. We highlight the ways youth engage in “line-stepping” (Gutiérrez et al., 2019) as productive resistance to youth-adult hierarchies.

## Conceptual framework

We draw conceptually from sociocultural perspective s (Holland et al., 1998) that view identity work as  both a personal and political endeavor (e.g., Avraamidou, 2020), shaped by interrelationships among social politics and histories, institutional practice s, normative discourses, identity resources, and lo cal interactional dynamics (Eisenhart & Allen, 2020). We understand identity to be developed, pe rformed, and contested in local practice (Holland, et al., 1998; Holland & Lave, 2009), and that efforts to be and become recognized as particular types of people intersect with patriarchal, racialized, and historicized structures of school and society (Cr enshaw, 1989). From this perspective, w e understand participants in EMPWER to bring with t hem histories of participation (Holland & Lave, 2009) in and constellations of experiences (Rogoff, 2003) within institutions of schooling, their communities, and STEM that shape their participatio n in the  local practice of our program. Despite EMPWER being held out side of school time, youth and educators still bring with them their i dentities as teachers or high school students to the learning environment that carry significance for how they participate in the program (Basu & Barton, 2010). How participants choose to engage (or not) in EMPWE R is reflective of their self-authoring (Holland et a., 2001). Such authoring may reaffirm or challenge established hierarchies within schooli ng environments, such as those that assume the teacher as intellectual authority. Our intent with the design of EMPWER is to create a n environment in which participants can engage as emerging historical actors (Gutiérrez et a., 2019) and work toward disrupting historicall y-shaped inequities and settled, normative axiological assumptions that oft en permeate learning environments (Annamma et al., 2013; Bang et al., 2016). Therefore, we draw on Gutiérrez and colleagues’ (2019) notions of double-bind and breach in social order to understand moments of tension when normative assumptions and settled practices become challenged or disrupted during our program.

## Method

We draw methodologically on critical ethnography (Trueba, 1999) and tools from interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) situated in a participatory design  research project (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Critica l ethnographic methods allow ed for us to explore how power dynamics unfolded acros s the multiple members of the project team, including youth, adult educators,  university faculty and students. Combined with too ls from interaction analysis, this  further enabled us to explore how participants authored identities within and against power dynamics and what aspects of the designed learning environme nt aided in disrupting or reinforcing patriarchal and normalized power relations.

## Research context

EMPWER is a year-long participatory design research project currently in a pilot phase. The program includes an initial fall kickoff weekend, followed by 4 additio nal day-long sessions (two in fall, two in spring), and spr ing symposium to conclude the program. During the progr am sessions, youth and adults participate together as learners to build data literacy and STEM research k nowledge and practices and pursue community-oriented projects that explore local issues associated with human health, air quality, and social and environmental equity. Author 1 facilitates session meetings, and a mix of  university faculty and graduate students lead acti vities associated with their particular area of expertise.  For example, one faculty member is a toxicologist and project co-PI. She leads activities in the wet lab to analyze cell dose responses and then discussion of material associated with the lab experiment, along with two graduate students in her lab. As a broader project, we are int erested in how the designed prog ram can facilitate community engagement through the  use of data science and locally-situated STEM research, and how such engagement can position participants as agentic, historical actors working toward desired versions of future selves and future social possibilities. The kickoff was organized around supporting partici pants in orienting to the program, each other, and basic data science and STEM research concepts. This included: (1) mapping primary locations of “favorite places” to visualize and represent personal stor ies; (2) establishing shared definitions of data th at emphasize data as a collection of information that we can sort and use to understand things, counts of occurrences, and re flections of personal and local stories; (3) Collecting particulate matter (PM) data with mobile sensors and noticing differences in PM readings; and (4) applying and examining lung  cell dose responses to diesel matter in a wet lab,  followed by imaging and analysis using visualization softwar e (ImageJ). Throughout each of the two days, Author  1 facilitated discussion with the participants about “what was emerging” for them in terms of questions, curiosities, and personal and community connections. The experie nce concluded with the group creating a list of sta rting questions and types of data necessary for exploring  these questions further. Participants collected ad ditional air quality data motivated by these questions and dose response data (from their imagining in the lab) tha t were brought with them to the next fall meeting.

## Participants and project team

Participants were recruited through social media and word of mouth through existing school district relationships. The PIs focused on inviting science teachers and students from schools situated near areas known in the local area as being especially affect ed by poor air quality and high traffic patterns, b ased on current research led by the geospatial scientist on the project. Our initial intent was to have school teams that included a teacher pair or triad and up to 5 students. Our final recruitment includ ed 5 youth participants and two science teachers from two high schools: Lakeside and Prairie (Table 1). One youth and the two educators were from Lakeside;  the other 4 youth were from Prairie. All youth were in 11th grade and were a racially experientially diverse group. The two teachers were both white females who had been teaching for o ver 15 years. Both schools are racially and economicall y diverse and meet our criteria of being situated in areas affected by heavy traffic, problematic infras tructure, and poor air quality. However, the schools are located in different cities with distinctly different histo ries regarding growth and industry, which came to inform conversations the team had regarding “the local stories” that show up in the data participants collected.

While University faculty and graduate students designed the initial program, the team was explicit in the recruitment of participants, in interviews, and during sessions  that we understood each participating member to bring valued expertise that could support our collective efforts in understanding local issues and in working toward mitigation strategies.  We also positioned participants as bringing importa nt knowledge as learners in the environment and designed multiple opportunities for feedback and designed iteration (Bang et al., 2016). During our interviews with participants prior to the start  of the program, we solicited their thoughts on pro gram design. One question we asked was about research project co nfigurations. The intent of the program is to provi de participants the opportunity to pursue questions of interest to them pertaining to environmental and social justice in their communities. Given this, we wanted to organize research teams around questions, not necessarily “roles”

– e.g., youth, adult; student, teacher.  Therefore, w e made a point to ask participants, particularly yo uth, about

their thoughts about working with teachers on a res earch project. When Author 1  asked about project configurations, she named explicitly her concern with power dynamics and defaulting into tra ditional studentteacher roles. Data sources and analysis Data used in this analysis come from interviews con ducted prior to the program start, and from video a nd fieldnotes collected during the kickoff meeting. In terviews were conducted over Zoom or in person and focused on why participants were interested in the program, their understanding of data science and STEM research, their STEM teaching or learning experiences, and what con nections they make between STEM and social justice. Interviews lasted between 25-60 minutes and were audio recorded and transcribed.  Video data were collected during most program activities over the kickoff weekend. These data were viewed by Author 2 and organized into content logs of 5-minute increments. Fieldnotes (Emerson et al., 2020) were also collected by two research team members during all program activities. Once initial content  logs were created, Author 2 then conducted a first phase of coding video data for power dynamics, iden tity work, and emotions. Author 1 and 2 then discus sed segments coded for power dynamics and identified moments that reflected a double-bind or breach in social order (Gutiérrez et al., 2019). From this, Author 1 drew on interact ion analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to further explore negotiations of power and identity authoring. We present three of these segments in this paper.

## Findings

We present three instances in which participants we re engaged in struggles over social positioning wit hin the context of EMPWER. In particular, we see participan ts positioning others and authoring selves in ways that reaffirm hierarchical power dynamics between youth and adults within learning environments, such as those that assume the teacher is the authority and primary driver of knowledge and those associated with traditional schoollinked identities. At the same time, youth in the program author selves against this traditional positioning in ways that offer bids for renegotiating who has authority, who has knowledge, and who is learning in the context of our program.

Despite having an explicit framing of being “all le arners,” both youth and adults regularly indexed school-linked roles in referring to one another. When teachers indexed these school-linked roles, it tended to place the responsibility of learning onto the youth and, at times, reestablishing a role of authority or “expert.”. For youth in the program, however, these roles were typically indexed a s a strategy for role breaching-that is, challenging established norms of intellectual authority and emphasizing the “all are learning” framing of the program. In this section, we present two instances i n which this struggle over who is learning/who are learners in the program unfolded. In the first, Everett (youth) positions t he “teacher group” as learners, inviting them to sh are questions they had after an activity collecting PM data around and near the university campus. In the second, Joanna (adult) does not answer a question (she seems to know the a nswer to) in order to allow youth to answer, and th en asks a follow-up question probing students to consider additional  analyses they could conduct to explore possible unexpected outcomes.

## Discussion and implications

In this analysis we explored how power dynamics unf olded within the context of a participatory design research project that included youth and educators work ing jointly to  explore local issues of environmental justice and human health. Across the three interactions presented in this analysis, participants drew on school-linked identities

– teacher, student – in how they referred to  each other. At times, drawing on these roles worked to reaffirm

traditional asymmetrical power dynamics in learning  environment s. One interpretation of this is that teacher participants wanted to cede learning opportunities to prioritize the learning of youth in the program, such as Joanna choosing to withhold sharing answers during a whole group discussion. Such an approach can reflect democratic commitments to teacher-student relations (Basu & Barton, 2010). However, m aintaining the stance of “youth as learners” further instantiated teacher-student dynamics that the program intentionally aim ed to disrupt. When social order breaches did occur, they seemed to originate from youth in the program. As we consider moments of youth line-stepping (Gutiérrez et al., 2019),  educator participants seemed less comfortable with taking the position of learner. Yet, this may not be the case across different activity systems. During the lab activities of the kickoff, power dyn amics among the group shifted, and those between yo uth and adults seemed to level, in some cases. Elizabeth accepted a positioning of “learner,” when earlier in the classroom she had resisted being Everett’s bid to position he r this way. This suggests we should investigate the role of the learning task and arrangement of the environment in  shaping identity authoring and positioning. For ex ample, in the lab may feel less “school like” in some ways with more  emphasis on  exploration; while the classroom environment may include more “familiar” practices in terms of what we typically see and do in classrooms. Our findings raise how challenging it can be to work within and against normative practices and set tled assumptions about knowledge and learning. Even when  there were espoused commitments by project team members and participants to take an  “all are learning” approach, this proved difficult in practice. As we continue to iterate on our design and conduct more retrospective analysis, particular attention should to the activity system and how the designed activities may reinforce settled axiologic al assumptions and normative stances within STEM and research.
