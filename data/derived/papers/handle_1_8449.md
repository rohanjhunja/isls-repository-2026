# Achievement Motivation in Writing: Exploring the Pursuit of Multiple Goals and Keystroke Behaviors During Writing Tasks

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Few studies have investigated the applicability of structured motivation frameworks across more subjective academic tasks such as writing. By using person-oriented analyses and fine-grained data channels to build upon existing theories of motivation in writing tasks, ongoing research can work towards capturing how motivation impacts students’ writing processes as they unfold. As such, this study examined the occurrence of multiple goal orientation profiles in undergraduate writing students and if those goal profiles differed in the average duration students spent engaging in multiple writing processes. A k-means cluster analysis (n = 60) revealed three student clusters with distinct multiple goal profiles. A one-way MANOVA did not find significant differences in mean keystroke activity times across these profiles, bringing into question the applicability of traditional achievement goal frameworks in writing contexts. Keywords: Achievement Goal Theory, Keystroke Analysis, Motivation, Writing Analytics

Keywords: Achievement Goal Theory, Keystroke Analysis, Motivation, Writing Analytics Achievement motivation in writing tasks Achievement goal theories have been used to researc h learners’ achievement motivation in educational settings (Elliot & Murayama, 2008), resulting in the development of self-report that are used to categorize students within a single achievement goal orientation. Previous res earch supports the relationship between students’ p ursuit of these goal orientations and beneficial academic out comes such as increased academic achievement or con tent interest (Senko et al., 2011), however renewed atte ntion to the impact of motivation within complex ta sks like writing, has prompted researchers to apply these me asures in context where measures of achievement are  more subjective (Wormington & Linnenbrink-Garcia, 2017).

As such, it is important to expand beyond the current reliance on self-report measures and exclusive goal endorsement (Elliot et al., 2011). In contrast with more objective STEM disciplines, the ‘right’ answer in writing is often subjective and situated within dynamic cognitive and socio-cultural factors (Graham, 2018). This level of subjectivity, paired with limited understanding of how motivation interacts with students’ writing processes may impact the relevance of a highly structured measure like the AGQ-R in writing contexts. For this reason, achievement goal research must exp land to better reflect the impact of less clearly delineated elements such as multiple goal profiles and contextual factors. While past studies examining the role of goal orientations in writing have found relation ships between performance goals and increased acade mic achievement (Senko et al., 2011), as well as mastery goals and beneficial behaviors like revision (Kaplan et al., 2009), researchers investigating the role of achiev ement goals in writing have also noted the existenc e of multicollinearity between goal orientations and par ticipant reports noting similar usage of revision s trategies across goal profiles, muddying our understanding of how achievement goals impact motivation in writing contexts (Paul et al., 2021). By shifting towards the use of person-centered research methods and granular data channels, researchers may be able to examine individual motiv ation profiles more closely (Urdan & Kaplan, 2020; Wormington & Linnenbrink-Garcia, 2017).

Theoretical frameworks Elliot and Murayama’s (2008) 2x2 achievement goal questionnaire-revised, (AGQ-R) has been widely used to asess learners’ achievement motivation across diverse educational contexts. Within the traditional 2x2 framework, learners are categorized into distinct achievement goal orientations, which Elliot and Murayama separate by both the definition of learners’ achievement goals (mastery and performance) as well as the valence of those goals (approach and avoidance), resulting in four distinct goal profiles (2008). Previous research on these achievement goal profiles (i.e., mastery approach [MAP], mastery avoidance [MAV], performance approach [PAP], and performance avoidance [PAV]; see Elliot & Murayama, 2008) has found positively valenced approach goals like MAP and PAP to be linked with desirable student outcomes such as increased academic achievement and content mastery, while negatively valenced avoidance goals are largely viewed as less-beneficial (Elliot & Murayama, 2008).

Graham describes writing as a complex task that is influenced by both cognitive and sociocultural factors, positing that a writer’s motivation or goals for the task ar e influenced by both internal (i.e., content knowle dge) and sociocultural (i.e., community support) factors, wh ich impacts learners’ writing processes and behavio rs (Camacho et al., 2021). Given the complex nature of  writing texts, the WWC model provides important in sight into contextual factors specific to writing tasks that may impact the writing process (Graham, 2018). This study used these theories to interpret writers ’ achievement goals and writing processes, utilizin g advances in analysis methods and fine-grained representations of participants writing processes to highlight the connections between participants’ motivation and writing processes. Current study This study sought to examine first-year undergraduate writing students’ self-reported achievement goals in relation to completing a self-reflective writing task, as well as the relationship between those goals and students’ keystroke behaviors during the writing task.

The participants in this study were undergraduate students (n = 60) enrolled in a first-year writing course. Students were asked to describe in as much detail the step-by-step process of how you complete a formal writing task for 30 minutes. Students had no requirements (e.g., specified length, structure, etc.) other than to respond to the prompt for the allotted duration and were explicitly told their responses would not be graded. Prior to the study, participants responded to a ser ies of questionnaires that included Elliot and Murayama’s (2008) AGQ-R. The AGQ-R contains four subscales (one for each of the achievement goal profiles) that consist of 3 questions for each subscale, for a total of 12 items. Participants indicated their agreement (from 1 = ‘strongly disagree’ to 5 = ‘strongly agree’) to statements to measure their achievement goals in relation to the writing course. Each of the four subscales have also shown high levels of internal consistency (mastery approach [=.84], mastery avoidance [=.88], performance approach [=.92], performance avoidance [=.94]) (Elliot & Murayama, 2008).

Keystrokes were logged using InputLog (Leijten & V an Waes, 2013) to record students’ input in Microsoft Word during the writing session (e.g., mouse movements, keystrokes, inter-key intervals, etc.) with its associated timestamp in milliseconds. Keystroke behaviors utilized within this study included the mean total time participants spent pausing, inserting, producing, and deleting text during the writing.

## Results

Research question 1: Did undergraduate writing students’ self-reported achievement goal endorsements suggest the occurrence of multiple goal profiles? Given the presence of significant multicollinearity between participants’ aggregate scores derived from the 2x2 AGQ-R, a cluster analysis was performed to identify multiple goal orientation profile types across participants. Hierarchical clustering was applied to participants ’ raw aggregate scores for each of the achievement goal subscales using Ward’s method, with changes in the agglomeration coefficients (AC) and assessment of the resulting visualizations (i.e., elbow technique) used to determine the ideal number of clusters (Wortha et al., 2019). k-means clustering was used to compare cluster centers and membership for the 2-and 3-cluster solutions. Given that the 3-cluster solution found large Euclideandistances between the final cluster centers and appropriate sample sizes for each cluster (n > 15), the 3-cluster solution was selected. The 3-cluster solution was further categorized based on the goal profile labeling procedures outlined in Wormington and Linnenbrink-Garcia’s 2017 meta-analysis. Cluster profile labels were based on each clus ter’s mean aggregate AGQ-R subscale scores (e.g., less th an 6 on a 15-point scale indicates low goal endorse ment, more than 12 indicates a high goal endorsement, and  scores within that range are viewed as moderate go al endorsement), where average mean aggregate scores for each of the four subscales were used to label the 3 clusters. Research question 2: What multiple achievement goal profiles did students pursue in relation to the writing task?

Cluster 1: High all goals Students in Cluster 1 (n = 24) were classified as belonging to the High All Goals profile, with final cluster centers (see Figure 1) exhibiting high endorsement of all f our achievement goal orientations, which suggests t hese students were highly concerned with both writing task mastery and their ability to appear as competent writers to others. While traditional achievement goal theories  may interpret the pursuit of all four goal orienta tions as ICLS2022 Proceedings 1218 © ISLS inherently contradictory, Wormington and Linnenbrink-Garcia’s meta-analysis found that students within High All Goals profiles displayed high levels of academi c achievement, suggesting some benefits of multiple  goal pursuit (2017).

Cluster 2: Average all goals Students in Cluster 2 (n = 16) were classified as belonging to the Average All Goals profile, with final cluster centers (see Figure 1) exhibiting average endorsement of all four achievement goal orientations, which suggests these students were also concerned with both writing task mastery and their ability to appear as competent writers to others, but at a more moderate level. Wormington  and Linnenbrink-Garcia found this goal profile to be the most prevalent, with students in Average All Goals profiles exhibiting lower scores on measures of engagement and well-being (2017).

Cluster 3: Mastery high goals Students in Cluster 3 (n = 20) were classified as belonging to the Mastery High Goals profile, with final cluster centers (see Figure 1) exhibiting high endorsement of both mastery approach and mastery avoidance goal orientations, which suggests these students were mo st strongly motivated by an intrinsic desire to mas ter the writing content. While the mastery avoidance cluster center (M = 11.60) fell below the established requisite score, its proximity to the cut off score (12) in addition to the overall high mastery approach score (M = 12.80) suggested Cluster 3 most closely fit the Mastery High goals p rofile described by Wormington and Linnenbrink-Garc ia (2017). Wormington and Linnenbrink-Garcia (2017) fo und this goal profile to be common and generally associated with higher academic performance outcome s than other goal profiles included within studies of multiple goal orientations.

## Discussion and future directions

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1219 © ISLS Given the presence of multicollinearity between goal profiles, a k-means cluster analysis used to answer research question one produced three student clusters, each with a distinct achievement goal endorsement profile. Results of research question two found that all three goal profile clusters exhibited high to moderate endorsement of two or more achievement goals simultaneously, supporting the occurrence of multiple goals and highlighting the complexity of students’ writing motivation (Graham, 2018). The third research question examined relationships between students’ achievement goal profiles and writing behaviors measured through keystroke logging. Although the mean keystroke activity time differences across the established goal orientation clusters were not statistically significant, the discrepancy between these findings and the established linkages between achievement goal orientations and academic performance highlights the need for further exploration of achievement goals in writing tasks to include more granular data (i.e., keystroke input).

Through the inclusion of multiple data channels and person-orientated methods like cluster analysis, researchers candevelop a deeper understanding of motivation in writing contexts, potentially challenging some of the long-standing assumptions regarding the achievement goal framework and its generalizability across more subjective tasks like writing.
