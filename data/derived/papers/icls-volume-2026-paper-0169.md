# Unmasking Impostor Feelings in Engineering: The Role of Gender and Belonging among Secondary Students

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The underrepresentation of women in STEM persists, with factors such as sense of belonging and the impostor phenomenon (IP) influencing motivation. While impostor feelings are well-studied in adults, few studies have examined adolescents. This study examined the relation between engineering belonging and IP among N = 1,187 diverse U.S. students in Grades 7-10. Results showed that girls reported stronger impostor feelings than boys in both middle and high school, with engineering belonging linked to feelings of impostor only in high school. Findings highlight the importance of belonging in shaping adolescents’ self-perceptions, pointing towards the importance of early efforts to foster inclusive engineering contexts and long-term persistence in engineering.

The underrepresentation of women in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) continues to be a pressing concern in education and workforce development (National Science Foundation, 2020). Although girls perform as well as boys in K–12 STEM courses, their participation declines sharply across high school and higher education, particularly in engineering and computer science (Master et al., 2025). This decline is shaped by social and psychological factors that influence motivation and persistence in STEM, including students’ sense of belonging (Good et al., 2012) and experiences of the impostor phenomenon, the feeling of being a fraud despite clear evidence of success (Clance & Imes, 1978; Tao & Gloria, 2019). Although extensively examined among college students and professionals (Vaughn et al., 2020), little research has explored how IP, also interchangeably known as impostor feelings —experienced by adolescents at rates comparable to adults —emerge within STEM contexts (Chakraverty & Cokley, 2024).Understanding these early psychological experiences is critical, as feelings of self-doubt and exclusion during middle and high school can weaken confidence and deter future engagement in STEM, particularly for girls (Chakraverty, 2019; Vaughn et al., 2020). The present study aims to address this gap by investigating whether gender moderates the relationship between sense of belonging in engineering and IP  among middle and high school students. Gender may act as a moderator because negative stereotypes about girls’ ability in STEM—and  especially in engineering and computer science—could make the link between belonging and IP stronger for girls than boys (Leroy et al., 2025). We focus specifically on engineering because this field stereotypically favors boys, which can impact girls’ self-perceptions, and reduce their retention in engineering-related fields (Master et al., 2025). This study addresses two research questions: First, do students report higher IP when they have a lower sense of belonging in engineering, across middle and high school? Second, does gender moderate this link? This study is important because it explores how early experiences of engineering belonging and IP shape adolescents’ STEM motivation and participation, especially in groups facing persistent stereotypes  and underrepresentation (Good et al., 2012; Leroy et al., 2025; Vaughn et al., 2020). By examining gender as a moderator, the study identifies when and for whom supportive efforts are most impactful. Prior research suggests that exposure to role models from underrepresented groups, such as women in STEM, can enhance belonging, and persistence by challenging stereotypes and expanding perceptions of success (Gladstone & Cimpian, 2021). was not significant  at that stage. In high school, however, both gender and belonging were linked to IP. These highlight the potential of diverse STEM role models to improve belonging, offering students tangible examples of success and fostering meaningful connections (Gladstone & Cimpian, 2021). Such initiatives require collaboration among educators, researchers, schools, and the broader STEM community, underscoring the power of intentional partnerships and role model integration in nurturing STEM  belonging, and motivating students of all genders to persist in STEM (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Good et al., 2012; Qasem et al., 2025).

## Theoretical framework

Social identity threat theory Social identity threat refers to the stress and performance risks that arise when individuals fear being judged or 1327 stereotyped based on their social identity —especially in contexts where their group is underrepresented, such as women in STEM (Murphy et al., 2024). These cues signal that one may not fully belong, undermining motivation, self-esteem, and self-perceptions (Chakraverty & Cokley, 2024). In STEM, gender-based stereotypes about lower competence heighten this threat and reduce women’s sense of belonging (Nguyen & Ryan, 2008). Sense of belonging The importance of  sense of  belonging are  linked to motivation, engagement,  and long-term academic success (Katsantonis, 2025). Higher levels of anticipated belonging in high school STEM courses predict higher enrollment interest for both girls and boys (Master et al., 2016). This is particularly crucial in engineering, where it may influence the gender gap in representation. Lack of belonging candeter young women from pursuing engineering paths (Good et al., 2012). This may affect girls' confidence and influence their decision to pursue STEM fields like computer science and engineering, in which only 21% of U.S. degrees are earned by women (Master et al., 2025). The Brilliance–Belonging model This model expands social identity threat theory by showing that brilliance-oriented cultures (such as STEM classrooms) intensify belonging threats for groups stereotyped as lacking innate genius —such as women, and students of color. This diminished belonging undermines psychological safety, increasing reluctance to take risks or reveal uncertainty for fear of confirming stereotypes (Bauer et al., 2025; Cook et al., 2012). These conditions foster IP, as students internalize messages that brilliance is rare and that people like them are not expected to possess it, leading to chronic self-doubt (Bauer et al., 2025; Clance & Imes, 1978). For example, lower levels of engineering belonging during secondary school may contribute to lower psychological safety, that leads to the development of IP, especially in girls, despite their achievements (Muradoglu et al., 2022). Adolescence is a particularly critical period, because STEM motivation tends to decline during this stage and academic identity solidifies, impacting academic decision-making and future STEM participation (Hoffman & Umaña-Taylor, 2023; Muenks et al., 2018). A strong sense of belonging may help  mitigate IP by reinforcing students' confidence in their place within academic communities; whereas a lack of belonging may heighten IP, leading students to doubt their achievements and attribute success to external factors, particularly for girls in stereotyped STEM contexts  (Muradoglu et al., 2022; Vaughn et al., 2020). Understanding this relation can help inform early efforts to improve belonging, involving different educational stakeholders.

## Participants

This investigation was part of a larger data collection effort designed by scientists affiliated with the Character Lab Research Network. Data for this study was collected in Fall 2021, with an original sample size N = 1,554. Participants who did not identify their gender as boy or girl (N = 59) were excluded because of the small sample size, as well as students who did not complete the survey items for engineering belonging and impostor feelings (N = 308). The final analytic sample for this study consisted of N = 1,187 students ages 11-17 in Grades 7-10, Mage = 13.51, SD = 1.19; 52% girls, 48% boys; 64% White, 19% Black, 9% multiracial, 7% Asian, 1% Native American; in addition, 43% Hispanic ethnicity. The sample was drawn from fifty-two diverse urban and suburban schools in the South and Midwest regions of the U.S. The study was conducted during class in school computers.

## Measures

Belonging Sense of belonging in engineering was measured with three items on a 6-point Likert scale (e.g., How much do you feel like you belong when you do these [engineering] classes and activities at school?; adapted from Master et al.,

2021) and showed high reliability, α =.85.

Impostor phenomenon The scale used was adapted from the Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale for adults with permission from Pauline Clance (Clance, 1985). To create a briefer scale, one item for each identified attribute (e.g., “attribution of success to luck”) was selected based on prior literature, e.g., Sometimes I feel my success was due to luck (Prince, 1989). The language of each item was modified to be more youth-friendly, and the revised scale was confirmed to be at an adolescent reading level using an online editing tool that assessed sentence length and complexity (Hemingway Editor, 2025). The scale format was adjusted to align with other survey measures, using a 6-point Likert scale (strongly disagree to strongly agre e) to reduce cognitive load for participants. The eight-item scale showed high 1328 reliability, α =.90.

This study has important implications for belongingness, IP, and STEM participation, especially in engineering. Belonging is a dynamic feeling that shifts across age and STEM  contexts. Recent mega-analytic evidence shows that belonging uncertainty begins in middle school but becomes more internalized by high school, when belonging declines across math, science, computer science, and engineering (Master et al., 2026). Declines are especially sharp for girls in computer science and engineering, and by high school, girls’ belonging especially shifts to be slightly negative. These developmental patterns help explain why engineering belonging predicted impostor feelings for high school students in the present study, but not for middle school students. The more competitive climate of high school may also intensify students’ self-doubt and impostor feelings when they feel they do not belong (Bauer et al., 2025). In middle school, only gender —but not  belonging—predicted  IP, suggesting that gendered self-doubt emerges early, while the impact of belonging strengthens with age. Some practices might include mentorship and inviting women from STEM as role models and “partners with purpose” to help build connections in middle and high school STEM classrooms (Kroeper et al., 2025); belonging-focused classroom practices, and strategies directly targeting IP (e.g., attribution retraining), that may help prevent declines in STEM belonging in general and reduce IP across genders. Future directions This study highlights the need for further research in adolescents to better understand the factors shaping STEM IP. Future work should examine intersectionality, such as gender, race and ethnic identity — as experiences of discrimination and microaggressions have been shown to heighten IP, particularly among girls of color in STEM. Additionally, because external attribution  (e.g., crediting success to luck) are central to impostor experiences (Schneider et al., 2025), investigating these patterns in adolescents may reveal how attributional styles contribute to these feelings.
