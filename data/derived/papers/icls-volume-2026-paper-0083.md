# Opening Black Boxes: Unpacking Blocks to Balance Usability and Expressiveness in Computational Modeling

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Computational modeling is a key practice in STEM classrooms. Block-based, domain-specific programming environments have made this practice more accessible but may constrain what students candesign compared to general-purpose languages. This paper introduces “block unpacking” —allowing high-level primitives to be opened, inspected, and modified—in a domain-specific modeling environment. We implemented this feature in a four-day lesson on eutrophication, in which high school students programmed computational models to represent interactions between algae, bacteria, fish, nutrients, and oxygen. We analyzed 36 interactions with six unpackable blocks across three modeling challenges, examining the computational complexity and scientific relevance of students’ modifications. We found that unpacking enabled students to modify high-level primitives, via parameter and code structure changes, to optimize models and articulate scientific reasoning. We discuss implications for the design of programming environments and instructional materials that aim to balance usability and expressiveness in computational modeling.

Over the last several decades, researchers have explored computational modeling as a medium for supporting learning in both science and computing (Sengupta et al., 2013; Sherin et al., 1993; Weintrop et al., 2016; White & Frederiksen, 1998; Wilensky & Resnick, 1999). Prior work highlights the benefits of students constructing their own models rather than relying on prebuilt ones  (Blikstein & Wilensky, 2009; Fuhrmann et al., 2018; Wagh & Wilensky, 2018). Constructing a computational model requires learners to represent a system’s characteristics in ways a computer can execute, a process that naturally engages computing concepts and practices (Wilensky & Reisman, 2006). Yet programming models also bring challenges common to programming in general, including dealing with syntax errors, learning of entire programming languages, and difficulty of debugging unexpected outcomes (Basu et al., 2016; Sherin et al., 1993).

A promising way to overcome these challenges is to use domain-specific, block-based environments, which reduce programming complexity and leverage students’ intuitions about scientific ideas (Aslan et al., 2020; Kahn, 2007; Wilkerson et al., 2015). Designing such environments, however, requires balancing usability and expressiveness (Repenning & Ambach, 1997): highly usable tools are easy to start with but may limit how complex ideas can be expressed; highly expressive tools support richer ideas but often have steep learning curves. To navigate this tension, we introduce “unpacking”  as a way to enable learners to expand a domain-specific block into its more granular computational elements so they can read, interpret, and modify how a procedure has been defined. Once familiar with its internal structure, learners can modify a block’s definition for their own purposes.

Using a design-based research approach, this paper r eports on one implementation cycle examining how unpacking shaped students’ engagement with computational modeling. Specifically, we ask: How did unpacking high-level blocks support students’ modifications to domain-specific primitives and the goals those modifications served?

In this study, we implemented unpacking in a four-day instructional unit on eutrophication, in which high school students designed computational models to represent interactions among algae, bacteria, fish, nutrients, and oxygen. Students used a web-based agent-based modeling environment (Fuhrmann et al., in press; Wagh et al, 2022). We catalogued students’ interactions with six unpackable blocks across three modeling challenges, examining the types of modifications students made and their corresponding goa ls. Our contribution is a detailed account of how unpacking high-level primitives into ‘smaller pieces’ enabled students to make parameter and code structure modifications to support optimization and scientific reasoning, helping balance usability and expressiveness while supporting plural programming styles. 717 Towards usability: Domain-specific modeling environments and primitives: We situate our work in prior research on developing domain-specific modeling environments designed to facilitate engagement with specific domains by offering tailored primitives and syntax (Aslan et al., 2020; Wagh & Wilensky, 2018; Hutchins et al., 2020; Kahn, 2007; Wilkerson et al., 2015). Domain-specific modeling languages are purpose-built languages that package domain concepts into clear, high-level primitives and representations, supporting model expression (Hutchins et al., 2020). Primitives are foundational components of a domain that encapsulate key properties or behaviors, making them easy to identify while abstracting complex underlying details (Kahn, 2007). In addition, prior work has opted for visual, particularly block-based, interfaces to represent domain-specific primitives and reduce the burden of syntax issues by providing drag-and-drop, constrained libraries that students can engage with. For instanc e, C2STEM (Hutchins et al., 2020)  is a block-based, domainspecific modeling language where users can create models for curricular topics such as kinematics by connecting physics-specific blocks such as <set position> and <set velocity>. Similarly, Frog Pond (Horn et al., 2014)  uses NetTango, a simplified programming interface tonetLogo, to introduce learners to natural selection by programming frogs using blocks specific to natural selection, such as “hop,” “hatch,” and “hunt,” to enable users to create models that produce emergent population-level changes. However, as environments that provide customdesigned primitives closely aligned with specific curricular topics (Aslan et al., 2020), domain-specific modeling environments also have potential drawbacks, such as limiting engagement with more complex computing and scientific concepts. This can be a challenge because the same curricular alignment that lowers the entry barrier might also “fix” which mechanisms are available and how they work, limiting learners’ range for expressing ideas beyond those previously encoded by researchers and designers. Towards expressiveness: Opening black boxes in computational modeling While an emphasis on usability can lower entry barriers to computing, how learners express their ideas is deeply shaped by the computational medium itself (DiSessa, 2000). Constructing computational representations benefits not only from having access to high-level primitives, but also from opportunities to tinker with their inner logic, enabling learners to express different ideas and epistemological styles (Resnick & Rosenbaum, 2013; Turkle & Papert, 1990). In the context of computational modeling, enhancing expressiveness also involves deciding which “black boxes” to open to foster more nuanced mechanistic understanding (Haskel-Ittah, 2023)  and carefully designing affordances that support such exploration (Resnick et al., 2000; Resnick & Silverman, 2005). For example, proposing responsivity to student ideas as a promising feature of modeling environments, Wilkerson et al. (2015) examined how middle-and high-school students adapted pre-built domain-specific primitives to their own questions and interests by editing the underlying code. Simila rly, Wagh et al. (2017) characterized how students can express a range of ideas in pre-built NetLogo models when given this freedom, ranging from making aesthetic changes to pursuing their own scientific investigations. Building on these findings, we investigate alternative ways to support students’ expression of ideas with domain-specific primitives while maintaining the accessibility of a block-based format.

“Unpacking” blocks: Balancing usability and expressiveness in domain-specific modeling The need to balance usability and expressiveness in the design of domain-specific environments was first articulated by Repenning and Ambach (1997) in their work with the AgentSheets authoring tool. They proposed design principles such as incremental program composition, ease of comprehension by others, and support for sharing across levels of expertise to achieve this balance. Similarly, Wilensky (2003)  described this tension as one between content-domain and general-purpose modeling languages: more general-purpose environments offer greater expressiveness but require a steeper learning curve, whereas domain-specific environments lower the barrier to entry by providing predefined primitives aligned with particular scientific concepts. A similar trade-off has been observed in block-based programming, where high school students found visual programming more interactive but less powerful than text-based environments (Weintrop & Wilensky, 2015). Subsequent research explored hybrid or dual-modality environments that integrate features of both paradigms, enabling smoother progression and fewer programming challenges (Weintrop & Wilensky, 2018). Building on this line of work, we introduce unpacking as a mid-level approach between high-level primitives and editable code. Unpacking allows learners to open the black box of selected domain-specific blocks, revealing their internal computational structure in a finer-grained but still block-based format. These unpacked elements remain easily modifiable, enabling students to adapt and extend them for different purposes. 718 This study is situated within design-based research (DBR; Barab & Squire, 2004) focused on developing and refining supports for computational modeling in domain-specific, block-based environments. The broader design effort centers on the conjecture that unpacking high-level primitives into editable block-based structures can expand students’ opportunities to inspect, adapt, and express ideas without sacrificing accessibility. This paper reports on one implementation cycle of that work: a four-day instructio nal unit on eutrophication. We aim to develop a fine-grained account of unpacking as a design mechanism by characterizing the modifications students made, the goals those modifications served, and the implications for subsequent design iterations. Modeling environment Students engaged with a domain-specific modeling unit implemented in MoDa, a web-based, agent-based block programming environment (Fuhrmann et al., in press; Wagh et al., 2022; Figure 1). MoDa allows users to create and run computational models by dragging, connecting, and editing blocks from a unit-specific library organized into conceptual groups (e.g., “Action” and “Control”). In this unit, students modeled eutrophication—a process in which excess nutrients trigger algae blooms and deplete oxygen—by programming the behaviors of three agent types: algae, bacteria, and fish. They could also adjust nutrient and oxygen levels through sliders and observe population changes in on-screen charts. The modeling design was informed by prior work (Hmelo-Silver et al.,

2017) and consultation with subject-matter experts.

## Participants and data sources

This study focused on five high school students from a public school in a suburban area of São Paulo state, Brazil, selected from a larger cohort of 15 based on consistent attendance across all four sessions. We intentionally chose this smaller sample to gain a more fine-grained understanding of students’ design decisions while modeling. The students worked in three groups: Leo & Mila, Foster & Grace, and Vincent (who worked individually). Vincent initially worked with a partner on day 1 of the unit, but because that student did not continue in days 2–4, he chose to work independently in the remaining sessions rather than join another pair.  All names are pseudonyms. In addition to their regular coursework, participants were enrolled in a vocational Informatics track, roughly equivalent to AP Computer Science in the U.S. Although they had prior programming experience (e.g., flowcharts, Portugol, and C), none had used block-based or agent-based modeling environments before. Data sources for the study included final student projects for each challenge, screen recordings of students interacting with MoDa, and post interviews. These interviews were conducted by the first author approximately one week after implementation and lasted between 25 and 30 minutes. Student pairs were asked to walk through their model and describe their goals for each unpackable block modification. Interviews were conducted online, recorded, and transcribed.

## Data analysis

To examine how students expressed ideas in their models, we analyzed their interactions with unpackable blocks—focusing on what they changed and why. We first identified all unpackable blocks that had been modified in students’ final models, resulting in 41 block modifications (refer red to throughout the paper as “cases”). For each modification, we reviewed screen recordings of student pairs and transcribed the moments when the changes 720 occurred. In addition, we conducted and transcribed artifact-based interviews (Brennan & Resnick, 2012; Haduong & Brennan, 2020)  in which we presented the modified blocks and asked students to explain their reasoning.

Students’ explanations for each modification were coded inductively (Saldaña, 2021). This process produced two analytic dimensions: (1) the type of modification made to the unpackable block and (2) the goal informing that modification. While the type of modification was straightforward to classify based on observable changes in the blocks, the goal dimension required closer interpretation of students’ verbal explanations and of code changes students make while tinkering with NetLogo (Wagh et al., 2017). To develop the coding scheme, we adopted a bottom-up approach in which the first three authors collaboratively reviewed and discussed 10 cases that were representative of the sample. After reaching consensus on the categories and their definitions, the first author coded the remaining data.

For the first analytic dimension, type of modification, we defined two categories: parameter changes (e.g., adjusting dropdown values or numerical inputs within the unpackable block) and code structure changes (e.g., deleting or rearranging commands within the unpackable block). Code structure changes could also include parameter adjustments as part of the modification process. For the second dimension (students’ goals), we identified two categories: optimization  (e.g., making changes to meet the challenges’ requirements or improve model performance), and scientific reasoning  (e.g., referring to concepts, processes, or mechanisms related to eutrophication or biological systems). Five cases were excluded from the analysis because students either could not provide a clear explanation (e.g., “I don’t remember”) or because their changes were purely aesthetic (e.g., changing fish color “to make them prettier”). The final dataset included 36 block modifications, each classified by both type and goal. To support interpretation of the analytic matrix, we selected four illustrative vignettes (one for each combination of modification type and goal) based primarily on how easy they were to explain clearly in the space available. These examples are intended as illustrative rather than representative of the full dataset. Our analysis revealed that unpacking supported students in expressing their ideas through different kinds of model modifications. To convey these findings, we first provide a quantitative overview of the types and goals of observed modifications in the final models. We then present four vignettes that illustrate how different combinations of modification type and goal took shape in practice. Our findings suggest that unpacking turned domain-specific primitives into scaffolds for extended engagement and expression. Across this implementation cycle, students modified both parameters and internal code structure, expressing ideas to optimize their models and to articulate their scientific reasoning. Even simple parameter changes, like those made by Foster and Grace in the “breathe” block, reflected reasoning about ecological systems. Through iterative modifications, students used computational tinkering to represent ideas that extended beyond what the predefined primitives could express. A similar iterative approach was described in Leo and Mila’s adjustments to the “reproduce” block to control the algae population growth. In this sense, students’ engagement with the “smaller pieces” that decompose primitive blocks aligns with prior work on the value of providing a wider range of materials for learners to interact with to more effectively express their ideas through computational media, including in the context of computational modeling (Resnick & Rosenbaum, 2013; Wagh et al., 2017). 723 In addition, the two main goals identified—optimizing and scientific reasoning —illustrate that unpacking did not lead to a single pathway but  instead created space for multiple forms of engagement. The diversity of student approaches also appeared to be influenced by their prior knowledge of topics such as photosynthesis and food chains, as well as by their attendance to the challenges’ requirements. These differences suggest that unpacking may serve as a lens to contribute to the literature on plural epistemological and programming styles (Blikstein et al., 2014; Turkle & Papert, 1990), supporting both performance-oriented and conceptual forms of engagement.

Finally, our findings illustrate the value of domain-specific primitives as scaffolds for extended engagement with scientific inquiry. The same primitive block, such as “breathe”, was repurposed by different groups to represent distinct mechanisms —fish consuming algae or algae absorbing nutrients —a similar process was done with the “decompose” block to represent photosynthesis. Students’ productive engagement with those primitives, particularly by inspecting and modifying their functioning, illustrates how unpacking can bridge the tension between usability and expressiveness (Repenning, 2017; Repenning & Ambach, 1997). Building on prior work that has navigated this tension through hybrid approaches that combine domain-specific and general-purpose languages through block and text-based interfaces (Lin et al., 2025; Weintrop & Wilensky, 2018), our study shows how, through unpacking, learners can rely on the familiarity of high-level primitives while also opening their black boxes to better understand and modify them.

From a design-based research perspective (Barab & Squire, 2004), these findings inform subsequent iterations of our environment. In this implementation cycle, we identified tensions around which blocks should be provided as unpackable high-level primitives  and how much of their inner code should be visible and modifiable. Because modeling necessarily simplifies real-world systems, unpacking also prompts reflection on how to represent each primitive’s inner structure in ways that support tinkering and understanding without overwhelming students with computational complexity. For example, although photosynthesis and nutrient consumption were not initially offered as unpackable primitives for different design reasons, students’ adaptations suggested their importance for reasoning about eutrophication. Similarly, omitting CO2 production from the “breathe” primitive created an entry point for students to inspect and revise primitives based on prior knowledge, as in Vincent’s reasoning with photosynthesis. Such decisions raise broader pedagogical questions about what levels of domain knowledge are most productive to make visible and modifiable (Haskel-Ittah, 2023). In parallel, we are developing causal maps to help students articulate the relationships they aim to r epresent before coding, as well as editable names for unpackable blocks so they can express their intentions more transparently in the interface. These features function as peripheral affordances to unpacking by supporting students’ thinking about what they can and want to represent through modifying unpackable primitives.

Our findings are subject to certain limitations that also inform directions for future work. This study focused on a small sample of students with prior programming experience, which limits the generalizability of our findings. However, this small sample also enabled a fine-grained analysis of students’ design decisions, providing detailed insights into how they engaged with unpackable blocks. Consistent with a design-based research approach, our goal in this study was not broad generalization, but a fine-grained account of how unpacking functioned in context and what design questions it surfaced for future iterations. As we refine and test the curricular unit in future cycles, we aim to expand the study to larger, more diverse groups of high school students with varying levels of familiarity with programming. In addition, teacher and researcher interactions during classroom discussions may have influenced students’ choices about what to model and how to express those ideas. Rather than treating this as noise, we see such mediation as part of the classroom ecolo gy through which unpacking was taken up, although the present Collaboration within pairs may also have shaped how ideas were proposed, negotiated, and implemented through unpacking, but comparing individual and paired engagement was beyond this analysis. Similarly, we did not systematically account for students’ prior knowledge in science and computing, which may also have shaped their engagement with unpacking. Moving forward, we plan to incorporate these factors to better understand students’ choices about what and how to represent through unpacking. Lastly, our analysis relied primarily on students’ final models for each challenge, which did not fully capture the iterative process of modification or designs discarded along the way. To address this, future work will draw more extensively on screen recordings and log data to trace students’ evolving engagement with unpackable blocks throughout the modeling process. These methods will also allow us to examine how students enact computing structures made visible through code-structure modifications. For example, Foster and Grace’s modification of the decompose block reflected an accurate understanding of conditional operators within if/and/or statements. Cases like this point to opportunities to investigate how students bring together scientific and computational ideas as they construct and refine their models (Sengupta et al., 2013; Weintrop et al., 2016). 724 Together, these efforts help clarify how unpacking and modifying blocks can support rich forms of engagement connecting science and computing in ways that are both accessible and expressive.
