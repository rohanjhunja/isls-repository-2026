# A Long Cycle of Expansive Learning in a Food Cooperative: A Multi-Lens Analysis

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study presents a longitudinal, multi-lens analysis of expansive learning within the Herttoniemi Food Cooperative, a social movement focused on sustainable food production located in Helsinki. We employ the theory of expansive learning to analyze a year-long process of collective learning among the cooperative's board members. The aim of this study is to understand the innovative learning process of the board as they faced the challenge of financial viability. The data of this study consist of recordings from 26 board meetings and a planning day. Our findings show that the concept of degrowth acted as a central integrative factor for developing tangible solutions. The starting point of innovations was real-world problems that participants encountered in the board meetings as they were trying to solve the complex object of financial sustainability. An intervention planning day was needed to refine new tools in their mature form to be implemented later successfully.

## Introduction

This paper presents a longitudinal and multi-lens analysis of expansive learning in a social movement focused on sustainable food production.   Alternative food movements strive for sustainable m ethods of production and consumption, but they face significant challenges in sustaining them selves against capitalist market pressures. The challenge is to learn to survive without succum bing to the logic of commoditization and capitalist  value creation. Wilson (2013) characterizes these movemen ts as autonomous food  spaces situated within the broader context of communities that seek to cultivate relationships based on mutual aid and non-market exchanges. The prevailing perspective in organization and mana gement studies literature often associates scaling with organizational growth. However, within the con text of post-growth organizations, scaling involves a more complex set of dynamics, as outlined in a recent study by Colombo et al (2024). The study identifies nine distinct scaling routes: organizational growth (vertical and  horizontal), organizational downscaling, impact on  policies, multiplication, impact on organizational culture, i mpact on societal cul ture, aggregation, and diffusion. It highlights that post-growth scaling requires the synergistic interaction  of different strategies, focuses on influencing societal culture, does not always neces sitate organizational growth, and is a relational p rocess embedded in socioecological systems. This typology p rovides post-growth organizations with an understanding of diverse scaling routes, unlocking their transformative potential and facilitating the shift towards a future where the advancement of human and ecological well-being takes precedence in economic endeavours. Alakuvaklar (2023) discusses how prevailing economi c relations enable non-capitalist forms of alternative organizing. It uses a case study of a free food store to illustrate how an alternative organizational form emerges through the entanglement of diverse economi c practices. The study emphasizes the importance of  use value in mediating alternative economic, symbolic, and politi cal relations. Findings of this study provides a holistic understanding of a community organization as an alternative substitute within the broader con text of economic relations.

Social movements are gaining attention in studies o f learning (e.g., Choudry, 2015; Curnow & Jurow, 2021). Access to affordable and healthy food is a signi ficant object of social movements. As Jurow and She a (2015, p. 289) point out, social movements such as the food justice movement provide useful settings f or investigating consequential learning, as these collective efforts are launched to change how knowledge, resources, and opportunities for future participation can be enacted. The social movement examined in this study is the H erttoniemi Food Cooperative established in 2011 and situated in the Helsinki metropolitan area. Wit h approximately 200 members, the cooperative leases  a field where a professional farmer cultivates vegetables for the collective. Throughout the harvest season, the vegetables are transported weekly from the field to distributi on points in the city, where members collect their portion.  Despite its increasing popularity, the sustainabili ty of the food cooperative is consistently under th reat. Small-scale ecological farming is labor-intensive and has to compete with heavily subsidized mass-produced farm products of large supermarket chains.

The activity-theoretical framework of expansive learning (Engest röm, 2015) has been used in a few studies to analyze learning in social movements (Ca ldwell & al., 2019). In this paper, we use the theo ry of expansive learning for a detailed analysis of a systematically documented, year-long process of collective learning within a food cooperative. The board of the food cooperative faced a recurring crisis concerning the cooperative's financial sustainability. The standard solution thu s far had been to launch annual drives to attract n ew members whose membership fees would alleviate the cooperati ve's financial strain. However, as we began our fieldwor k, the board members grew uneasy with the repeated hig h-pressure efforts to expand membership. A collective pursuit for an alternative solution began to emerge.

## Theoretical framework and research questions

The theoretical apparatus of the present study cons ists of the interconnected concepts of (1) expansiv e learning cycle and expansive learning actions, (2) contradictions and their discursive manifestations, (3) tangible solutions and (4) integrative conceptualizations.  Expansive learning is the qualitative transformation of collective activity systems, such as work units or communities. Expansion refers to a type of learning in which the object and scope of the activity are transformed, but the exact nature of this transformation is not predetermined. Expansive learning leads to the formation of a new, expanded object and pattern of activity (Engeström, 2015). Expansive learning begins w hen individuals question accepted practices, leading to a collectiv e movement for transformation. Expansive learning process unfolds as a stepwise process involving specific phases known as learning actions. The learning actions of an ideal-typical expansive cycle are (1) questioning, (2) analysis, (3) modeling a new solution, (4) examinin g the new model, (5) implementing the n ew model, (6) reflecting on the process, and (7) consolidating th e new practice. However, expansive learning process  often deviates from the ideal-typical order, marked by iterations, repetitions, digressions, and non-expansive actions. Expansive learning cycles occur at various scales. The formation of the New York City Community Land Initiative was analyzed as a six-year expansive learning cycle (Caldwell et al., 2019), representing a macro-scale cycle (Foot, 2001). Formative interventi ons can trigger meso-scale expansive cycles, lasting a few months (Engeström et al., 2013), while micro-scale expansive cycles may endure for a few hours or even less (Rantavuori et al., 2016). Macro-scale cycles are typically analyzed retrospectively using historical documents and interviews, lacking the comprehensive observational and discour se data needed for systematic analysis of learning actions. This study is the first to cover a macro-scale cycle and analyze a body of discourse and observational data from a continuous series of crucial meetings.  Expansive learning is triggered and driven by contr adictions that cry for solutions within and between the activity systems i nvolved. Based on our ethnographic fieldwork and hi storical interviews, Figure 1 presents a working hypothesis of the contradictions in the activity system of the board of the food cooperative.

Figure 1 Working Hypothesis of Contradictions in the Activity Systems of the Board of the Food Cooperative The two-headed, lightning-shaped arrows in Figure 1 represent the key contradictions. The primary contradiction is located within the object. The board must ensure  the production and delivery of sufficient amounts of diverse, high-quality produce while also ensuring that there are enough members whose membership fees cover production costs. This has proven very difficult, resulting in  a recurring shortage of money needed to pay farmer s’ salaries and cover other expenses. This primary contradictio n generated two secondary contradictions: one between the object and the rules of balanced budget, and another between the object and the instruments (land, labor, money). Contradictions are identifiable through manifestations such as dilemmas, conflicts, critical conflicts, and double binds (Engeström & Sannino, 2011). Tracing the appearance of discursive manifestations of contradictions through a process of expansive learning opens a window into the motivational dynamics of this type of learning. The expansive learning process goes beyond individu al cognitive structures and skills, resulting in tangible solutions that transform collective activi ties. It is essential to trace the emergence of the se tangible solutions to understand the process and consequence s of expansive learning. Complex problems and contradictions often lead to partial solutions, which gain impact as they come together and reinforce one another. These partial solutions are explicit and deliberate  efforts to bring about change, “ongoing and situat ed accommodations, adaptations and alterations” (Orlik owski, 1996, p. 66).  Participants of expansive learning integrate and explain the tangible solutions they d evelop, generating conceptualizations aimed at explicating the expanded object in the making (Engeström & Sannino, 2012). These conceptualizations may arise without labels, or may be defined verbally before practical impleme ntation (Engeström, 2013), serving as partial stabi lizations of the newly emerging expanded object (Cussins, 1992).

The research questions for this study are as follow s: (1) How did the long learning process in the food cooperative evolve and to what extent did it corres pond to the logic of an expansive learning cycle?  (2) How did discursive manifestations of contradictions evolve in the learning process?  (3) What kinds of tangible solutions were developed, and did they lead to integrative conceptualizations? (4) How were the solutions implemented and what kind of impact did they have in practice? We seek to answer the research questions by means of longitudinal analysis of the dynamic interplay between expansive learning actions, discursive manifestations of contradictions, tangible solutions, and integrative conceptualizations, evolving through the 27 meetings of the board.

## Data and methods

The participants of this study are the board members. The data includes audiotaped board meetings, a videotaped planning day, general meetings, interviews, and archival materials, with primary data derived from discussions in regular meetings and the planning day, transcribed by a professional transcriber. An intervention in the form of a planning day aimed to tackle financial sustainability challenges by identifying cooperative's main issues, devising solutions, and formulating a plan to implement these solutions. In our analysis, we first divided the meetings tran scripts into conversational episodes based on their substantive topical contents. Next, we identified e pisodes containing tangible solutions to the financ ial viability challenge of the cooperative suggeste d by the participants. Then, we identified expansive learning actions (Engeström et. al, 2013) and examined discursive manifestations of contradictions (Engeström & Sannino 2011). After that, we searched and identified attempts at integrative conceptualiza tions in the data. Two integrative conceptualizations were identified. The first one w as focused on the idea of giving up efforts to incr ease the size of the cooperative. We call this integrative concep tualization “degrowth.” The second one was focused on multiplying food cooperatives similar to the Herttoniemi one and nurturing cooperation among them. We call this integrative conceptualization “network of cooperati ves.” Through these steps, we constructed and analyzed th e phases of the food cooperative board's learning process in relation to the expansive learning cycle model.

## Overview of the findings

In Figure 2, it is possible to see a temporal evolution of each analysis category over the course of the 27 meetings of the board of the cooperative. These analysis categories are:  1) tangible solutions, 2) expansive learning actions,

3) manifestations of contradictions; 4) integrative conceptualizations. The evolution of each category is presented

in a separate line chart.

Figure 2 Evolution of Frequencies of Main Types of Each Analysis Category Over the Course of the Meetings of the Cooperative.

In the discussions of the board, we identified five  tangible solutions created by the participants: (1) limiting the number of members; (2) reducing the work force and improving the organization of work in the field; (3) reducing the species of plants and the cultivated field area; (4) bringing forward the annual rhythm of the cooperative; and

(5) purchases from other farms. A common thread amo ng these solutions was the aim to achieve financial

sustainability by reducing farming costs.  The tangible solution s that were discussed most extensively were reducing the workforce and better organization of wo rk in the field  (347 times) and limiting the number of members (304 times). Reducing the variety of species of plants and the  cultivated field area was also discussed relatively often (177 times). Bringing forward the yearly rhythm of the cooperative (74 times) and purchases from outside (58 times) were discussed less frequently.

Expansive learning actions were spread out largely covering all meetings, but it was also possible to identify clusters of actions in the data. The most prevalent expansive learning action was examining (380 times). The second most frequent learning action was analyzing (265 times) followed by the action of implementing (96 times), and the action of modeling (38 times). The less common actions were questioning (10 times) and reflecting on the process (one time).

Dilemmas were the most common discursive manifestation of co ntradictions (114 times), followed by conflicts (62 times). Double binds (15 times) and critical conflicts (three times) were also identified. Integrative conceptualizations of degrowth  (35 times) occurred more often than integrative conceptualizations the network of cooperatives (six times).

Figure 2 shows that meetings 14 and 18 played a cru cial role in the learning process, as a significant number of tangible solutions, expansive learning ac tions, and discursive manifestations of contradicti ons frequently emerged in these two meetings. The combined number of occurrences in all categories of analysis was exceptionally high in these two meetings: 286 in meeting 14 and 391 in meeting 18. The more detailed qualitative analysis revealed tha t the learning actions in this case followed substantially in meaningful order the ideal-typical theoretical model of expansive learning cycle. That is, the first three learning actions occurred for the most part in the first hal f of the meeting cycle. Similarly, the subsequent three learning actions occurred mostly during the second half of the meeting cycle (meetings 15 to 27). A meeting 14 (a planning day of the cooperative) served as a bridge between two parts. This indicates that the overall learning process of the board of the cooperative followed th e logic of the expansive learning cycle.  Conflicts were quite common type of contradictions  in the learning process, especially between meeting s 11 and 19. After that, conflicts did not appear anymore. This indicates th at after the board was able to make important decis ions, the implementation process was more equanimous and stra ight-forward. Five different tangible solutions were identified in the data, eac h of them trying to tackle the problem of the finan cial viability of the cooperative. We were able to trace complete or nearly complete expansive learning cycles for each solution. The overall expansive cycle thus took the shape of five parallel and coalescing sub-cycles.

## Conclusions

Findings revealed that the emerging concept of degr owth played a crucial role as a binding integrative  factor between the tangible solutions, while the network of cooperatives remained more isolated from the solutions, and its integrative potential remains to be realized. The solution of limiting the number of members was implemented successfully. The board could consequently reduce t he workforce and the number of plant species, as we ll as reorganize the work in the field. The planning of f arming was based on disposable income, and by sendi ng the invoices to the members at the beginning of the season, the annual rhythm of the cooperative was changed The results show that innovations are not isolated events. A specific intervention, a planning day, made it possible for new tools  to emerge, but these tools did not come out of nowhere. The st udy revealed a developmental trajectory for each new tool, conside red an expansive learning process. This process beg an seven months prior to the actual intervention and included 13 meetings focused on discussing issues related to financial sustainability. The starting point of innovations w as real-world problems whic h participants encountered in the board meetings as they were trying to solve the com plex object of financial sustainability. In the mee tings, the tools were still incomplete and at a preliminary phase in their development. An intervention was needed to refine new tools in the mature form to be implemented later successfully. After the data collection we have followed the development of the Herttoniemi food cooperative from a distance. The cooperative has found a new field in Herttoniemi, where most of the members live. The new field made it possible to create a new type of membership, where a professional farmer takes care of the cultivation, but members themselves can pick their own share of vegetables from the field. The location of the new field has also provided a possibility to arrange events in the field, where new members can be recruited, and the products of the field have been sold tonew customers. The founder also launched an online store for selling products of local farmers.
