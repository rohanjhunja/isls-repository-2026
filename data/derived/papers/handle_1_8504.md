# How Different Types of Teacher Dashboards Impact Teachers’ Problem Detection Accuracy

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Little is known about how design choices in teacher dashboards (i.e, digital tools that summarize and visualize information to support the teacher in the teaching process) impact teachers’ monitoring and interpretation of critical events. Therefore, this study examined how designs that offer different levels of support impact pre-service teachers’ monitoring. Three dashboard designs (from least to most supported: mirroring, alerting, and advising) were compared. In three conditions teachers (n = 50) processed nine teacher dashboards of a particular design. Teachers were required to detect the critical situations in each dashboard. Results demonstrated that the alerting dashboards resulted in more successful problem detection than the mirroring dashboards. Though advising dashboards, in theory, offer more support, no additional benefit over alerting dashboards was found.

## Introduction

Teacher noticing refers to teachers’ successful monitoring and manag ement of students. It is imperative for effective education and encompasses teachers’ cognitive processes about attending to noteworthy events, making sense of such events, and responding to these events to make informed decision in a tea ching context. In short, teacher noticing includes three main aspects: (a) identifying what is important in a teaching situation, (b) making connections between specific events and broader principles of teaching and learning, and (c) using what one knows about the context to reason about a situation (van Es & Sherin, 2002). The introduction of computer-based learning impacts teacher noticing, but also presents opportunities to enhance it with for instance, teacher dashboards. Teacher dashboards are accumulations of learning an alytics that aid teachers’ monitoring and management of students’ progress (Verbert et al., 2013). They often contain visualizations (e.g., social interaction, time spent, and test results) that enable teachers to get insight into processes that would otherwise go unnoticed (Verbert et al., 2013). Research shows that teacher dashboards can facilitate teacher noticing (Vrikki et al., 2021) but also that their effectiveness to do so depends on specific design features that offer guidance (Le euwen and Rummel, 2019; Soller et al., 2005). For example, dashboard that contain features that make relevant information more noticeable, can foster attending (Mottus et. al., 2015). Dashboards that support teachers to interpret and give meaning to the data to decide what is relevant or useful for their teaching can foster sense making, (Verbert et al., 2013). Dashboards that, for example, help teachers to assess the urgency and gravity of a detected sit uation and decide on how to follow up can foster responding (Molenaar et. al., 2019; van Leeuwen et. al., 2019). Based on the presence or absence of guiding feature s, teacher dashboards can be grouped differently. Soller et al. (2005) categorized them as mirroring and guiding tools. A similar categorization was created by van Leeuwen and Rummel (2019) who distinct between mirroring, alerting and advising dashboards. Both agree that mirroring dashboards provide the least support and leave the interpreta tion mainly to teachers. In these dashboards, teachers decide what information should be paid attention to and how to respond without an y alert (Soller et al., 2015). When guidance is added, this  is usually in the form of alerts and/or advices. I n alerting dashboards, possibly relevant information is pointed out to t eachers (Schwarz et al., 2018) while in advising dashboards teachers receive guidance in the process of interp retation of the given information and the actions they could take (Martinez-Maldonado, 2019). Van Leeuwen and Rummel (2020) showed that guiding tools benefit effectiveness. They found that advising dashboards,  compared to mirroring dashboards, lead to an incre ased amount of detection of problematic situations, decrease in effort, and an increase in confidence about decisions. Though a general conception might be that all steps of teacher noticing are ideally supported in teacher dashboards, presenting too much information can be burdening instead of facilitating them (Van Leeuwen, 2015). Research demonstrates that there is a risk of incre ased orchestration overload when the information provided to teachers increases (Martinez-Maldonado, 2019). Redu ndant or confusing information can result in increa sed cognitive load which will negatively impact effectiveness (Mayer & Moreno, 2010). Moreover, over structuring can create an ambiance of overreliance and reduce effortful processing and critical thinking and narrow teachers’ focus. For example, when the designed support inten tionally highlights something, teachers could become over reliant on what is highlighted and might neglect other potentially important things (Brunvand & Fishman, 2006). To conclude, teacher dashboards have unique affordances, but their effectiveness depends on how they are used. They should be carefully tailored to achi eve their full potential. Teachers should be suppor ted but not ICLS2022 Proceedings 1425 © ISLS overwhelmed (Mottus et.al. 2015) or limited in their noticing (Brunvand & Fishman, 2006). Understanding how support features in teacher dashboards impact teacher noticing can advance dashboard design, theory, and praxis. This study explores the impact of three levels of support: 1) mirroring, 2) alerting, and 3) advising dashboards.

## Method

The study followed a quasi-experimental design with  three conditions. Conditions only differed in the type of teacher dashboard: mirroring, alerting, and advising. Data was collected through virtual meetings where teachers processed nine simulated dashboards. After the teac hers were introduced to the study, they went throug h the dashboard simulations one by one, at their own pace. Teachers were required to detect critical cases i n each dashboard and report this on a questionnaire. For each reported case they were asked to clarify why this case was critical by answering a selection list (indicating problem) and open question (asking for rationale). Participants were preservice science teachers enrolled in a four-year science teacher education program of a public university in Turkey. To ensure that they had sufficient pedagogical knowledge, the sample was drawn from teachers in the 3rd and 4th year of their education. In terms of gender distribution (male/female) the sample was representative of the population of preservice science teachers in the university. Conditions were similar in terms of size and demographics (Table 1).

## Results

A one-way between-groups ANOVA was conducted with condition as independent variable (i.e., mirroring, alerting, and advising), and the number of detected intended problems as dependent variable. The results revealed a significant difference, F (2, 47) = 5.04, p = 0.010, eta squared 0.18. Post-hoc comparisons using the Tukey HSD test indicated that teachers in the mirroring condition (M = 1.51, SD = 1.20) performed significantly poorer than the teachers in the alerting condition (M = 3.00, SD = 2.04, p = 0.023) and advising condition (M = 2.98, SD = 1.33, p = 0.023). There was no significant difference between advising and alerting condition (p = 0.999). In addition, a one-way between-groups ANOVA was conducted with condition as independent variable (i.e., mirroring, alerting and advising) and detected unintended problems  as dependent variable. The results revealed a significant difference, F (2, 47) = 4.01, p = 0.025, eta squared 0.15. Post-hoc comparisons usi ng the Tukey HSD test indicated that, on average, teachers in the mirroring condition (M = 51.06, SD = 23.10) performed significantly poorer than the teachers in the alert ing condition (M = 70.76, SD = 23.50, p = 0.032), but did not differ from the teachers in advising condition (M = 67.65, SD = 18.04, p = 0.076). There was no significant difference between advising and alerting conditions (p = 0.910).

## Discussion

In this study, the impact of different levels of su pport provided in teacher dashboards on teachers’ noticing was investigated. In line with our hypothesis, teachers ’ noticing improved when they worked with dashboard  types that included some form of support (i.e., alerts or  advices). Teachers using alerting and advising das hboards detected the critical cases that were indicated via  the support more often than teachers who worked wi th a dashboard that merely ‘mirrored’ the data. In general this aligns with findings in literature where researchers found that providing support increased teachers’ detectio n accuracy of critical situations (Casamayor et al., 2009; Schwarz et al., 2018). But the results seemingly co ntradict with findings by van Leeuwen et al. (2019). In their study, dashboard type did not affect successful problem detection, but they did notice that teachers using advising dashboards demonstrated more in-depth interpretation of the provided information. Though the previous finding indicates that providing support might be the ideal option, it should be noted that over-structuring or providing too much support can cause less effortful processing. Meaning that note-worthy information might go unnoted. Teachers in the current study were encouraged to interpret all the data, instead of just looking at the summary page. Therefore, their detection of problems that were not addressed in the alerts or advices was also evaluated. The results revealed th at the participants using alerting dashboards detec ted unintended problems better than the participants us ing mirroring and advising dashboards. Unexpectedly, the participants using advising dashboards (with the highest level of support) performed similar to participants who worked with the mirroring dashboards (no support). This showed that although the advising dashboard supported teachers in the detection of ‘intended problems’, the high level of support might have limited teacher’s noticing. This finding corroborates the idea that a high level of support for pre-service teachers might cause a “mimicking” effect (Gaudin & Chalies, 2015). That is, when highly structured support is provided, pre-service teachers might over rely on the support and might simply express w hat they perceive as “right” based on support, inst ead of evaluating the data critically (Brunvand & Fishman, 2006; Gaudin & Chalies, 2015). Based on this, we carefully conjecture that more support does not always equal more effective use. This supports the general consensus that the level of support in dashboards should be tailored to the needs of the users (Brouwer & Robijns, 2014). Overall the findings support the conclusion that teacher dashboards with support (like alerts or advises) are likely to be more effective thandashboard that only mirror the data. However, designers should be wary that ICLS2022 Proceedings 1427 © ISLS more detail (like advices for further pedagogical actions) might not necessarily benefit teacher’s interpretation of the provided data. There is a risk of mimicking and suboptimal processing. With these findings, it should be noted that the sample size was relatively small and that the participants were pre-service teachers who were relatively inexperienced on teaching collaborative learning and using teacher dashboards. Therefore, they are more likely to demonstrate a tendency to over rely on support. Bas ed on the insights of this study, it can be said th at, further design attempts for this kind of dashboards should be careful with providing too much support, but different target groups might respond differently. The findings emph asize that dashboards should be carefully tailored to the experiences and needs of the users.
