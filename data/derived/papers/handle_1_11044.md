# Centering Relationality When Investigating the Utility of Mathematics with Early Adolescents

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While there are benefits of perceiving mathematics as useful, most perspectives on mathematical utility center on usefulness for individual advancement or for the economy and global competitiveness. Given the emphasis in many communities on interdependence and the importance of prosocial goals and social responsibility in early adolescence, we argue for integrating the notion of relationality—between both humans and the more-than-human world—into conceptions of utility in mathematics.

## Introduction

Mathematics has held a position of high status in U.S. society, serving as a gatekeeper and perpetuati ng notions that only some people belong in mathematics, despite calls of STEM for all. Societal narratives focus on the need for mathematics in service of  the economy and global competition (e.g., Gutstein, 2009), emphasizing the usefulness of mathematics for future schooling, car eers, and money-related activities. Needed are considerations of potential uses of mathematics that center relationality. In the spirit of this year’s conference theme, we theorize about the value of integrating a focus on relationality into the study of the  usefulness of mathematics with early adolescents, drawing on data from interviews conducted with 39 middle school students to provide examples. Utility value Research on usefulness is largely guided by expectancy-value theory, which indicates that an individual’s values and expectations of success directly influence acad emic-related outcomes (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002). One such value is utility value, defined as the relevance of a task for one’s goal s. Interventions focused on measuring and enhancing utility value have shown that perceiving a subject as useful can have numerous positive bene fits including enhanced interest and improved course performance (Hulleman et al., 2010; Hulleman & Harackiewicz, 2009). At the same time, this work has been limited  to conceptions of utility focused on future careers, specific everyday activities, and current or future schoolin g; and has primarily represented the  perspectives of White, middle-class college students (Dobie, 2019a). In a study of  early adolescents who predominantly identified as M exican, Mexican  American, or Chicanx, youth mentioned many money-and career-related uses of mathematics but also how useful it was to engage with math in particular ways, such as throug h collaboration and sharing their thinking  (Dobie, 2019a). These discussions echoed other interdependent value s (Grossmann & Varnum, 2011)  that the students voiced, such as the importance of family and using math to help their families (Dobie, 2019b; Esparza & Sanchez, 2008). Such foci, however, are left out of most societal n arratives about the usefulness of mathematics, whic h typically emphasize mathematics in service of capitalism and global competition (e.g., Greer & Mukhopadhyay, 2003; Gutstein, 2009). These narratives encourage a view of mathematics education “as something that prepares for a job” rather than “for full citizenship…in a critical and conscious way” (D’Ambrosio, 1990, p. 21). Adding a lens of relationality to utility value Learning scientists have increasingly called for and  enacted research that centers relationality betwee n peoples and connection with the more-than-human world, drawing on Indigenous traditions and epistemologies (e.g., Bang et al., 2018; Vossoughi et al., 2020). While there are some examples of such framings in research on utility value

– e.g. Brown et al. (2015) found that undergraduate students were more motivated to engage in biomedical science

research when the work was framed in terms of its u tility for helping others – a focus on connection is typically missing from research focused on the utility of mat hematics. Here we propose that incorporating the notion of relationality might be especially powerful when supporting early adolescents to view mathematics as useful, given the focus on prosocial behaviors and social respons ibility during adolescence (Wentzel, 2014). Furthermore, centering relationality might have added benefits f or students belonging to communities where interdep endence is emphasized (e.g., Esparza & Sanchez, 2008).

This poster will include examples of how relationality emerged in in terviews with 39 youth ages 11-14, as well as considerations of relationality that did  not emerge. These examples and absences will offer a view of how this lens of relationality can enrich utility v alue research. One theme that repeatedly emerged in interviews was using math to help one’s family, as in Daniel’s comment below: When my sisters don't feel like doing math and we go to a swimming pool…my mom she just tells me, ‘Oh okay, Daniel can you add up on how much it will be?’ So, I quickly add up and tell my mom the price it would be for all of us to go in the pool or like to the swimming pool. On the other hand, discussions of using mathematics  to help one’s community were sparse, and no studen ts mentioned the power of math for connecting with or supporting the more-than-human world.

## Discussion

This research advocates for  merging the lens of relationality with utility valu e in mathematics. We argue that centering the lens of relationality challenges comm on societal narratives about mathematics in service  of the economy and global competition (e.g., Greer & Mukho padhyay, 2003; Gutstein, 2009). We highlight how youth already think about relationality in ways that can be tied more explicitly to mathematics, as well as new directions for framing the usefulness of mat hematics for helping others. Expanding the boundaries of how  we think about the usefulness of mathematics has the potential to help a broader range of students see themselves in mathematics and see mathematics as beneficial to them and their communities.
