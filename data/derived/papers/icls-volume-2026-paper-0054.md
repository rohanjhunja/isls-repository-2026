# Manga as Counterspaces for Queer Youth Identities

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
When tasked with learning, queer youth often encounter limited opportunities to see themselves in ways that recognize their identities, cultures, and epistemologies as sources of knowledge and creativity to learn. Using identity-in-practice, visual storyte lling, and queer rightful presence, we sought to see how queer identities were dignified during a summer camp. Eleven youth participated in a two-week manga-based maker camp where they made animations, comics, and other artistic representations. Through a data analysis process called crystallization, we identified two themes related to queer youth identity: escapism and embodiments of defiance. We argue that counterspaces such as the maker camp offer room for queer youth to play with their identit ies. We offer ways for others to use counterspaces like maker camps as ways to be responsive to youth and their needs by connecting to things they are already interested in and letting them explore identities within those spaces. Purpose Although public acceptance of lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, and other diverse gender and sexuality-identifying (LGBTQ+) individuals has grown in recent years (GLAAD, 2025), the current sociopolitical landscape is marked by legislative and cul tural attacks on queer and trans (QT) communities at the local, state, and federal levels (Quinan, 2025). These measures uphold the conservative Eurocentric ideals, situated in a white supremacist, settler colonial, and cisheteropatriarchal ideology that s eeks to erase deviant experiences (Leyva et al., 2022). Educational institutions participate in these logics through book bans, surveillance of teachers, and conditions of learning that suppress joy, love, and affirmation, further reifying these ideologica l values (Tran & Pierre, 2025). Within these environments, QT youth often encounter limited opportunities to see themselves in valuable ways, recognizing that their identities, cultures, and epistemologies are meaningful sources of knowledge and creativity to learning. In recent years, scholars have turned towards makerspaces and other creative learning environments as generative contexts for young people to explore their identity in relation to self and community as a sociocultural, historical, and political means (Cala brese Barton & Tan, 2020; Vossoughi et al., 2016). Makerspaces blend art, design, STEM, and storytelling to create conditions for young people to engage in making as a relational, imaginative, and epistemic process related to their learning, historicities, and personal trajectories (Lindberg et al., 2020). These environments can affirm marginalized identities and cultivate a sense of belonging through collaboration, material engagement, and shared authorship (Peppler & Thompson, 2024). Research particularl y focusing on queer and trans youth in makerspace education illuminates that making invites multiple epistemologies and affective-expressive ways that often exceed traditional disciplinary boundaries (e.g., Floegel, 2019; Kitzie & Moorefield-Lang, 2018; Suárez et al., 2023; Tofel-Grehl et al., 2024). Within this research landscape, we argue that these spaces hold additional significance, given how they offer room for QT youth to play with their identity in ways that are often contentious, messy, or otherwise contradictory to dominant social norms that would affirm their self-definition and collective world-making as queer and trans individuals (Leyva et al., 2022; Suárez et al., 2022). Situated within the broader fields of learning sciences, design education, and queer and trans studies in education, this study considers how our creative and insubordinate pedagogies cultivated a queer rightful presence (Tofel-Grehl et al., 2024) by examining how QT youths’ identity is expressed, negotiated, and affirmed within queer-focused makerspace learning environments. We focus on a summer camp program that centers on arts-based, design-oriented learning, where QT youth engage in visual storytelling through manga to explore self and community within a semi-rural, highly conservative community located in the Intermountain West. In particular, this work explores how makerspaces candignify queer and trans ways of knowing and being, creating in contexts where such ways are often marginalized (and otherwise). Guided by this framing, we investigate the conditions under which visual storytelling can affirm QT identities in rural informal learning contexts. Identity is posited as an ongoing process of doing, being, and becoming within a sociocultural and material relation of power of the learning context (Gee, 2000). In makerspace education, identity reflects how one’s competence, recognition, and performance are continually negotiated and enacted through participation in disciplinary practices (Andrews & Boklage, 2024; Avraamidou, 2019; Carlone & Johnson, 2007). Within this framing, identity-in-practice refers to the ways learners construct who they are through participati on in patterned activities (Pattinson et al., 2020; Gee, 2000; Wenger, 1998); these interactions create conditions in which learners engage with a community of practice and material conditions, shaping their sense of self and their roles within a collective. While traditional views of identity-in-practice frameworks emphasize participation in authentic settings and activities as central to identity development (Carlone, 2017), for queer and trans youth, such involvement in makerspaces often entails navigating normative boundaries of gender, sexuality, and embodiment that structure epistemological legitimacy (Suárez et al., 2022; Tofel-Grehl et al., 2024). A queer reframing of identity-in-practice foregrounds how identity works in both relational and resistant ways — that is, how queer and trans youth perform, disrupt, and reconfigure participation through affect, aesthetics, and marginalization. In a sense, QT youth are drawing on their identities in practice as building blocks that shape their every day acts of performance and being within a learning context that affirms their lived realities and their place in STEM worlds (Muñoz, 1999; Tan et al., 2013). In other words, these acts are situated in the material and emotional landscapes of their learning environments and are informed by their interactions (e.g., with themselves, their surroundings, and epistemic tools). From this lens, identity-in-practice is an engagement of identity work, a practice of presence, and a process of self-definition for QT youth. Positioning queer identity-in-practice within a practice-based framework foregrounds the significance of tools, media, and representational forms that mediate participation in makerspace education. Visual storytelling, for instance, functions as both a representational and epistemic practice within design education (Sandoval et al., 2000), which enables youth to externalize ideas, test concepts, and communicate solutions in ways that integrate technical reasoning with personal and cultural narratives and epistemologies (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Tran, 2026). Moreover, multimodal and visual practices support identity development by supporting youth as producers of knowledge rather than passive recipients (Vossoughi et al., 2016); thus, sensemaking through visual storytelling affirms youth’s lived experiences as valid foundations for learning and identity building. For this particular work, we situate manga — a Japanese style of comic book and graphic novels — as a form of visual storytelling, which we argue encapsulates both representational and epistemic practices that embolden youth to make meaning of their knowledge production through makerspaces. Indeed, manga offers youth a medium through which they can engage in design thinking while incorporating identity work in ways that are personally meaningful, socially situated, and congruent with their personhood and well-being. In our previous work, we articulated that manga served as an accessible and identity-affirming medium for QT youth, where engaging in makerspace practices and education supports the reimagining through character development, setting, and narrative lines that reflect the lived experiences, aspirations, and complex emotions of QT youth (Tofel-Grehl et al., 2024; Suárez et al., 2022). Building upon this notion of cultivating an identity-affirming makerspace education, this work draws on queer rightful presence (Tofel-Grehl et al., 2024) as the conceptual anchor. Queer rightful presence posits that QT youth should not only have access to learning spaces (e.g., representation in makerspaces, inclusion as absolution, etc.), but also be able to engage and participate as their whole selves without identity negotiation, assimilation, or erasure. This situated view challenges the cisheteronormative norms and assumptions that often define makerspace culture (Andrews & Boklage, 2024), thereby legitimizing QT epistemologies in technical disciplines. Taken together, these theoretical perspectives inform our understanding of makerspace and design education, where QT youth can engage in identity-affirming practices. The development of their queer and trans identities is intrinsically tied to the conditions under which they are recognized, valued, and able to participate without compromising their gendered, sexual, and cultural selves.

## Theoretical framework

We draw on a multitude of theoretical perspectives to situate this work – identity-in-practice, visual storytelling, and queer rightful presence (Holland et al., 1998; Sedawi et al., 2025; Sandoval et al., 2000; Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). Identity is posited as an ongoing process of doing, being, and becoming within a sociocultural and material relation of power of the learning context (Gee, 2000). In makerspace education, identity reflects how one’s competence, recognition, and performance are continually negotiated and enacted through participation in disciplinary practices (Andrews & Boklage, 2024; Avraamidou, 2019; Carlone & Johnson, 2007). Within this framing, identity-in-practice refers to the ways learners construct who they are through participati on in patterned activities (Pattinson et al., 2020; Gee, 2000; Wenger, 1998); these interactions create conditions in which learners engage with a community of practice and material conditions, shaping their sense of self and their roles within a collective. While traditional views of identity-in-practice frameworks emphasize participation in authentic settings and activities as central to identity development (Carlone, 2017), for queer and trans youth, such involvement in makerspaces often entails navigating normative boundaries of gender, sexuality, and embodiment that structure epistemological legitimacy (Suárez et al., 2022; Tofel-Grehl et al., 2024). A queer reframing of identity-in-practice foregrounds how identity works in both relational and  resistant ways — that is, how queer and trans youth perform, disrupt, and reconfigure participation through affect, aesthetics, and marginalization. In a sense, QT youth are drawing on their identities in practice as building blocks that shape their every day acts of performance and being within a learning context that affirms their lived realities and their place in STEM worlds (Muñoz, 1999; Tan et al., 2013). In other words, these acts are situated in the material and emotional landscapes of their learning environments and are informed by their interactions (e.g., with themselves, their surroundings, and epistemic tools). From this lens, identity-in-practice is an engagement of identity work, a practice of presence, and a process of self-definition for QT youth.

Positioning queer identity-in-practice within a practice-based framework foregrounds the significance of tools, media, and representational forms that mediate participation in makerspace education. Visual storytelling, for instance, functions as both a representational and epistemic practice within design education (Sandoval et al., 2000), which enables youth to externalize ideas, test concepts, and communicate solutions in ways that integrate technical reasoning with personal and cultural narratives and epistemologies (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Tran, 2026). Moreover, multimodal and visual practices support identity development by supporting youth as producers of knowledge rather than passive recipients (Vossoughi et al., 2016); thus, sensemaking through visual storytelling affirms youth’s lived experiences as valid foundations for learning and identity building. For this particular work, we situate manga — a Japanese style of comic book and graphic novels — as a form of visual storytelling, which we argue encapsulates both representational and epistemic practices that embolden youth to make meaning of their knowledge production through makerspaces. Indeed, manga offers youth a medium through which they can engage in design thinking while incorporating identity work in ways that are personally meaningful, socially situated, and congruent with their personhood and well-being. In our previous work, we articulated that manga served as an accessible and identity-affirming medium for QT youth, where engaging in makerspace practices and education supports the reimagining through character development, setting, and narrative lines that reflect the lived experiences, aspirations, and complex emotions of QT youth (Tofel-Grehl et al., 2024; Suárez et al., 2022). Building upon this notion of cultivating an identityaffirming makerspace education, this work draws on queer rightful presence  (Tofel-Grehl et al., 2024) as the conceptual anchor. Queer rightful presence posits that QT youth should not only have access to learning spaces (e.g., representation in makerspaces, inclusion as absolution, etc.), but also be able to engage and participate as their whole selves without identity negotiation, assimilation, or erasure. This situated view challenges the cisheteronormative norms and assumptions that often define makerspace culture (Andrews & Boklage, 2024), thereby legitimizing QT epistemologies in technical disciplines. Taken together, these theoretical perspectives inform our understanding of makerspace and design education, where QT youth can engage in identity-affirming practices. The development of their queer and trans identities is intrinsically tied to the conditions under which they are recognized, valued, and able to participate without compromising their gendered, sexual, and cultural selves.

## Context

We invited queer youth and allies to participate in a two-week long summer camp in summer 2024. This study took place in the Mountain West of the United States in a state that is highly conservative and religious with 80% of the population identifying as white (U.S Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025). The county in which the camp took place is designated as urban, with a population of around 150,000 people; many of whom are employed in trades 447 (i.e. farming) and manufacturing (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025). Participants (n = 11) were recruited after signing up to participate in the camp; all research was conducted under [university blinded] IRB # [blinded number]. We describe participants in our findings using pseudonyms and use pronouns that the participant used. In cases where the participant did not declare pronouns or the researchers were unsure, we use the gender-neutral pronoun “they.” The maker camp invited youth to make their own manga, a style of comic and animation popularized in Japan (Ito, 2005). Throughout the camp, participants had the option to present their work to peers in a “story circle” that was intentionally styled as a design critique session. Participants were asked to share their work, focusing on what they designed, what they enjoyed doing, and what they wanted to improve on. Other participants were provided with design critique prompts to scaffold their feedback and encourage interaction beyond aesthetics or surfa ce-level feedback. In the story circle sessions we analyzed, participants shared their artwork freely, but many chose to have only their artwork on camera (instead of their faces and the artwork). We collected video data and images of participant-produced manga, all of which provide rich nuances in dignifying QT youth identities. Instead of traditional data triangulation, we use the process of crystallization (Ellingson, 2009). Data triangulation is  a two-dimensional data collection and analysis strategy that gathers a comprehensive understanding of a phenomenon (Carter et al., 2014). In contrast, crystallization is a three-dimensional data collection and analysis strategy that “combines multiple forms of analysis and multiple genres of representation into a coherent text or series of related texts” (Ellingson, 2009, p. 4). The central imagery is a crystal, which combines symmetry and substance with an infinite variety of shapes, substances, transmuta tions, multidimensionalities, and angles of approach, providing a deeper, more complex understanding of a topic. As an observer, viewing our collected data as crystal allows for varied and nuanced interpretations of that data (Tran, 2023). In addition, Ellingson (2009) argues that a rich description of multi-genre representations would offer the researcher a deeper understanding of the sensemaking process and afford more than one way of knowing, especially since crystallization is philosophically grounded in social constructivist and critical paradigms. For participants’ drawings and animations, we followed Bell’s (2004) conceptualization to determine values and variables within participants’ manga. Within Bell’s visual content analysis, images are analyzed for values which contain variables. These multimodal approaches not only honor the plurality of perspectives represented in the data corpus but also the lived experiences of our QT youth. Here, we present crystallizations of manga created by camp participants, focusing on the youth’s depictions of themselves in their manga as well as the ways in which they describe themselves on paper and on video. We present participants’ manga and depictions as cases. Not all themes were represented in all participants’ mangas or descriptions; those we focus on are particularly poignant. We present participants' experiences with the artistic artifacts they made in camp and quotes from video recordings. We report on two themes within: escapism and embodiments of defiance and present the representations participants made to elucidate them. The first theme that crystallized was escapism, which we define as imagining one’s life as different from its current state. Margaret and Sally’s descriptions of their manga in the story circle illuminate this theme particularly clearly. Margaret described her manga as “a slice of life” manga, a type in which characters experience mundane experiences (Brenner, 2007).  Margaret described that their manga would show them and their two friends “hanging out” at a coffee shop. When asked about the ages of their characters, Margaret said they were college students, “just living their lives.” This reflects the concept of escapism: Margaret did not wish to depict their current life as a young adult in a hyper-religious, conservative location with few coffeeshops or third spaces for youth to gather. Margaret’s manga also showed them and their friends at a different age than when they were in the study: young adults (“college age”) living mundanely. Being able to live mundanely as many youth in other places do by going to a coffee shop with friends contrasts sharply with the uncontrollableness of existing in a hyper-conservative, religious location, where coffee is considered a drug and abstained from by members of the dominant religion.  Thus, Margaret’s slice-of-life manga, featuring an act that is “mundane” for many other youth, speaks volumes about their current lives and their desire to escape the realities they face. Sally’s manga also featured escapism, in which her main character was in a hospital and had terminal cancer. Sally made sure to point out that her manga “had no happy endings,” but was the experience of a girl in a hospital who was dying. She also indicated that she knew it was sad, but that she wanted to convey it through her art. She especially wanted to show that the girl was not alone in her journey towards death, as she had friends and a love interest who would come visit her in the hospital. In terms of escapism, Sally’s manga contrasted with Margaret’s. Sally’s manga was not related to a fairy tale ending but parallels QT's lives and the realities of what happens to many queer youth: hospital visits in which they may be outed, healthcare professionals  who do not 448 recognize their needs, or visits for treating self-harm. Sally’s manga embodied escapism in two ways: a final escape from reality into death, and an escape into a healthcare system in which one’s bodily autonomy is neither questioned nor actively harmed.

The second theme that crystallized in participants’ manga and descriptions of their manga was that of the embodiment of defiance. We define the embodiment of defiance as a visible expression of refusal or resistance to existing structures of power. River, Sara, and Florence created embodiments of defiance —specifically, insubordination—through their artwork. For River, this was creating an animation of a hypersexualized, mature woman waving to the audience with a smile (Figure 1). In River’s drawing, the woman has blond hair and makeup but is dressed in a crop top, a short skirt, and lace-up knee-length sandals. This defies expectations of what a “good” female role model is in the dominant hyper-religious culture that emphasizes a specific form of beauty: long blond hair, subtle makeup, and modest dress. River’s drawing emphasizes epistemic disobedience that disrupts colonial power dynamics.

450 Manga as a counterspace Manga, as an arts-based medium, operates as a counterspace that enables QT youth to reimagine themselves and their worlds. Drawing on the concepts of counterculture (Warner, 2002), these spaces offer youth opportunities to reject dominant ideologies and ar ticulate modes of being that resist the moral, aesthetic, and epistemic norms of cisheteronomative logics and community contexts. Within such counterspaces, QT youth mobilize creative practices to engage in acts of epistemic disobedience (Mignolo, 2009), asserting their own ways of knowing, feeling, and imagining their own queer futurity (Muñoz, 2019). Manga provides QT youth an avenue to envision queer futurism as both resistance and defiance, as our data suggested. In River’s artwork, for instance, the depiction of a blonde girl wearing a crop top, short skirt, and lace-up sandals disrupts normative gender scripts and logics bounded within the geographical sociopolitics. More specifically, within a highly conservative region shaped by Latter-day Saint cultural values, femininity is often tethered by modesty and purity. River’s illustration destabilizes expectations, portraying femininity through a lens of power, play, and epistemic refusal; we argue that the boldness of the character’s clothing and expressi on reflects River’s assertation of queer aesthetics that contests colonial and religiously inflected boundaries of propriety. We contend that this particular act of reimagining is River’s visual declaration of epistemic obedience, embodying a critique of what counts as “good,” “moral,” or “respectable” within the dominant culture of the geographical sociopolitical context. In another example, Florence’s manga offers another instantiation of manga as counterspace. Their comic depicts a group of friends –each with distinct, queer-coded features such as spiky orange or short blue hair – on a camping trip. This depiction queers both the representation of nature and the social dynamics of who is imagined to belong outdoors. Through playful dialogue and expressive visuals, Florence crafts a narrative that challenges heteronormative and gendered assumptions about outdoor recreation in rural, conservative spaces. Notably, the comic’s open-ended conclusion–whether the monster encountered by the characters is friend or foe –is left to the reader’s interpretation. This refusal to resolve the story, we argue, underscores the creative agency of artist and audience in co-constructing meaning beyond normative moral binaries. Florence extends this defiance into the temporal and social domain by illustrating characters who camp overnight. We argue that the extended time together signifies intimacy, companionship, and community, experiences often denied, monitored, or regulated for queer and trans youth within religiously conservative geographies. Both River’s and Florence’s creations exemplify how QT youth use manga to materialize themselves in counterworlds that disrupt, reimagine, and reclaim the cultural spaces they inhabit.

Uncertainty, windows, and mirrors Within these counterpublic spaces, QT youth engage with imagination and defiance as ways of being in relation to ambiguity. Nested within this is an affective dimension of uncertainty that is familiar to QT youths’ lived experiences. That is, while manga s erves as a counterspace for defiance, it also becomes an opening into the layered realities of their lives– a window through which others can glimpse the textures of QT existence, and a mirror that returns the conditions that shape those realities. Through these visual stories, we encounter the everydayness of uncertainty that marks QT life. In conversation with Bishop’s (1990) framing of windows and mirrors, manga becomes a reflective and projective space—a mirror that returns glimpses of self and a window that opens onto imagined worlds that offer recognition and possibility. Across our youths’ artistic work, uncertainty becomes a clear part of how they live and learn to make sense of themselves and their communities within the geographical and sociopolitical context.

Sally’s manga, for instance, evokes this sense of uncertainty through her portrayal of a girl in a hospital with terminal cancer; the narrative unfolds through scenes of stillness and companionship, inviting the reader to inhabit the uncertainty of a life moving toward an unspoken end. The absence of resolution, paired with moments of tenderness, conveys how QT youth encounter uncertainty as a familiar condition, one that is bound to care, often to mortality, and the search for dignity. Sally’s choice to center these moments within her manga invites reflection on how creative work becomes a space for articulating experiences that cannot be easily narrated elsewhere.  In another instance, Sara and Florence’s project elicits a similar sensibility. Their creative processes (e.g., shifting ideas, recurring figures, and shared motifs) make visible the relational dimensions of uncertainty as well. Sara’s move from a story about the park rangers and monsters to an animation about a cooking show signals a rhythm of experimentation that resists closure; Florence’s continued engagement with the monster figure keeps uncertainty present as a motif of curiosity and relation. Read together, their works highlight uncertainty as a generative practice, a way of staying with the unfinished and allowing new possibilities to develop through shared imagination.

451 Manga as praxis The youth in our study spent two weeks learning how to produce manga: learning varied drawing styles popular to the medium, storyboarding, giving and receiving design critiques, and using software to animate their drawings. We specifically invited queer yo uth and allies to participate in the camp, providing a space to be creative, use a makerspace, and be around others who either identified as they did, or were supporters. The manga camp allowed for youths to enact their own ways of being into the world. In  terms of practical implications for our work, we believe that allowing participants to have full autonomy in their manga versus asking them to all create certain types of manga (i.e. slice of life, coming of age, magical girl, mecha) or around certain topics (i.e. STEM, school life), ensured that QT youth were able to explore different aspects of their identities. Additionally, sharing their manga with one another during the camp allowed for rich stories to develop and afforded youth with the ability to clarify their work by sharing it.
