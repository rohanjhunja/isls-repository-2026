# Positioning Self and Others Through Sympathizing, Empathizing, Anthropomorphizing, and Zoomorphizing: A Case Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We investigate the relationship between learning and identity by analyzing students’ positionings and attendant reasoning in the context of a university seminar designed to engage STEM undergraduate and graduate students in examining the broader impacts of their STEM fields and practices. Specifically, we highlight students’ discursive use of sympathizing, empathizing, anthropomorphizing, and zoomorphizing as ways of projecting self onto humans and nonhumans, reasoning “as if the other.”

## Introduction and Theoretical Framework

Sociocultural studies of learning and identity have often highlighted processes of positioning—of self, group, and others—as key sites for investigating students’ reasoning, disciplinary (Bell, van Horne, & Cheng, 2017) and beyond. Here we contribute to these efforts by anal yzing how students reason to position themselves and their science in socially and ethically articulate ways. The student discourse and interactions we study played out in a university seminar designed to engage STEM undergra duate and graduate students in examining the broade r impacts (societal, cultural, environmental) of their STEM fields and professional practices. We borrow from sociocultural and critical theories of learning and frame identity as lenses through which one positions oneself (through discursive moves; Ge e, 2001) and through which other practice participa nts position such a person relative to socially and cul turally relevant categories, locally or more broadl y defined (Azevedo & Mann, 2021). For example, in the context of schooling children develop academic and disciplinary identities—say, as “good,” “average” or “bad” stude nts and as “doers” or “non-doers” of mathematics or science—and these processes are seen in the mutual,  reflexive positionings of individuals and their in teracting parties, both peers and teacher (Bell, Van Horne, & Cheng, 2017; Nasir & Saxe, 2003).

## Empirical Context and Methods

Data for this study were drawn from a larger, ongoi ng project of design-based research (Barab & Squire, 2004) on a three-credit semester-long course taught by th e first author on the broader social and ethical im pacts and consequentiality of science. While offered via a geosciences program, the course drew students from many STEM disciplines, including geology, environmental sciences, physics, math, computer science, STEM education, and others. A total of 26 students were enrolled in the two semesters it was taught, approximately 42% of whom were female. Our data corpus consisted of 48 whole-class forum discussions, over 66 hours of recorded video of Zoombased class discussions, three iterative drafts of issue investigations for every student, and the rea dings and resources that students assembled and cited in framing their issue investigations. The research team systematically examined these as artifacts of student participation in the course, independently logging aspects of their work that revealed the ways they reasoned about their science  and identity, and meeting periodically to share and code logged data.

## Analysis and Results

We identify four highly visible ways that students positioned self and others in thinking ethically ab out their discipline.

In mathematics, homomorphism refers to a variety of ways that one mathematical s tructure might be mapped onto another of similar form, and here we have adapted the word as a general term for a number of kinds of empathetic and sympathetic reasoning where students identify themselves with other humans. In responding to a writing prompt about whether science can be good or bad (as opposed toneutral), Caroline made the rhetorical choice to humanize science, reasoning that science is carried out by scientists, who are, after all, p eople: “I probably believe in ‘good’ or ‘bad’ science to some extent but it’s kind of like believing in ‘good’ or ‘bad’ people.” (written reflection, Jan 20, 2021). This kind of homomorphizing enabled her to sympathize with science, and map characteristics, imperfections and accountabilities she knows as a human onto science as a group of hu manized others. Students also reasoned empathetically. In r esponse to another student discussing the historica l mistreatment of certain groups by scientists, Aiden  agreed and posed the rhetorical question: Consider ing this history of mistreatment, “why would those other groups now start to simply accept scientific discoveries at face ICLS2022 Proceedings 2142 © ISLS value?” He did not include himself in “those other groups,” but rather empathized with them in a way that allowed him to push back on received Western notions of science as objective, neutral and uniformly beneficial. Anthropomorphizing refers to ways of attributing human characteristic s, behavior, and feelings to nonhuman things in the world, be they living or oth erwise. One culturally ubiquitous example of anthropomorphizing that clearly permeated the discourse of the students was their reasoning about mother earth. Prompted in the first week of class to write about her scientific interests, Rebecca, a geology major, described her research on geothermal energy as the study of “how we can use the heat that comes from Earth's center in a way that doesn't hurt our Earth,” revealing anthropomorphic reasoning that positioned her in care of the earth (written response to prompt). Responding to the same prompt, Sylvia, an undergraduate in environmental science, showed signs of anthropomorphic reasoning for similar positioning, writing about “the world” as something she wants to “give back to,” in part by figuring out “ways in which humans can join with the world” and better understand the “world’s problems” (written response to prompt).

Finally, zoomorphism is a type of discursive device where traits of non-human living things are imagined in or ascribed to humans (c.f. anthropomorphizing human traits to non-humans). We use zoomorphizing to refer to a type of positioning move through which a perso n maps or ascribes aspects of non-human living things to humans. In the closing passage of Caroline’s final paper on anthropocene science, she presented a brief vignette about a neighborhood walk that she frequently took past a patch of cacti, cacti that had been impacted  by an extremely deep and long freeze. Previously, Caroline and others had linked this freeze to anthropogenic climate change. In equating the “somewhat messy transition state” of the recovering cacti to her own self, Caroline formed an emotionally articulate relationship to the non-h uman world, and positioned herself as something of a cactus grappling with anthropocene conditions of climate change: initially “stalwart,” then “defeated” by the freeze, now “naive and pliable,” still vulnerable, but remember ing and growing. Through this reasoning, she drew f rom the non-human world to pattern a characterization of her own affective state, and in doing so made some sense of her own identity as a scientist living in the Anthropocene.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Acts of positioning self and others in the course of practice participation, moment-to-moment and over the long haul, are key events in which a student enacts and/or works out multiple identities relative to school subjects and disciplinary practices. Prior research has helped u s understand students’ identity work and developmen t, attempting to work out terms for how one positions and is positioned by important local actors in the learning process (notably student peers, teacher, and parents) (e.g., Nasir & Saxe, 2003). Our analysis adds to current understandings by (1) showing how student positioning considers actors more remotely detached from their daily lives and sometimes imagined/idealized (i.e. distal “others,” including non-humans), and (2) showing the ways in which stud ents achieved these positionings (i.e., through dis cursive devices of sympathizing, empathizing, anthropomorphizing and zoomorphizing). Crucially, these various forms of projecting self onto human and nonhuman others (and vice versa) afforded students a means of pivoting into a different, imagined “world,” much like Vygotsky’s (1967) formulation of imaginary play. Here we find students’ use of certain discursive devices allowed them to pivot into the imagined perspective of other actors, and to draw insights that would not be possible otherwise.

It is likely that students deploy a wide variety of subtly different strategies for reasoning “as if the other.” For example, we note that chremamorphizing is a common way of writing that is similar to zoomorphizing, except that humans are ascribed aspects of non-living thin gs. All of the discursive strategies of morphizing we have examined here appear to be particularly salient to and tractable in student’s disciplinary identity work. This is due to both the conceptual and affective affordances they provide for knowing and valuing different imagined worlds, worlds that students might ultimately realize as more inclusive, equitable and just.
