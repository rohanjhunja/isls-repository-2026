# The Evolution of a Design Thinking Course Through Designer-Researcher Collaboration

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study describes the development of a design thinking course created through collaboration between researchers and design practitioners in higher education. It addresses challenges to teaching design thinking, such as oversimplification and lack of guidance for diverse student groups, by incorporating real-world contexts and scaffolding. Using design-based research (DBR), the course was refined to teach interdisciplinary undergraduate students, with reflective practice guiding its evolution. The collaboration emphasized integrating professional insights and theoretical frameworks and led to adaptive scaffolding based on team progress and problem complexity. The study underscores the significance of researcher-practitioner collaboration in enhancing design thinking pedagogy in higher education.

## Introduction and background

To bridge the skills gap between higher education and industry demands, parti cularly in light of AI-driven changes, students must cultivate critical thinking and problem-solving abilities that are less susceptible to automation (Benhayoun et al., 2021; Edmondson, 2016). Integrating design thin king into higher education can prepare graduates for complex problem-solving in a rapidly evolving job market (Wolnicki et al., 2019). Students need help to embrace failure, understand implicit processes, and a dapt tonew ways of thinking (Micheli et al., 2018). Providing appropriate scaffolding and real-world contexts can help students navigate these challenges effectively (Herrington et al., 2009).

Design education must reflect the complexity of the real world (Cros s, 1982), requiring students to develop relevant cognitive and communication capabilities. To deepen their understanding, students benefit from observing experts in action and practicing divergent and convergent thinking (Boling et al., 2020). Involving experts enriches learning by offering real-world examples and insights, which help students grasp professional practices and societal interactions (Usenyuk-Kravchuk et al., 2023). Such collaborative, hands-on experiences prepare students with skills aligned with industry standards, transfor ming how design is learned and applied (McMahon & Bhamra, 2016).

While navigating real-world tasks, students require structured support. Wood, Bruner, and R oss (1976) defined scaffolding as the dynamic support that more knowledgeable others offer, facilitating learners' progression beyond their current capability level.  Scaffoldings enable students to learn from experiences and support the m throughout complex tasks (Tabak & Reiser, 2022). The scaffolding litera ture thus guides researchers and practitioners to support students' learning in real-world contexts effectively. Effective scaffolding implementation also requires clear communication of scientific knowledge between researchers and practitioners (Wright, 2010). Researchers can promote dialogue and collaboration to transfer knowl edge from theory to practice and facil itate new knowledge creation. Practitioners contribute by identifying practical  challenges and bringing their field experiences to inform the educational approach (Schaik, 2018). Su ch researcher-practitioner collaboration can significantly improve educational quality (Gredig et al., 2020). This paper describes the collaborative development and iterative refi nement of a design thinking course within a higher education setting over three semesters to address t hese challenges. Input for the course was provided by stakeholders, including students, four practitioner-instructors, and two researchers. Reflective practice (Schön, 1983) provided a foundation for iterative refinement, fostering a creative, collaborative environment essential for design education. Thus, this study seeks t o answer the question: How did professional designers as practitioners and researchers contribute to developi ng and delivering a design thinking course in higher education?

## Method

This study used design-based research (DBR) as an iterative and collaborative approach (McKen ney & Reeves,

2020) to develop and refine the design thinking course by systematically t esting, evaluating, and revising

instructional strategies over multiple semesters. Reflective practice (Schön, 1983) was essential for collaboration, with regular reflections on student progress and feedback. Collabora tion in this study took place between the researchers and practitioners, as seen in Figure 1. Researchers bro ught a systematic approach to reflection, while practitioners contributed field expertise, enhancing the learning experience. Figure 1 Practitioners and Researchers DBR promotes collaboration among researchers and practitioners, allow ing theoretical frameworks to be continuously tested and aligned with practical teaching and learn ing needs. This partnership enabled the researchers and practitioners to integrate theory-backed scaffolding frameworks (e.g., Richardson et al., 2022; vande Pol et al., 2010) and adapt them to actual course settings, creating a feedback loop between theory a nd practice. Additionally, DBR seeks to generate both practical solutions and theoretical insights. This study also aimed to provide revised design principles for designing more effective instructional strategies for teaching design thinking in higher education.

## Setting and participants

The course aims to teach design thinking to undergraduate students from various disciplines and is offered as an online elective course throughout the 2022-2023 Fall, 2022-2023 Spring, and 2023-2024 Fall semesters. The first author was one of the practitioner-instructors (Figure 1) who delivered the course during all three se mesters— each semester, around 25 students completed the course, comprising a n interdisciplinary group from design and architecture departments and various engineering, social sciences, and humanities disciplines. The course focused on group work to solve real-world problems using design thinking in an online setting. Students completed weekly tasks to advance in the design process and received feedback from instructors based on their progress. During the midterm, students presented their work in the problem area. For the final exam, they had to present their entire process, including both the problem and solution areas. Each semester, the course evolved based on theoretical insights, with changes aimed at improving student engagement and learning outcomes. ## Findings

Semester 1: "Dipping the Toes in the Water" The initial semester served as a foundational stage for the desig n thinking course. The research team and practitioners, informed by prior materials from former instructors, identified the issue of oversimplification due to the use of pre-made canvases. These ready-to-use templates led students to follow steps without understanding the purpose of design thinking, thus hindering deeper engagement with the pr ocess. To address this, the team decided to introduce the design process gradually, beginning with discussions on the purpose of each task before introducing the templates.

Since students came from various disciplines, non-design majors often required additional support for hands-on tasks (McLaughlin & Lodge, 2019). To support student motivation and engage ment (Delson, 2001), instructors allowed students to identify problems based on their interests.  However, at the end of the semester, evaluations revealed that while students gained initial exposure to des ign thinking, many faced challenges in selecting appropriately complex problems. Furthermore, not all stude nts could articulate their learning process, indicating the need for more personalized scaffolding and reflection methods. Reflection forms were developed using Richardson et al.'s (2022) framework of scaffolding types, which cat egorized scaffolding into conceptual, metacognitive, procedural, strategic, and motivational types. The focus on reflection allowed students to engage more deeply with their work, and the incorporation of feedback loops between students and instructors was emphasized. Howeve r, despite the benefits, challenges emerged: Not all problems required the same l evel of reflection or depth simultaneously, making task-based reflection questions less effective for some teams. It was a lso noted that pre-designed scaffolding worked effectively for initial tasks but did not fully accommodate the unique  challenges of each team’s evolving design process. The instructors needed additional support to provide adaptive scaffolding, which would better match each team's progress and needs.

Semester 3: "Reflective practice for scaffolding" By the third semester, instructors reflected on their teaching practices and identified the need for more adaptive scaffolding rather than over scaffolding. In response, instructors re placed predefined templates with reflective practices, such as creating personas and formulating "how-might-we" questions, encouraging students to independently define their needs.

Instructors also adapted their scaffolding techniques based on the specif ic needs of each team. The use of real-time prompts and tailored questions —such as, "Have we understood the team’s current level of understanding and process?" and "What scaffolding is crucial to support the  team’s progress?" —was a key component of adaptive scaffolding (vande Pol et al., 2010). This met hod provided more autonomy to students while ensuring that the scaffolding was responsive to their evolving design processes. While the thematic focus on sustainability (e.g., well-being, community) provided a broad framework, it was noted that some  teams struggled with the lack of structure, leading to slower starts and less depth in problem exploration. Thus, instructors recognized the need for further refinement in guiding teams through complex, open-ended problems. ## Discussions and implications

This study sought to refine a design thinking course for students coming from a range of disciplines by incorporating real-world contexts and scaffolding. Using a design-based research approach over three semesters, the study relied heavily on collaboratio n between researchers and practitioners. This collaborative approac h allowed for the continuous evolution of the course design based on ongoing obse rvations and reflections. The goal was to address common challenges in design thinking education, such as a  lack of guidance for students, oversimplification of the processes, and difficulty applying design t hinking to complex, real-world problems (Boling et al., 2020; Markauiste, 2010; McLaughlin & Lodge, 2019; Panke, 2019). The way the researchers and the design practitioners designed, deli vered, and evaluated the course illustrated evidence to overcome the challenges. They explored how to s caffold effectively for complex learning settings by learning more about scaffolding and improved the course design by incorporating adaptive scaffolding. As vande Pol and her colleagues (2010) argued for the importance of  context while providing scaffolding, the instructors considered how each team created their context by formi ng differe nt teams, selecting different problems, and tailoring their design process through the adaptive scaffolding integrated into the course design. For practitioners and academic leaders, this study underscores the importance of adopting flexible scaffolding frameworks that cater to the evolving needs of students. By incorporating real-world contexts and scaffolding that is reflective and responsive t o student progress, practitioners can create learning environment s that more effectively simulate the challenges of professional desi gn practice. Future research can evaluate the influence of scaffolding on students’ design cognition and design process.  These can help to validate the design principles and further contribute to design thinking pedagogy.
