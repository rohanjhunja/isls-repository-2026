# To Wait or Not to Wait: Adding to the Debate on Immediate Versus Delayed Feedback

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Prior work investigates the impact of feedback timing on math performance; however, when feedback is most effective remains unclear. We define immediate and delayed feedback and examine how timing affects student algebra performance. 1,629 students were randomly assigned to an immediate or delayed feedback condition, completed a two-hour intervention solving math problems in an online tutoring system, and received immediate or delayed feedback. We found overall learning gains, but no differences between the conditions.

## Objectives and theoretical framework

Feedback is commonly used across educational contexts. However, there have been contradictory findings as to when it is most effective to provide students with feedback (Fyfe et al., 2021). Feedback timing has been examined in labs (e.g., Brackbill et al., 1963), classrooms (e.g., Sturges, 1978), and online settings (e.g., Kehrer et al., 2013), making it difficult to compare the effects of timin g across different environments. Further, prior res earch has defined immediate and delayed feedback differently, creating a challenge to distill findings. Immediate feedback has been used in reference to feedback given post-a ttempt (Brackbill et al., 1963) as well as immediat ely after completing a problem set or test (Kulhavy & Anderso n, 1972). Delayed feedback has been used in reference to feedback delivered at some point after immediate feedback, such as a few seconds post-item (Lintz & Brackbill, 1966), immediately post-assignment (Sturges, 1978),  or minutes (Mason & Redmon, 1993) to months postassignment (Sassenrath et al.1968). Further, research on the effects of feedback timing has shown mixed results: studies have found differing results when comparing  the effects of immediate and delayed feedback (Keh rer et al., 2013). For example, Many Classes (Fyfe et al., 2021) compared the effects of immediate feedback, defined as immediately post-assignment, and delayed feedback, defined as one to five days post-assignment. Across  38 college classes and 2,081 participants, the authors did not find a significant effect of timing posttest assessments. We add to the ongoing debate by testing the effect of feedback timing in an online algebra intervention for middleschoolers. In the current study, we employed two ti mings of feedback: immediate and delayed. We define immediate feedback as hints and correctness feedback provide d immediately after a response to a problem and delayed feedback as correctness feedback provided immediat ely after completing an assignment. We pose the following questions:

1. Do students improve their mathematical understanding after completing four assignments?

2. Does immediate or delayed feedback on practice assignments impact students’ learning gains?

3. Does students’ prior knowledge moderate the effect of feedback timing?

## Methods, data sources, and materials

The sample is from a randomized controlled trial ru n in a district in the Southeastern US during the 2 020-2021 school year (Woodrow et al., in prep). We investiga te the effects of immediate vs. delayed feedback wi thin ASSISTments (Heffernan & Heffernan, 2014), an onlin e tutoring system. Our sample includes 855 seventh graders (51% immediate; 49% delayed) who started pr e-and end-of-semester assessments. Genders were 50 % male and 50% female. In terms of race/ethnicity, 52% were White, 27% Asian, 13% Hispanic/Latino, 8% other.

## Procedure

All study sessions were administered online in math  classrooms during instruction periods. Students wo rked individually at their own pace on their own device. The analyses focused on the assessment data at 2 time points: start of the semester, and end-of-semester after four 30-minute intervention sessions. Students in both conditions solved the same math problems in ASSISTments. The problems were adapted from three open-source curricula. During the intervention sessions, students had 25 minutes to complete the problems, and five minutes to review their performance on each question. In the immediate feedback condition, the hints and correctness feedback were ICLS2022 Proceedings 1910 © ISLS available during and after problem-solving. As students answered the question s, they received immediate correctness feedback after each answer attempt. If students answered the question correctly on the fir st attempt without using any hints, they received a green chec k as correctness feedback. If students entered an i ncorrect answer or used a hint, they received an X. Students received this correctness feedback after each answer attempt and had to enter the correct answer to advance to t he next problem. In the delayed feedback condition, the hints and correctness feedback were available only in the session report after assignment completion. Students could not request hints and did not receive correctness feedback during the intervention sessions; they simply answered the problem then advanced to the next question. When students submitted their answer to a question, a blue dot appeared on the screen indicating that the system recorded their answer. The algebra knowledge pre-assessment consisted of 10 items from a previously validated measure that tapped into conceptual understanding, procedural kn owledge, and flexibility with a mirrored end-of-sem ester assessment (Star et al., 2015). Each item was scored as correct (1) or incorrect (0). The total score on the end-ofsemester assessment was included as the outcome and the score on the pre-assessment was included as a covariate in analyses.

## Results

RQ1. Across both conditions, students’ end-of-semester assessment scores were, on average, 0.63 points (95% CI: [0.48,0.77]) higher than their pre-assessment scores, t854=8.5, p<.001. RQ2. When condition was the sole predictor, a regression model suggests a small benefit of immediate feedback on students’ end-of-semester scores (0.248±0.260), p<.10. When including students’ pre-assessment scores, fifth-grade state standardized achievement scores, English to Speakers of Other Languages status, Early Intervention Program status, and Individualized Education Program status, gender, and overall academic achievement (above or below grade level) as covariates, the effect of being assigned to immediate feedback was somewhat smaller (0.195±0.248; t723=1.5, p=.12). Although we cannot confidently determine the sign of the average effect of feedback timing on students’ end-of-semester assessment scores, the magnitude of the effect was small (d<.17).

RQ3. The interaction between condition and pretest scores was tested with the covariates, and the model shows that the Condition × Pretest interaction (0.062±0.088) was not statistically significant, p=.169.

## Conclusions and significance

Aligning with the mixed findings in the literature,  our results showed that effects of feedback timing  on performance—if any—was small in magnitude. However, we did find that students improved from pre-assessment to end-of-semester assessment across conditions, suggesting that both feedback interventions might be beneficial.
