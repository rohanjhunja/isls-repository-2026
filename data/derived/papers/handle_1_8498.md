# “With Statistics, That’s Supposed to Take Bias Away”: Divergent Teacher Views on Engaging Students With Race in Data Sets

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examined what pedagogical potential teachers see in data sets that include race as a variable. Through interviews with 11 secondary teachers, we show a range of views about the role race plays with respect to students’ opportunities to engage with data analysis. From analysis of teachers’ noticings of pedagogical potential and justifications for what data sets they would prefer in a constrained choice task, we identify areas where the learning sciences can support teacher learning and curriculum design with race-inclusive data sets.

## Introduction

Recently, there have been a number of urgings for y outh to learn more about data science as part of th eir K-12 education (e.g., Bargagliotti et al., 2020). Our concern is to explore how teachers whom we anticipate are among the most likely to be enlisted in teaching data sci ence – namely, secondary statistics and mathematics  – think about the pedagogical potential of various data sets (Lee & Delaney, 2021). As data sets are already abundant but also being continuously generated, we are examining how teachers judge the suitability and usability of data sets in their teaching. Developing a model of teacher th inking about the pedagogical potential of different  data sets could help inform future efforts to design curricula, curate data for pedagogical use, recommend possible uses of data in the classroom, and enhance teacher professional learning experiences. In the early stages of research shared in this paper, our aim has been to explore how teachers think about teaching with data sets that prominently feature race. In the United States, where we are based, race is a complex social construction that is deeply connected to longstanding issues of power, privilege, and injustice. These are topics that the learning sciences, particularly in North America, are actively discussing (Booker & Esmonde, 2016). While our work directly speaks to challenges in the US, we believe it still has implications for other nations as matters of equity and injustice are concerns that transcend geopolitical boundaries. Our primary ques tion in this paper is: What considerations are stat istics teachers making regarding matters of race when appraising the pedagogical potential for quantitative data sets?

## Theoretical Perspectives

This work is based on the premise that, while popular discourse would suggest otherwise, data are not objective. They are the product of humandecisions, subject to  their own biases. They are markers of the social w orld rife with matters of unevenly distributed power, injustice, and oppression. Enthusiasm about what the potential of data science is rooted in “imagined objectivity” of data (Benjamin, 2016). With respect to race and data, learning scientists  have observed important opportunities where teachers can engage students. Philip et al. (2016) proposed the field attend to racial-ideological micro-contestations as a space for conversations about race with data. These are social interactions where the meanings and consequences of race are raised, contested, and negotiated. In P hilip et al.’s work, this took the form of dialogue  in a class viewing data about movie rentals in different regio ns of a large, segregated city. Their analysis show ed how a specific classroom teacher needed to make immediate decisions on how to respond to the topic of race as it was raised in classroom discussion and was unable to us e that moment to navigate students through a potent ially powerful discussion involving both data and racial literacies. This is a challenge noted by other learning scient ists. Enyedy & Mukhopadyay (2007) described a design experiment to provide students with culturally-relevant mathematics pedagogy. Students were intimately familiar with inequities represented in the geospatial data that Enyedy & Mukhopadyay brought to a high school classroom such that the utility of the mathematics being taug ht was unclear. The design experiment made prominen t the tensions in goals and norms of their intended cultu rally-relevant pedagogy and those of their mathemat ics pedagogy. Reconciling those tensions is complex des ign and teaching work. Some cases model promising potential new approaches (Rubel et al., 2017). Still, there is much to be examined, especially as it r elates to the prospect of high school statistics teachers taking on the work of teaching data science.

## Research Participants

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1401 © ISLS Eleven high school mathematics and statistics teach ers from public, private, charter, and magnet schoo ls were recruited for this exploratory study through existi ng contacts and snowball sampling. All teachers tau ght in ‘majority minority’ schools where White students co nsisted of less than 50% of the student population.  These teachers had an average of 16 years’ experience, ranging from 4.5 to 35 years, and some taught other courses (e.g., computer science) or held other professional roles (e.g., coach, administrator). 3 of the 11 identified as teachers of Color, and 5 of the 11 identified as female.

## Procedure

Teachers participated in 60-minute interviews that were videorecorded on the Zoom conferencing platfor m. During the interview, teachers were presented two p airs of publicly provided side-by-side data sets (F igure 1) regarding the same topics. Of the two pairs of data  sets presented, one set was on policing and the ot her set contained technology company employment data. Teachers viewed four data sets in total, and all contained racial variable columns, with some mixing race and ethnicity. Teachers were asked to think aloud and comment on what they noticed when viewing them. Then the interviewe r posed a series of questions, designed to target t eachers’ perceptions of student interest in the data, potential elements of student struggle with or about the data, authentic appearance of the data, and pedagogical affordances  of the data. Each viewing session concluded with t he teacher’s decision and explanation as to which data set they would choose to teach with, and why.

## Data analysis

The interviews were transcribed and subject to multiple coding passes, parsed into two analytical phases. The first phase focused upon which data set was selected and why. Here, we analyzed teachers’ expressed preferen ces toward a particular data set, observed when and how  teachers referred to race as a rationale for selec tion (pedagogical or contextual), and identified a set of 12 rationale-based codes across teachers (e.g., “Opportunities for inquiry about race,” “Examine a trend over time,”). The second phase examined in what ways teacher s conceptualized race when the data sets were present ed. Specifically, we explored the degree to which r ace was framed as a pedagogical concern for students’ inquiry of data and learning about contemporary issues o f power and injustice. The authors met for joint data review sessions, focusing on what was being discussed in a data set and how social phenomena were being evoked by the teacher in relation to their learning goals for students.

## Results

How do teachers engage with matters of race in their judgments of data sets? Teachers varied in the extent to which they centered race in their noticings and its role in student learning. Some articulated the importance for students to learn ab out contemporary social issues while others express ed pedagogical affordances provided by race (for examp le, the ability to make statistical comparisons by racial subgroupings). Others expressed hesitation or resis tance toward the centrality of race within the data. The following sections represent teachers’ divergent responses toward race and pedagogical potential of data sets. Race as essential to consider For three teachers (one Black, one Latinx, and one White), their final choice of a data set was supported by their perception that data could better enable race to be a discussion topic informed. For the two teachers of color, this appeared for only the policing data. For the other teacher, who is White, it appeared across both poli cing and employment data. As was the case across teachers’ judgments about data sets, there was much complexity in what ICLS2022 Proceedings 1402 © ISLS pedagogical value was recognized. To illustrate, we provide an excerpt from Gavin, a Black male teache r in an urban school, who elaborated upon his preference for selecting policing data set 2: “So, if I had to pick one, I think once again, the one on the right [data set 2] is just more interesting. I think it’s more mature, and I would definitely do it with a mature set of students. But I think the conversation doesn’t have to be White versus Black. I think it’s important that we do talk about Native Americans, that we talk about Hispanics, that we talk about Asians. I feel like our society is so Black and White sometim es that’s what we focus on.”  (Gavin, interview transcript, p. 22-23) Key aspects of this portion of Gavin’s comment tha t we noted include: the data pertained to a topic that would be more interesting to students, (perhaps relatedly) they engaged topics requiring certain levels of maturity, and that they would support the teacher’s desire to  have students develop expansive, rather thandicho tomous, recognitions of how race is present in society. Gavin further expanded on his preference for data set 2 with: “But here, we have a diverse amount of crimes, we have a lot of data about different groups of people, and, murder, rape, prostitution, sex offenses, we don’t talk about these things, but these things are real. And when are you ever going to tal k to kids about these things?” (Gavin, interview transcript, p. 23) Here, we noted that Gavin was attending to breadth and diversity in the data. The data had exploratory possibilities with numerous possible categorical values to examine. Being under-engaged as a topic (“w e don’t talk about these things”) and relevant “these thing s are real” also factored into Gavin’s justificatio n. As will be illustrate below, this quality and its use for teaching varied. Several other teachers in our study cited the association between race and crime as a reason to avoid data set 2 because such topics featured prominentl y could unknowingly trigger some students or reinforce specific negative discourses regarding race and crime. However, with teachers like Gavin in mind, we could envision that valued data sets would have a breadth of cate gories to consider, would not shy away from complex social concerns, and might be made even more helpful if they were coupled with some recommended analyses and variable  selections that Gavin could use with his students to explore and challenge assumptions and narratives that might exist around the topics of race and policing. Race as a useful variable While Gavin’s statement that data set 2 provided affordances toward necessary classroom discussions about race as a general societal construct, far more teachers in our study (6 of the 11: 2 for policing, 2 for employment, 2 for both) emphasized pedagogical opportunities the race  variables provided with respect to data inquiry mo re than the underlying matters of injustice that can appear in the data. In these teachers’ purview, race as a variable might lead students to better query the data and make comparisons between subgroups. The following utterance by Brent (White male teacher) illustrates one such instance: I think what you have a great opportunity here to l ook at running your conditionals two ways. Which is, let’s say I have side-by-side pie charts.  Do I want to compare White people to nonWhite people? Or, do I want to compare the ethnicity breakdown of murder versus, what’s a nonviolent crime...property crime? … So, I think in that way, there’s two different types of questions you could ask. You could compare one ethnicity to a nother, versus comparing one crime to another.” (Brent, interview transcript, p. 19-20).

As Brent describes, the provision of two categorica l variables (race and offense) affords students the opportunity to examine conditional variables, data displays of conditional variables, and appropriate graphical representations of conditional variables. In addition, a deeper investigation could be conducted by “running your conditional two ways,” permitting students to learn various strategies and interpretations of data arrangements in association to statistical questions. Brent seemed comfortable seeing the ways in which common statistics topics could be engaged, yet this utterance did not show active consideration of the challenging history and the emotional dimensions of race. Teacher educators may see this as illustrating a need to help Brent and others lea rn how to recognize matters of injustice and how to thoughtfu lly and sensitively engage students so that they ca n see statistical ideas without disconnecting from the more complex social injustices reflected in the data. Race as too sensitive to be paired with student learning ICLS2022 Proceedings 1403 © ISLS One other judgment of racial variables came in the form of anticipating students’ sensitivities.  Two teachers described words within data sets, or more broadly, the presence of race paired with offenses in policing data set 2, with negative valence as they envisioned how their particular students might feel about data. Acknowledging race was something teachers cold envision as being too uncomfortable. For example, when Iris (White fe male teacher) was asked where her students would struggle, she explained: “Every person has a different story and perspective and background, and is maybe triggered by this information more than others. When you’re talking a bout sensitive topics like this, I think that people are going to be triggered. How do you come at this from an unbiased perspective? Biased. I think your bias...does bias have an effect on how we look at data? So yeah, can you totally take your bias out? With statistics, that’s supposed to take bias away, you’re just looking at numbers. But is that something we need to look at...this could be sensitive to a person or group of people.” (Iris, interview transcript, p.19) Iris suspected that “people are going to be trigger ed” with the policing data. Iris’s utterance and justification reflects tensions regarding bias and objectivity in our thinking about data. According to Iris, data – “just looking at numbers” -  should take bias away.  At the same time, Iris is aware human bias does ex ist. Her statement seems to suggest that the bias may not be  within the data but rather in the interpreter. Rec all that our perspective is that data are social constructions t hat are made by humans with biases. This view that data are objective may be the type of issue that needs to be  explored more deeply with teachers. Seeing bias as  residing only on the side of the human who views data runs t he risk of delegitimizing the experiences and conce rns of students and limit teacher supported classroom examination of why the data look the way they do, both in their creation and in what may be inferred. At the same time, productively engaging with data (while also recognizing that bias exists within data) is another matter that teachers need support to explore.

## Discussion

While it is a modestly-sized study, this examination of teacher judgments about data sets where race is a prominent variable suggests there is visible variation in how teachers think about race and data. The variation among even our small sample of teachers regarding if and how race should be engaged in data sets invites questions about how to best support teacher growth – through professional learning and with designed curriculum. Notably, the teachers in this study were attending to both their conjectures of data science instructi on and perceptions of race within data sets simultaneo usly. Our findings do not yet take teachers’ uncert ainties of data science (Manz & Su árez, 2018) into consideration. Future analyses will  develop a more nuanced coding scheme that considers the interaction of race, data  science, and teachers’ inherent uncertainty when m aking inthe-moment judgments. Yet, our work thus far offers preliminary insights to how teachers consider race in data sets in relation to their students’ opportunities t o learn. In the learning sciences, important learni ng interactions such as racial-ideological micro-contestations are beginning to be recognized. The work here may help us identify means to ensure that teachers can make such moments  thoughtful and informative about the nature of dat a and the social world of which they are a part.
