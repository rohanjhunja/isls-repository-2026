# Collaborating with Generative AI for Learning?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) tools, driven by large language models (LLMs), are increasingly explored for their potential in educational contexts. However, significant concerns remain regarding their efficacy, cognitive impacts, and ethical implications. The Computer Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) community faces critical questions regarding the broader implications of this technology for the field. While GenAI offers advanced opportunities for collaborative learning through conversational interactions and other functions, research in this area is still in its early stages. This symposium presents five contributions that explore the opportunities, challenges, and initial findings of integrating GenAI into educational settings. The goal is to provide evidence-based recommendations for practice and identify key research directions and challenges for the future of CSCL in the context of GenAI.

## Introduction

Tools like Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenA I), which utilize large language models (LLMs), pro duce text and various forms of content in response to prompts for a wide range of tasks. This technological advancement is raising significant discussion and emerging research questions about the opportunities and challenges it brings to educational technology research and practice (Giann akos et al., 2024; Bond  et al., 2024). For example, ongoing research suggests GenAI’s capabilities could improv e several teaching and learning practices, such as learning design, regulation of learning, automated generatio n of content, or the provision of feedback. At the same time, the research community emphasizes the need to deepl y understand the efficacy, epistemic depen dency, further cognitive impacts, ethical and environmental consid erations, and pedagogical validity of these practic es. Indeed, it is possible to envision dangers in hastily adopting GenAI tools in education without those careful considerations (Giannakos et al., 2024; Bond  et al., 2024). In alignment with the ISLS Annual Meeting (h osting the CSCL conference) “Educating for world-making: Envisioning and enacting sustainable soluti ons to global crises”, we see that these dangers pose a threat for a global c risis in education. There is a responsibility for t he research community to envision and steer towards solutions t hat promote education for positive world-making in an age of AI.

In this context, and because prompts for interactio n with GenAI can be written by humans, there is a pressing question that clearly interpellates the CS CL field: Is it possible to collaborate with Generative AI fo r productive learning? This question challenges the traditional assumption s of CSCL, where collaboration occurs between humans (human-human interactions) with computers serving to media te interactions, design and orchestrate scaffolds to guide collaboration struct ures, or analyze the interactions that result (Cress et al., 2021). Yet, the question also opens up opportunities to expand the field by exploring new roles for computer applications in supporting learning through conversational inter action. It invites exploration into varied scenario s for interactions with generative machines, moving beyond or being different to the more controlled (rule-based, nongenerative) approach of conversational agents used so far in the field to, for example, support produc tive talk (Kumar & Rosé, 2010; de Araujo et al., 2024). Sharples (2023) describes GenAI as having the potential to enhance this social learning process through assisting in setting shared goals, collaborating on tasks, exploring possibilities, and engaging in discussions to reach agreements. Ra ther than viewing these interactions as mere exchan ges of prompts and responses, they can be understood as a social process of exploration and dialogue. In his view, Sharples (2023) sees GenAI can play a v ariety of social roles within learning environments, such as socratic opponent, collaboration coach, possibility engine, co-designer, exploratorium, and storyteller. More rese archers also anticipate potential in leveraging LLMs to sup port collaboration in educational settings, while at the same time calling also for a careful reflection of their  ethical implications (Tan, Chen, and Chua, 2023; C ress & Kimmerle, 2023; Thierfelder et al., 2023). However,  there is still very limited research that studies learning scenarios in which argumentative dialogue among hum ans and GenAI tools can be achieved or supported wi th appropriate prompts or GenAI functions (Koyuturk et  al., 2023). Those first studies are starting to il luminate the possibilities and limits of the promises envisaged in thoughtful designed scenarios  where GenAI acts a collaborative learning partner (Gutiérrez -Ferré et al., 2024), feedback provider (Pahi et al., 2024; Florido et al., 2024a), and support for discussion through note-taking (Sato et al., 2024). Yet, the CSCL community  has not articulated a coordinated statement about how those scenarios advance or further redefine the field or if there are other broader implications.

It is clear that this line of research is still in early stages and, given the risks of hastily adopti ng GenAI tools in education, there is a responsibility for t he CSCL community to offer a response from the fiel d that can guide evidence-based practice a nd directions for future research. This symposium b rings together five contributions that discuss current perspectives answering the question of whether collaborating with GenAI offers opportunities for learning. The contributions inclu de descriptions of current projects and studies and  discuss the challenges faced and lessons learned. The outcome o f the symposium is to draft a list of recommendatio ns for practice, along with a list of "grand challenges" in GenAI for CSCL, aimed at guiding future research. Contribution #1: GenAI-supported interdisciplinary research practices Jun Oshima, Tsubasa Minematsu, Kana Matsueda, Shotaro Naganuma Perspective Interdisciplinary research practices stem from the social need to solve complex problems (e.g., climat e change, infectious diseases) (Latour, 2023; Repko & Szostak, 2020). While scholars often reported that interdi sciplinary education programs are advantag eous for students in higher education to develop th eir expertise to work productively with their team members to create new knowledge (e.g., Warkentien et al., 2022), the foll owing critical questions have not been thoroughly address ed. Markauskaite et al. (2024) articulated the three research questions: “What characterizes students’ productive  interdisciplinary engagement?” “What kinds of resourcefulness enable them to contribute constructively to interdisciplinary teamwork?” “How can we design for productive learning across and beyond disciplines?” (p. 2).  The third research question is the most critical challenge for educators who design interdisciplinar y education programs. The question should be approa ched from the following two perspectives. First, in desi gning the programs, learning should be considered t he knowledge-creation practice (Paavola & Hakkaranien, 200 5). It is assumed that students with diverse experti se (Repko & Szostak, 2020) are engaged in “what is not yet there” (Engeström, 2016), e.g., interdisciplinary research proposals. Second, the knowledge-creation practice by interdisciplinary research needs to “break(ing) boundaries between disciplines” (Kali, 2024). While studies ha ve examined interdisciplinary education programs fr om the boundary-crossing perspective, their implementation is still  challenging because students’ diverse disciplinary backgrounds should be considered (e.g., Muukkonen & Kajamaa, 2024). Research conducted Naganuma et al. (2024) implemented generative artif icial intelligence (GenAI) as a broker to support d octoral students from various fields in developing interdisciplinary research themes by crossing field boundaries. In their pilot study, Naganuma et al. utilized ChatGPT 4.0 to generate five interdisciplinary research themes based on the research abstracts provided by group members. These  themes were then offered to students as resources for developing their ideas. A discourse analysis of 38 groups (n = 56)  revealed that relatively small groups successfully used the GenAI-supported resources to improve their ideas. The rea sons behind these results were discussed as students began their idea improvement by considering all the possibilities of themes given by GenAI but not by selecting promising ideas within 45 minutes. To address these issues, we refined the design as follows. We encouraged students to evaluate the promisingnes s of their ideas with seven themes generated by Gen AI within 70 minutes using Research Assistant GPT developed by Dify. The findings indicated that eight of the nine groups improved their proposed ideas through discou rse with GenAI in 2024, compared to only 13 out of 38 groups in the pilot study (χ² = 8.802, df = 1, p = 0.003).

## Discussion (challenges, lessons learned)

The findings revealed that even doctoral students h ad difficulty improving their ideas beyond their di sciplines (i.e., interdisciplinary research practice), and in struction of the promisingness judgment was product ive in facilitating their interdisciplinary idea improvement. After their promisingness judgment, they need to “improve” their ideas. We must develop how GenAI would suppor t students’ engagement in idea improvement through collaborative discourse. In our contribution, we de monstrate our developing  GPT agent collaborating with students in their interdisciplinary research practice.

Contribution #2: Hybrid collaboration – learning from 20 years of CSCL Ulrike Cress Perspective ChatGPT and other generative AI tools can and should be used as collaborative learning partners. Despite lacking understanding or conceptual knowledge, these tools enable human-like interaction and facilitate hybrid knowledge construction.

Research conducted Our approach is firmly rooted in foundational CSCL theories, particularly collaborative writing (Scard amalia & Bereiter, 1987) and collaborative knowledge construction (Cress & Kimmerle, 2008). Scardamalia and Bereiter’s distinction between knowledge telli ng and knowledge transforming provides a key concep tual framework. It suggests that novice writers often default to straightforward knowledge telling, while experienced writers engage in the more complex process of knowledge transformi ng. This latter process involves iterating between content and rhetorical elements, resulting in coherent text s and conceptual growth on the writer’s side. Trans formations of this kind, supported by self-explanation prompts and argumentation structures, a re vital for meaningfu l learning. They also provide the foundation for successful interaction with AI as a collaborative learning partner. In collaborative knowledge building, it is clear th at arguments, counterarguments, and contexts drive collective understanding. This understanding is str uctured and refined over time. We propose that know ledge construction can also occur as cognitive syste ms interact with AI systems, following the co-evolution model of Cress and Kimmerle (2008). Prompts and AI outputs s timulate cognitive “irritations” encouraging users to question, clarify, and restructure their knowledge and use the AI’s outputs. This mode l is based on Piaget’s concept of socio-cognitive conflict, which states that mismatches be tween expectations and outputs drive cognitive adjustments. While AI lacks self-awareness, its responses, shaped by probabilistic l anguage models, undoubtedly evoke productive cognitive challenges that aid in learning.

## Discussion (challenges, lessons learned)

The most efficient use of generative AI is through an interaction format that goes beyond simply provi ding questions. Users must enable active, constructive dialogues with AIs. The ICAP framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014) is the ideal model for categorizing lear ning engagement from passive to interactive. To rea ch the highest levels of engagement, learners must be able to co-construct knowledge with AI. This requires treating  it as an information source and a dialogic partner within a structured collaborative pro cess. To refine these engagement strategies, ethnographic and experimental studies o n human-AI interaction are essential. They must ensure that AI’s associative responses support cognitive growth  on the user’s side rather than hinder it (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023).

Contribution #3: Facilitating mindful engagement with Generative AI in knowledge building Bodong Chen, Xinran Zhu, Fernando Díaz del Castillo Perspective Since ChatGPT’s debut in November 2022, the rapid d evelopment and deployment of generative AI tools ha ve impacted many social sectors including education. A s we navigate the rapid shifts, two key points emer ge. First, because AI is poised to transform society, students should develop necessary knowledge and skills to thrive in  the changing environment (Leander & Burriss, 2020). Sec ond, the new wave of GenAI is also impacting human creative endeavors in various domains; as a result, it challenges traditional domains of human creativity that were once thought untouchable for AI (Vinchon et al., 2023). It raises new questions on the aims of education, including what is still worth teaching and how things should be taught in an AI-mediated society. Along with other scholars, we argue that education needs to proactively engage students in building meaningful, ethical relationships with AI that may augment student thinking and creativity (Kasneci et al., 2023). It is important to create opportunities for students to interact with GenAI mindfully and authentically so they candevelop AI literacy while forging partnerships with  AI for learning and creative pursuits. Bearman and  Ajjawi

(2023) argue that AI is not defined by its underlying technology but “the contextually bound relationship between

the person and the technology” (p. 4). These argume nts move beyond an AI-centered view to consider humandispositions and competencies essential for critica l engagement with AI (Markauskaite et al., 2022). S uch engagement needs to be mindful, in that the learner goes through effortful, metacognitive processes of configuring the human –technology partnership (Salomon et al., 1991). It n eeds to be also reflective, so that students understand how AI-enabled technology can alter their activity and can choose how AI is incorporated in learning (Markauskaite et al., 2022).

Research conducted Informed by the perspectives above, we conducted a study to integrate GenAI in a high-school classroom that followed a pedagogy named Knowledge Building (KB; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2022). In a nutshell, KB aims to foster a knowledge community where studen ts are positioned as epistemic agents who are respo nsible for their own ideas and work together to advance their commun ity knowledge (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2022). To KB, students’ epistemic agency needs to remain front-and-center when introducing intelligent technologies. I n this study, we integrated GenAI in students’ knowledge b uilding to achieve two goals: (a) integrating AI to  support student creative work and, by doing so, (b) facilitating students’ AI literacy. To achieve these goals, we formed a design partnership with a teacher to experiment with concrete strategies of aligning GenAI with knowledg e building discourse moves. We engaged high school students in not only using GenAI – in particular ChatGPT – in learning but also interrogating multiple ways Ch atGPT could support (or hinder) their work. We inve stigated how students incorporated ChatGPT in their knowledg e building, focusing on dimensions such as concrete  use cases, processes, challenges, and coping strategies. We also examined how students  developed AI literacy, in areas including AI mechanisms, strengths, weaknesse s, risks, societal implications, and human-AI relationship. Research findings based on student interviews nuanc ed processes of using GenAI in the classroom. Learn ing became harder, according to their report, due to extra supply of AI-generated content and additional work of factchecking. Research also revealed students’ insights in AI risks and implications despite limited knowledge of AI mechanisms.

## Discussion (challenges, lessons learnt)

Our study aligns with the broader vision of meaning fully integrating GenAI in education, aiming to fos ter AI literacy and augment student creative work by carefully configuring student-AI relationships to achieve  particular goals in knowledge building pro cesses. As AI is increasingly introduced to educati on, it is pivotal to carefully negotiate the distribution of agency and labor betw een AI and students. This study offers both empiric al insights in student experience and practical guidance on how to maintain students’ epistemic agency in the KB context. This study also suggests the need for creating opportunities for students to build understanding of GenAI and reflect on their personal relationship with GenAI. In the study, students went through effortful, metacognitive processes of interacting with Chat GPT for knowledge building. When introducing GenAI to the classroom, educators need to carve out dedicated space for stu dents to develop AI literacy and nurture mindful pr actice. Importantly, robust professional learning opportuni ties need to be created for teachers to take on thi s challenge while leveraging their existing repertoire of expertise. Contribution #4: Learners’ collaboration with AI in enhancing peer feedback literacy Wenli Chen, Qianru Lyu, Xuanyu Chen, Lishan Zheng Perspective Peer feedback constitutes a reciprocal loop where learners provide, receive, and act upon reviews of each other’s work (Hattie & Timperley, 2007), fostering both lea rning performance and high-order skills (Chen et al., 2024). However, its complex nature ch allenges learners’ feedback literacy as they progre ss along the “Ladder of Feedback” (Perkins, 2003). Integrating AI into peer  feedback mitigates the limitations of traditional approaches by offering real-time and personalized information, empowering learn ers to craft feedback or revise their work based on feedback received (Darvishi et al., 2024).  Recent advancements in generative AI have transfor med its role from an automated feedback source (i.e., AI-generated feedback) to an interactive, collaborative partner (i.e., AI-supported feedback) throughout the feedback process. While prior research has predominantly focused on “AI as a tool” to promote learning performance (Milano et al., 2023), questions remain regarding how learn ers perceive and collaborate with “AI as a partner” in enhancing feedback and its effectiveness compared w ith traditional peer feedback. This study aims to explo re AI’s role across various feedback stages and pro vide a comprehensive understanding of how AI can be better designed to support students’ peer feedback activity. Research conducted This study examines the role of different modes of AI support (GPT-led; student-led) at led; no GPT support) at feedback loop stages: feed-up (where am I going?), feedback (where I am?), and  act-upon (where to gonext?) (Carless & Boud, 2018). AI support is tailored thro ugh a series of role-defining prompts, delivering either direct (GPT-led) or indirect (student-led) responses during the feedback process. There are two research questions:

1. Does AI augment learners' peer feedback in rubri c understanding (feed-up), providing feedback

(feedback), and feedback act-upon (feedforward)?

2. What is the impact of different modes of AI supp ort (GPT-led, student-led, no GPT support) on

students’ peer feedback engagement and feedback literacy? This study employs an experimental design approach.  For the feed-up stage, students first conduct a rubric-based self-assessment; the experimental group receives AI supp ort, while the control group works independently. In the feedback stage, the participants in the experimental group provide feedback direct or indirect AI support, and the participants in the control group are unaided. For the act-upon stage, students refine their work based on feedback: the experimental group revise th eir work with direct (GPT-led) or indirect (student-led) AI support while the control group revises their work unaided.

## Discussion (challenges, lessons learnt)

This study seeks to illuminate the dynamics of huma n-AI collaboration in facilitating the peer feedback loop. A significant challenge lies in the structured design  of AI prompts, which, though effective in ensuring  task alignment, restricted learners’ auto nomy to explore varied strategies when interacting with AI as a collaborative partner. This rigidity may have inadvertently const rained their ability to cultivate a more adaptable feedback literacy. To mitigate this, this study offers learn ers the flexibil ity to select their preferred feedback source (ChatGPT or peer) and determine how to integrate AI  responses into their work. This approach underscor es the potential of generative AI to scaffold the feedback process, wherein the leader-follower dynamic in GPT/studentled collaborations highlights the synergy of augmented intelligence, optimizing the strengths of both humans and AI to achieve more effective collaboration.

Contribution #5: GenAi in CSCL scripting and orchestration: tensions in the learning design Davinia Hernandez-Leo, Emily Theophilou Perspective The opportunities and challenges, including risks a nd ethical implications, that GenAI brings to educa tional scenarios (Giannakos et al., 2024) pose a series of  tensions that need to be considered when making de cisions about how to integrate GenAI in teaching and learning practices (Hernández-Leo, 2024). One of the challenges is the need for prior knowledge and critical thinking when interacting with text-generating GenAI based on LLMs. Yet, this situation is not completely new to scenarios being tackled by CSCL. Collaboration among humans share the dependency on prior knowledge and skills in the  design of the best possible scenarios to trigger p roductive learning (Chen et al., 2018). Like any partnership requires effort, learning with technology such as those based on GenAI is no exception (Salomon et al., 1991). This process demands active, mindful engagement from the learner while the technology responds dynamically, in a kin d of Human-AI collaborative and interactive learning environment. When humans are not sufficiently skill ed, free collaboration does not lead to productive learning and there is the need for the teacher to monitor and regulate the process or to design scripts that guide such process (Radkowitsch et al., 2020; Dillenbourg et al., 2009; Amarasinghe et al., 2020). These two well-known approaches in CSCL can be also applied to scenarios where ther e is collaboration with CSCL. Yet, the risks and et hical implications are so relevant when considering GenAI, that the effectiveness of the learning  designs considering the use of GenAI in the set design should be significant and outweigh the costs (Hernández-Leo, 2024). We need evidence.

Research conducted We have conducted research in the following lines that align with our described perspective: a) GenAI and CSCL scripting, b) GenAI and classroom orchestration, c)  supporting responsible decision making in learning  design integrating or/and collaborating with GenAI.

Regarding a), inspired by Sharples' (2023) concept of human-AI educational interactions as social processes of conversation and exploration, we have investigated the potential of GenAI as a collaborative peer in synchronous CSCL environments, aiming to sti mulate discussions and critical thinking skills. We  have investigated the effects of a GenAI agent within the CSCL PyramidApp scripting tool (Gutiérrez-Ferré, A. et al., 2024; Sánchez -Reina et al., 2025). PyramidApp scaffolds knowledge  building from individual answers to increasingly larger groups, including peer rating t asks before new increasingly larger groups are crea ted. This script enables users to engage in dialogic interact ions that support knowledge construction while guid ing them through structured stages of the activity. A GenAI agent based on GPT (versions of ChatGPT 3 and 4 included in the research) was used to participate in the script  acting as another peer, submitting individual answ ers, rating others’ answers, and participating in the knowle dge-building discussion.   With a within-subjects quasi-experimental design, we evaluated the effect of Gen AI chatbot in student knowledge building and online interactions. The research involved nine PyramidApp  activities, 295 students and we evaluated the effect of the GenAI chatbot activity in 105 conversational chat r ooms corresponding to the PyramidApp phases in the nine scripts. Most tasks were open-ended (analysis of cases) in higher education conte xts. The findings revealed that the GenAI chatbot provides useful feedback as students rate the chatbot’s answers higher than their peers’ answers (MChatbot = 4.11, MStudents = 3.91). The analysis of final answer ac curacy revealed a significant improvement in precision for initial submissions when the GenAI  agent was participating in the groups. However, de spite the observed trends toward reduced confusion and enhanc ed precision in group submissions, statistical sign ificance was not achieved in these cases. Regarding the effe ct of chatbot integration in comput er-mediated behavior, our findings showed that students in chatbot-enabled rooms produced fewer but longer and more su bstantive messages, reflecting a shift toward more deliberate  and meaningful exchanges. This behavior suggests t hat the chatbot might serve as a stabilizing influence, gui ding students toward goal-oriented communication. However, the decrease in total message count per room raises  questions about whether some participants may defe r to the chatbot's input rather than engaging fully themselves. Indeed, our additional research in scripts to support learning processes involving creativity suggests that while that collaboration with GenAI in problem-solving tasks can enhance productivity (generation of more ideas, and  more diverse ideas), it may n egatively affect cognitive processes such as fantasy (submitted). Other scenar ios of interaction with agents where the agent is n ot acting as another peer but takes other roles, bring more ligh t into the possibilities for script design incorpor ating GenAI. For example, we explored the use of an agent to deliver storytelling elements as part of “narrative scripts”, which expose students to different scenarios to improve their socio-emotional skills (Theophilou, Lobo et al., 2024). Regarding b), we have preliminary explored the role  of GenAI to participate in the co-orchestration of problem-solving classrooms (Florido & Hernández -Leo, 2024a). Through a web-based classroom orchestration tool, extending the Lantern approach (Florido & Hernández-Leo, 2024b; Alavi & Dillenbourg, 2012), students can request the support of the teacher while at the  same time asking their questions also to GenAI. Th e study gathers data from students’ perspectives (vocationa l training) and their interactions w ith a web-based problemsolving classroom orchestration tool augmented with generative AI capabilities (48 students, 2 sessions, problem solving). The results indicated that students’ engagement with GenAI integrated into the classroom orchestration tool enhanced their active participation and provided imm ediate responses to their doubts, in collaboration with teachers who oversee and complement student support  during classroom sessions. Results also showed tha t students see potential benefits and drawbacks  in the use of GenAI and we also saw the relevance o f guiding the crafting of well-formulated prompts in alignment with the tasks. Overall, in the co-orchestration study (Florido et al., 2024a), students have positively recognized th e utility of the tool for accessing supplementary examples and explanations, which serve as valuable additions to teacher-led orchestration. Yet, at the same time, the scena rio adds a new type of teacher supervision needs, i.e. supporting the writing of prompts and supervising the generating answers.

Regarding c), a study with teachers illuminates how difficult it is still for the teachers to make informed decisions about whether and how to incorporate GenA I in the design of their lesson plans, including th ose involving collaborative learning (Hernande z-Leo, 2024). Our research articulated in workshops w ith teachers show that while teachers are able to see some benef its to improve effectiveness, efficiency and enjoym ent facets of learning designs, they are unsure about if the p otential harms (from cognitive dependency effects t o environment impact) could surpass the benefits. The y wondered how, with careful management and ethical considerations, the benefits of AI could substantially outweigh its drawbacks.

## Discussion (challenges, lessons learned)

Our research calls for more evidence and more human-centred approaches to conceiving the introduction o f GenAI in collaboration for learning approaches. In relation to a) GenAI and CSCL scripting, we see opportunities to contribute to scaffolding individual and group c ontributions and to enriching group discussions. Ye t, we also have seen potentially negative effects in cognitive processes (e.g. fantasy in cognitive empathy) and overreliance on the generated contributions. We believe that thi s evidence can inform strategies for Human-AI scripted collaboration of designing for “where interaction should happen (Dillenbourg et al., 2009)”, to maximize benefits and counteract the negative effects. Regarding b), we have first evidence illustrating the value for G enAI to support help-seeking as part of the orchestration of problem-solving classroom sessions. Yet, the study also shows that these scenarios add further requirements to th e orchestration tasks of the teacher, related to su pervising the use of GenAI. Fin ally, in relation to c), it is clear that future re search and practice should seek answers to the question addressed in this symposium by identifying the best possible strategies that maximize the provision and protection of the students, while ensuring the part icipation of stakeholders in the design process, in cluding researchers, technology developers, as well as   teachers and learners. To mitigate these risks and maximize the benefits of GenAI, evidence-based and human-centered approaches are essential.
